Across Barriers: Blood–Brain and Gut Barrier Signaling in Psychiatric Disorders
Authors: Luisa Bandeira Binder, Laura Menegatti Bevilacqua, Flora Gro Lorentzen Thomassen, Manon Lebel, Benjamin A H Jensen, Caroline Menard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPsychiatric disorders affect millions of people worldwide. Despite widespread use of conventional treatments targeting monoaminergic systems, remission rates remain low, and many individuals experience treatment resistance or relapse. Consequently, there has been growing interest in the involvement of other systems, with exacerbated immune responses and barrier alterations reported in clinical settings and preclinical models. Indeed, emerging evidence supports disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and intestinal barrier in the etiology and progression of psychiatric conditions, notably major depression, bipolar disorder, and generalized anxiety. The BBB is a highly selective structure whose integrity is maintained by endothelial cells, astrocytes, pericytes, and cellular adhesion molecules. Loss of BBB integrity has been increasingly recognized not only as a marker of psychiatric disorders but also as a contributing factor in their development. The BBB and intestinal barrier share anatomical features and functions, especially with the gut–vascular barrier, which remains understudied. Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a hallmark of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a condition with a high rate of comorbidity with psychiatric disorders. Both barriers are characterized by similar cellular components and signaling pathways regulating permeability. Psychological stress, a major risk factor for psychiatric conditions and IBD, renders the BBB and intestinal barrier hyperpermeable, feeding a vicious cycle of exacerbated inflammation and ultimately, mood changes as discussed here. We highlight key signaling pathways linked to barrier development and function, including Wnt/β‐catenin, VEGF, and FGF‐2, and argue that they may contribute to the pathophysiology of mental disorders and IBD, and could be targeted to develop innovative diagnostic tools and treatments. Key limitations and knowledge gaps are reviewed. To sum up, barrier‐related alterations have long been reported in clinical studies in psychiatry and are now receiving increasing attention at the mechanistic level, as they may be relevant to uncovering new therapeutic targets beyond traditional monoamine‐focused treatments. Keywords: anxiety, blood–brain barrier, depression, gut–brain axis, gut–vascular barrier, stress

Introduction

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is the brain's first line of defense, composed of a complex network of endothelial cells, astrocytes, and pericytes (Abbott et al. 2010). The BBB regulates bidirectional trafficking of ions and molecules between the blood and the brain parenchyma, while preventing the entry of harmful substances (e.g., toxins, immune cells, etc.) into the central nervous system (CNS). Optimal BBB function is essential for CNS homeostasis, metabolism, and neuronal function, with BBB dysfunction associated with neuroinflammation, maladaptive stress responses, and psychiatric disorders (Galea 2021; Takata et al. 2021; Dudek et al. 2025; Menard et al. 2017).

Emerging evidence reveals striking parallels between the BBB and the intestinal barrier, both of which rely on cellular adhesion molecules (CAMs) and immune‐regulatory mechanisms to maintain their integrity (Aburto and Cryan 2024). Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), characterized by chronic intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction of temporal intensity, has been linked to a higher risk of psychiatric disorders (Bisgaard et al. 2022). A plausible feature connecting gut barrier dysfunction to psychiatric disorders is the spillover of local to systemic inflammation, which eventually propagates to the brain and further compromises BBB integrity (Doney et al. 2022; Mitchell et al. 2022). Conversely, psychological stress is known to impair both the intestinal barrier and BBB, highlighting shared mechanistic pathways (Dion‐Albert, Cadoret, et al. 2022; Doney et al. 2024).

In this review, we first provide an overview of the BBB structure, function, and signaling, followed by a summary of studies supporting the contribution of BBB alterations in major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). We also explore how gut–brain barrier interactions may contribute to elevated vulnerability to psychiatric disorders observed in IBD patients. Next, we highlight cellular and molecular signaling pathways relating BBB and intestinal barrier dysfunction to psychiatric conditions. With barrier dysfunction increasingly recognized as a component of mental health‐related conditions, identifying pathways driving brain and gut barrier hyperpermeability is critical to better understand the pathophysiology of mental disorders and help identify novel therapeutic targets.

Prevalence and Diagnosis of Mental Health Disorders

Psychiatric disorders affect almost one billion people worldwide and are characterized by a clinical disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotional regulation, and behavior (American Psychiatric Association 2013). Among these conditions, GAD and MDD are the most prevalent, affecting 4.05% and 3.5% of the global population, respectively, while BD is diagnosed in 2.4% of individuals (Javaid et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025). To date, the diagnosis of mental health disorders is based solely on self‐report symptom assessments with the support of classification guides like the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders—V. Psychiatric disorders are heterogeneous, complex, and express a wide range of symptoms that often overlap between conditions. This contributes to high rates of misdiagnosis, with estimates suggesting that up to 30% of patients with MDD and 60% of those with BD are initially misdiagnosed (Nasrallah 2015). In addition, delayed diagnosis can lead to a lack of functional improvement and lower rates of remission (Bradford et al. 2024).

Unfortunately, commonly prescribed antidepressant drugs can result in side effects such as weight gain, sleep disturbances, sexual dysfunction, and others, leading to high rates of discontinued treatments and low remission (Nasrallah 2015; Pillinger et al. 2023; Scholten et al. 2023). Moreover, a large proportion of individuals are unresponsive or respond poorly, with treatment resistance or relapse estimated at 30%–50% for MDD and 26%–52% for BD (Krishnan and Nestler 2008; Monroe and Harkness 2022). Another key factor overlooked in psychiatric research is sex‐specific prevalence and symptomatology. Women are twice as likely to develop MDD and GAD as men (Howell et al. 2001). Although BD doesn't have a well‐established sex‐related prevalence, women show longer duration of untreated illness and higher severity of mania when compared to men reviewed in (Dion‐Albert et al. 2022).

Among the systems involved in psychiatric disorder pathology, immune dysregulation and neuroinflammation have been increasingly associated with reduced treatment response, increased susceptibility, and symptom severity (for more, refer to Doney et al. 2022; Kouba et al. 2024). As an example, PET‐scan studies have shown neuroinflammatory responses in individuals with MDD (Richards et al. 2018) and post‐traumatic stress disorder (Attwells et al. 2017). Overall, these findings reinforce the need to further investigate non‐neuronal systems, such as the involvement of inflammation and BBB alterations, in the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders.

The Blood–Brain Barrier: From Wall to Dynamic Frontier

The BBB is a complex structure that maintains the immune‐privileged environment of the CNS (Wolburg et al. 2009). It is formed by a monolayer of brain endothelial cells sealed by intercellular junctional complexes, formed by CAMs (Figure 1) composed of tight junction (TJ) (e.g., claudin and occludin, OCLN), cytosolic (e.g., zonula occludens, ZO‐1), and adherens junction (AJ) proteins (e.g., VE‐cadherin protein), regulating the passage of ions and molecules while establishing cell polarity and barrier integrity (Greene et al. 2019; Vorbrodt and Dobrogowska 2003) (Table 1). CAMs are essential for maintaining BBB integrity, preventing the entry of harmful molecules from the periphery. Claudin‐5 (CLDN5), a hallmark of the BBB, is the most expressed TJ protein in brain endothelial cells, fundamental for establishing a paracellular seal that provides electrical resistance (Greene et al. 2019). CLDN5 deficiency leads to increased BBB permeability, promotes neuroinflammation, and induces endothelial transcriptional changes related to angiogenesis, cellular structure, and proliferation (Menard et al. 2017, 2024; Vázquez‐Liébanas et al. 2024), supporting a key role for CLDN5 in maintaining BBB structure and function. CNS immune response and cell recruitment are regulated by endothelial cells through the expression of CAMs. In response to the release of inflammatory mediators, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), or viral infection, P‐ and E‐selectins mediate leukocyte adhesion to endothelial cells. Endothelial cells then express intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM‐1) and the vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM‐1), further promoting leukocyte adhesion (Kong et al. 2018; Singh et al. 2023), extravasation into the parenchyma, and culminating in T‐cell activation, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response (Singh et al. 2021).

Cellular and molecular components involved in blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity. The BBB is a dynamic structure composed of endothelial cells (red), pericytes (orange), and astrocytic endfeet (green) that modulate brain homeostasis. Endothelial cells are connected by tight junctions (Claudins 3/5, Occludin, ZO‐1/2/3) and adherens junctions (VE‐cadherin), while also expressing PECAM1 and connexin‐43 (Cx43), which contribute to barrier selectivity and leukocyte trafficking. Astrocytes modulate barrier function through their perivascular endfeet, which are enriched in aquaporin‐4 (Aqp4), a water channel involved in fluid regulation; Kir4.1, a potassium channel important for ion homeostasis; and Cx30/Cx43, which facilitates intercellular communication. They also secrete trophic factors such as glial cell‐derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), which enhance endothelial stability. Pericytes regulate endothelial signaling, vascular permeability, and capillary diameter via PDGFB/PDGFRB signaling, and are characterized by markers including NG2, CD13, and Desmin. Disruption of these molecular pathways can compromise BBB integrity, contributing to the development of psychiatric disorders, such as depression, bipolar disorder, and generalized anxiety disorder.

Brain endothelial cells' physiology is regulated by two main signaling pathways: Wnt/β‐catenin and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling, both essential for angiogenesis and BBB formation. Wnt/β‐catenin signaling is vital during embryogenesis, driving vascular development and BBB formation (Xiang et al. 2025), while maintaining barrier integrity and pericyte coverage via Platelet‐derived growth factor β (PDGFβ) in adulthood (Tran et al. 2016). It promotes the transcription of BBB‐related genes, including TJs and the glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) gene, the main transporter across the BBB (Obermeier et al. 2013). Thus, dysregulation and inhibition of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling are linked to impaired angiogenesis and BBB leakage, along with diminished glucose transport to the brain, which is vital (Liebner et al. 2008; Parab et al. 2023). In parallel, VEGF‐A and VEGF‐B, the most expressed factors in BBB cell components, are critical for angiogenesis and inflammatory responses (Argaw et al. 2012). VEGF‐A, mainly released by astrocytes, acts via VEGF receptor 2 as a primary driver of vessel formation during development. In adulthood, besides its localized expression to vascular beds, VEGF‐A production in response to hypoxia and inflammatory challenges leads to vascular leakage. VEGF‐B, released by neural progenitor cells, activates VEGF receptor 1, directing new vascular formation (Obermeier et al. 2013). Overall, VEGF signaling regulates endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and survival, playing a critical role in guiding vascular sprouting during CNS development. Dysregulation of this pathway can compromise BBB integrity and has been implicated in various mental disorders (Castillo et al. 2020; Lee and Kim 2012; Olsson et al. 2006).

Astrocytes, another crucial component of the BBB, are the most abundant glial cell type in the CNS. Their perivascular endfeet enwrap the vasculature, covering over 95% of the outer surface of endothelial cells (Benz and Liebner 2020; Mills et al. 2022), acting as a physical barrier and being involved in metabolic support, release of trophic factors, and regulation of vascular regulators essential for optimal BBB function. Ion homeostasis is maintained by aquaporin 4 (AQP4), a water channel considered a marker of perivascular astrocytes (Nagelhus and Ottersen 2013) (Table 1). Astrocytes actively respond to CNS insults such as trauma, infection, and inflammation. Reactive astrogliosis is a heterogeneous response that ranges from altered gene expression to permanent tissue rearrangement (e.g., scar formation) and cell hypertrophy (Anderson et al. 2014). The latter is observed through increased expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a structural filament protein considered the gold standard marker for characterizing astrocyte morphology. Through abundant expression of gap junction proteins like connexin 30 (Cx30) and 43 (Cx43), astrocytes form a complex glial network that supports and modulates BBB and neuronal function, acting both locally within microdomains and across brain circuits (De Bock et al. 2017). Disruptions of this network, via signaling or connexin dysfunction, can compromise BBB function by impairing astrocyte communication with endothelial cells, reducing neurogliovascular coupling, and weakening TJ regulation (De Bock et al. 2017; Ezan et al. 2012).

Astrocytes are also essential for the release and regulation of VEGF‐A, fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), and glial‐derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF). In adulthood, VEGFA expression is directly linked to brain endothelial cell fenestration and BBB leakage (Dobrogowska et al. 1998; Xiang et al. 2025). Both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that in neuroinflammatory conditions, VEGFA production by astrocytes leads to decreased levels of CLDN5/OCLN and BBB leakage (Argaw et al. 2009). FGF2, a major component of the FGF family, is primarily released by astrocytes and is well known for its angiogenic properties. It induces the migration and proliferation of brain endothelial cells (Dono 1998) and can prevent the inflammatory activation of the endothelium, as well as subsequent loss of barrier integrity, by modulating Cldn5 expression (Pallotta and Nickel 2020; Menard et al. 2025). Lastly, GDNF is an astrocytic trophic factor known for its role in plasticity and cognition (Popova et al. 2017). There has been growing interest in the role of GDNFs in BBB function. In vitro studies suggest that it can upregulate expression of Cldn5 and VE‐cadherin, essential for inflammatory response and BBB integrity (Kanjanasirirat et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024). Xiaoling et al. (2024) also demonstrated that AAV‐mediated expression of Gdnf can lead to antidepressant‐like behavior and reduced gut inflammation in a neurodegenerative mouse model, highlighting its potential in modulating both the BBB and intestinal barrier.

Pericytes are mural cells found enclosed in the same basement membrane as brain endothelial cells, considered essential for BBB development and maintenance. They suppress endothelial transcytosis, promote TJ expression, and polarize astrocyte endfeet (Armulik et al. 2010). Pericytes are recognized to play crucial roles in BBB functioning, although their precise definition remains debated. They can be identified by their perivascular position and ubiquitous expression of PDGF receptor β (PDGFRβ) (Smyth et al. 2018; Winkler et al. 2010), Desmin (Rustenhoven et al. 2018), and CD146 (Chen, Luo, et al. 2017) (Table 1). Although PDGFRβ is not a specific marker for pericytes, it is necessary for their recruitment by endothelial cells. PDGFβ is released by angiogenic brain endothelial cells, creating a concentration gradient in the basement membrane that attracts PDGFRβ+ pericytes. PDGFβ‐PDGFRβ signaling is essential for developing optimal pericyte coverage of blood vessels and stabilizing pericyte‐endothelium physical interactions (Armulik et al. 2005). A recent single‐cell transcriptomics study identified Atp13a5 as a cell‐specific marker for mouse pericytes (Guo et al. 2024), enabling interrogation of pericyte heterogeneity and function (Menard et al. 2024). Pericytes also express non‐cell‐specific markers according to their location; for example, neural/glial antigen 2 (NG2) and α‐smooth muscle cell actin (αSMA) are found on arterial pericytes, but not in pericytes ensheathing capillaries (Crisan et al. 2012).

Pericytes are also essential for regulating cerebral blood flow, hypoxia, and hypoglycemia response, maintaining CNS homeostasis (reviewed in Armulik et al. 2011). Loss or dysfunction of pericytes, observed in aging, neuroinflammation, and after chronic stress exposure, compromises BBB integrity and uncouples the neurogliovascular unit (Brown et al. 2019; Menard et al. 2024; Takata et al. 2021). In addition to their vascular roles, pericytes participate in immune‐related regulation by secreting cytokines and chemokines, facilitating leukocyte recruitment, and extravasation under inflammatory conditions (Rustenhoven et al. 2018; Stark et al. 2013).

Molecular Signatures of the BBB in Psychiatric Disorders: Clinical and Preclinical Evidence

Both preclinical and clinical research indicate that BBB dysfunction is not only a secondary effect of psychiatric disorders but may actively drive their onset and progression (Hochman et al. 2023; Menard et al. 2017). At the cellular level, brain endothelial cell dysfunction is marked by reduced expression of TJ proteins such as CLDN5 and ZO‐1, leading to compromised endothelial integrity and increased permeability to peripheral molecules like cytokines, fibrinogen, and immunoglobulins (Greene et al. 2020; Voirin et al. 2020). These peripheral factors, once they enter the brain, can trigger or amplify neuroinflammation, a process increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of psychiatric conditions (Bauer and Teixeira 2021). Intriguingly, MDD and BD are more prevalent in individuals with a history of inflammatory and vascular diseases (Halaris 2016; Rosenblat and McIntyre 2015). This comorbidity may be partly explained by chronic stress, a known risk factor for both psychiatric disorders and vascular dysfunction, including BBB disruption (Dion‐Albert et al. 2023). Moreover, brain regions critically involved in emotion regulation, including the prefrontal cortex (PFC), hippocampus (Hipp), amygdala (Amy), and nucleus accumbens (NAc), are particularly susceptible to BBB alterations (Daigle et al. 2025; Greene et al. 2020). In addition to stress and inflammation experienced during adulthood, adverse exposures occurring in utero or early in life may impact BBB development and contribute to long‐term psychiatric vulnerability (Gómez‐González and Escobar 2009). Prenatal stress and maternal immune activation disrupt barriergenesis by altering endothelial differentiation and TJ protein expression (Rasile et al. 2022). As for early‐life dysbiosis, it impairs gut barrier maturation and induces systemic inflammation that, in turn, compromises BBB formation (Sarkar et al. 2021). These developmental conditions may lead to heightened permeability and maladaptive responses later in life, becoming a risk factor for mood and anxiety disorders development. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

MDD is the most prevalent psychiatric disorder, representing a major contributor to the global burden of disease (GBD 2022). MDD is multifactorial and can be influenced by genetic predisposition, neurotransmitter dysfunction, and environmental stressors. Notably, the COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with a 25% global increase in MDD diagnosis, highlighting the disorder's sensitivity to social stressors (Santomauro et al. 2021). Clinically, MDD is characterized by persistent depressive mood, sleep disturbances, changes in cognition, and suicidal ideation. Despite being recognized as a mental disorder since the emergence of modern psychiatry in the 18th century, its etiology remains elusive more than two centuries later (Kendler 2020).

Initially, MDD was considered an illness resulting from the dysfunction of neurotransmitter systems, particularly catecholamines. According to the monoaminergic theory of depression, reduced levels of neurotransmitters in the synaptic cleft would alter neuronal transmission and lead to behavioral alterations. This theory gained traction in 1952 with the discovery of the first monoamine‐based antidepressant, originally used to treat tuberculosis (Loomer et al. 1957). Since then, various classes of antidepressants, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), monoamine‐reuptake inhibitors (tricyclic antidepressants), and monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), have been developed, all targeting the bioavailability of monoamines (Cui et al. 2024). Despite their widespread clinical use, these treatments are far from ideal, with only 30% of individuals presenting persistent remission (Monroe and Harkness 2022).

In 1979, the hypothesis of psychoneuroimmunology was proposed based on the work of Herzog et al. (1979), that demonstrated the first link between immune challenges and behavioral outcomes. In 1991, Maes et al. reported that individuals with MDD had several components of the immune system upregulated, with an increased secretion of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), IL‐1β, and interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) (Maes et al. 1991, 1993, 1995). Extensive studies further support involvement of cytokines in emotion regulation (Dantzer 2006; Dantzer et al. 2008). Elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines like IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IFN‐γ correlate with greater MDD severity and/or vulnerability (Harsanyi et al. 2022).

Alongside inflammation, BBB dysfunction has been increasingly associated with MDD pathology. Coppen (1960) was the first to report altered BBB permeability in MDD, when peripheral injection of 24Na radioisotope was used to evaluate infiltration rate from the blood to the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Individuals with MDD presented an altered infiltration rate when compared to healthy controls and fully remitted patients. In 1984, changes in the CSF‐to‐serum ratio of peripheral markers were observed in individuals with MDD, indirectly implicating compromised BBB integrity (Niklasson and Agren 1984). As for treatment‐resistant depression, high circulating level of tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‐α) was noted, suggesting exacerbated immune responses (Eller et al. 2008), possibly leading to impaired BBB integrity.

Blood–Brain Barrier Alterations in MDD

When brain endothelial cells are compromised, peripherally elevated inflammatory mediators commonly found in MDD, including IL‐6, TNF‐α, and C‐reactive protein (CRP), can infiltrate the CNS and contribute to the dysregulation of neural circuits and glial function (Karlović et al. 2012; Powers et al. 2019). These cytokines alter brain endothelial function by downregulating the expression of TJ proteins, initiating a feedforward cycle of neuroinflammation and barrier dysfunction (Kealy et al. 2020). Emerging imaging data support this mechanism: MRI has revealed increased BBB permeability in the Hipp, anterior cingulate gyrus, putamen, and thalamus of MDD individuals, with leakage levels correlating with symptom severity in both women and men (Shang et al. 2024). Postmortem analysis corroborates these findings with decreased levels of CLDN5 in corticolimbic areas, including the NAc, Amy, and Hipp, alongside signs of vascular disruption (Dudek et al. 2020; Greene et al. 2019; Maridaki et al. 2025).

In animal models, chronic stress recapitulates brain endothelial cell dysfunction seen in MDD. Indeed, exposure to chronic social stress creates cerebral microbleeds with extravascular red blood cells in brain tissue, reflecting compromised TJ between brain endothelial cells and BBB disruption (Lehmann et al. 2020). The chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) model, a well‐established paradigm for inducing depressive‐like behaviors in rodents, disrupts BBB integrity in mood‐regulating regions such as the PFC of female and NAc of male mice, driving anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors (Dion‐Albert et al. 2022; Menard et al. 2017). CSDS induces sex‐specific endothelial transcriptomic changes similar to the vascular alterations observed in women with MDD (Dion‐Albert et al. 2022). We further demonstrated how BBB disruption underlies depressive phenotypes: stress‐susceptible mice show reduced Cldn5 expression, a loss not observed in resilient mice. Importantly, Cldn5 expression rescues behavioral deficits, directly linking BBB integrity to mood regulation (Menard et al. 2017). Other stress paradigms, such as chronic unpredictable mild stress and maternal separation, also induce downregulation of TJ proteins, including CLDN5, OCLN, and ZO‐1, along with increased immune cell infiltration in the Hipp and PFC (Kealy et al. 2020; Wu, Chen, et al. 2023).

Astrocytic alterations are also well‐documented in MDD, with individuals exhibiting a loss of astrocytes and reduced expression of astrocytic markers, including GFAP, AQP4, S100β, and glutamatergic transporters (Rajkowska et al. 2013). Moreover, postmortem tissue of individuals with MDD who died by suicide has a widespread downregulation of astrocytic connexin 30 (Cx30, Gjb6) and 43 (Cx43, Gja1) mediated by epigenetic factors (Nagy et al. 2017). In the anterior cingulate cortex (Acc), reduced expression of Cx30 suggests impaired glial communication that may contribute to MDD pathology (Tanti et al. 2019). Beyond neuroimaging and postmortem findings, peripheral biomarkers offer additional evidence of endothelial‐astrocyte dysfunction in MDD. Elevated serum levels of S100β, GFAP, and matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9) have been reported in both early‐stage and treatment‐resistant MDD (Fang et al. 2016; Hsu et al. 2025; Navinés et al. 2022; Xie et al. 2023). Notably, serum S100β has emerged as a potential biomarker for MDD antidepressant treatment response, with low baseline levels predicting the response to venlafaxine, a serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), and imipramine, a tricyclic antidepressant (Ambrée et al. 2016).

Similarly, chronic stress reduces astrocyte density and downregulates AQP4 and glutamate transporters in mice (Imbe et al. 2012; Wei et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019). These effects exhibit sex specificity, with male mice showing greater AQP4 loss, increased BBB leakiness, and heightened behavioral vulnerability compared to females (Bollinger et al. 2025). Impaired astrocyte calcium signaling in the medial PFC is linked to synaptic deficits and depressive‐like behaviors in corticosterone‐treated mice, with optogenetic stimulation of astrocytic calcium activity rescuing these impairments (González‐Arias et al. 2023). Interventions such as physical exercise and environmental enrichment can prevent stress‐induced astrocyte dysfunction by upregulating Fgf2, which preserves endothelial Cldn5 expression and reduces inflammation (Li et al. 2021; Menard et al. 2025). Antidepressants can also modulate astrocyte activity. Ketamine, a fast‐acting antidepressant, has been shown to suppress astrocytic calcium signaling (Stenovec et al. 2020), while fluoxetine reduces reactive astrocyte activation (Fang et al. 2022). As for fluoxetine, it has been implicated in the regulation of angiogenic factors like VEGF‐A in both chronic stress models and individuals with MDD (Allaman et al. 2011). Pharmacological blockade of VEGF‐A receptor is sufficient to prevent BBB leakiness and depressive‐like behaviors following chronic stress exposure (Matsuno et al. 2022). Supporting a central role for astrocyte‐derived VEGF‐A in BBB integrity, mice with astrocytic VEGF‐A depletion exhibit reduced barrier permeability and lower immune cell infiltration (Argaw et al. 2012). Finally, astrocytic cannabinoid receptor 1 expression in the NAc can promote stress resilience by modulating BBB‐related vascular and inflammatory responses (Dudek et al. 2025).

Although less studied, pericytes have recently been implicated in MDD. In a chronic unpredictable stress model, NG2+ cell density was elevated in the Hipp of rats (Treccani et al. 2021). However, this change was not mirrored in post‐mortem MDD samples (Zhang et al. 2014). Notably, NG2 is not a pericyte‐specific marker and can also label oligodendrocyte precursor cells, which may account for discrepancies observed between rodent and human findings (Chang et al. 2000). Recent work demonstrates that pericyte dysfunction in the PFC induces anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors in female mice (Menard et al. 2024). These stress‐induced changes are also noticeable in the PFC of women with MDD, highlighting a sex‐ and possibly region‐specific contribution of pericyte pathology in MDD (Menard et al. 2024).

Bipolar Disorder (BD)

BD is a complex psychiatric condition marked by severe mood fluctuations, ranging from low depressive to manic or hypomanic states. While the disorder has a multifactorial etiology, the biological mechanisms underlying the opposing mood phases remain unclear (Lee et al. 2022). Clinically, BD shares substantial overlap with MDD, particularly during depressive episodes, which are often indistinguishable between the two conditions. This frequently results in misdiagnosis, especially in early stages when only depressive symptoms are present.

The biochemical investigation on BD began in 1944, when elevated circulating adrenaline levels were reported during manic and depressive episodes (Baird 1944). Later, the catecholamine theory of affective disorders was proposed, establishing the unbalanced monoamine neurotransmission as a common factor of MDD and BD pathology (Schildkraut 1965). Currently, BD pathology is associated with the dysregulation of glutamatergic and dopaminergic signaling in limbic regions, which affects sleep, appetite, sexual function, and emotional regulation (Tondo et al. 2017; Ashok et al. 2017; Guglielmo et al. 2022). Yet, each of these pathways explain only a subset of BD symptoms.

Lithium, a mood stabilizer introduced 70 years ago, remains a first‐line treatment for BD. Despite its efficacy, only 30% of lithium‐treated patients achieve full remission, while an additional 33% exhibit a partial response (Fountoulakis et al. 2022; Mamdani et al. 2004). Lithium can modulate the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and TNFα. Evidence from animal models further supports lithium's potential to attenuate neuroinflammation by reducing cytokine levels and enhancing anti‐inflammatory responses (Boufidou et al. 2004; Damri and Agam 2024).

The first biochemical evidence of BBB disruption in BD came in 2007, when Andreazza et al. reported elevated serum levels of the astrocytic marker S100β in both depressive and manic states. Later, BBB disruption and neuroinflammation were proposed as contributing mechanisms to the etiology of BD (Patel and Frey 2015). Over the past decade, transient or sustained loss of BBB integrity has been associated with the development and progression of BD (Endres et al. 2016). BBB dysfunction in BD pathology has received increasing support in the psychiatric research field (Millett et al. 2025), with neuroimaging studies reporting BBB leakage in up to 30% of individuals with BD, and a correlation with symptom severity (Karakatsani et al. 2019). Thus, MRI‐based assessments of BBB permeability may be useful as a biomarker for severe or treatment‐resistant BD (Kamintsky et al. 2020).

Blood–Brain Barrier Alterations in BD

Region‐specific vascular remodeling has been observed in individuals with BD, including increased vessel diameter in the Hipp and orbitofrontal cortex, alterations not found in other psychiatric conditions like MDD or schizophrenia (Greene et al. 2020). Additionally, reduced expression of TJ, particularly CLDN5 and OCLN, has been reported in corticolimbic areas such as the Acc, Hipp, and PFC, suggesting BBB disruption in brain areas critical for emotion regulation and cognitive processing (Greene et al. 2019). These TJ alterations reflect dysfunction in the brain endothelial cells.

Peripheral biomarkers further support BBB disruption in BD. Elevated serum levels of CLDN5 have been detected in individuals with BD, regardless of mood state, suggesting a persistent alteration in barrier integrity (Kılıç et al. 2020). Notably, Hochman et al. (2023) reported increased CLDN5 in BD but not in MDD, pointing to a potential neurovascular biomarker that distinguishes between mood disorders (Hochman et al. 2023). Additional peripheral markers of endothelial cell activation and inflammation, such as VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1, MMP‐9, and CRP, are also elevated in individuals with BD, further indicating systemic vascular involvement (Angrand et al. 2021; Hsu et al. 2025; Schaefer et al. 2016). Conversely, levels of circulating endothelial progenitor cells, which modulate vascular repair and maintenance, are reduced and show a negative correlation with manic symptom severity (Liou et al. 2021, 2022). Importantly, glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3β) signaling, a key regulator of endothelial cell stability and inflammation, has been implicated in the therapeutic effects of lithium. Lithium directly inhibits GSK‐3β, stabilizing endothelial cell function and enhancing BBB integrity (Beaulieu et al. 2009; Freland and Beaulieu 2012). Notably, GSK‐3β is a central regulator of the Wnt/B‐catenin pathway, which is essential for maintaining BBB integrity (Liebner et al. 2008).

Astrocytic involvement in BD has been reported with less consistency. Postmortem analyses have not shown significant differences in astrocyte density between individuals with BD and matched controls (Gigase et al. 2019). However, circulating levels of the astrocytic marker S100β have been frequently observed as elevated during manic episodes (Andreazza et al. 2007; da Rosa et al. 2016), although more recent studies have reported mixed findings, ranging from no change to decreased levels (Zhao et al. 2022). These discrepancies may reflect differences in mood phase, medication status, or methodological variability. To elucidate the functional role of astrocytes in BD, preclinical models have focused on ion regulation mechanisms in which astrocytes play a critical role. The Na+/K+‐ATPase pump, highly expressed in astrocytes, includes subunits with distinct distributions: α2 is predominantly astrocytic, while α3 is mainly neuronal but interacts with glial signaling (Kirshenbaum, Clapcote, et al. 2011). Disruption of the α3 subunit in mice results in manic‐like behaviors, such as hyperactivity, suggesting a role for Na+/K+ imbalance in the pathogenesis of mania. Conversely, Atp1a3 heterozygous mice subjected to chronic stress show increased susceptibility to depressive‐like behaviors, suggesting that pump dysfunction may modulate mood polarity depending on environmental context (Kirshenbaum, Saltzman, et al. 2011). Similarly, intracerebroventricular administration of ouabain, a potent Na+/K+‐ATPase inhibitor, induces manic‐like behavior in rodents, reversible by lithium. Although ouabain affects both neurons and glia, its impact on astrocytic control of extracellular potassium and glutamate likely contributes to the observed phenotypes (Valvassori et al. 2019).

To our knowledge, no study has directly explored the role of pericytes in BD. However, converging evidence from other psychiatric conditions, such as MDD, where reduced pericyte coverage and BBB dysfunction have been noted (Menard et al. 2024), suggests that pericyte dysfunction could represent a critical yet understudied component in BD pathophysiology.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Anxiety‐related psychiatric disorders are characterized by excessive and persistent fear triggered by various stimuli (e.g., objects, situations, internal and external emotional cues) in the absence of a real danger (Kong et al. 2014). Excessive concern, restlessness, difficulty sleeping, tiredness, irritability, sweating, and trembling are some of the symptoms that may be present, strongly affecting the quality of life of individuals with GAD (Strawn et al. 2018). Similar to MDD, the burden of anxiety‐related disorders has increased since the COVID‐19 pandemic, with a reported 25.6% rise in prevalence (Santomauro et al. 2021).

Although pharmacological treatments can reduce symptoms of fear and anxiety, only 40% of individuals show long‐term improvement (Hoffman and Mathew 2008). Current pharmacotherapies for GAD include SSRIs, SNRIs, benzodiazepines, and MAOIs, initially developed for MDD or psychosis but adapted at lower doses for GAD management (Strawn et al. 2018). The efficacy of monoamine‐related treatments suggests that dysregulation of the monoaminergic system is at least partially responsible for GAD symptoms. GAD has long been associated as a comorbidity of both MDD and BD (Roomruangwong et al. 2018), with anxiety diagnosis considered a risk factor for subsequent development of MDD. Despite symptomatic and mechanistic overlaps, these conditions are clinically distinct, and the lack of disorder‐specific treatments brings to light the major gaps in the pathophysiology of GAD and anxiety‐related disorders.

Like MDD, chronic stress is a major etiological factor for anxiety‐related disorders and is known to exacerbate inflammatory response in both the CNS and periphery, potentially inducing BBB dysfunction. One of the earliest indications of altered neuroimmune regulations in GAD was from Šakić et al. (1994), who reported anxiety‐like behaviors in an animal model of autoimmune disease. Schrott and Crnic (1996) observed similar effects following induction of IFN‐γ signaling. Shalev et al. (2009) later proposed the BBB hypothesis of mood regulation, linking BBB disruption, astrocytic activation, and neuroinflammation to behavioral alterations. Clinically, individuals with GAD exhibit elevated peripheral markers of inflammation, including CRP, IL‐6, and IL‐1β (Copeland et al. 2012; Passos et al. 2015).

Blood–Brain Barrier Alterations in GAD

Emerging evidence indicates vascular dysregulation in the onset and persistence of anxiety symptoms. Individuals with GAD show abnormal cerebral blood flow regulation, reversible with successful GAD treatment, highlighting vascular dysfunction as a contributor to anxiety disorders (Guo et al. 2017). Peripheral indicators of endothelial integrity have also been linked to GAD. Lower circulating levels of VEGF and elevated IL‐1β correlate with higher anxiety scores in healthy young adults, reinforcing a potential inflammatory‐endothelial link to anxiety etiology (Katsuura et al. 2011). In individuals with acute coronary syndrome and comorbid GAD, reduced levels of endothelial progenitor cells are associated with anxiety severity (Felice et al. 2015). Similarly, adults with high‐anxiety scores display impaired flow‐mediated dilation of the brachial artery, suggesting that anxiety contributes to systemic endothelial dysfunction, which could extend to the neurovascular coupling (Narita et al. 2007).

Preclinical models have provided mechanistic insights into how brain endothelial cell dysfunction may contribute to anxiety phenotypes. For instance, impairment of cerebrovascular activity by a reduction of CO2 in the Amy exacerbates anxiety‐like behaviors (Wenzel et al. 2020). Moreover, social stress paradigms, such as repeated social stress, upregulate VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1 gene expression in the PFC and Amy, key anxiety‐processing regions, thereby promoting immune cell recruitment and neuroinflammation via chemokine signaling (Sawicki et al. 2015). Additionally, endothelial IL‐1R1 has been identified as a critical mediator of anxiety‐like behaviors following social stress. Mice with endothelial IL‐1R1 knockdown exhibit attenuated inflammatory responses and were protected from stress‐induced anxiety, despite immune cell infiltration (Wohleb et al. 2014). In parallel, the IL‐1‐driven expression of inflammatory mediators, such as ICAM‐1, lipocalin‐2 (LCN2), and cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2), in brain endothelial cells contributes to hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation and anxiety‐like behavioral phenotypes. Indeed, pharmacological inhibition of COX‐2 mitigates these effects, supporting a causal role for brain endothelial inflammation in anxiety disorders (Yin et al. 2022).

Beyond vascular regulation, brain endothelial cells can influence emotional behavior by modulating neuronal systems. Endothelin‐1 (ET‐1) is an endogenous peptide produced by endothelial cells known for its role as a vasoconstrictor and regulation of vascular blood flow (Thorin and Webb 2010). In the brain, ET‐1 modulates excitability in the basolateral Amy, with high‐anxiety mice exhibiting reduced endothelin‐1 expression, and its knockdown exacerbating anxiety‐like phenotypes (Chen, Yan, et al. 2017). ET‐1 can be used to induce a local stroke, and injection into the NAc induces BBB disruption, glial activation, and social avoidance without affecting anxiety behaviors, suggesting that vascular disruption by ET‐1 could have a region‐specific impact on behaviors (Bouchard et al. 2025).

Astrocytic dysfunction has also emerged as a contributing factor to anxiety. Notably, GDNF is elevated in a monoamine‐independent manner following amitriptyline antidepressant treatment (Hisaoka et al. 2007). Clinically, increased serum GDNF levels have been observed in individuals with GAD, social phobia, and obsessive‐compulsive disorder, indirectly supporting the hypothesis of BBB dysfunction in GAD (Moreira et al. 2018). Astrocytic glucocorticoid receptors further modulate stress responses by regulating calcium signaling and the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (Lu et al. 2022). Disruption of these glucocorticoid receptors compromises neurovascular coupling and increases vulnerability to anxiety phenotypes (Tertil et al. 2018). In juvenile mice, chronic social isolation leads to anxiety‐like behaviors specifically in females, accompanied by microglial activation, elevated cytokines, and downregulation of Cldn5 in the Amy, suggesting sex‐specific astrocyte‐mediated BBB alterations (Wu et al. 2022). Furthermore, hippocampal astrocyte calcium signaling modulates anxiety‐like behaviors through enhancing excitatory transmission in the dentate gyrus, indicating that hippocampal astrocyte activity is a potential therapeutic target for anxiety (Cho et al. 2022).

The role of pericytes in anxiety phenotypes has only been suggested recently. Our group showed that in the PFC of stressed female mice and women with MDD, pericytes exhibit transcriptional, morphological, and functional abnormalities. Disruption of PDGFβ‐PDGFRβ signaling compromises BBB integrity and induces anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors. In contrast, enhancement of this endothelial cell–pericyte communication pathway promotes stress resilience (Menard et al. 2024), suggesting that pericyte dysfunction represents a shared pathophysiological mechanism across mood and anxiety disorders.

Barrier Breakdown in Intestinal Inflammation and Mental Disorder Comorbidities

The intestinal barrier, also known as the gut barrier, refers to the structural and functional components that maintain the integrity of the small and large intestines (Jensen et al. 2023). It consists of a mucus lining, an epithelial barrier, and a gut–vascular barrier (GVB), which together facilitate nutrient absorption while protecting the internal environment from harmful substances and microbes present in the gut lumen (Aburto and Cryan 2024; Martel et al. 2022). The protective mucus lining (Figure 2) comprises a single layer in the small intestine, while in the large intestine, it is organized into two layers: an outer, loose layer that harbors commensal microbes, and an inner, dense layer that remains largely free of microbes under healthy conditions (for detailed overviews, see Jensen et al. 2021, 2023). The structural and functional components of these mucus layers are glycosylated proteins called mucins (Table 1), which are categorized into two types: transmembrane mucins that cover the apical surface of enterocytes, and gel‐forming mucins that are synthesized and secreted by goblet cells (Paone and Cani 2020). The mucus layer also contains immunoglobulin A, secreted by plasma cells in the lamina propria, which binds to bacteria and bacterial toxins, thereby preventing their adhesion to and invasion of the epithelial barrier (Martel et al. 2022).

Structural and molecular organization of the intestinal barrier. The intestinal barrier is composed of a mucus lining (which, in the large intestine, consists of two layers: an outer loose layer and an inner, dense layer), an epithelial monolayer with immune cells residing in the lamina propria, and a gut vascular barrier (GVB), all of which work together to regulate nutrient absorption and prevent the translocation of microbes, microbial antigens, and toxins. The outer mucus layer harbors commensal bacteria that, among other functions, aid in digesting fibers. The inner mucus layer primarily acts as a physical and biochemical defense against pathogens. The main component of the mucus lining is mucin‐2, a gel‐forming protein secreted by goblet cells (shown in yellow) in the epithelium. Beneath the mucus, enterocytes (shown in orange) and other specialized cells, including stem cells, tuft cells, and enteroendocrine cells, form a continuous epithelial layer that is sealed by tight junction (TJ) proteins, such as Claudins and Occludin, as well as adherens junctions (AJs) and junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), which regulate paracellular transport. The gut vascular barrier consists of fenestrated endothelial cells. Enteric glial cells (purple) and pericytes (light yellow) support the GVB and contribute to vascular exchange. Endothelial expression of plasmalemma vesicle‐associated protein‐1 (PV1) is linked to fenestrae formation, and its upregulation increases permeability. Disruption of various components of the intestinal barrier has been linked to increased intestinal permeability, altered immune signaling, and systemic inflammation triggered by microbial translocation, all of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of mood and anxiety disorders.

The intestinal epithelial barrier is composed of absorptive enterocytes, as well as specialized cell types, including goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells, tuft cells, and intestinal stem cells. These cells, similar to the brain endothelial cells of the BBB, are interconnected by TJs, adherens junctions, and other CAMs, including epithelial CAM (EpCAM/CD326), often used as an intestinal epithelial marker (Table 1) (Aburto and Cryan 2024; Chen et al. 2020). A wide variety of claudin isoforms are expressed throughout the intestinal epithelium, with their expression patterns varying depending on the intestinal segment (Table 1) (Lu et al. 2013). Beneath the epithelium, innate and adaptive immune cells reside in the lamina propria, poised to eliminate invading pathogens (Martel et al. 2022). Notably, enteric glial cells regulate intestinal barrier function similarly to astrocytes in the BBB, sharing identical morphological characteristics and expression markers (Table 1) (for further details, refer to Yu 2014).

The GVB, only recently recognized as a distinct component of the intestinal barrier, comprises the intestinal microvasculature and forms a physical barrier that prevents bacteria and harmful substances from translocating from the lamina propria into the systemic circulation (Carloni et al. 2021). The GVB shares several similarities with the BBB. It consists of endothelial cells interconnected by TJs and adherens junctions (Table 1). These cells also express adhesion molecules, such as VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1, which participate in the recruitment of immune cells. Moreover, glial cells and pericytes are closely associated with the intestinal endothelium, analogous to the BBB structure (Aburto and Cryan 2024; Jingjie and Jun 2023). Precise functions of these supporting cells in maintaining GVB integrity remain to be elucidated. Current evidence suggests that enteric glial cells may contribute to GVB integrity by releasing S‐nitrosoglutathione, a signaling mediator that enhances TJ protein expression, reducing paracellular permeability (Savidge et al. 2007). Despite similarities, the GVB is considerably more permeable than the BBB, allowing the passage of molecules up to approximately 4 kDa, compared to the much tighter cutoff of the BBB (0.4–0.6 kDa) and unlike the continuous endothelial cells of the BBB, intestinal endothelial cells are fenestrated, containing small pores (Jingjie and Jun 2023; Wu, Wang, et al. 2023). These fenestrations are regulated by plasmalemma vesicle‐associated protein‐1 (PV1) (Table 1), which is crucial for forming diaphragms in fenestrated endothelium and modulating vascular permeability (Carloni et al. 2021; Jingjie and Jun 2023).

Interestingly, intestinal barrier disruption can lead to chronic intestinal inflammation (Turpin et al. 2020), which has been associated with increased BBB permeability (Mitchell et al. 2022). In line with this, IBD, a chronic inflammatory condition of temporal intensity, characterized by intestinal barrier dysfunction, is strongly linked to stress and exhibits high comorbidity with mental disorders (Bisgaard et al. 2022; Doney et al. 2022). A large meta‐analysis reported pooled prevalence rates of 32.1% for anxiety symptoms and 25.2% for depressive symptoms in IBD patients, with even higher rates in periods of active disease (Barberio et al. 2021). In addition, two separate population‐based studies identified an increased risk of BD in individuals with IBD (Bernstein et al. 2019; Kao et al. 2019). Animal models of IBD are characterized by anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors (Zhou et al. 2023; Talley et al. 2021; Nyuyki et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2024). Conversely, psychosocial stress disrupts both the intestinal barrier and the BBB (Doney et al. 2024; Menard et al. 2017), suggesting a bidirectional relationship where intestinal inflammation can influence emotional regulation, while stress exacerbates intestinal inflammation (Doney et al. 2022).

Given the structural and functional parallels between the gut barrier and the BBB, and the critical involvement of BBB dysfunction in MDD, GAD, and BD, unraveling how impairments in both barriers interact in psychiatric disorders and their comorbidities, including IBD, could uncover novel therapeutic strategies to alleviate anxiety and depressive symptoms. The gut and brain communicate bidirectionally through neural, endocrine, metabolic, and immune pathways (Cryan et al. 2019). While the gut microbiota critically shapes these interactions, and dysbiosis itself can induce barrier dysfunction and contribute to mental disorders, this aspect was previously extensively reviewed (Aburto and Cryan 2024; Parker et al. 2020; Zhuang et al. 2024). In the following section, we will focus on immune‐related and shared signaling mechanisms that may link intestinal barrier disruption and inflammation to BBB dysfunction, offering mechanistic insights into IBD and mental disorder comorbidities.

Intestinal‐Barrier Disruption in IBD

Disruption of the intestinal mucus lining and increased permeability, resulting from epithelial barrier and GVB dysregulation, allow microbial translocation into the lamina propria and systemic circulation, triggering immune activation and inflammatory cascades (Aburto and Cryan 2024; Guan 2019). Such barrier dysfunctions and resulting intestinal inflammation are hallmarks of IBD, an umbrella term for the chronic intestinal inflammatory conditions Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis (Guan 2019). Breakdown of the mucus lining includes decreased goblet cell numbers and reduced expression of mucin protein 2 (MUC2), both essential for barrier integrity (Nyström et al. 2021; Singh et al. 2022; van der Post et al. 2019). In mice, goblet cell loss increases susceptibility to dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)‐induced colitis (Nyström et al. 2021), a widely used ulcerative colitis animal model (Chassaing et al. 2014). Similarly, Muc2‐deficient mice spontaneously develop colitis and exhibit worsened DSS responses (Van Der Sluis et al. 2006).

Claudin proteins are key to intestinal barrier function, and altered expression has been reported in both clinical and preclinical IBD studies. For instance, CLDN3, one of the most abundant intestinal TJ proteins, is downregulated in individuals with IBD, and its loss increases intestinal permeability and worsens DSS‐induced colitis in mice (Ahmad et al. 2017, 2023). Similarly, CLDN7 is reduced in the large intestine of individuals with IBD and in DSS‐treated mice, and Cldn7‐deficient mice display increased intestinal permeability and heightened susceptibility to colitis (Wang et al. 2021). CLDN12, uniformly expressed throughout the gut, is also reduced in Crohn's disease biopsies (Lameris et al. 2013) and DSS‐treated rats (Arimura et al. 2011).

ZO‐1, another component of the intestinal barrier, has long been considered essential for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity (Chelakkot et al. 2018), with reduced expression reported in individuals with colitis (Kuo et al. 2021). However, recent findings suggest its primary role may be in epithelial repair rather than in permeability regulation. Kuo et al. (2021) demonstrated that ZO‐1 contributes to Wnt/β‐catenin signalingand loss during DSS‐induced stress leads to apoptosis, impaired epithelial renewal, delayed mucosal healing, and exacerbated tissue damage. Intestinal EpCAM expression is similarly reduced in colitis and DSS‐treated mice, compromising intestinal immune homeostasis, epithelial function, and TJ stability, ultimately compromising barrier integrity (Furth et al. 2006; Lei et al. 2022; Ouchi et al. 2021). EpCAM mutations are further linked to IBD risk, and DSS‐treated mutant mice develop more severe colitis, with increased expression of MMPs, reduced TJ protein expression, and elevated lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in circulation (Nie et al. 2024).

Although endothelial dysfunction is recognized in chronic inflammation, the contribution of GVB changes in IBD remains underexplored (Jingjie and Jun 2023) despite increased intestinal vascular leakage as revealed by confocal endomicroscopy (Box 1). GVB dysfunction due to IFN‐γ‐induced AJ disruption has been observed in both human IBD and DSS models (Langer et al. 2019). Ulcerative colitis biopsies show increased PV1 expression, associated with GVB hyperpermeability and serum LPS levels, suggesting that GVB disruption facilitates translocation of microbial components into the circulation (Carloni et al. 2021). Consistently, DSS‐treated mice exhibit PV1 alterations and increased immune cell infiltration (Carloni et al. 2021). Together, these findings emphasize that disruptions across the intestinal barrier layers contribute to chronic inflammation in IBD and may drive systemic inflammation, potentially promoting BBB dysfunction in the brain.

Key methods to evaluate barrier integrity and dysfunction.

In vivo functional assays Vascular permeability markers Assessment of BBB permeability is commonly performed via systemic administration of fluorescent or chromogenic dyes with defined molecular weights. The extravasation of these compounds into the brain parenchyma serves as an indicator of BBB disruption (Natarajan et al. 2017). Similarly, in gut permeability assays, the translocation of orally administered dyes from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream reflects compromised intestinal barrier integrity (Ahmad et al. 2017). Evans blue is one of the commonly used dyes for BBB assessment but is rarely used in gut permeability assays. With a molecular weight of 961 Da, it binds to serum albumin and forms a macromolecular complex of approximately 68 kDa. (Chassaing et al. 2014). After injection, this complex forms within 5 min, and extravasation occurs only in regions with severely compromised BBB. Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)‐Dextran are polysaccharides available in a range of molecular weights (4–70 kDa), used in both BBB and gut studies to assess permeability to solutes, ions, and proteins of varying sizes (Natarajan et al. 2017). In the gut, FITC‐dextran is typically administered orally, and its appearance in serum indicates luminal‐to‐blood translocation (Ahmad et al. 2017). Dextrans can be administered in short or long intervals, providing information on the time‐course and duration of barrier leakage. Pros: Quantitative and size selective. Cons: Low to medium cost and time‐restricted analysis. In vivo imaging Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) MRI is a non‐invasive method for imaging brain structure and function. When combined with contrast agents (e.g., gadolinium), it can provide visual quantification of BBB permeability changes (Bendszus et al. 2024). This method can be used several times in the same subject, allowing longitudinal studies and BBB leakage dynamics. Pros: Non‐invasive, whole‐brain mapping, longitudinal assays. Cons: High cost, low sensitivity to minor leakage. Two‐photon microscopy Two‐photon microscopy allows high‐resolution, real‐time imaging of BBB permeability at the level of individual blood vessels. It requires a cranial window implantation, making the technique highly invasive, but along with longitudinal imaging over days to weeks. Using near‐infrared lasers, two‐photon excitation minimizes light scattering and confines excitation to the focal plane, enabling deeper tissue imaging compared to single‐photon techniques. However, imaging depth remains limited to superficial cortical regions. Various dyes of different molecular weights are used to study both paracellular and transcellular leakage (Kucharz et al. 2022). Pros: High‐resolution dynamics, longitudinal assays. Cons: Invasive, limited to cortical regions. Confocal Laser Endomicroscopy CLE enables real‐time visualization of the gut epithelial barrier during colonoscopy, with fluorescein‐based detection of epithelial gaps and leakage (Langer et al. 2019; Macé et al. 2015). Pros: Dynamic in vivo imaging, clinical translatability, high diagnostic accuracy. Cons: High cost, narrow field of view. In vitro functional assays Transendothelial/epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) TEER measures the electrical resistance across epithelial and endothelial cell monolayers, reflecting the integrity of CAMs in both the BBB and the intestinal barrier. The resistance values (Ω cm2) indicate barrier tightness and can dynamically monitor leakiness following experimental manipulations. While limited to in vitro models, TEER preserves cellular integrity for subsequent assays (Ahmad et al. 2023; Benson et al. 2013). Pros: Quantitative, real‐time evaluation. Cons: Limited to in vitro assays. 3D BBB and gut models (organoids/spheroids) Brain organoids and spheroids are three‐dimensional, multicellular constructs that replicate several BBB characteristics, including TJ formation, transporter expression, and drug efflux mechanisms (Bergmann et al. 2018). Similarly, intestinal organoids reproduce aspects of gut barrier architecture, including goblet cell differentiation and mucus secretion (Boonekamp et al. 2020). These models allow evaluation of barrier permeability and pharmacological responses in a physiologically relevant and controlled in vitro environment. Pros: Complex cell–cell interactions, useful for drug screening, and mechanistic barrier studies. Cons: Limited access to inner layers, complex optimization. Structural and molecular assays Immunofluorescence Immunofluorescence enables the spatial detection of barrier‐related proteins (e.g., CLDNs, ZO‐1, refer to Table 1) in tissue sections through antibody labeling. This technique allows for qualitative and semi‐quantitative assessment of both BBB and intestinal barrier integrity, with insight into regional expression patterns and co‐localization of markers. Pros: Spatial resolution, regional changes, co‐localization. Cons: Single time‐point analysis, semi‐quantitative, indirect measure of function. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) EM provides high‐resolution images of the cellular ultrastructure of the BBB and intestinal barrier, such as TJs, membranes, and organelles. When combined with immunogold labeling, where antibodies are linked to gold nanoparticles, it allows the detection of proteins at the nanometer scale (Cornford and Hyman 2005; Shen et al. 2011). Pros: High‐resolution, detailed structural morphology. Cons: High cost, non‐functional. Bacterial localization assays Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or 16S rRNA staining FISH and 16S rRNA staining can be used to detect bacterial translocation across the intestinal epithelium by labeling bacterial DNA or RNA (Berry et al. 2015). Pros: Spatial resolution of microbial location, identifies specific bacteria taxa. Cons: Labor‐intensive, requires tissue preservation. Alcian Blue and PAS staining Alcian Blue and PAS staining is used to quantify goblet cells and assess mucus thickness. Alcian Blue stains acidic mucins, while PAS staining detects neutral mucins (Jensen et al. 2021). Pros: Semi‐quantitative distinction between mucin types. Cons: Requires careful interpretation, mucus is easily disrupted. Omics and biomarkers Serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers Intact BBB and intestinal barrier restrict the movement of proteins and molecules. Therefore, altered levels of barrier‐related markers in peripheral fluids can indicate dysfunction. In the CNS, increased serum levels of brain‐derived proteins (e.g., CLDN5, OCLN, AQP4, S100B) or elevated albumin ratios in CSF can indicate BBB leakage (Maridaki et al. 2025). Likewise, in intestinal barrier studies, elevated levels of LPS and LPS‐binding protein in blood can indicate increased permeability and microbial translocation (Stevens et al. 2018). Pros: Non‐invasive, translational, longitudinal. Cons: High individual variability, indirect measures. RNA Sequencing (RNA‐seq) and proteomics.Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses enable region‐ and cell‐specific profiling of barrier components in both the BBB and gut under physiological and pathological conditions. These techniques provide mechanistic insights into molecular alterations, signaling pathways, and disease‐associated gene and protein expression (Munji et al. 2019). In the gut, these techniques can be used to analyze mucus composition, antimicrobial peptide expression, or regional barrier dysfunction. Proteomic profiling of feces, serum, or mucus can aid in biomarker discovery. Pros: High sensitivity, cell type‐specific, mechanistic insights. Cons: High cost, complex data interpretation.

The extravasation of these compounds into the brain parenchyma serves as an indicator of BBB disruption (Natarajan et al. 2017). Evans blue is one of the commonly used dyes for BBB assessment but is rarely used in gut permeability assays. With a molecular weight of 961 Da, it binds to serum albumin and forms a macromolecular complex of approximately 68 kDa. (Chassaing et al. 2014). After injection, this complex forms within 5 min, and extravasation occurs only in regions with severely compromised BBB.

Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)‐Dextran are polysaccharides available in a range of molecular weights (4–70 kDa), used in both BBB and gut studies to assess permeability to solutes, ions, and proteins of varying sizes (Natarajan et al. 2017). In the gut, FITC‐dextran is typically administered orally, and its appearance in serum indicates luminal‐to‐blood translocation (Ahmad et al. 2017). Dextrans can be administered in short or long intervals, providing information on the time‐course and duration of barrier leakage.

Pros: Quantitative and size selective.

Cons: Low to medium cost and time‐restricted analysis.

In vivo imaging

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

MRI is a non‐invasive method for imaging brain structure and function. When combined with contrast agents (e.g., gadolinium), it can provide visual quantification of BBB permeability changes (Bendszus et al. 2024). This method can be used several times in the same subject, allowing longitudinal studies and BBB leakage dynamics.

Pros: Non‐invasive, whole‐brain mapping, longitudinal assays.

Cons: High cost, low sensitivity to minor leakage.

Two‐photon microscopy

Two‐photon microscopy allows high‐resolution, real‐time imaging of BBB permeability at the level of individual blood vessels. It requires a cranial window implantation, making the technique highly invasive, but along with longitudinal imaging over days to weeks. Using near‐infrared lasers, two‐photon excitation minimizes light scattering and confines excitation to the focal plane, enabling deeper tissue imaging compared to single‐photon techniques. However, imaging depth remains limited to superficial cortical regions. Various dyes of different molecular weights are used to study both paracellular and transcellular leakage (Kucharz et al. 2022).

Pros: High‐resolution dynamics, longitudinal assays.

Cons: Invasive, limited to cortical regions.

Confocal Laser Endomicroscopy

CLE enables real‐time visualization of the gut epithelial barrier during colonoscopy, with fluorescein‐based detection of epithelial gaps and leakage (Langer et al. 2019; Macé et al. 2015).

Pros: Dynamic in vivo imaging, clinical translatability, high diagnostic accuracy.

Cons: High cost, narrow field of view.

In vitro functional assays

Transendothelial/epithelial electrical resistance (TEER)

TEER measures the electrical resistance across epithelial and endothelial cell monolayers, reflecting the integrity of CAMs in both the BBB and the intestinal barrier. The resistance values (Ω cm2) indicate barrier tightness and can dynamically monitor leakiness following experimental manipulations. While limited to in vitro models, TEER preserves cellular integrity for subsequent assays (Ahmad et al. 2023; Benson et al. 2013).

Pros: Quantitative, real‐time evaluation.

Cons: Limited to in vitro assays.

3D BBB and gut models (organoids/spheroids)

Brain organoids and spheroids are three‐dimensional, multicellular constructs that replicate several BBB characteristics, including TJ formation, transporter expression, and drug efflux mechanisms (Bergmann et al. 2018). Similarly, intestinal organoids reproduce aspects of gut barrier architecture, including goblet cell differentiation and mucus secretion (Boonekamp et al. 2020). These models allow evaluation of barrier permeability and pharmacological responses in a physiologically relevant and controlled in vitro environment.

Pros: Complex cell–cell interactions, useful for drug screening, and mechanistic barrier studies.

Cons: Limited access to inner layers, complex optimization.

Structural and molecular assays

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence enables the spatial detection of barrier‐related proteins (e.g., CLDNs, ZO‐1, refer to Table 1) in tissue sections through antibody labeling. This technique allows for qualitative and semi‐quantitative assessment of both BBB and intestinal barrier integrity, with insight into regional expression patterns and co‐localization of markers.

Pros: Spatial resolution, regional changes, co‐localization.

Cons: Single time‐point analysis, semi‐quantitative, indirect measure of function.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

EM provides high‐resolution images of the cellular ultrastructure of the BBB and intestinal barrier, such as TJs, membranes, and organelles. When combined with immunogold labeling, where antibodies are linked to gold nanoparticles, it allows the detection of proteins at the nanometer scale (Cornford and Hyman 2005; Shen et al. 2011).

Pros: High‐resolution, detailed structural morphology.

Cons: High cost, non‐functional.

Bacterial localization assays

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or 16S rRNA staining

FISH and 16S rRNA staining can be used to detect bacterial translocation across the intestinal epithelium by labeling bacterial DNA or RNA (Berry et al. 2015).

Pros: Spatial resolution of microbial location, identifies specific bacteria taxa.

Cons: Labor‐intensive, requires tissue preservation.

Alcian Blue and PAS staining

Alcian Blue and PAS staining is used to quantify goblet cells and assess mucus thickness. Alcian Blue stains acidic mucins, while PAS staining detects neutral mucins (Jensen et al. 2021).

Pros: Semi‐quantitative distinction between mucin types.

Cons: Requires careful interpretation, mucus is easily disrupted.

Omics and biomarkers

Serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers

Intact BBB and intestinal barrier restrict the movement of proteins and molecules. Therefore, altered levels of barrier‐related markers in peripheral fluids can indicate dysfunction. In the CNS, increased serum levels of brain‐derived proteins (e.g., CLDN5, OCLN, AQP4, S100B) or elevated albumin ratios in CSF can indicate BBB leakage (Maridaki et al. 2025). Likewise, in intestinal barrier studies, elevated levels of LPS and LPS‐binding protein in blood can indicate increased permeability and microbial translocation (Stevens et al. 2018).

Pros: Non‐invasive, translational, longitudinal.

Cons: High individual variability, indirect measures.

RNA Sequencing (RNA‐seq) and proteomics.Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses enable region‐ and cell‐specific profiling of barrier components in both the BBB and gut under physiological and pathological conditions. These techniques provide mechanistic insights into molecular alterations, signaling pathways, and disease‐associated gene and protein expression (Munji et al. 2019).

In the gut, these techniques can be used to analyze mucus composition, antimicrobial peptide expression, or regional barrier dysfunction. Proteomic profiling of feces, serum, or mucus can aid in biomarker discovery.

Pros: High sensitivity, cell type‐specific, mechanistic insights.

Cons: High cost, complex data interpretation.

Stress‐Mediated Alterations of the Intestinal Barrier: Implications for IBD and Psychiatric Comorbidities

Stress is increasingly recognized as a common contributing factor to psychiatric disorders and IBD (Bisgaard et al. 2022; Johnson et al. 2019). In a longitudinal study, individuals with IBD experiencing stressful life events had a higher risk for active disease within 6 months (Duffy et al. 1991). Likewise, long‐term perceived stress is linked to symptom exacerbation during remission (Levenstein et al. 2000) and can trigger flares (Sun et al. 2019). Even asymptomatic individuals who develop rectal mucosal abnormalities report higher levels of perceived stress compared to those without such lesions (Levenstein et al. 1994). Similar to its effects on the BBB, stress exposure disrupts the intestinal barrier in multiple ways (Doney et al. 2022). Activation of the HPA axis has been linked to increased intestinal inflammation and compromised epithelial barrier function. In humans, acute stress elevates levels of corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH), which triggers the release of proteases and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, ultimately increasing intestinal barrier permeability (Vanuytsel et al. 2014). Similarly, in mice, chronic social stress activates hypothalamic CRH‐releasing neurons, which in turn stimulate enteric noradrenergic neurons in the colon. This stimulation enhances IFN‐γ‐producing immune cells, leading to increased intestinal barrier permeability and endotoxemia (Russo et al. 2023). Chronic elevation of glucocorticoids further drives the emergence of a pro‐inflammatory subset of enteric glia, which promotes monocyte and TNF‐α‐mediated inflammation, exacerbating colitis (Schneider et al. 2023).

A recent study combining mouse models and in vitro experiments suggests that acute stress may increase intestinal permeability by disrupting TJ proteins through reductions in short‐chain fatty acids, byproducts of bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber (Rosell‐Cardona et al. 2025). However, the impact of stress on intestinal TJ expression varies according to the type and duration of stress exposure, with notable sex differences. For example, 10 days of CSDS increased Cldn3 expression in the small intestine of male but not female stressed mice. Six days of chronic variable stress exposure were sufficient to reduce Cldn3 expression in females, whereas prolonged exposure was required to achieve similar effects in males (Doney et al. 2024). Subacute stress in rats increased intestinal TJ gene expression, whereas chronic stress showed no effect (Lauffer et al. 2016).

Chronic and acute stress also alter the intestinal mucus lining in different ways. Chronic repeated water immersion stress reduced colonic Muc2 expression by 40% and depleted goblet cells after 7–14 days, but not after an acute exposure of 3 days (Shigeshiro et al. 2012). Chronic mild restraint stress selectively reduced Muc13 expression across multiple intestinal regions, without significantly affecting other mucins. Moreover, Muc13 deletion was sufficient to induce gut microbiota shifts resembling those caused by chronic stress, resulting in increased behavioral despair (Rivet‐Noor et al. 2024).

Intestinal inflammation can, in turn, influence stress levels via HPA axis activation (Reichmann et al. 2015) and increase vulnerability to stress. Mice initially resilient to chronic unpredictable mild stress became susceptible after DSS‐induced colitis, developing depression‐like behaviors (Komoto et al. 2022). Together, these findings suggest that stress‐induced intestinal barrier dysfunction contributes to the bidirectional link between intestinal inflammation and psychiatric symptoms. Stress activates the HPA axis and autonomic pathways, disrupting epithelial and mucus barriers, promoting dysbiosis and endotoxemia which may further aggravate intestinal inflammation and stress sensitivity.

Intestinal Inflammation as a Mediator of BBB Dysfunction in Psychiatric Disorders

Chronic intestinal inflammation, such as in IBD, may alter brain activity by disrupting the BBB and promoting neuroinflammation, potentially contributing to a higher prevalence of anxiety and mood disorders (Mitchell et al. 2022; Talley et al. 2021; Barberio et al. 2021). While psychological factors like stigma, shame, and the daily burden of chronic illness contribute (Armuzzi and Liguori 2021), growing evidence supports biological mechanisms linking intestinal inflammation to neuroinflammatory changes in brain regions involved in emotion regulation and cognition (Bisgaard et al. 2022). Functional MRI reveals global and local brain network changes in individuals with colitis, which are correlated with increased psychiatric symptoms (Wang, Labus, et al. 2022; Wang, Wu, et al. 2022), as well as alterations in gray matter volume that may underlie emotional and cognitive dysfunction (Depping et al. 2018; Kornelsen et al. 2021). Decreased activity in the Hipp, Amy, thalamus, and cerebellum in response to positively valenced images indicates diminished sensitivity to positive emotion (Agostini et al. 2011). In Crohn's disease, altered gray matter volume in various brain regions has been associated with psychiatric symptoms (Bao et al. 2015), and differences in Amy connectivity observed in individuals with comorbid depression and anxiety (Sun, Sun, et al. 2024; Sun, Wang, et al. 2024).

Rodent models provide mechanistic insights into these clinical findings. DSS‐induced colitis may disrupt BBB integrity, evidenced by decreased expression of ZO‐1, OCLN, and CLDN5, particularly in the Hipp (Han et al. 2018; Shendy et al. 2024). These disruptions resulting from intestinal inflammation may promote neuroinflammation (Han et al. 2018; Shendy et al. 2024) with neuroinflammatory responses exacerbated in rodent models of both colitis and Crohn's disease (Zonis et al. 2015; Haj‐Mirzaian et al. 2017; Heydarpour et al. 2016). Mice with colitis exhibit depressive‐ and anxiety‐like behaviors, accompanied by increased hippocampal gene expression of Toll‐like receptors, which sense pathogen‐ and damage‐associated signals, as well as pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐6 (Haj‐Mirzaian et al. 2017). Elevated TNF‐α levels are found in the Hipp of a murine model of Crohn's disease (Heydarpour et al. 2016). Other brain regions are also affected, with increased levels of IL‐6 found in the cerebral cortex and hypothalamus (Wang et al. 2010). Bioluminescence imaging of caspase‐1, an inflammasome marker, has localized neuroinflammation to the hindbrain during colitis, coinciding with elevated levels of IL‐1β, TNF‐α, S100A9, neutrophil recruitment, and inflammasome activation (Wang, Song, et al. 2018). Pharmacological inhibition of S100A9, a protein involved in cytokine secretion, mitigated both intestinal and CNS inflammation (Talley et al. 2021).

One proposed mechanism linking chronic intestinal inflammation to BBB disruption and neuroinflammation involves microbial products, such as LPS, entering the systemic circulation (Aburto and Cryan 2024; Wei et al. 2024). While LPS is known for inducing sickness behavior, characterized by symptoms overlapping with depression and anxiety, it also elicits depressive‐like behaviors that extend beyond the effects of acute sickness (O'Connor et al. 2009). LPS may compromise the BBB either directly, by binding to endothelial cells (Banks and Robinson 2010), or indirectly, by triggering peripheral immune responses that release pro‐inflammatory cytokines (Yin et al. 2023). Supporting the latter, increased IL‐6 levels correlate with markers of intestinal barrier permeability, including ZO‐1, in individuals who attempted suicide (Ohlsson et al. 2019). Under physiological conditions, peripheral cytokines are too large to cross the BBB freely (Reyes‐Martínez et al. 2023). However, if BBB integrity is compromised, they can pass the barrier and infiltrate the brain parenchyma (Menard et al. 2017). Peripheral cytokines may also access the brain through naturally permeable structures like the circumventricular organs and choroid plexus, or via cytokine‐specific transport mechanisms across the BBB (Dantzer et al. 2008; Reyes‐Martínez et al. 2023). In addition, IL‐1 receptors on perivascular macrophages and endothelial cells of brain venules detect circulating cytokines and, through prostaglandin‐mediated signaling, activate glial cells in the brain parenchyma to produce more cytokines (Dantzer et al. 2008; Reyes‐Martínez et al. 2023). Cytokines further impair BBB integrity by downregulating TJ proteins, increasing permeability, and fostering a vicious cycle of neuroinflammation (Doney et al. 2022). Once inside the brain, cytokines activate glial cells, amplifying neuroinflammatory signaling and contributing to neuronal dysfunction (Dantzer et al. 2008; Reyes‐Martínez et al. 2023).

Beyond innate immune mechanisms, emerging evidence highlights the role of systemic adaptive immune cells in the pathogenesis of psychiatric disorders. Proinflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells, as well as IL‐17, their main effector cytokine, are elevated in the blood of individuals with MDD or GAD (Chen et al. 2011; Davami et al. 2016; Mamun‐or‐Rashid et al. 2024). The precise mechanism by which systemic Th17 cells and IL‐17 contribute to these disorders is not fully understood. However, under inflammatory conditions, IL‐17 has been shown to compromise BBB integrity, thereby facilitating the migration of Th17 cells into the brain (Charabati et al. 2023; Kebir et al. 2007). Within the brain parenchyma, IL‐17 acts on astrocytes and microglia to promote inflammation and interacts with neurons to alter excitability and synaptic function, processes linked to depression‐ and anxiety‐like behaviors (Bliźniewska‐Kowalska et al. 2023). Notably, IBD is characterized by robust activation of Th17 cells (Chen et al. 2023). Recent findings suggest that gut‐derived T cells can traffic to specific areas of the brain, even under homeostatic conditions (Yoshida et al. 2025). In murine models of chronic colitis, Th17 cells, resembling those driving intestinal inflammation, migrate into the hypothalamus, inducing neuroinflammation and behavioral changes. This suggests that pathogenic Th17 cells originating in the gut may also contribute to brain inflammation during IBD causing the development of psychiatric symptoms (Mickael et al. 2022). Accordingly, restoring the balance between Th17 and regulatory T cells alleviates both intestinal inflammation and anxiety‐like behaviors in DSS‐induced colitis, in a gut microbiota‐dependent manner (Xia et al. 2023).

Taken together, these findings underscore the impact of intestinal barrier dysfunction and systemic inflammation in weakening the BBB, thereby contributing to neuroinflammation and the development of psychiatric conditions.

Shared Signaling Pathways Across Barriers: Mental Health and Psychiatric Disorders

The BBB communicates bidirectionally with the surrounding CNS environment through tightly regulated signaling, which is essential for its development, postnatal maturation, lifelong maintenance, and repair (Daneman and Prat 2015). Several molecular pathways that modulate BBB function under physiological conditions are dysregulated in psychiatric disorders, supporting the view that BBB dysfunction is not merely a consequence of brain pathology but may actively contribute to disease onset and progression (Kealy et al. 2020). Here we highlight three major signaling pathways with central roles in BBB biology and their relevance to mental disorders: (i) Wnt/β‐catenin pathway; (ii) VEGF signaling; and (iii) FGF2 signaling. These pathways interact with inflammatory cascades, stress responses, and neurotrophic regulation, forming a complex signaling network that underlies BBB function in health and disease (Menard et al. 2017). They are not confined to the CNS and operate in the intestinal barrier as well, where they maintain epithelial and endothelial integrity, vascular function, and immune homeostasis. This overlap highlights a path through which IBD‐associated intestinal inflammation may influence brain function and psychiatric outcomes (Figure 3).

Shared pathways regulating blood–brain and intestinal barriers in psychiatric disorders. In the blood–brain barrier (BBB, top panel), Wnt/β‐catenin and growth factors (FGF2, VEGF‐A) regulate angiogenesis, barrier integrity, and inflammation. Disruption via GSK3β reduces endothelial stability in major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD), while selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and lithium modulate this pathway. Additional cascades, including VEGFR2/PI3K/AKT and FGFR1/MEK/ERK, further regulate angiogenesis, proliferation, and neuroinflammation. In the intestinal barrier (bottom panel), Wnt/β‐catenin supports epithelial renewal, mucosa healing, and tight junction expression (claudins, ZO‐1), whereas dysregulation promotes permeability and inflammation in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and MDD. VEGF‐A further drives vascular leakage, endothelial fenestration, and leukocyte infiltration. These overlapping mechanisms contribute to systemic inflammation and barrier dysfunction, supporting a gut–brain axis model where intestinal dysfunction exacerbates BBB breakdown, neuroinflammation, and vulnerability to mood and anxiety disorders.

Wnt/β‐Catenin Pathway

The canonical Wnt/β‐catenin pathway modulates BBB development and maintenance, regulating endothelial differentiation, TJ protein expression, and vesicular trafficking. It is initiated when Wnt ligands bind to Frizzled (FZD) receptors and co‐receptors LRP5/6 on the cell surface, triggering a cascade that inhibits GSK‐3β. Under resting conditions, GSK‐3β is part of the destruction complex that phosphorylates β‐catenin, targeting it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway disrupts this complex, allowing β‐catenin to accumulate in the cytoplasm and translocate to the nucleus, where it interacts with transcriptional factors to regulate genes essential for cell survival, synaptic plasticity, and BBB integrity (Moon 2005).

Clinical evidence supports altered expression of key components of the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway in mood and anxiety disorders (Liebner et al. 2008). Decreased levels of frizzled‐6 (FZD6) receptors, mediating Wnt signaling, have been reported in individuals with MDD. In mice, FZD6 downregulation alters GSK‐3β phosphorylation creating an imbalance in cytoplasmic/nuclear levels of β‐catenin resulting in depressive‐like behavior, further supporting involvement of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in psychiatric symptoms (Han et al. 2024). Antidepressants, such as SSRIs and SNRIs, reverse these molecular alterations by increasing β‐catenin levels and promoting hippocampal neurogenesis through GSK‐3β inhibition (Hui et al. 2014; Mostany et al. 2008). In chronically stressed mice, Wnt signaling is downregulated in brain regions involved in stress responses, such as the Hipp, PFC, and NAc (Chen et al. 2012; Dias et al. 2014; Wilkinson et al. 2011). This downregulation is associated with BBB dysfunction, including the downregulation of Cldn5 and increased endothelial permeability (Sun, Sun, et al. 2024; Sun, Wang, et al. 2024). Activation of Wnt signaling, either genetically or pharmacologically via GSK‐3β inhibition, restores BBB integrity and alleviates depressive‐like behaviors in mice (Ji et al. 2021; Laksitorini et al. 2019).

Similarly, postmortem studies revealed reduced expression of β‐catenin and GSK‐3β in the PFC and temporal cortex of individuals with BD (Pandey et al. 2015). Inhibition of GSK‐3β by lithium, a standard BD treatment, prevents β‐catenin degradation, enabling its nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity (Rowe et al. 2007; Valvezan and Klein 2012). Accordingly, transgenic mice overexpressing β‐catenin exhibit behavioral phenotypes similar to those induced by lithium (Gould et al. 2007). Lithium's beneficial effects on the neurogliovascular unit may depend on its ability to enhance Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in brain endothelial cells, leading to restoration of TJ proteins and reduced transcytosis (Ji et al. 2021). Protective effects are not observed in mice lacking Gpr124, a co‐receptor for the Wnt ligand, which is expressed specifically in brain endothelial cells (Martin et al. 2022). Finally, the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway interacts with factors related to neuroplasticity, such as Sonic hedgehog and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor, suggesting a convergence of molecular and signaling cascades (Tayyab et al. 2018).

Although clinical studies on Wnt signaling in GAD are limited, preclinical evidence supports its involvement with stress‐induced models of anxiety characterized by reduced levels of β‐catenin, particularly in the ventral Hipp (Zhao et al. 2018). Antidepressants such as fluoxetine and vilazodone restore β‐catenin signaling and normalize neurogenesis in these models, suggesting that activation of the Wnt pathway may contribute to their anxiolytic effects (El‐Kadi et al. 2024; Mishra et al. 2019). In female rats, vilazodone reversed stress‐induced degradation of β‐catenin mediated by Axin‐1, a scaffold protein that forms part of the β‐catenin degradation complex (Dias et al. 2014; El‐Kadi et al. 2024). Anxiety‐related BBB disruption may also be driven by inflammatory mechanisms suppressing endothelial Wnt/β‐catenin signaling. Activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), a transcription factor involved in inflammatory responses, inhibits Wnt activity in brain endothelial cells, leading to increased permeability and neuroinflammation. Restoration of Wnt signaling by NF‐κB inhibition counteracts these effects and improves BBB integrity (Huang et al. 2024).

In the intestines, Wnt/β‐catenin signaling regulates the proliferation and differentiation of stem cells at the base of the crypts within the epithelial barrier, enabling continuous epithelial renewal to counteract mechanical stress and pathogens (Moparthi and Koch 2019). Human and mouse IBD studies reveal altered Wnt‐related gene expression, indicating dysregulated signaling in chronic intestinal inflammation (Bradford et al. 2017; Serafino et al. 2014; You et al. 2008). Wnt/β‐catenin activation suppresses protective innate lymphoid cells, critical for maintaining epithelial integrity, by reducing their survival, proliferation, and production of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐22, thereby exacerbating intestinal inflammation (Hao et al. 2024). In addition, intestinal inflammation induces abnormal Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, leading to increased permeability of the GVB, as indicated by elevated PV1 expression, systemic IL‐6 elevation, and neuroinflammation (Carloni et al. 2021). In a murine colitis model, circulating LPS activated Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in pericytes and vascular endothelial cells of the choroid plexus, altering vasculature permeability (Carloni et al. 2021). These findings suggest that inflammation‐driven dysregulated Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in IBD may compromise intestinal barrier integrity, promote systemic inflammation, and alter CNS vascular barrier function. However, further research is needed to determine whether intestinal inflammation influences the BBB via Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in IBD individuals with comorbid MDD, BD, and GAD.

Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A Pathway

The first discovered vascular endothelial growth factor, VEGF‐A, was described as a potent permeability factor not found in normal plasma or serum (Senger et al. 1983). VEGF‐A, the most expressed component of the VEGF family in the CNS, is the principal angiogenic factor during development, with its partial or complete deletion leading to embryonic lethality (Carmeliet et al. 1996; Ferrara et al. 1996). During adulthood, VEGF‐A acts as a critical inducer of BBB permeability, especially in the circumventricular organs, where fenestrated capillaries are vital (Argaw et al. 2009). VEGF ligands act on three tyrosine kinase receptors: VEGFR1, VEGFR2, and VEGFR3, whose activation induces intracellular processes common to many growth factor receptors, such as cell proliferation, migration, and survival (Olsson et al. 2006). VEGF‐A binds mainly to VEGFR2, but also to VEGFR1, and responds to hypoxia with hypoxia inducible factor‐1 release, triggering VEGF‐A transcription and angiogenesis in poorly oxygenated regions. VEGF‐A activates additional signaling cascades, such as PI3K‐Akt and MAPK pathways, the former being associated with permeability and survival, and the latter with proliferation (Wang, Bove, et al. 2020; Wang, Li, et al. 2020). As for VEGF‐B/VEGFR‐1, it plays a role in pathological response. Despite not being actively regulated by hypoxia, activated signaling has been tied to ischemia, and neuroinflammation (Xie et al. 2013; Yue et al. 2014). VEGFR‐1 can also be presented as a soluble splice variant, acting as a decoy that binds to VEGF‐A and prevents its interaction with VEGFR2, thereby inducing antiangiogenic adaptation (Boucher et al. 2017). Activation of VEGFR1 leads to heterodimer formation with VEGFR2, negatively regulating its angiogenic function (Cudmore et al. 2012).

Increased circulating levels of VEGF‐A have been reported in individuals with MDD, and it has been considered a potential diagnostic biomarker for BD (Shi et al. 2020; Castillo et al. 2020). Elevated plasma levels of VEGF‐A are correlated with increased CSF total proteins, a measure of BBB permeability, in both MDD and preclinical models (Matsuno et al. 2022). VEGFR1 gene polymorphisms correlate with symptom severity in MDD, potentially serving as a prognostic biomarker (Nunes et al. 2022). Despite the potential of regulating the VEGF/VEGFR pathway to suppress inflammation in brain pathologies, its essential roles in development and angiogenesis raise safety concerns. For instance, anti‐VEGF treatment for macular degeneration can promote anxiety and depressive symptoms (Senra et al. 2017; Van Der Aa et al. 2021). Nevertheless, further understanding of VEGF signaling and its involvement in BBB dysfunction in the context of psychiatric disorders could inform diagnosis and novel therapeutic avenues.

Astrocytes form a complex glial network that regulates neurotransmission, metabolic function, and the release of trophic factors, influencing both microdomains and broader regions. Connexins are essential for the formation of astrocytic networks, as they enable direct intercellular communication and the efficient propagation of calcium signaling (De Bock et al. 2017). Cx43 facilitates astrocyte calcium signaling, which in turn regulates the vesicular release of trophic factors, such as VEGF‐A. Altered Cx43 expression leads to reduced VEGF‐A and BBB leakage (De Bock et al. 2022; Schumacher et al. 2024), and it has been associated with inflammatory responses in brain endothelial cells (for more, see Okamoto et al. 2021). VEGF‐A production is increased by neuroinflammation, leading to decreased expression of Cldn5 and Ocln both in vitro and in vivo (Argaw et al. 2009). Inactivation of astrocytic VEGF‐A prevents leukocyte infiltration and BBB breakdown in a neuroinflammation animal model (Argaw et al. 2012). Chronic stress exposure similarly induces BBB permeability by reducing Cldn5 expression via elevation of VEGF‐A and VEGFR2 (Lee et al. 2018).

Intestinal inflammation is associated with hypoxia, which increases VEGF‐A expression, promoting angiogenesis (Bakirtzi et al. 2014). Beyond its angiogenic role, VEGF‐A also drives inflammation by inducing leukocyte adhesion, worsening disease severity in DSS‐induced colitis models (Scaldaferri et al. 2009). Conversely, inhibitory VEGF‐A isoforms, which can inhibit VEGF signaling, exhibit anti‐inflammatory effects in a mouse model of colitis (Cromer et al. 2013). As elevated VEGF‐A levels have been reported in both the mucosa and blood of IBD individuals and murine colitis models (Cromer et al. 2013; Scaldaferri et al. 2009), IBD could potentially contribute to BBB disruption via VEGF‐A‐induced downregulation of CLDN5 and OCLN (Argaw et al. 2009; Michalak et al. 2019). However, to our knowledge, no study has yet directly examined VEGF‐A levels or VEGFR activation in the brain following intestinal inflammation.

Fibroblast Growth Factor‐2 (FGF2) Pathway

The fibroblast growth factor family is composed of 22 factors, subdivided into six sub‐families that exert diverse functions during development, CNS homeostasis, and pathological responses (Beenken and Mohammadi 2009; Turner et al. 2016; Xie et al. 2020). Altered FGF signaling can lead to pathological conditions through either gain‐ or loss‐of‐function in these ligands. The FGF2 family, considered the fundamental FGF, exhibits paracrine regulation acting near the source of its expression (Beenken and Mohammadi 2009). FGF2 is key for brain development, acting as a potent regulator of angiogenesis and stimulating endothelial cell proliferation and migration (Woodbury and Ikezu 2014); however, its mechanism of action remains unclear. Signaling of FGF2 occurs via high‐affinity tyrosine kinase receptors FGFR1–4, with FGFR1 being the most expressed in the CNS (Fon Tacer et al. 2010). Several studies attempted to understand its role in development and vascular function. Fgf2 knock‐out mice are viable and show normal development, possibly resulting from a compensatory mechanism from other angiogenic trophic factors, particularly given their structural similarities and functional overlap (Khurana and Simons 2003). However, studies have observed minor cortical alterations at birth and a reduced response to vasoconstrictors (Dono 1998; Zhou et al. 1998), thus indicating its role in maintaining endothelial vascular tone. Moreover, lack of FGF2 signaling has been linked to altered neuronal function, dysfunctional endothelial cell migration, and delayed wound healing (Ortega et al. 1998; Pintucci et al. 2002). Exogenous FGF2 stimulates proliferation and migration of endothelial cells, in addition to improving inflammatory response in vitro (Li et al. 2018) and in vivo (Pan et al. 2020).

The involvement of FGF2 in inflammation and BBB permeability has been increasingly investigated. An intracerebral hemorrhage mouse model demonstrated, via Evans blue assay (Box 1), that FGF2 treatment reduced brain edema, improved neuronal function, and decreased BBB leakage; an effect that was abolished by inhibition of its receptor, FGFR1 (Huang et al. 2012). Moreover, FGF2 knock‐out mice subjected to chronic inflammation showed increased brain infiltration of T cells and macrophages when compared to wild‐type mice, supporting loss of BBB integrity (Rottlaender et al. 2011). Astrocytic‐endothelial contact is essential for BBB development and function. FGF2 knock‐out mice have reduced astrocytic endfeet coverage of blood vessels but no impairment in BBB integrity or TJ morphology (Saunders et al. 2016), which could be linked to developmental compensation from other FGFs.

FGF2 has been associated with the pathophysiology of mental disorders, with decreased FGF2 and an increase in FGFR1 levels noticed in both MDD and BD (Deng et al. 2019; Turner et al. 2016). Overexpression of FGFR1 may represent a compensatory mechanism in response to reduced FGF2 availability (Gaughran et al. 2006). Accordingly, FGF2 knock‐out mice showed increased anxiety‐like behavior, associated with decreased glucocorticoid receptor and increased HPA axis activation. Interestingly, treatment with exogenous FGF2 in adulthood can rescue anxiety‐like behavior in mice (Salmaso et al. 2016). In addition, animals phenotypically selected for high‐anxiety response exhibited decreased anxiety‐like behavior after early‐life exposure to exogenous FGF2 (Turner et al. 2011). Similarly, animals with high anxiety and low response to novelty showed decreased levels of FGF2 in the Hipp, which was then rescued after a 3‐week treatment of FGF2 (Perez et al. 2009). We observed that CSDS alters FGF2 expression in the NAc and PFC of male mice, a change negatively correlated with Cldn5 (Menard et al. 2025). Therapeutic strategies, such as environmental enrichment and physical activity, prevent stress susceptibility through FGF2‐dependent signaling. In vitro, FGF2 treatment blunted the inflammatory response triggered by a TNF‐α immune challenge and improved wound healing. Moreover, increased circulating FGF2 in the blood of men and women with MDD supports the translational value of these findings (Menard et al. 2025).

As in the brain, several FGFs are expressed in the intestine (for review, see Danopoulos et al. 2017). In healthy conditions, FGF2 is expressed near the basement membrane beneath crypts and the lower part of the villus epithelium. Following injury, its expression expands throughout the lamina propria, particularly around regenerating crypts and in small submucosal blood vessels (Houchen et al. 1999). FGF2 plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal stem cell pluripotency (Lotz et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2015) and promotes stem cell survival after injury (Houchen et al. 1999). Notably, FGF2 levels are elevated in the blood of IBD patients (Bousvaros et al. 1997; Kanazawa et al. 2001) and the colons of mice with DSS‐induced colitis (Song et al. 2015). Mechanistically, dysbiosis following epithelial barrier disruption in DSS‐induced colitis triggers upregulation of TGFβ1, which subsequently induces FGF2 expression, primarily through regulatory T cells. FGF2 then acts synergistically with IL‐17 to promote the expression of genes involved in epithelial repair (Song et al. 2015). Given that systemic FGF2 has been implicated in angiogenesis, modulation of vascular permeability, and neuroinflammatory processes, gut‐derived FGF2 may reach the brain and influence BBB integrity. In theory, damage to the intestinal barrier could stimulate FGF2 release, as evidenced by elevated blood FGF2 levels in IBD. A recent clinical trial showed the potential use of a serum biomarker panel, including FGF2, for mid or long‐term relapse in individuals with Crohn's disease (Pierre et al. 2024). However, no study has yet directly tested whether intestinal permeability contributes to altered systemic FGF2 levels in MDD or whether this may be related to mood disorder.

This body of evidence suggests that FGF2 endothelial function is directly related to inflammatory response, as well as BBB function and permeability, with direct impact on mood regulation. FGF signaling has also been linked to antidepressant treatment response (Evans et al. 2004; Wang, Li, et al. 2018), suggested as a potential diagnostic biomarker for GAD (Graham 2017), and a prognostic IBD biomarker. In summary, this section highlighted the fundamental role of FGF2 signaling in inflammation and stress response, maintenance of the BBB and gut–barrier, and its role in mental disorders. Nonetheless, much is yet to be understood about FGF2 regulation of barrier permeability and mood regulation.

Discussion

Integrative Overview of Signaling Pathways

Biological barriers stability relies on coordinated signaling between trophic factors, inflammatory response, CAMs, and metabolic support. Among these, Wnt/β‐catenin, VEGF, and FGF2 pathways are crucial regulators of both BBB and intestinal barrier integrity. In the BBB, Wnt/β‐catenin drives endothelial specialization and angiogenesis, with Ctnnb1 knockdown impairing VEGFR2 expression and vascular sprouting (Rudloff and Kemler 2012; Daneman et al. 2009; Martowicz et al. 2019). Astrocyte‐derived Wnt ligands further stabilize endothelial β‐catenin, supporting BBB integrity and preventing astrogliosis in stroke models (Zhou et al. 2014; Song et al. 2021). GSK‐3β acts as a convergence point: phosphorylation leads to β‐catenin degradation and reduced angiogenesis, while inhibition promotes β‐catenin accumulation and VEGFR2‐PI3K/AKT, enhancing endothelial survival and TJ stability (Kim et al. 2002; Skurk et al. 2005). In turn, FGF2 and VEGF‐A are essential for cerebrovascular growth, cortical structure, and tissue repair, though their crosstalk remains poorly understood (Maurer et al. 2014; Ramirez et al. 2013; Halim et al. 2025; Miller et al. 2000). FGF2 can induce ERK‐dependent expression of VEGFR2 and regulate VEGFR1 splicing to generate soluble decoys, thus both amplifying and constraining VEGF‐A signaling (Xiao et al. 2007; Jia et al. 2021). Therapeutic modulators such as lithium further illustrate that targeting GSK‐3β and trophic factor production can simultaneously affect pathways relevant to barrier stability (Muneer 2017).

Similar mechanisms operate at the intestinal barrier in IBD, where Wnt/β‐catenin supports epithelial proliferation and repair while regulating PV1‐dependent endothelial fenestration (Moparthi and Koch 2019; Chugh et al. 2025; Denzer et al. 2023). FGF2 enhances this process by activating the noncanonical Wnt/β‐catenin–AKT/GSK‐3β cascade, supporting endothelial proliferation (Kim et al. 2018; Tokuhira et al. 2015). VEGF‐A promoter region presents several binding sites for β‐catenin, with dysregulation contributing to colon cancer and neoplasia (Easwaran et al. 2003). Clinically, elevated VEGF levels are observed in Crohn's disease (Lin and Cao 2024), and FGF2 in IBD (Li et al. 2025). FGF2 also promotes epithelial repair through IL‐17 signaling, while interacting with VEGF pathways (Song et al. 2015; Di Sabatino et al. 2004). Despite growing evidence of altered angiogenesis and intestinal barrier dysfunction in IBD, little is known about the parallel regulation between these signaling pathways in response to inflammation or stress.

In the Spotlight: Rising Pathways in Barrier Dysfunction

Although this review focuses on Wnt/β‐catenin, VEGF, and FGF2 signaling pathways in barrier dysfunction and psychiatric disorders, additional mechanisms like tryptophan (Trp) metabolism have emerged. Trp metabolism through the kynurenine (KYN) pathway, primarily activated by indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase (IDO) in response to inflammation, generates neuroactive metabolites such as quinolinic acid. These molecules can alter BBB permeability, enhance glial reactivity, and promote excitotoxicity, thereby contributing to mood disorder pathophysiology (Too et al. 2016). In MDD, elevated IDO activity is associated with reduced serotonin availability and increased KYN production, a biochemical pattern that correlates with symptom severity and responsiveness to SSRI treatment (Zoga et al. 2014). Furthermore, vascular dysregulation, including altered Trp‐KYN metabolism, endothelial dysfunction, and BBB disruption, has been linked to suicidal behavior, potentially serving as both a biomarker and a mechanistic driver of suicide risk in psychiatric conditions (Lengvenyte and Courtet 2025). Peripherally, the gut plays a central role in Trp availability and processing. Trp is primarily absorbed in the small intestine and metabolized through the serotonin and KYN pathways, while unabsorbed Trp is degraded by the gut microbiota via indole signaling in the large intestine (Roth et al. 2021). Upregulation of IDO and the KYN pathway is observed in IBD patients and preclinical models, leading to serotonin depletion and overproduction of neuroactive KYN metabolites (Barceló‐Batllori et al. 2002; Zhao et al. 2023; Ferdinande et al. 2008; O'Mahony et al. 2015).

In stressed rats, a Trp‐enriched diet shifts Trp metabolism from the KYN pathway toward the serotonin pathway, reducing gut barrier disruption, decreasing neuroinflammation, and alleviating anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors (Wang, Wu, et al. 2022). Given that Trp serum levels are reduced in individuals with IBD (Truyens et al. 2022) and Trp supplementation in DSS‐treated mice mitigates intestinal inflammation (Islam et al. 2017), dietary Trp may have benefits for IBD‐associated psychiatric symptoms. A randomized controlled trial with IBD patients in remission found that oral 5‐hydroxytryptophan supplementation elevated circulating serotonin; however, KYN levels remained unchanged, which may explain the lack of improvements in depression and anxiety scores (Truyens et al. 2022). The beneficial impact of Trp supplementation may also depend on the gut microbiota composition. For instance, fecal microbiota transplantation from depressed individuals increases the KYN/Trp ratio, highlighting the role of microbial communities in shaping Trp metabolism and its downstream effects (Kelly et al. 2016). In addition, microbiota‐derived indole metabolites from dietary Trp can protect against gut permeability defects in colitis models (Scott et al. 2020), and certain indole‐producing bacterial strains display psychobiotic potential by exerting antidepressant‐like effects by dampening neuroinflammation (Qian et al. 2024). This growing body of evidence warrants the need for further exploration of Trp‐KYN signaling in the context of barrier dysfunction and mood disorders.

Intestinal Barrier and BBB Dysfunctions: The Chicken‐and‐Egg Dilemma

The bidirectional relationship between the intestinal barrier and the BBB raises a key question of causality: Does intestinal barrier dysfunction precipitate BBB disruption through systemic inflammation and microbial translocation or does BBB impairment and neuroinflammation drive or exacerbate gut permeability through altered autonomic and neuroendocrine signaling? This chicken‐and‐egg dilemma remains unresolved, with evidence supporting both directions.

Beyond the well‐documented increased risk of developing mental disorders after IBD diagnosis (Barberio et al. 2021), depression and anxiety can also worsen disease activity (Fairbrass et al. 2022). Elevated anxiety and depression risk may appear up to 5 years before diagnosis of immune‐mediated inflammatory diseases, including IBD (Marrie et al. 2019). New‐onset depression also raises colitis risk (Frolkis et al. 2019), suggesting that psychiatric symptoms may contribute to this disease pathogenesis. This could occur via barrier alterations along the gut–brain axis since traumatic brain injury, associated with BBB disruption, causes intestinal dysfunction (Cannon et al. 2023). Psychological stress alters the gut microbiota, promoting inflammation and barrier dysfunction (Aburto and Cryan 2024). Dysbiosis may even precede intestinal inflammation in IBD pathogenesis (Glymenaki et al. 2017), implying that stress‐related microbial shifts could drive intestinal barrier breakdown and inflammation. Stress alone impairs intestinal barrier integrity, potentially via HPA and vagal pathways, or through stress‐induced BBB disruption feeding systemic inflammation (Doney et al. 2024, 2022; Chang et al. 2024). Conversely, mental disorders preceding IBD diagnosis may reflect a preclinical phase marked by underlying intestinal inflammation contributing to psychiatric symptoms. Depression increases IBD risk only when gastrointestinal symptoms are already present, suggesting psychiatric disorders may be an early disease manifestation (Blackwell et al. 2021).

Importantly, as reviewed here, intestinal inflammation can disrupt the BBB (Mitchell et al. 2022; Stevens et al. 2018), potentially initiating the development of mental disorders. This is supported by findings that LPS administration in rodents disrupts the BBB and induces depression‐like behavior. Furthermore, fecal microbiota transplantation from individuals with an inflammatory bowel syndrome and comorbid anxiety induces anxiety‐like behavior in mice (De Palma et al. 2017), underscoring gut‐derived signals affecting the brain.

An evolutionary perspective offers further insight: despite its costs, depression remains prevalent. The infection‐defense hypothesis proposes that depressive symptoms, such as social withdrawal and fatigue, evolved to conserve energy for immunity against infections and reduce pathogen spread (Anders et al. 2013). During intestinal inflammation, such sickness behavior may initially support immune function and tissue repair; however, in chronic inflammation, it may become maladaptive and contribute to clinical depression (Reichmann et al. 2015).

Brain and gut barrier dysfunctions may result from shared genetic and environmental factors. For instance, CLDN5 variants implicated in BBB disruption in depression (Gal et al. 2023) could theoretically also affect intestinal barrier integrity. High‐fat diets disrupt intestinal TJ expression and mucus lining, stimulate inflammation, and alter microbiota composition (Rohr et al. 2020). Similarly, diet‐induced obesity increases BBB permeability and neuroinflammation (Feng et al. 2024). Future longitudinal studies, conducted in both humans and rodent models, would help clarify the timing and causality of gut–brain barrier dysfunction.

Decoding Signals: New Approaches for Diagnostics and Treatment of Mental Disorders

Clinical heterogeneity within psychiatric populations, characterized by overlapping symptoms, comorbidities, and biological profiles, further complicates diagnosis and treatment choice. Instrumental research is ongoing to refine diagnostic tools and identify innovative therapeutic avenues.

Diagnostics

The lack of objective diagnostic methods for mental disorders contributes to the overall burden of these conditions. Identification of brain and/or blood signatures, via MRI scans or analysis of circulating biomarkers, has been thoroughly explored in recent years. Inflammation is a common factor between mental disorders, with serum inflammatory markers like TNFα and IL‐6, correlated with BD manic, depressive, and euthymic episodes (Poletti et al. 2024), or MDD severity (Harsanyi et al. 2022). Regarding BBB dysfunction, circulating levels of CNS proteins, such as S100β and MMP‐9, have been associated with the severity and treatment response of MDD and BD (Li et al. 2022; Machado‐Vieira et al. 2002). Albumin blood/CSF ratio, a strong indicator of BBB leakage, has been associated with mental disorders (Sørensen et al. 2022). Still, findings are not consistent, possibly due to the population studied and sample size.

Low cost and applicability to large populations are major advantages of blood profiling. However, it primarily represents indirect measures, and it can be influenced by confounding factors, such as infection. In contrast, brain imaging allows direct identification and quantification of BBB integrity loss. Dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI scanning correlated BBB leakage with disorder severity for individuals with BD or MDD (Kamintsky et al. 2020; Shang et al. 2024). Nonetheless, detecting small BBB alterations in vivo is a challenge for clinical use (Karakatsani et al. 2019). Positron emission tomography (PET) scan takes advantage of high‐temporal resolution dynamic imaging to estimate cerebral blood flow and radiotracers to measure the BBB transport rate, providing more detailed functional analysis (Chung et al. 2025). PET scans have been exploited to uncover potential therapeutic targets and pharmacodynamics of antidepressant treatment in individuals with MDD. Despite its potential, PET scanning is neglected in the diagnosis of mental disorders when compared to neurodegenerative diseases primarily because of its high cost and limited accessibility.

Increased risk of mental disorders before IBD diagnosis may reflect early signs of underlying intestinal inflammation (Blackwell et al. 2021). Intestinal barrier dysfunction has been identified as a potential predictor biomarker for MDD (Morena et al. 2025). Non‐specific early symptoms of IBD, such as fatigue and malaise, may be mistaken for mental health issues, leading to missed and delayed diagnosis of IBD (Bisgaard et al. 2022). Classical assessment of intestinal barrier integrity can be performed using noninvasive methods or biomarker analysis, including serum levels of LPS and ZO‐1 (Wasiak and Gawlik‐Kotelnicka 2023; Wu, Wang, et al. 2023). These markers may hold potential for early detection or screening of individuals at risk for psychiatric disorders. Overall, efficient and reliable diagnostic tools for mental disorders and IBD are still lacking and are an active area of research, since they could be relevant to identified subpopulations of vulnerable individuals.

Treatments

Most first‐line medications for psychiatric disorders prescribed today were discovered/developed 40–70 years ago (Nutt 2025). Despite major investment, several drugs have failed in clinical trials over the past decades. The challenge of translating findings from preclinical to clinical trials and ultimately to patients is associated with several limitations in the field.

A significant challenge in developing novel drugs is the efficiency of drug delivery systems to bypass the BBB. This restricted permeability has prompted the development of novel methods to improve CNS drug delivery. With a pathological increase in BBB permeability seemingly contributing to psychiatric vulnerability, treatments focusing on reducing inflammation could indirectly restore BBB integrity improving mood regulation. In parallel, novel therapeutic approaches aiming at transiently and selectively enhancing BBB permeability have become increasingly popular to overcome drug delivery challenges. Focused ultrasound (FUS) is a novel noninvasive therapeutic technology that uses precisely targeted sound waves to interact with deep tissues (Meng et al. 2021). Combined with microbubbles injection, FUS disrupts endothelial TJs, resulting in temporary opening of the BBB and a short time window for drugs (chemotherapy, neurotrophic factors, proteins) to enter the CNS freely (Carpentier et al. 2016; Meng et al. 2021). Interestingly, FUS delivery of GDNF‐loaded microbubbles reduces depressive‐like behaviors in a rat model of depression (Wang, Li, et al. 2020). Injection of escitalopram‐loaded microbubbles, a known SSRI, increased levels and time availability of the drug, also improving the depressive phenotype.

Development of treatment for mental disorders has focused essentially on neuronal function. A growing body of research is exploring whether drugs modulating the BBB could have the potential to treat psychiatric conditions (Matsuno et al. 2022), but, to our knowledge, no specific BBB‐related drug has ever been developed in this context. Exacerbated inflammatory signaling has been targeted by treatment with TNF‐α inhibitors, such as minocycline and infliximab, with antidepressant‐like effects reported in animal models (Amani et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2019). While minocycline failed in randomized trials, infliximab showed an increased improvement of depressive scale in individuals with elevated CRP serum levels prior to treatment (Hellmann‐Regen et al. 2022; Raison et al. 2013). Although significant progress has been made in understanding the neurobiology of mental disorders, effective treatments remain limited, highlighting the need to explore non‐neuronal mechanisms, which could lead to novel or adjunctive therapeutic approaches. Many common IBD treatments target inflammation through anti‐inflammatory or immunosuppressive mechanisms (Kobayashi et al. 2020). Some, such as anti‐TNF therapies, have been associated with improvements in depression and anxiety scores (Siebenhüner et al. 2021). Conversely, antidepressants (e.g., SSRIs) reduce IBD risk, possibly via anti‐inflammatory effects (Frolkis et al. 2019), suggesting that certain psychiatric medications may also offer therapeutic benefits for intestinal inflammation.

Lifestyle interventions like physical exercise reduce symptoms and severity of mental disorders, including MDD and GAD (Hearing et al. 2016; Budde et al. 2025; De Souza Moura et al. 2015). Physical exercise also has a positive impact on comorbid conditions (i.e., anxiety and insomnia), thereby improving the general quality of life in individuals with MDD (Cicek et al. 2025; Wegner et al. 2014). In combination with pharmacotherapy, physical exercise has improved antidepressant effects in individuals with MDD and BD (Fernandes et al. 2022; Lafer et al. 2023); however, the biological mechanisms behind these benefits need to be further explored. Exercise‐mediated antidepressant effect also occurs in stressed rodents, and it can be blocked by chronic administration of a VEGFR2 inhibitor. In line with a role for trophic factors, we observed that stress resilience is associated with increased voluntary physical activity, FGF2, and Cldn5 expression, resulting in intact BBB integrity despite stress exposure (Menard et al. 2025). In addition to physical exercise, dietary interventions also show promise in mitigating psychiatric disorders (Jacka et al. 2017). Nutritional improvements may benefit mental health by enhancing barrier function and modulating the gut microbiota (Wang, Wu, et al. 2022). With changes in microbiota populations associated with both psychiatric symptoms (De Palma et al. 2017; Kelly et al. 2016) and gut–brain barrier integrity, it represents a potential promising therapeutic target for mental illnesses (Aburto and Cryan 2024). Similarly, probiotics, that is, live microorganisms conferring health benefits when administered, showed antidepressant effects in individuals with MDD (Tian et al. 2022) and anxiolytic effects in ulcerative colitis (Liu et al. 2025). A recent randomized controlled trial did not find significant improvements with probiotics but reported that prebiotics—nondigestible food ingredients that selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial microbes—can mitigate stress and anxiety levels (Freijy et al. 2023). Notably, in murine models, specific prebiotics attenuate anxiety‐like behavior in a time‐ and sex‐specific manner, in part by changing the expression of TJ proteins in both the distal colon and hippocampus (Jiang et al. 2023).

To sum up, psychiatric research is increasingly focusing on non‐neuronal systems, such as the BBB, gut barrier, and gut microbiota, to hopefully identify promising targets for innovative treatments.

Gaps and Limitations in the Field

Despite growing interest in biological barriers dysfunction as a contributing factor in psychiatric disorders, several critical gaps remain unaddressed. These include the persistent underrepresentation of sex as a biological variable, the limitations of preclinical models in recapitulating complex human pathology, and the lack of attention to comorbid physical illnesses that influence both barrier integrity and mental health states. Understanding how biological sex, experimental design, and comorbidity profiles influence barrier‐related pathophysiology is crucial for enhancing the translational relevance of preclinical findings and for developing effective interventions.

Sex Differences in Barrier Dysfunction and Psychiatric Disorders

Sex differences are particularly relevant when considering the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders, as epidemiological data consistently reveal striking disparities in prevalence and symptoms (Sweeney et al. 2019). Women are twice as likely to develop MDD and anxiety‐related disorders compared to men (Diflorio and Jones 2010; Kuehner 2017; McLean et al. 2011). Men report more impairments in work‐related functioning and cognition, whereas women report greater social impairment during depressive episodes. Importantly, BBB disruption is not homogeneous across sexes, underscoring the need to consider this factor in both clinical and preclinical research (Dion‐Albert et al. 2022).

Sex hormones modulate BBB integrity, with estrogen upregulating CLDN5 expression in young and middle‐aged females, suggesting that aging affects estrogen responsiveness along with BBB vulnerability (Sandoval and Witt 2011). Estrogen also protects against inflammation‐induced BBB disruption by strengthening TJs and reducing immune cell infiltration; again, these effects diminish with age or estrogen depletion (Maggioli et al. 2016). Astrocytes, which are hormonally responsive, exhibit sex‐ and cycle‐dependent differences in GFAP expression and morphology (Arias et al. 2009). Early‐life steroid exposure can also influence astrocyte distribution and density, as demonstrated by neonatal hormone manipulation studies (Conejo et al. 2005). Despite these insights, female models remain underrepresented in preclinical BBB research, limiting our understanding of the impact of sex on barrier biology.

These concerns extend to the intestinal barrier. While Crohn's disease is more prevalent in men during childhood, its incidence shifts toward women in adulthood. Colitis, in contrast, affects both sexes at similar rates across the lifespan (Kobayashi et al. 2020). Nonetheless, most preclinical studies continue to rely predominantly on male mice, neglecting potential sex‐dependent responses to intestinal inflammation (Bábíčková et al. 2015). This oversight is particularly relevant given that female mice tend to show greater resilience to DSS‐induced colitis, developing less severe disease than their male counterparts (Chassaing et al. 2014). Sex differences are also found in the psychological comorbidities associated with IBD. Women with Crohn's disease are more likely than men to report anxiety and depressive symptoms, whereas in colitis, this pattern appears reversed (Barberio et al. 2021). These findings highlight the need for future studies to investigate whether males or females are more susceptible to behavioral alterations in response to IBD and to delineate the underlying sex‐specific immune and neurobehavioral mechanisms.

Challenges in Preclinical Models of Psychiatric Conditions

The study of barrier dysfunction in psychiatric disorders is significantly limited by the lack of animal models that fully replicate the complex pathology observed in humans. In BD, most existing preclinical models focus on mimicking either manic or depressive episodes but fail to reproduce the hallmark mood cycling that defines the disorder (Beyer and Freund 2017; Logan and McClung 2016). Efforts to model this mood switching, such as repeated cocaine administration, have yielded inconsistent and controversial results, limiting their reliability (Antelman et al. 1998). Similarly, widely used models for anxiety‐ and depressive‐like behaviors, including CSDS, maternal separation, and chronic unpredictable mild stress, were initially developed for depression research (Nestler and Hyman 2010). While these paradigms can induce behavioral changes that resemble anxiety‐like symptoms, they fail to fully capture critical features of GAD, such as persistent hypervigilance and anticipatory threat processing (Golden et al. 2011; Millstein and Holmes 2007; Willner 2005). This overlap enhances the complexity of differentiating anxiety‐specific neurobiological alterations from those linked to depressive symptoms, posing a major challenge for preclinical research (Drzewiecki and Fox 2024).

Similarly, preclinical models of intestinal inflammation, such as DSS‐induced colitis, face translational limitations. Although useful for studying immune and barrier responses, its relevance to human disease is limited. DSS induces colitis mainly through direct epithelial injury followed by immune activation, which does not fully reflect the dysbiosis‐driven pathogenesis of the human condition (Glymenaki et al. 2017). Interpreting behavioral outcomes in this model remains challenging, as reductions in locomotion and exploratory behavior may derive from general sickness or pain rather than anxiety‐ or depression‐like states (Nyuyki et al. 2018). Although DSS models reliably produce behavioral phenotypes associated with anxiety and depression (Zhou et al. 2023; Talley et al. 2021; Nyuyki et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2024), inconsistencies across studies remain, likely driven by factors such as the severity of inflammation, strain, and age of the animals, and testing conditions (Saré et al. 2021). Notably, studies investigating BBB disruption in the context of intestinal inflammation have primarily focused on acute colitis models (Han et al. 2018; Shendy et al. 2024), which do not adequately represent the chronic nature of human IBD (Perše and Cerar 2012). Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether BBB integrity is restored after colitis remission and whether such recovery parallels improvements in mood and cognitive symptoms. These insights are crucial for clarifying the causal relationships between peripheral inflammation and brain dysfunction in psychiatric comorbidities.

Minimal Attention to the Comorbidity Profile

A major limitation in barrier‐focused psychiatric research is the insufficient consideration of the complexity of symptom presentations and co‐occurring diagnoses, such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, Type 2 diabetes, and, as highlighted in this review, IBD (Lindekilde et al. 2022; Shen et al. 2023). Despite strong epidemiological links between these conditions and elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and cognitive dysfunction, they are rarely addressed within neurovascular or gut–brain axis frameworks (Horton and Barrett 2021). Cardiometabolic diseases, in particular, are associated with chronic low‐grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and neurovascular impairment, pathophysiological mechanisms that converge with those seen in mood disorders (González‐Castro et al. 2021). In obesity, elevated proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6) and altered adipokine signaling are associated with BBB disruption, reduced TJ expression, and microglial activation in the Hipp (Alexaki 2021; Yoo et al. 2016). Similarly, Type 2 diabetes is characterized by hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, which induce oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, further compromising BBB structure and neurovascular coupling (Bogush et al. 2017). These mechanisms may interact with psychiatric vulnerability by amplifying neuroinflammation, reducing treatment efficacy, and contributing to the persistence of symptoms (Van Dyken and Lacoste 2018). Yet, direct investigations of BBB or gut barrier dysfunction in individuals with psychiatric and metabolic comorbidities remain scarce. Given that metabolic disorders alter immune signaling, vascular health, and pharmacological response, their integration into barrier‐focused psychiatric models is crucial for advancing translational insight and guiding more personalized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

The BBB and its cellular components, long overlooked in the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders, are emerging as promising targets for therapeutic interventions as well as valuable tools for diagnosis. Increasing evidence implicates BBB dysfunction in the development of mood and anxiety disorders, with consistent findings of altered endothelial integrity, TJ protein expression, and disrupted neurovascular signaling linked to depressive and anxiety‐like phenotypes in both clinical and preclinical studies. Nevertheless, critical questions remain about the causality, timing, and underlying mechanisms of these BBB alterations. Evidence suggests that BBB disruption may not only be a consequence of disease progression but also an active contributor to behavioral vulnerability, with notable sex‐specific effects. In addition to cytokine‐driven barrier alterations, immune‐mediated mechanisms such as peripheral immune cell infiltration and microglial activation are increasingly recognized as drivers of neuroinflammation and psychiatric vulnerability. Integration of these immune pathways with barrier‐focused studies will be essential for a complete understanding of disease etiology. Insights from intestinal barrier research, particularly from models of induced colitis, highlight the broader significance of barrier dysfunction within the gut–brain axis. Despite structural and functional differences, the BBB and gut barriers share key molecular regulators, respond to similar environmental and physiological stressors, and exhibit sex‐ and hormone‐dependent modulation. To advance our understanding of how barrier dysfunction shapes psychiatric vulnerability, future research must employ integrative, longitudinal approaches that simultaneously assess brain and gut barriers while accounting for sex as a biological variable. While this review has emphasized immune‐mediated pathways, it is essential to recognize the contributions of nonimmune mechanisms, including endocrine, neural, and metabolic signaling, in the comorbidity between intestinal inflammation and mental disorders. Understanding the mechanisms that interact and regulate barrier integrity across systems may uncover novel preventive and therapeutic targets, ultimately transforming our understanding of psychiatric disorder etiology and progression.
