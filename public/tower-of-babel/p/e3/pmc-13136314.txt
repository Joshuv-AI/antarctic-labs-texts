Mediating role of self-efficacy in the association between multidimensional resource support and postdoctoral job satisfaction
Authors: Xinqiao Liu, Xinyuan Zhang, Yan Li, Huirui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the core research talent in the challenging and competitive research field, the job satisfaction of postdocs is highly important for research output, technological advancement, and the future reserve of talent. This study explores the impact of resource support on postdoctoral job satisfaction using data from Nature’s 2023 postdoc survey. The findings showed that postdocs generally report high levels of job satisfaction, but there are significant differences in the resource support provided by their institutions, with career development support particularly in need of improvement. Supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support all positively predict job satisfaction among postdocs, with self-efficacy playing a significant mediating role. The results emphasize the importance of increasing resource support for postdoctoral work and suggest that postdoctoral job satisfaction can be enhanced by guaranteeing postdoctoral research autonomy, building an inclusive work culture, improving postdoctoral career development skills, and increasing career opportunities.Keywords: New quality productive forces, Postdoc, Job satisfaction, Resource support, Self-efficacySubject terms: Business and management; Business and management; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Talent constitutes the most critical element in developing new-quality productive forces, with high-quality scientific researchers directly determining the overall level of human capital. Postdoctoral researchers are a core group of high-quality talent reserves for scientific innovation and technological advancement1,2. Their academic and professional achievements, often rooted in disciplinary and industrial frontiers, may serve as pivotal indicators of transformative progress, holding profound significance for socioeconomic advancement3. However, postdocs are often overlooked and forgotten in academia. In an academic ecosystem with increasingly intense research competition, postdoctoral researchers often encounter diverse and complex mental health symptoms, which can adversely impact their job satisfaction4. Notably, empirical evidence reveals suboptimal job satisfaction among the group5. A survey from the United States demonstrated that approximately 20% of postdoctoral researchers have lost interest in the goal of pursuing a tenure track academic position6. Postdoctoral researchers lack adequate work support in career development, diminishing job satisfaction evaluations7. Prolonged exposure to such circumstances may engender adverse impacts on both individual career trajectories and broader societal development.

The well-being of highly educated individuals is receiving widespread attention8. Job satisfaction, as a crucial component of well-being, typically refers to an individual’s cognition of their work environment and a positive emotional state generated in pursuit of work goals9. As a critical topic in management and economics research, enhancing job satisfaction can effectively improve job performance, reduce employee turnover, and strengthen organizational cohesion10. From an academic perspective, improving postdoctoral job satisfaction can foster positive work attitudes and encourage the formation and implementation of innovative research ideas. It can also enhance academic enthusiasm and ultimately influence work performance11. Moreover, positive perceptions of work may permeate daily life, exerting extensive influences on postdoctoral researchers’ physical and mental health as well as social relationships through positive emotional spillover effects12,13. Therefore, sustained efforts to improve postdoctoral researchers’ satisfaction and well-being in their work and living conditions represent a long-term strategy to ensure the continuous production and supply of scientific research talent. As a subjective experience, the level of job satisfaction is inherently associated with individual factors14, including value systems, cognitive capacities, and emotional states. For researchers, material resources such as salaries certainly affect their evaluation of job satisfaction. Notably, the fulfillment of intrinsic needs, such as professional autonomy, intellectual independence, academic accomplishment, and a sense of fulfillment, also constitutes fundamental motivators for career development. This reality necessitates enhanced resource support from organizational environments.

In this context, understanding the factors influencing postdoctoral job satisfaction is highly important for providing better practices to promote postdoctoral success. This study explores the impacts of different types and levels of resource support on postdoctoral job satisfaction from multiple perspectives and further elucidates the underlying mechanisms involved. The results provide empirical evidence and recommendations for initiatives to improve the survival situation of postdocs and enhance their job satisfaction, thereby contributing to the enhancement of national scientific research capabilities and innovative vitality.

The job demands-resources model

The job demands-resources (JD-R) theory, a classic framework for analyzing the relationships among job demands, burnout, and job performance, provides theoretical support for exploring the connection between postdoctoral resource support and job satisfaction15. This theory systematically categorizes work conditions into two dimensions16. The first is job demand, which refers to the expenditure of energy on work, including aspects such as work pressure and emotional requirements. The second is job resources, which denote support in the work environment for achieving work goals, encompassing factors such as autonomy, environmental support, and social support. From the perspective of resource composition, individual resources and social resources constitute the two major aspects of job resources, serving as the strongest stimulants for work motivation and wellbeing17. In this sense, when the resource supply dynamically matches job demands, work effectiveness is significantly enhanced.

For postdoctoral researchers engaged in scientific endeavors, their resource support system has distinctive complexity. This system requires institutional resources such as career development channels and reasonable salaries provided by the organization. It also relies on professional resources such as professional guidance from the supervisory team and technical support from the scientific research platform18. This multidimensional resource framework not only directly facilitates the achievement of research goals but also, more importantly, enhances individuals’ psychological capital, thereby optimizing their work-related emotional experiences19. This resource-driven positive reinforcement mechanism ultimately manifests in postdoctoral researchers’ job satisfaction evaluations, establishing a complete pathway from resource provision to psychological perception. Given that the quantity and quality of available resource support vary significantly and represent key points for institutional policy intervention, this study primarily focuses on the resource pathway within the JD-R theory. This constitutes a resource-centered extension of the JD-R model, aiming to deepen our understanding of resource-driven pathways.

Resource support and postdoctoral job satisfaction

The relationship between postdoctoral researchers’ research effectiveness and supervisor support is being validated. Supervisors, who serve as the primary leaders of postdoctoral researchers and provide them with professional and psychosocial support20, may directly influence the psychology and behavior of postdocs through interpersonal interactions and the shaping of work conditions21–23. Emotional support from supervisors for postdoctoral researchers, such as assistance, encouragement, and respect, can foster positive emotions in the workplace, enabling them to maintain high levels of work engagement and enhance job dedication24. In addition, instrumental support from supervisors, such as facilitating access to research resources, providing academic guidance, and creating career development opportunities, can indirectly enhance postdocs’ professional competence and resource availability25. Based on previous research, we propose Hypothesis 1.

Hypothesis 1

Supervisor support has a direct positive predictive effect on postdoctoral job satisfaction.

Financial support directly affects the survival needs of postdocs, and its core contradiction lies in the mismatch between the labor value and remuneration. The career status and emotional experience of postdoctoral researchers are closely related to their salary and welfare26,27. Financial problems such as institutional budget cuts and insufficient funding have become more prominent in recent years28. According to the result of global postdoctoral survey conducted by Nature, financial struggles have caused postdocs to feel strongly exploited, which is the main factor causing anxiety and pain29. Based on this, we propose Hypothesis 2.

Hypothesis 2

Financial support has a direct positive predictive effect on postdoctoral job satisfaction.

Organizational resource support constitutes the structural basis for postdoctoral career development. Empirical studies have demonstrated that job satisfaction has a significant correlation with the holistic development of work environment30. This is particularly true for occupations that are highly dependent on the work environment, where a highly supportive environment enhances workers’ positive emotional experiences18. Concurrently, strategic resource allocation and organizational safeguards effectively promote diversified career pathways and alleviate psychological stressors for postdocs31,32. Based on previous research, we propose Hypothesis 3.

Hypothesis 3

Organizational support has a direct positive predictive effect on postdoctoral job satisfaction.

Career development support is an effective motivational factor for postdoctoral job satisfaction. However, the current situation is that the bleak prospects of career development and the lack of promotion opportunities have led to the erosion of academic enthusiasm and confidence among postdoctoral researchers33,34. In addition, the instability of postdoctoral career is mainly reflected in the high competitiveness of academic path and the lack of social recognition of non-academic path. A survey revealed that 85% of postdoctoral researchers wish to pursue academic careers in the future, yet fewer than 3% secure tenured positions35. Postdoctoral researchers’ transient employment status places them in professionally precarious positions36,37, and transitions to industry or the public sector are often perceived as career compromises38. These dilemmas exacerbate postdocs’ anxiety about the future, and have been identified as critical predictors of reduced positive job evaluations among postdocs39,40. Based on existing research, we propose Hypothesis 4.

Hypothesis 4

Career development support has a direct positive predictive effect on postdoctoral job satisfaction.

Research has emphasized the necessity of constructing support systems that extend beyond the singular dimension to enhance the academic engagement and career development of early career researchers41. In this sense, the impacts of multifaceted resource support on the work implementation and job satisfaction of researchers have attracted extensive attention and scholarly discussion42,43. It is worth noting that demographic differences, such as gender and duration in postdoctoral positions, are closely associated with variances in how postdoctoral researchers perceive work environments and engage with institutional resources. Subjective factors, including values and cognitive abilities, also contribute significantly to the heterogeneity in job evaluations. A previous study demonstrated that female postdoctoral researchers exhibit notably lower levels of job satisfaction and institutional belonging than their male counterparts do44.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy represents an important personal resource, which plays a crucial role in an individual’s achievements and future development45,46. According to JD-R theory, job resources can predict personal resources, and personal resources can play a similar role as job resources47. On the one hand, resources support in the workplace are believed to facilitate the development of self-efficacy. Postdoctoral researchers often require research independence and autonomy in their work48, which cannot be achieved without the resource support and inclusive environment provided by their work settings. A lack of research autonomy can undermine professional confidence and academic enthusiasm, reduce motivation in work and research, and weaken individual self-efficacy. On the other hand, individuals with high self-efficacy possess stronger beliefs in their ability to control their environment, which exerts a positive impact on their well-being. Self-efficacy theory holds a similar view that self-efficacy serves as a robust predictor of job satisfaction and is closely linked to personal achievements49. When individuals experience enhanced self-efficacy, they exhibit greater confidence in task completion and stronger recognition of their achievements and capabilities50.

Research has shown that individual feelings of achievement and satisfaction are critical factors among the variables influencing postdoctoral academic persistence48. This finding indicates that the academic career development of postdocs relies on achieving accomplishments and recognizing their own capabilities. An empirical study has also shown that organizational support is indirectly associated with postdoctoral job satisfaction through personal achievement, with work interest playing a moderating role in this relationship51. These findings offer new insights into the mechanisms through which resource support influences postdoctoral job satisfaction. Resource support can enhance postdocs’ recognition of personal achievements and capabilities by increasing work outcomes and shape emotional evaluations of the research process by strengthening self-efficacy52. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 5.

Hypothesis 5

Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between four types of resource support and postdoctoral job satisfaction.

Theoretical and practical implications

Existing studies have provided a preliminary depiction of the significance of resource support for postdoctoral researchers’ academic career development and job satisfaction, yet several critical research gaps remain. First, there is a lack of systematic exploration of resource support. Most investigations focus on supervisor support, with relatively limited inquiry into other types of resource support. Second, the current literature reveals that few studies have examined the mediating role of personal accomplishment in the relationship between organizational support and job satisfaction among postdocs. However, they adopted an overly narrow conceptualization of resource support and lacked more empirical validation. The conclusions may be subject to biases stemming from sample selection and methodological design, necessitating further verification. Third, the extant literature has failed to develop novel research perspectives in response to rapidly evolving social contexts. As postdocs face increasingly complex external contexts over time, dynamic research on the resource support necessary for their career development remains scarce. Against this backdrop, this study employs JD-R theory to empirically investigate the relationship between resource support and postdoctoral job satisfaction (Fig. 1).

Theoretical research framework.

It is important to note that career development support is identified as a distinct resource dimension, which has often been overlooked in previous JD-R research focusing on corporate environments. Theoretically, this study constructs and validates a more comprehensive explanatory model of postdoctoral work experience. This not only expands the application of JD-R within academic career contexts but also deepens our understanding of the underlying processes through which resource support influences individual attitudes and emotions. At the practical level, the findings provide empirical evidence and concrete directions for research institutions and policymakers, aiming to achieve a win–win outcome for both individual development and organizational innovation.

Materials and methods

Participants

The data utilized in this study were derived from Nature’s 2023 postdoc survey. It collected information on postdoctoral researchers’ demographic characteristics, current work status, satisfaction with the work environment and organizational support, career development preferences, and artificial intelligence usage. The survey was administered over a seven-week period and distributed via multiple channels to nature subscribers in three language versions: English, Spanish, and Chinese. A total of 6571 postdocs from 93 countries voluntarily participated, yielding 3838 valid samples after data screening. Among the 3838 respondents, 49% were male, 39% conducted postdoctoral research in their home countries, 86% were under 40 years of age, 48% had been in postdoctoral positions for two years or less, and 4% had worked outside universities or research institutions. The sample spans a broad range of academic disciplines, including the natural sciences, engineering, humanities and social sciences, and interdisciplinary fields, ensuring diverse disciplinary representations.

Measures

Postdoctoral job satisfaction

Postdoctoral job satisfaction was measured by a single item: “How satisfied are you in your current postdoc”. The respondents rated their satisfaction on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (“extremely dissatisfied”) to 7 (“extremely satisfied”), with higher scores indicating greater job satisfaction.

Resource support

Supervisor support was measured by two items assessing the quantity of guidance/communication provided by supervisors and the extent to which supervisors promptly address postdocs’ needs. The respondents self-reported their level of satisfaction or agreement with the following statements: “Amount of guidance from and communication with supervisor/PI” and “My manager/supervisor/PI has a good awareness of support services and was able to signpost me to them if needed”. Responses were captured on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “extremely dissatisfied” or “strongly disagree”, 7 = “extremely satisfied” or “strongly agree”), where higher scores indicated greater perceived supervisor support.

Financial support was measured by postdoctoral researchers’ perceived level of support in terms of compensation. The respondents were asked the following item: “Thinking about your current postdoc, how satisfied are you with the following?—Salary/compensation”. The response options ranged from 1 (“extremely dissatisfied”) to 7 (“extremely satisfied”), with higher scores indicating greater perceived financial support.

Organizational support was measured with seven items assessing postdocs’ perceptions and recognition of the support provided by their current work organization. The respondents self-reported their agreement or satisfaction with the statements, one of which was “Mental health and wellbeing services in my workplace/university are tailored and appropriate to the needs of postdocs”. Response options ranged from 1 (“strongly disagree” or “extremely dissatisfied”) to 7 (“strongly agree” or “extremely satisfied”). The mean score across all the items represented the overall level of perceived organizational support, with higher mean scores indicating greater perceived organizational support.

Career development support was measured via two items. Postdocs were asked to respond “How do you feel about your future job prospects?” (rated on a 5-point scale where 1 = “extremely negative” and 5 = “extremely positive”) and “Thinking about your current postdoc, how satisfied are you with the following? —Career advancement opportunities” (rated on a 7-point scale where 1 = “extremely dissatisfied” and 7 = “extremely satisfied”). Higher scores on both items indicated greater perceived career development support.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured via postdocs’ self-reported recognition of achievement, rated on a 7-point scale (1 = “extremely dissatisfied”, 7 = “extremely satisfied”). Higher scores reflect stronger self-efficacy and greater endorsement of personal accomplishments.

Control variables

The control variables included gender, age, academic discipline, continent of the workplace, and whether postdoctoral research was conducted in the home country.The complete scale for this study can be found in Appendix.

Data analysis

This study employed Stata 17.0 and Mplus 7.0 for the statistical analyses. First, Stata 17.0 was used to process the variables and conduct descriptive statistical analyses. Second, regression analysis was performed to examine the relationship between resource support and postdoctoral job satisfaction. Finally, Mplus 7.0 was used to test the mediating role of self-efficacy in the hypothesized relationships. To ensure effective comparison and aggregation in regression and mediation analyses, we standardized all continuous variables to z-scores.

Model 1 examined the effect of supervisor support on postdoctoral job satisfaction. The results showed that the quantity of guidance/communication from supervisors (β = 0.355, p < 0.01) and the fulfillment of guidance needs (β = 0.178, p < 0.01) both positively predict job satisfaction. Postdocs with more frequent guidance/communication and more timely fulfillment of their guidance needs reported greater job satisfaction. Age negatively predicted job satisfaction (β = − 0.100, p < 0.05), indicating that younger postdocs were more satisfied with their job. Additionally, postdocs conducting research in their home countries reported higher job satisfaction (β = − 0.125, p < 0.05). Hypothesis 1 was confirmed.

Model 2 examined the effect of financial support on postdoctoral job satisfaction. The results indicated that financial support significantly and positively predicted job satisfaction (β = 0.400, p < 0.01), with higher levels of financial support associated with greater postdoctoral job satisfaction. Gender (β = 0.118, p < 0.05), age (β = − 0.203, p < 0.01), and workplace (β = − 0.143, p < 0.01) also predicted job satisfaction. Specifically, male postdocs, younger postdocs, and those conducting research in their home countries reported higher job satisfaction. Hypothesis 2 was confirmed.

Model 3 examined the effect of organizational support on postdoctoral job satisfaction and revealed that organizational support was a significant positive predictor of postdoctoral job satisfaction (β = 0.754, p < 0.01). In other words, higher levels of perceived organizational support were associated with greater postdoctoral job satisfaction. Similar to the results of Model 1, younger age (β = − 0.122, p < 0.05) and conducting postdoctoral research in the home country (β = − 0.133, p < 0.05) were both associated with greater job satisfaction. Hypothesis 3 was confirmed.

Model 4 examined the effect of career development support on postdoctoral job satisfaction. The results revealed that both job prospects (β = 0.260, p < 0.01) and promotion opportunities (β = 0.399, p < 0.01) positively predict job satisfaction. Specifically, postdocs with more optimistic job prospects and greater access to advancement opportunities reported higher levels of job satisfaction. Additionally, a significant association emerged whereby those conducting postdoctoral research in their home countries presented greater job satisfaction (β = -0.170, p < 0.01), which is consistent with findings from prior models. Hypothesis 4 was confirmed.

Model 5 was designed to examine the overall effect of resource support on postdoctoral job satisfaction, with findings indicating that all four support dimensions positively predict job satisfaction. Postdocs with more frequent guidance/communication (β = 0.245, p < 0.01) and more timely fulfillment of their support needs (β = 0.030, p < 0.05) reported higher job satisfaction. Higher levels of financial support (β = 0.208, p < 0.01) and organizational support (β = 0.233, p < 0.01) were associated with greater job satisfaction. Within career development support, more optimistic job prospects (β = 0.155, p < 0.01) and greater access to career advancement opportunities (β = 0.158, p < 0.01) were also linked to greater satisfaction. Consistently, the analysis revealed that postdocs conducting research in their home countries reported higher job satisfaction (β = -0.131, p < 0.01), a pattern that was consistent across multiple models.

Model 6 analyzed data from Chinese postdocs, utilizing a smaller sample size of 91 individuals. The results revealed that financial support, organizational support, and career development support presented significant positive predictive relationships with postdoctoral job satisfaction. Specifically, greater financial support (β = 0.241, p < 0.05) and stronger organizational support (β = 0.289, p < 0.1) were associated with greater job satisfaction. In terms of career development support, more optimistic job prospects (β = 0.220, p < 0.1) and greater access to career advancement opportunities (β = 0.301, p < 0.01) also contributed to greater satisfaction. Notably, while supervisor support had a positive effect, its influence on postdoctoral job satisfaction was not statistically significant in the Chinese context after controlling for other support dimensions (p > 0.1).

Overall, after controlling for other variables, supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support were found to positively predict postdoctoral job satisfaction. These findings suggest that resource support has a direct positive effect on postdoctoral job satisfaction.

Direct effect tests revealed that supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support all directly and positively predict postdoctoral job satisfaction (p < 0.01). The standardized estimated coefficients for these dimensions were all greater than 0, and their 95% confidence intervals did not include zero. These results confirm that when supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support are better, postdocs are more satisfied with their work environment.

When self-efficacy was incorporated into the analysis as a mediator, the indirect effects of guidance/communication quantity and guidance need fulfillment were 0.105 (p < 0.01) and 0.040 (p < 0.01) respectively. Both effects had 95% confidence intervals excluding zero. These findings suggest that self-efficacy plays a significant role as a mediator in the pathway through which supervisor support affects job satisfaction. The indirect effect of self-efficacy on the relationship between financial support and job satisfaction was 0.069 (p < 0.01), with a confidence interval of [0.052, 0.087]. Similarly, the indirect effect of self-efficacy on the relationship between organizational support and job satisfaction was 0.147 (p < 0.01), with a confidence interval of [0.127, 0.166]. These results suggest that the positive predictive effects of financial support and organizational support on job satisfaction are partially achieved through enhancing postdoctoral self-efficacy. Finally, in terms of career development support, the estimated indirect effects mediated by self-efficacy for job prospects and promotion opportunities on job satisfaction were 0.040 (p < 0.01) and 0.122 (p < 0.01), respectively, with 95% confidence intervals for both estimates excluding zero. The findings indicate that self-efficacy plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between career development support and job satisfaction.

Overall, self-efficacy partially mediates the relationship between postdocs’ resource support and job satisfaction. Supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support all have direct positive effects on job satisfaction, which can be further reinforced by indirect effects mediated through self-efficacy, confirming Hypothesis 5.

Discussion

First, statistical analyses reveal that overall scores of postdoctoral job satisfaction are relatively high, whereas substantial variations exist in the level of resource support provided by institutions. Specifically, the level of organizational support is relatively high, whereas career development support is the most inadequate, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies5. This discrepancy may be associated with postdocs’ expectations for academic career development and the highly competitive nature of the academic labor market. Postdoctoral researchers currently face significant pressures in their academic career progression53. On the one hand, the continuous increase in the number of academic researchers has led to a gradual shift in the academic labor market toward a buyer’s market. On the other hand, the temporary nature of postdoctoral positions may amplify their job insecurity, exacerbating concerns about future academic career development54. Thus, their demand for career development support far exceeds that for other dimensions of support.

Second, the regression results indicate that supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support all positively predict postdoctoral job satisfaction. Higher levels of this resource support are associated with greater job satisfaction among postdocs, validating the core proposition of the job demands-resources theory. It further confirms that postdocs’ emotional and cognitive perceptions of their work are the result of combined effects from multiple resource support factors17,19. This mechanism finds resonance in broader organizational behavior research. For instance, studies in management have demonstrated that supportive resources have a positive impact on employees55. Although increasing overall resource support levels is a valid strategy based on the global findings, the non-significant effect of supervisor support in the Chinese sub-sample highlights the need for culturally nuanced approaches. In the Chinese context, prioritizing financial support, organizational support and career development support may yield greater immediate benefits for satisfaction than generic increases in supervisor interaction frequency. This may be attributed to potential biases arising from the small sample size or associated with the unique teacher‒student cultural patterns within China’s traditional education system56. Specifically, supervisor‒student relationship has evolved into an employment relationship under the influence of hierarchical and teacher-esteem moral culture57. Students tend to passively receive guidance in such a context, resulting in limited effectiveness in enhancing satisfaction.

Third, this study further reveals that self-efficacy mediates the positive effects of the four dimensions of resource support on postdoctoral job satisfaction. The finding indicates that increases in resource support spur postdoctoral researchers’ work engagement, thereby enhancing their job performance. This process increases their personal achievements and strengthens self-recognition of their accomplishments. Ultimately, it leads to more positive evaluations of job satisfaction. For universities and research institutions, this finding highlights the need to continuously increase resource support for postdoctoral researchers. It also underscores the importance of understanding the underlying logic of resource investment.

Implications for educational practice

From a national strategic perspective, postdoctoral researchers serve as a core force in talent cultivation under the new system for mobilizing resources nationwide, and their cultivation and development merit increased resource support and prioritization. To promote the sustainable development of postdocs, the following recommendations are proposed.

First, resource supports are key approaches to pave new paths for improving postdoctoral cultivation quality. The effectiveness of resource support can be sustainably enhanced by introducing artificial intelligence technologies to upgrade mental health services58, implementing flexible work systems, and optimizing postdoctoral evaluation metrics. Working conditions, welfare, and anti-discrimination measures should be among the primary concerns of research institutions59. Additionally, addressing the pressing need for postdoctoral career development and enhancing professional competitiveness in multiple dimensions are essential. For research institutions, they should strengthen support and guarantees by broadening employment prospects and increasing promotion opportunities, thereby enhancing postdocs’ sense of security in their professional environment. At the policy level, policymakers need to explore effective approaches to balance the supply of postdoctoral talent with market demands. They must ensure coordination between safeguarding their academic career aspirations and maintaining stable employment order. Efforts should also be made to guide postdocs in adopting diverse career perspectives and proactively aligning their professional choices with socioeconomic development needs60.

Second, enhancing postdoctoral self-efficacy represents an effective approach to improve their job satisfaction. Self-efficacy stems from performance accomplishments, vicarious experiences, forms of social persuasion, and physiological/emotional indexes61. It is essential to design phased skill development programs, leveraging incremental achievements to boost self-efficacy. Training supervisors to provide specific, growth-oriented feedback is also an effective strategy.

Third, attention should be given to the work experiences of nonnative postdocs to attract a sustained inflow of global research talent. Universities and institutions should promote communication and mutual understanding among postdocs from diverse cultural backgrounds by providing inclusive programs and activities, as well as establishing multicultural organizations and service centers. The feelings of loneliness and cultural barriers among nonnative postdocs should be alleviated to enhance their job satisfaction.

Limitations

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, this study is based on data from a published survey. The pre-existing nature of this data imposes inherent limitations on the measurement of our variables. The measurement strategy this study employed represents the closest possible approximation to the theoretical constructs, given the constraints of the available data. Second, the data collection employed a cross-sectional design, which, although revealing associations between resource support and postdoctoral job satisfaction, cannot strictly validate causal relationships among variables. Third, while the study included a valid sample size of 3,838, only 91 were Chinese, which may undermine the reliability of the analytical results in the Chinese context. Additionally, academic cultures and perceptions of supervisor roles vary across countries or regions, which could differentially influence the effects of resource support. However, this study did not systematically examine the moderating role of cultural differences. Future research should explore relevant mechanisms through mixed-method designs, multidimensional measurement scales, the collection of longitudinal data, stratified sampling methods, and cross-cultural comparisons. Finally, this study focused on examining the role of resource support but did not incorporate specific job demands (e.g., workload, publication pressure, job instability) into the empirical model. Future research should construct a comprehensive model incorporating job demands-resources interactions to more fully validate the application of JD-R theory among postdoctoral researchers.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study conducts an empirical analysis using data from global postdoctoral researchers to explore the impact of resource support on job satisfaction. The findings reveal that postdoctoral job satisfaction is generally high, whereas structural disparities exist in terms of resource support, with notable deficiencies in career development and financial support. Supervisor support, financial support, organizational support, and career development support all positively predict postdoctoral job satisfaction, and self-efficacy plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between resource support and job satisfaction. These conclusions fill a gap in global comparative research on postdoctoral working conditions. Furthermore, the unique pattern observed within the Chinese sub-sample highlights the need for culture-sensitive investigations in future research, despite its size limitation. More nuanced and culturally grounded work is required to unpack the pathways shaping the association between postdoctoral resources and satisfaction.
