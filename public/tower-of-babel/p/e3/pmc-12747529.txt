Establishment and micropropagation of axenic in vitro cultures of salt marsh grasses within the genus Sporobolus
Authors: Elena L Peredo, Suzanne Thomas, Zoe G Cardon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseSalt marshes in the North Atlantic United States are dominated by grasses from the genus Sporobolus, which are perennial C4 plants known for tolerating saline, anoxic, and flooded coastal sediments. Establishing in vitro cultures of Sporobolus species remains a major challenge due to frequent seed contamination from ergot and endophytic bacteria in vegetative tissues.Methods and ResultsHere, we demonstrate that in vitro axenic cultures of Sporobolus alterniflorus can be established in four weeks and micropropagation chains in three months with a 75% success rate. Additionally, this method is easily transferable to other salt marsh grasses, such as S. cynosuroides.ConclusionsThe strength of our method lies in bypassing adventitious regeneration or somatic embryogenesis. Instead, we remove the hypocotyls and endosperm, primary sources of fungal contamination, and induce adventitious roots in the epicotyl. We also detail culture‐ and PCR‐based methods to screen for contamination in vitroplants of S. alterniflorus.Keywords: axenic culture, multiplication, Mycobacterium, root induction, salt marsh, seeds, Spartina alterniflora, Spartina cynosuroides

Salt marshes are key coastal habitats that provide habitat for fish, birds, shellfish, and other fauna to complete their life cycle. On the East Coast of the United States, these ecosystems are highly productive, accumulating large quantities of organic matter through root production and rhizodeposition. In addition, salt marshes help to protect coasts from erosion and contribute to improving water quality by reducing eutrophication.

Salt marshes in the North Atlantic region of the United States are dominated by near monocultures of the ecosystem engineer Sporobolus alterniflorus (Loisel.) P.M. Peterson & Saarela (Peterson et al., 2014). Sporobolus cynosuroides (Loisel.) P.M. Peterson & Saarela (Peterson et al., 2014), commonly known as big cordgrass, is another salt‐tolerant species often found in the same salt marshes of the United States as S. alterniflorus, S. pumilus (Roth) P.M. Peterson & Saarela, and Phragmites australis (Cav.) Trin. ex Steud. (Vázquez‐Lule et al., 2022). Sporobolus alterniflorus and S. cynosuroides were previously classified as Spartina alterniflora Loisel. and Spartina cynosuroides Loisel., respectively. Their current placement in the genus Sporobolus remains under active discussion within the scientific community (Bortolus et al., 2019). These plants are widespread, large, perennial C4 grasses that attain dominance in low intertidal areas due to their ability to tolerate the saline, anoxic, flooded sediments of coastal plains. Sporobolus alterniflorus is native to the temperate Atlantic coasts of the Americas where it is naturally distributed from Brazil to Canada (Mobberley, 1956; Costa and Davy, 1992) and invasive following introduction in the West Coast of the United States, Europe, South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and China (Liu et al., 2019, 2020).

Sporobolus alterniflorus also tolerates—and even thrives in—high sulfide concentrations that accumulate in salt marsh sediments as a byproduct of sulfate‐reducing bacteria (Chambers et al., 1998). Sulfide is highly phytotoxic, impairing the plant metabolic activity by enzyme poisoning and particularly targeting mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase. The sulfide tolerance of S. alterniflorus has been attributed to several mechanisms, including its substantial capacity for catalyzing sulfide oxidation in root tips (Lee et al., 1999), presence of aerenchyma (Maricle and Lee, 2002), the constitutively low cytochrome c oxidase activity (Martin and Maricle, 2015), and its close relationship with diverse sulfur‐oxidizing bacteria that detoxify the rhizosphere by transforming sulfide into sulfate (Kolton et al., 2020; Rolando et al., 2022; Pérez Castro et al., 2023). Sporobolus cynosuroides is also tolerant to sulfur compounds (Hackney and Avery, 2015) and occurs along the East Coast of the United States from Maine to the Gulf of Mexico, favoring low tidal amplitude sites with salinity levels ranging from 0 to 10 ppt. This species often dominates transitional zones between freshwater upper marshes and seaward salt marshes, as well as brackish bays and river mouths (Stalter and Lonard, 2022).

Globally, marshes are increasingly threatened by human‐mediated degradation of coastal systems, including urban sprawl (Gedan et al., 2011), pollution (Bergen et al., 2000), drought (Hughes et al., 2012), and accelerating sea level rise (Snedden et al., 2015; Valiela et al., 2023). The physiological adaptations of Sporobolus species alone are not sufficient to ensure the long‐term survival of salt marshes under the current climate change scenarios. One common strategy to recover these areas is through salt marsh restoration efforts, which often involve repopulating damaged areas with seedlings and/or transplants (Bergen et al., 2000). However, to preserve population structure and enhance restoration outcomes, it is important to consider the genetic characteristics of the planting stock, as different genotypes of S. alterniflorus exhibit distinct growth habits (Seliskar et al., 2002; Proffitt et al., 2003) and local adaptations can influence transplant success (Mateos‐Naranjo and Redondo‐Gómez, 2016; Crosby et al., 2017).

One strategy to address the limited availability of plant material from vulnerable populations is laboratory‐based multiplication of donor plants. In vitro multiplication offers the advantage of producing large numbers of plants in a short period, independent of seasonal constraints, and requires less space and fewer resources than ex vitro methods. This technique has been successfully applied to severely endangered or even extinct‐in‐the‐wild species, demonstrating its value for ex situ germplasm conservation, restoration, and plant breeding (Bunn et al., 2011; Delmail et al., 2012; Carmona‐Martín et al., 2014; Werden et al., 2020; Salazar‐Díaz and Urrea Trujillo, 2025). Furthermore, in vitro techniques enable biotechnological applications such as genetic transformation, gene editing, pathogen eradication, and germplasm cryopreservation. Axenic plants are also valuable tools for studying physiological responses and gaining mechanistic insights into host–microbiome interactions.

Despite the potential of biotechnological approaches, few protocols exist for the in vitro propagation of S. alterniflorus (Wang et al., 2003; Czakó et al., 2006; Utomo et al., 2008) or S. cynosuroides (Li and Gallagher, 1996). Attempts to establish axenic culture seeds, roots, or apical shoots have historically failed due to persistent bacterial and fungal contamination (Musselman, 1977). Our own trials using rhizomes and young shoots testing various combinations of surface sterilization methods, carbon‐free media, antibiotics, and fungicides resulted in initial growth, but ultimately failed due to contamination. A major challenge is the diverse endophytic microbiome of Sporobolus, which is not eliminated by surface sterilization alone. For example, S. alterniflorus harbors endophytic Cyanobacteria and Spirochaetes (Hong et al., 2015). Additionally, the establishment of seed‐based in vitro cultures is particularly problematic due to the presence of Claviceps purpurea (ergot), a seed‐borne fungal pathogen commonly found in S. alterniflorus and S. cynosuroides (Eleuterius and Meyers, 1974). Our initial efforts to introduce in vitro surface‐sterilized seeds failed as the seeds (>500) uniformly displayed fungal contamination within days and fungicide treatments using carbendazim (0.5–5 mg/L in Murashige and Skoog [MS] medium) were ineffective. These results align with prior reports of ergot's resistance to multiple fungicides (Kaur et al., 2015).

To circumvent these issues, previous protocols relied on indirect organogenesis or somatic embryogenesis induced from immature inflorescences or seedling tissues. However, these methods are often multi‐step, time‐consuming processes with varying success rates. For instance, Wang et al. (2003) employed a sequential process including callus induction, shoot formation, and root development, achieving only ~12% shoot regeneration. Utomo et al. (2008) reported high rates of pre‐embryogenic callus and somatic embryo induction, although the initial success rate for friable callus was not specified. Czakó et al. (2006) successfully transformed S. alterniflorus pre‐embryonic callus tissue.

Here, we present an efficient and reproductive micropropagation protocol for the establishment of axenic in vitro cultures of salt marsh grasses that overcome the traditional challenges associated with Sporobolus species. We first describe a highly effective and transferable tissue culturing approach that bypasses endogenous bacterial and fungal contaminants, and we then define culture‐based and PCR‐based methods for verifying the axenicity of the resultant vitroplants.

METHODS AND RESULTS

Seed collection and storage

Mature caryopsis (hereafter referred to as “seeds”) of S. alterniflorus were collected from Little Sippewissett (Cape Cod, Massachusetts, USA) during late October and early November 2022. Mature seeds of S. cynosuroides were collected in Plum Island along the Parker River during October 2023 and January 2024. Entire panicles were cut from the plants and transported to the laboratory in plastic bags containing wet paper towels. Spikelets were manually removed and stored in clean plastic zip‐lock bags with fresh, wet paper towels. To prevent desiccation, tightly closed zip‐lock bags were placed inside sealed containers and stored at 4°C in the darkness for at least two weeks prior to use.

Ex vitro germination under controlled conditions

Seeds were germinated ex vitro in sterilized germination boxes under controlled conditions. Germination boxes were prepared by filling reusable culture vessels to one‐quarter of their volume with soil. The soil was saturated with water and then autoclaved for 1 h. After a 48‐h incubation at room temperature (20–22°C), the containers were autoclaved a second time for 1 h. Repeated autoclaving after incubation is a common soil sterilization technique intended to allow any spores that survived the first cycle to germinate and be eliminated during the second cycle (King et al., 2024).

Before sowing, the seeds were surface sterilized. In the Massachusetts populations tested here, and in many other areas, Sporobolus seeds frequently carry fungal contaminants, thus complete seed sterility cannot be achieved at this stage. While this step could be considered optional, as germination occurs ex vitro and seeds are not axenic at this stage, we strongly recommend it. This procedure significantly reduces the risk of bacterial or fungal contamination that could compromise seedling health during germination. During surface sterilization, approximately 50 seeds per tube were placed in conical Falcon tubes containing 40 mL of commercial bleach diluted to 20% (v/v) in water. Tubes were gently agitated to ensure full contact with the solution. After 20 min, the bleach solution was discarded, and seeds were rinsed for 5 min in autoclaved distilled water. This process was repeated three times.

Surface‐sterilized seeds were then placed in the germination boxes and incubated at 20–22°C under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of approximately 100 μmol m− 2 s− 1. The soil was kept saturated by adding autoclaved water as needed. Under these conditions, seedlings began to emerge 10–15 days post‐sowing. Once seedlings developed a robust aerial structure (~5 cm in length) and a deep green coloration, typically 7–10 days after emergence, they were considered ready for transfer to in vitro conditions.

Evaluation of genetic variability in seedlings

Genetic variability among the S. alterniflorus seedlings was confirmed using random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) analysis. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from seedlings using the Maxwell RSC PureFood GMO and Authentication Kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA). For PCR amplification, 10 ng of DNA was used per reaction with Platinum SuperFi DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massuchusetts, USA), following the manufacturer's recommendations. All amplifications were carried out in duplicate, using 10‐mer primers OPA04–OPA11 from the RAPD Kit A (Eurofins Genomics, Ebersberg, Germany), following the amplification conditions and scoring criteria described in Peredo et al. (2009). The rationale for evaluating diversity was that culturing methods are often restricted to responsive genotypes (Phillips and Garda, 2019). Here, we tested a variety of seeds collected from a range of plants in the same salt marsh. Each tested seedling displayed a unique RAPD profile, confirming genetic heterogeneity consistent with seed‐derived (non‐clonal) material.

Removal of the radicle system

Each seedling was carefully removed from the soil using sterile forceps, taking care not to damage the crown area. To remove adhering soil particles, seedlings were placed in 50‐mL Falcon tubes containing autoclaved, room‐temperature water and gently agitated. After rinsing, the cleaned seedlings were transferred to a new sheet of wax paper, where any remaining outer caryopsis and endosperm tissue were carefully removed using autoclaved fine tweezers. A sterile surgical blade was then used to excise any root tissue below the crown area.

The removal of tissue below the crown is critical for the successful in vitro introduction of Sporobulus species as the presence of the primary root, and particularly any residual seed‐derived tissue such as the endosperm, consistently led to fungal contamination. To minimize contamination risk, we recommend excising the radicle approximately 0.5 cm below the crown (Figure 1). Some seedlings may display secondary roots around the crown area at this stage. These secondary roots can be retained, as we observed no increase in contamination or reduction in the success of in vitro establishment when they are left intact.

Surface sterilization of trimmed seedlings

The trimmed seedlings were now ready for surface‐sterilization prior to in vitro introduction. In a laminar flow hood, the trimmed seedlings were transferred to a clean, sterile Falcon tube containing 40 mL of a 10% (v/v) bleach solution and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. After sterilization, the seedlings were rinsed three times with autoclaved distilled water, with each rinse lasting 5 min, to remove any residual bleach.

In vitro introduction of trimmed seedlings

Working within a laminar flow hood, surface‐sterilized, trimmed seedlings were placed in Petri dishes containing 25 mL of MS medium (Murashige and Skoog, 1962), either unsupplemented or supplemented with plant growth regulators. All media combinations contained 30 g/L sucrose as a carbon source, had pH adjusted to 5.8, were solidified with 8 g/L of agar, and were autoclaved for 40 min prior to Petri dish preparation under sterile conditions.

Each surface‐sterilized trimmed seedling was positioned with the crown in direct contact with the agar surface. Plates were sealed with parafilm and maintained under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of approximately 100 µmol m− 2 s− 1. We were able to regenerate the radicle system in vitro. Emergence of adventitious roots was typically observed within 2–3 days of culture (Figure 1); these newly formed roots were thin, fleshy, light‐colored (nearly white), and lacked root hairs. By day 7, most trimmed seedlings had developed at least two new roots, ranging from 0.5 to over 1 cm in length. Simultaneously, seedlings produced new leaves. By day 10, most seedlings had formed at least two additional new leaves, while the cotyledon exhibited browning. Overall, we achieved root induction rates of up to 75% (Table 1).

Effect of culture media on root system development in Sporobolus

We tested four different media formulations (Table 1). First, we used a commercially available version of MS basal media supplemented with vitamins (reference M519; Phytotech Labs, Lenexa, Kansas, USA). To assess the effects of auxins in root induction, we supplemented MS medium with either indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA, 1 mg/L) or 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4‐D, 1 mg/L). The fourth media combination that we tested, hereafter referred to as the root induction medium, is a commercially available modified version of MS medium supplemented with vitamins as described in Linsmaier and Skoog (1965), with ferric sodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a replacement for ferrous sulfate and disodium EDTA (reference M527, Phytotech Labs), and a combination of plant growth regulators (1.0 mg/L kinetin and 0.3 mg/L IAA) that is traditionally used to induce cell division and tissue regeneration. All four media formulations were supplemented with 30 g/L of sucrose as a carbon source, had pH adjusted to 5.8, and were solidified with 8 g/L of agar.

We observed differences in the regeneration of radicle systems among these treatments, indicating that root development during the in vitro introduction of trimmed Sporobolus seedlings was heavily influenced by the composition of the culture medium (Table 1). Adventitious root production was markedly higher in the root induction medium (75% success) compared to the basal MS medium (18%) or MS medium supplemented with IAA (16%) or 2,4‐D (28%) (Table 1). In addition to lower root induction rates, we also observed a higher incidence of plantlets with altered root morphology. The roots regenerated in MS media were shorter and thicker compared to those induced in the root induction medium, and this altered morphology persisted weeks after transferring the seedlings to the plant‐growing medium (Figure 2).

Transfer of re‐rooted seedlings to plant‐growing media and establishment of the micropropagation chain

After 10 days on the Petri dishes with the root induction medium, the re‐rooted seedlings were transferred to plant multiplication vessels for the establishment of the micropropagation chain. For routine culture of the vitroplants, we used glass wide‐mouth 1‐L culture jars (reference C607, Phytotech Labs) containing ~150 mL of autoclaved root induction medium prepared as described above. Transfers were performed under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood. Each re‐rooted seedling was carefully removed from the Petri dish using sterilized forceps and placed upright in the center of a culture jar, ensuring that the roots were fully embedded in the medium without damage.

Vitroplants were maintained at 25°C under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of approximately 200 µmol m− 2 s− 1. Within days of transfer, the vitroplants produced new roots, including fine, fibrous structures, and initiated new leaf growth (Figure 2). New lateral shoots emerged from the basal meristems of the primary stem and developed their own root systems, enabling micropropagation. These lateral shoots were carefully separated from the main vitroplant using autoclaved forceps and transferred to fresh medium, where they continued to grow as independent vitroplants. In addition to the lateral shoots, vitroplants occasionally formed rhizome‐like structures that were also effective propagules for micropropagation. Subculturing was typically required every 6–8 weeks. Once the micropropagation chain was established, we observed no decline in growth or rooting when using plant growth regulator or sucrose‐free MS medium. Vitroplants subcultured into MS medium with 0.8% agar, pH adjusted to 5.8, and with or without added sucrose exhibited sustained and vigorous growth.

Transferability of this approach to other salt marsh grasses

The protocol developed for S. alterniflorus was also applied to seeds of S. cynosuroides (big cordgrass). This species was chosen because, as with S. alterniflorus or the hybrid S. ×townsendii (H. Groves & J. Groves) P.M. Peterson & Saarela and its derived allopolyploid S. anglicus (C.E. Hubb.) P.M. Peterson & Saarela (Raybould et al., 1998; Fisher et al., 2007), seeds of S. cynosuroides are frequently infected by the fungal pathogen Claviceps purpurea (Eleuterius and Meyers, 1974). Moreover, as was the case for S. alterniflorus, pre‐existing culturing methods for S. cynosuroides were limited, primarily relying on callus‐derived materials (Li and Gallagher, 1996).

Surface‐sterilized seeds of S. cynosuroides germinated two weeks after being transferred into the germination boxes with water‐saturated, autoclaved soil. Seven‐to‐10‐day‐old seedlings were collected, rinsed, and trimmed to remove the root system and residual endosperm, following the same protocol established for S. alterniflorus. Trimmed seedlings were then transferred to Petri dishes containing either hormone‐free MS medium or the root induction medium (modified MS medium with 1.0 mg/L kinetin and 0.3 mg/L IAA). After seven days in culture, approximately 80% of the trimmed seedlings remained axenic and had developed 3–4 adventitious roots. These re‐rooted seedlings were subsequently transferred to glass culture jars containing 150 mL of fresh media. By eight weeks, the vitroplants produced new lateral shoots suitable for establishing a micropropagation chain. No signs of bacterial or fungal contamination were detected.

To assess the effects of growth regulators on shoot and root development, we performed independent two‐sample t‐tests using the statistical software PAST (version 4.13) (Hammer, 2023). At eight weeks, root induction rates and morphology were comparable across the two media types (Table 1), with no significant differences in the number of roots produced per plant (5.5 ± 0.7 vs. 6.2 ± 1.0; n = 8; t‐test for equal means: t = 1.65, df = 14, P = 0.12). This robust rooting response across different media types in S. cynosuroides contrasts with the media‐specific root induction observed in S. alterniflorus. However, the root elongation differed significantly between treatments, with plants grown on regulator‐free media developing significantly longer roots than those cultured with growth regulators (57.1 ± 13.2 mm vs. 28.2 ± 7.0 mm; n = 8; t = 5.45, df = 14, P = 0.00004). This indicates that our method is transferable to other Sporobulus grasses and could facilitate research in Sporobulus sensu lato. However, small adjustments in media composition may be required to fully optimize the protocol for different species.

Assessment of axenicity in vitroplants

In vitro plants are grown in nutrient‐rich media and are therefore highly susceptible to microbial contamination, which can significantly hinder their growth and viability. Such contamination often arises from bacteria and fungi that proliferate either within the plant tissue or in the growth medium, impeding the development of the explants. Microbial contaminants may produce toxins or degrade essential nutrients, leading to symptoms such as browning, reduced root and shoot growth, and, in some cases, tissue necrosis culminating in plant death (Cuba‐Díaz et al., 2020). Therefore, eliminating exogenous and endogenous contaminants ensures the long‐term success of the cultures.

During the root induction stage, we observed that 16–20% of surface‐sterilized, trimmed seedlings developed signs of contamination (Table 1). This was primarily fungal in nature and consistent with the contamination observed when untrimmed seedlings were germinated in vitro. In most cases, the contamination likely resulted from incomplete removal of the endosperm during the trimming step. Despite this, the results represent a substantial improvement over the 100% fungal contamination rate observed when seeds were surface sterilized and germinated directly on plant media without radicle removal.

In later stages of micropropagation, we identified a small proportion (~5%) of S. alterniflorus vitroplants germinated from different seeds displaying bacterial contamination. Intriguingly, the contaminated samples displayed the same bright yellow colonies around the roots but none of the visible symptoms traditionally linked to the presence of microorganisms, such as tissue discoloration or altered growth patterns (Figure 3B). Notably, this contamination was absent in S. cynosuroides. The bacterium was isolated from contaminated tissues on solid lysogeny broth (LB) media and identified as closely related to Mycobacterium sp. 60 ENV421. Mycobacteria have previously been reported as beneficial to plant health (Golubev et al., 2021), particularly under challenging environmental conditions such as salinity. Functional annotation of the genome revealed multiple genes involved in the degradation of polyaromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) (Peredo et al., 2025).

To evaluate axenicity beyond visual inspection of vitroplants and to detect possible cases of latent contamination that is not immediately evident (Izarra et al., 2020), we developed both culture‐dependent and PCR‐based detection protocols. We tested 25 vitroplants, each representing a different clonal line, selected randomly from the 76 genotypes introduced in vitro using the rooting media (Table 1). As positive controls for contamination, we included two seedlings germinated in soil prior to surface sterilization, as previous germination assays indicated these are carriers of fungal and bacterial microorganisms commonly found in the plant microbiome. Among the 25 vitroplants selected for axenicity testing, we included tissue from a vitroplant with visible bacterial contamination as positive control and to test for tissue‐specific infection in vitro. Tissue samples including entire seedlings, ~3‐cm leaf segments, and ~3‐cm root sections were collected under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood to eliminate external contamination and then placed in sterile Eppendorf tubes. Samples for molecular analysis were stored at –20°C, while those for culture‐based microbial detection were processed immediately.

Culture‐based microbial detection approaches

Each tissue sample was homogenized in 500 µL of autoclaved distilled water using a sterile micropestle. One hundred microliters of the homogenate was plated onto two different culture media: LB medium with 1% agar (pH 7) and MS medium supplemented with 30 g/L sucrose with 0.8% agar (pH 5.8). All plates were incubated under the same environmental conditions as the rooting experiments, and each assay was performed in duplicate.

Extensive microbial growth, both fungal and bacterial, was observed on plates inoculated with homogenates from the two soil‐grown seedlings within two days of inoculation (Figure 3A). In contrast, no growth was observed on plates inoculated with axenic vitroplant tissues (n = 25, roots and leaves), even two weeks post‐inoculation. The only exception was the plates inoculated with the homogenates from a vitroplant showing visible bacterial contamination (Vitroplant #4). This plant had been selected as a positive control within the experimental samples (Figure 3B, C). Although visual inspection of the vitroplant suggested that contamination was restricted to the roots, both root and leaf tissues from this vitroplant yielded bacterial colonies on LB medium, indicating a systemic infection.

PCR‐based approaches

Claviceps purpurea, like many fungal contaminants, grows readily on sucrose‐rich media adjusted to an acidic pH (~5), making it an ideal candidate for detection via culture‐based methods. However, this is not the case for multiple bacterial contaminants, which limits the effectivity of culture‐based approaches to the small fraction of plant‐associated microorganisms that are cultivable. To expand detection power, we implemented a culture‐independent, PCR‐based detection approach using universal primers that are commonly employed in microbial barcoding studies. However, PCR‐based detection in plant tissues is often complicated by co‐amplification of host organellar DNA. To address this, we tested two primer pairs targeting the 16S rRNA gene (Table 2): the universal set 27F/1492R (Frank et al., 2008), which amplifies a ~1450‐bp fragment, and 799F/1192R (Ma et al., 2022), which amplifies a ~414‐bp fragment and is specifically designed to reduce chloroplast DNA amplification (Ma et al., 2022).

Primers used in the PCR‐based detection of microbial contamination.

DNA was extracted from leaves and roots of 25 vitroplants (including the contaminated Vitroplant #4) using a Maxwell RSC PureFood GMO and Authentication Kit (Promega). We used bacterial DNA (one sample), dilutions of bacterial DNA (1:10), and mixtures of plant and bacterial DNA as technical controls. We also extracted DNA of seedlings germinated in soil, as bacterial presence was expected on those samples. DNA purity and concentration were quantified using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Qubit 2.0 dsDNA Quantification Broad Range (BR) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCR reactions included 15 ng of DNA and were run in duplicate using Platinum SuperFi DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's recommendations. The protocol consisted of 10 cycles of touchdown PCR with an annealing temperature starting at 60°C, decreasing by 0.5°C per cycle, followed by 25 cycles with an annealing temperature of 55°C. Amplified fragments were run on 1.5% agarose gels for 2 h at 100 V.

The 27F/1492R primer pair produced a ~1.5‐kbp band across all samples. Sanger sequencing of a subset of samples revealed strong co‐amplification of the host chloroplast 16S rRNA gene, rendering this primer set unsuitable for detecting bacterial contaminants in plant tissue. The 799F/1192R primer pair also exhibited some cross‐amplification with host DNA; however, the bacterial and host amplicons differed in size, enabling rapid visual identification of bacterial presence on a standard agarose gel. Positive controls using pure bacterial cultures produced a single ~420‐bp band, corresponding to the expected 16S rRNA fragment in prokaryotes (Figure 4A). Axenic vitroplants yielded a single ~800‐bp fragment, which Sanger sequencing identified as a product of the off‐target amplification of a region on the plant mitochondrial genome (Figure 4A). The mixture of plant and bacterial DNA, as well as three non‐axenic controls including two soil‐grown seedlings and one vitroplant with visible contamination (Vitroplant #4), simultaneously produced two distinct bands: the expected ~420‐bp bacterial band and the ~800‐bp mitochondrial fragment (Figure 4).

Axenic vitroplants produced clean, single‐peak electropherograms.

The mitochondrial host co‐amplification served as an internal control, suitable for confirming successful PCR amplification and allowing us to rule out PCR failure in samples lacking the ~420‐bp bacterial band. As a result, we were able to discontinue the additional quality control of the plant DNA for PCR amplification with matK primers, using standard amplification protocols described elsewhere (Muhammad and Ki, 2022). The PCR‐based method outperformed culture‐dependent assays in bacterial detection, revealing bacterial contamination in the root tissue of one vitroplant (Vitroplant #22) that appeared healthy and had tested negative in the culture‐based assay.

CONCLUSIONS

The establishment of axenic in vitro cultures of salt marsh grasses has traditionally required complex, multistep protocols to eliminate the microorganisms present in the different plant tissues. Here, we present a simple, efficient, and transferable protocol for initiating in vitro axenic cultures of S. alterniflorus and S. cynosuroides in as little as four weeks, with micropropagation chains established within approximately three months and achieving an average success rate of 75%. Our approach focuses on removing sources of endophytic bacteria and inducing adventitious root formation from seedlings. This effective and scalable protocol enables reliable in vitro multiplication of keystone salt marsh grasses, offering immediate value for ex situ multiplication, research, and biotechnological applications.
