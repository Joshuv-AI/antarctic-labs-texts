Development and validation of prediction model for intrapulmonary metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma based on machine learning
Authors: Zhenglong Wang, Xiaobo Liu, Mingyue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a leading cause of cancer-related death, with intrapulmonary metastasis (IPM) delaying diagnosis and worsening prognosis. Despite machine learning (ML)’s promise in metastasis prediction, specific models for LUAD-IPM are lacking. The present study aimed to construct a ML algorithm to accurately predict the risk of IPM in patients with LUAD.MethodsWe analyzed 61,519 LUAD patients diagnosed between 2018 and 2022 from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, including 7,696 with IPM. Thirteen clinicopathological and demographic variables were assessed, covering demographics (age, sex, marital status, race), tumor features [size, primary site, tumor stage (T stage), node stage (N stage), bone/brain/liver/lung metastasis], and treatments (radiotherapy, chemotherapy). After excluding patients with missing key data, univariate and multivariate logistic regression (LR) identified independent prognostic factors (P<0.05). The Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) addressed class imbalance, and the balanced dataset was split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. Twelve ML models were constructed, with 10-fold cross-validation, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) for validation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis quantified feature contributions and enhanced model interpretability.ResultsT stage, N stage, bone/brain/liver metastasis, radiation, and chemotherapy were independent prognostic factors. The light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) model outperformed others, achieving a testing cohort area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.818, sensitivity 0.782, specificity 0.717, and F1-score 0.421. SHAP analysis confirmed T stage, radiation, and N stage as the top 3 influential features shaping predictions.ConclusionsThis is the first ML model specifically predicting LUAD-IPM. The LGBM model enables accurate risk stratification, supporting personalized surveillance and treatment optimization to improve clinical outcomes by identifying high-risk patients and avoiding unnecessary interventions.

Introduction

Lung cancer, particularly the lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). According to 2022 statistics, the disease accounted for approximately 2.5 million new cases and as many as 1.8 million deaths globally (2). Population aging, accelerating industrialization, and tobacco exposure constitute the core drivers of the rising incidence of LUAD; furthermore, a growing body of emerging evidence in recent years has further demonstrated that air pollution and occupational exposure also markedly elevate the risk of developing this disease (3). Notably, this subtype exhibits a significantly higher incidence in never-smokers than in smokers, and also has a greater prevalence in females than in males (4). Prior studies have confirmed that lung cancer in never-smokers ranks as the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide (5); in pathological classification studies of never-smoking lung cancer patients, adenocarcinoma is the most common histotype, followed by squamous cell carcinoma (6). LUAD represents one of the most predominant pathological types of lung cancer, accounting for 50–70% of surgically resected lung cancer cases, thereby solidifying its status as the most common subtype of NSCLC (7). Its prognosis is tightly linked to the timing of diagnosis: the 5-year survival rate of early-stage patients stands at approximately 50%, whereas this figure plummets to a mere 20% for those with advanced disease (8,9). Owing to the lack of typical early clinical manifestations, LUAD is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage (10). The root cause of this grim scenario is delayed diagnosis: most patients already present with distant metastases at initial diagnosis, with common metastatic sites including the bones and brain, often accompanied by occult intrapulmonary metastasis (IPM) (11). IPM is generally regarded as regional metastasis when confined to the ipsilateral lung, and is classified as distant metastasis if lesions involve the contralateral lung.

In recent years, the application of machine learning (ML) technology in oncology has demonstrated tremendous potential, with its use cases spanning medical image analysis, treatment regimen formulation, prediction of patient survival outcomes, and point-of-care drug synthesis (12,13). By leveraging large-scale clinical, imaging, and therapeutic data, ML algorithms are capable of capturing complex non-linear correlations within datasets and identifying subtle patterns that are inaccessible to traditional statistical methods (14). Currently, a growing body of relevant research has emerged in academia, which is dedicated to constructing ML models for the accurate prediction of distant cancer metastasis. From the perspective of technical principles, the core advantage of ML lies in the fact that computers can autonomously learn regularities from data without being explicitly programmed with instructions, and further generate predictive analyses or assist in decision-making based on the learned patterns (15). Furthermore, such algorithms possess the capacity to efficiently process high-dimensional and heterogeneous data, integrate various clinical indicators including age, sex, and tumor stage, and extract valid features therefrom, thereby markedly improving predictive accuracy (16).

To date, numerous studies have adopted ML models to predict distant metastases of lung cancer, including common metastatic subtypes such as brain, bone and liver metastases. However, research focusing specifically on predictive models for LUAD-IPM remains scarce. Accordingly, the present study aimed to develop a ML algorithm for the accurate prediction of intrapulmonary metastatic risk in patients with LUAD. Integrating such ML tools into clinical workflows enables dynamic, data-driven risk assessment and facilitates the formulation of individualized treatment strategies, thereby markedly improving the precision and efficiency of clinical management for advanced LUAD (17). We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0649/rc).

Methods

Data selection

Figure S1 outlines the workflow for our study design and analysis. For this investigation, we drew on data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (SEER 17 Regs Research Data, 2000–2022; version 9.0.42). We included patients diagnosed with LUAD between 2018 and 2022, with the following exclusion criteria applied: (I) individuals age ≤20 and ≥80 years, due to the high likelihood of incomplete staging workup and missing data granularity in this population; (II) those with missing race or histological grade data; (III) patients for whom the primary tumor site was unknown; (IV) cases with incomplete tumor staging (T staging) and node staging (N staging) information; and (V) patients with undetermined metastatic status in the bone, brain, liver, or lung. Marital status was collected and included as a covariate, reflecting its established role as a surrogate for social support influencing healthcare access and detection rates.

Data collection

This study evaluated 13 clinicopathological and demographic variables. These included demographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, and race), tumor features (primary tumor size, primary site, T stage, N stage, and the presence of bone, brain, and liver metastases), as well as treatment modalities (radiotherapy and chemotherapy). All patients with LUAD were staged according to the SEER staging system. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Statistical analysis

We first identified significant variables among patients with LUAD using univariate logistic regression (LR) analysis, with statistical significance set at P<0.05. Variables yielding statistical significance were subsequently incorporated into a multivariate LR analysis. Those remaining significant in the multivariate model (P<0.05) were selected for further evaluation using ML models. Correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between the chosen features. Given the class imbalance arising from the low incidence of IPM (12.5%), the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to balance the dataset, with resampling of the minority class to achieve a 1:1 distribution ratio (original distribution: 87.5% non-metastatic vs. 12.5% metastatic). The final balanced dataset was randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets: the training subset was used for model fitting, while the testing subset served for unbiased evaluation of predictive performance. To ensure the robustness of our findings, model performance metrics including the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), precision, sensitivity, and F1-score were calculated separately for each subset and are reported in the “Results” section.

ML models

Variable selection was guided by the results of univariate LR analysis. Patients were randomly divided into two groups: 70% were assigned to the training set, and the remaining 30% to the testing set. Using these selected predictors, we developed 12 ML models: extreme gradient boosting (XGB), random forest (RF), multi-layer perceptron (MLP), support vector machine (SVM), LR, naive Bayes (NB), decision tree (DT), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), adaptive boosting (AB), gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and categorical boosting (CB). Each model offers distinct strengths: LR models the probability of a binary outcome by means of a logistic function. It provides high interpretability and works well for linear relationships (18). DT is a structured tree-based prediction model that selects optimal feature variables and determines optimal split nodes, and it is well suited to capturing non-linear correlations and interaction effects (19). RF adopts DT as the base classifier to recursively construct multiple trees, with the final prediction determined by a voting mechanism (20). Moreover, it achieves optimal performance when processing high-dimensional data. LGBM is a gradient boosting framework using tree-based learning algorithms. It features high efficiency and accuracy, with the capability to handle large datasets and support various features (21). XGB is an optimized iterative implementation of the gradient boosting framework, featuring parallel computing capability and automatic missing value processing (22). GBDT is an ensemble learning method that adopts tree-based DT and gradient boosting techniques, and iteratively refines predictive results by introducing new models to rectify the errors of prior models (23). AB is an ensemble learning-based Boosting algorithm that combines multiple weak learners together, yielding a high-performance supervised model with superior accuracy and excellent generalization capability (24). The core principle of the NB model is based on Bayes’ theorem, and ultimately selecting the category with the highest probability as the prediction result (25). SVM is a supervised learning model that analyzes data and identifies patterns for classification or regression tasks. Its working principle is to find an optimal hyperplane that maximizes the margin between two classes (26). KNN classifies instances by measuring proximity in the feature space and relies on majority voting among the nearest neighbors to generate predictions (27). MLP is a neural network-based algorithm that captures complex non-linear relationships through interconnected layers and optimizes weights via backpropagation (28). CB is a tree-based gradient boosting algorithm, which is optimized to handle categorical features and reduce biases associated with feature importance estimation (29). This implementation adopts a default log loss function dedicated to binary classification tasks. To identify the optimal diagnostic model, we evaluated model performance using metrics including the AUC, specificity, sensitivity, F1-score, and accuracy. For the top-performing model, we further implemented 10-fold cross-validation to reaffirm its generalization ability and robustness, helping to mitigate overfitting. Finally, calibration curves, precision-recall (PR) curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were employed to assess the model’s calibration and clinical utility.

Model interpretability

Interpreting ML models in an intuitive, clinically meaningful way is critical to their practical utility. To improve model interpretability, we computed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to quantify how each feature contributes to model predictions. We generated a SHAP summary plot to rank feature importance and visualize their overall impact across all samples. Heatmap scatter plots were used to explore interactions between individual features and their corresponding SHAP values, offering deeper insights into how specific variables shape the model’s decision-making process. Model reliability was evaluated by comparing observed trends in the target variable with predicted trends across different feature variables.

Results

Analysis of information on patients and feature importance

A total of 61,519 cases with LUAD were available, including 53,823 (87.49%) cases without IPM and 7,696 (12.51%) cases with IPM, age, size, sex, married status, primary site, T stage, N stage, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis variables were significantly different between the two groups (all P<0.05) Detailed information is summarized in Table 1. The median age was 68 years [interquartile range (IQR), 62–74 years]. The median size of the IPM group was 48 mm (IQR, 30–74 mm), significantly larger than that of the non-IPM group (28 mm; IQR, 18–47 mm) (P<0.001). The majority of patients were female (53.87%). There were significant racial differences, with the majority of patients being White (76.23%). The proportion of white patients in the IPM group (71.24%) was significantly lower than that in the non-IPM group (76.94%), but the proportion of Asian or Pacific Islander patients in the IPM group (15.54%) was significantly higher than that in the non-IPM group (10.37%) (P<0.001). Marital status was similar among the groups, with a higher proportion of married individuals in the IPM group (54.05%, P=0.049). There was almost no difference in tumor location between the groups, with the upper lobe of the lung being the most common in both the IPM group and the non-IPM group (P=0.002). Additionally, the proportion of advanced T stage tumors (T3/T4) in the IPM group was higher than that in the non-IPM group (P<0.001). Similarly, the proportion of regional lymph node metastasis (N3 stage) in the IPM group (35.11%) was more common than that in the non-IPM group (11.72%, P<0.001). Bone metastasis, brain metastasis, and liver metastasis were all more common in the IPM group than in the non-IPM group (P<0.001). In terms of treatment, the frequency of radiotherapy use in the IPM group (40.29%) was lower than that in the non-IPM group (44.11%, P<0.001). However, the chemotherapy rate in the IPM group (61.40%) was much higher than that in the non-IPM group (42.58%, P<0.001). The higher rate of chemotherapy use in the IPM group is more likely to reflect more advanced disease stage.

Spearman’s correlation and feature importance

We assessed correlations among the 11 features using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, with results visualized as a correlation heatmap (Figure 1). This analysis revealed no strong correlations between the 11 features, indicating good independence across these variables and thus supporting subsequent modeling and related analyses.

Spearman correlation heatmap of the correlation of features. N, node; T, tumor.

To facilitate feature interpretation and explore potential clinical associations, we additionally conducted univariate and multivariate LR analyses (Table 3). Univariate analysis revealed significant differences in the risk of lung metastasis across several variables, including age, tumor size, sex, race, T stage, N stage, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy (P<0.05). Subsequent multivariate LR confirmed T stage, N stage, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy as independent prognostic factors for lung metastasis. Variables identified through these analyses contributed significantly to outcome prediction across all 12 models. Notably, T stage emerged as the most influential feature across a majority of models (Figure 2).

Evaluation and internal validation of model performance

We developed and evaluated 12 ML models in this study. In internal validation using the training dataset, the AUC values of AB, CB, GBDT, LGBM, and XGB ranked among the top (Figure 3, Table 4). The research team used the training dataset to train all models and optimize their parameters, with final model performance validation and selection guided by evaluation metrics from the independent validation cohort. As consistent evidence from the detailed performance metrics in Table 5 demonstrates, LGBM model exhibited good robustness and generalizability in the testing cohort. Based on these combined validation findings, the LGBM model was identified as the top-performing model in this study. To further assess the robustness and generalizability of the LGBM models, we performed 10 rounds of bootstrap cross-validation to replicate the evaluation of their predictive performance (Figure 4).

Discussion

Lung cancer is one of the malignant tumors with the highest morbidity and mortality rates worldwide (2). Distant metastasis is the primary cause of mortality in the majority of cancer patients (30). Previous studies have demonstrated that approximately 40% of patients with newly diagnosed lung cancer exhibit metastatic lesions at the time of initial diagnosis (31). Lung cancer frequently metastasizes to the liver, bone, brain, and lung parenchyma (32). Currently, data regarding the metastatic patterns and survival outcomes of distant metastasis in lung cancer are predominantly derived from retrospective studies. Several small-sample retrospective analyses have indicated that lung cancer patients with bone or brain metastasis may achieve improved survival benefits following surgical intervention (33-35). However, the clinical benefits of surgical treatment for lung cancer patients with liver metastasis remain controversial, and relevant research on IPM of lung cancer is also scarce. A retrospective study demonstrated that patients with lung cancer who had IPM in the same lobe as the primary tumor obtained better clinical benefits from surgical treatment, in comparison with those with metastasis in distinct lobes of the ipsilateral lung (36).

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to construct a ML model targeting LUAD-IPM, which fills the gap in the development of specific algorithms for this field. The LGBM model exhibited excellent classification performance in the stratified identification of patients with high and low risks of IPM. Although the ROC curves of multiple models overlapped, indicating consistency in their discriminatory efficacy, the LGBM model achieved an optimal balance of performance in the independent validation cohort, with an ideal sensitivity (0.784) and specificity (0.738), together with an acceptable F1-score (0.421). These results confirmed that the LGBM model had outstanding discriminatory efficacy and calibration accuracy. The above findings fully demonstrate that the LGBM model enables accurate risk stratification assessment of IPM in patients with LUAD, thereby providing an objective and reliable decision-making basis for clinical risk prediction. This risk stratification model can accurately identify high-risk populations who would benefit from intensified follow-up and surveillance, while avoiding unnecessary over-clinical intervention in low-risk patients, which holds important clinical value for optimizing the individualized diagnosis and treatment strategies of LUAD.

Accurate tumor staging is an important prerequisite for the implementation of effective treatment for LUAD (37,38). Among the existing cancer staging systems, the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification system is widely recognized as the optimal method for evaluating the prognosis of patients with NSCLC (39). The results of this study showed that the higher the T or N stage, the significantly increased risk of IPM in patients, which is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies (40,41). There is inherent overlap between T stage and IPM in the 8th TNM staging system, so the strong association between advanced T stage and IPM is an expected result by definition. LGBM model is not intended to replace TNM staging, but to provide dynamic, quantitative IPM risk assessment before pathological confirmation. By integrating imaging-based T stage, N stage, and other metastatic sites status, the model combines local staging with systemic tumor burden to identify high-risk patients, which aligns with the updated concept proposed by Rami-Porta et al. and delivers independent clinical value beyond conventional staging tables (42). In addition, radiotherapy was associated with a lower risk of IPM. Radiation induces extensive DNA damage by depositing energy into genetic material, creating an insurmountable barrier to tumor cell adaptability, and ultimately leads to cell death or cell cycle arrest (43).

ML models provide valuable insights into the complex interrelationships among independent prognostic factors. Although Univariate and multiple LR identified several risk factors, the SHAP values of some of these variables in the feature importance ranking were negligible. This discrepancy highlights the superiority of ML, as it can eliminate irrelevant features and thereby reduce the risk of overfitting (44). Such advantages make ML more robust than traditional regression models in prognostic prediction, as it avoids the interference of redundant variables and improves the stability of predictive performance.

There are several limitations in this study. First, detailed clinical information regarding tumor subtypes and distant metastasis sites in the SEER database was not available until 2010, which may compromise the extrapolability and generalizability of the study results. Second, the database does not track disease recurrence or newly developed metastasis sites, which largely limits our ability to assess the long-term risk of LUAD-IPM. Third, due to the absence of records on specific treatment regimens and drug dosages for chemotherapy patients in the database, this study cannot further explore the potential effects of different chemotherapeutic drugs on LUAD-IPM. Fourth, the variables in the database are dominated by clinical indicators, lacking in-depth features such as molecular biology and gene expression profiles, which to a certain extent restricts the further improvement of the model’s predictive performance. Fifth, important exposure factors closely associated with the onset and progression of LUAD (e.g., dust exposure, tobacco exposure, and alcohol use) are missing from the SEER database, resulting in the inability to incorporate these key variables into the model for analysis. Simply regarding T stage and N stage as core predictive factors tends to result in circular reasoning and fails to reflect independent prognostic value. However, the core significance of our model lies in that it enables individualized and prospective assessment of IPM risk before definitive diagnosis without relying on postoperative pathological staging. Although the incidence of LUAD-IPM is relatively low, its significant impact on patient prognosis and close correlation with high morbidity fully demonstrate the necessity and clinical value of developing risk stratification tools. It should be emphasized that the model developed in this study is not intended to replace the professional judgment of clinicians, but to serve as an auxiliary decision-support tool to provide references for clinical practice.

Conclusions

This is the first ML model specifically developed to predict IPM in LUAD, filling a critical gap in clinical practice. The LGBM model demonstrates robust discriminatory ability and good calibration, enabling accurate risk stratification of patients. By identifying high-risk individuals who may benefit from intensified follow-up, imaging surveillance, or personalized treatment adjustments, the model helps optimize clinical decision-making, reduce unnecessary medical interventions for low-risk patients, and improve resource allocation. Notably, the prominence of T/N stages and radiation in predictions underscores the clinical value of precise TNM staging and comprehensive metastatic evaluation. This tool complements clinicians’ professional judgment, providing an objective data-driven reference to enhance early intervention, potentially improve patient survival outcomes, and lay the foundation for future prospective validation and integration into clinical workflows.
