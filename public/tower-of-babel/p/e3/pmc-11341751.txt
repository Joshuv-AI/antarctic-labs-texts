Low nailfold capillary density in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension: biomarker of clinical outcome?
Authors: A J Smits, K Isebia, C Combee-Duffy, S van der Wal, E J Nossent, A Boonstra, A Vonk-Noordegraaf, H J Bogaard, E H Serné
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNailfold capillary density is lower in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). It is unclear whether this observation signifies a unique systemic manifestation of PAH, or reflects microcirculatory dysfunction secondary to pulmonary hypertension (PH). Capillary density and loop dimensions were measured by nailfold-capillaroscopy (NC) in 30 PAH (23 idiopathic, or iPAH, 7 hereditary, or hPAH), 17 chronic thromboembolic PH (CTEPH) patients and 48 controls. NC-Measurements were repeated after pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) or balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) in CTEPH patients. We examined whether NC-measurements were related to markers of disease severity and predictive of time to clinical worsening (TTCW) as tested by univariate linear/logistic regression and cox-regression analysis, respectively. Capillary density was significantly lower in PAH (7.5 ± 1.1, p < 0.001) and in CTEPH (8.4 ± 1.5, p < 0.001) compared to asymptomatic controls (10.3 ± 1.0 capillaries/mm). Capillary density was similar in iPAH and hPAH and unrelated to hemodynamics in either PAH or CTEPH. A lower capillary density was predictive of clinical worsening in PAH (p 0.05). After normalization of pulmonary artery pressures by PEA or BPA, capillary density remained reduced in CTEPH patients. Capillary loop apex, capillary and venous- and arterial limb diameter were increased in patients with PAH and CTEPH compared to controls. Nailfold capillary density is reduced to a similar extent in iPAH, hPAH and CTEPH. Normalization of hemodynamics by PEA or BPA does not lead to a restoration of capillary density in CTEPH. Capillary dimensions were increased in both patients with PAH and CTEPH. Lower capillary density was predictive of clinical worsening in PAH. Our findings indicate that a loss of peripheral capillaries is not specific to PAH and is not related to the hemodynamic disturbance per se, but that shared mechanisms may account for a simultaneous development of a systemic microangiopathy and pulmonary vascular remodeling.Keywords: Pulmonary arterial hypertension, Pulmonary hypertension, Chronic thrombo-embolic pulmonary hypertension, Nailfold-capillaroscopy, Microvascular, DiagnosisSubject terms: Cardiovascular biology, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare heterogeneous pulmonary vasculopathy defined hemodynamically by an increased mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg, normal pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) ≥ 160 dyn·s·cm−5 (≥ 2 Wood units), which ultimately leads to right ventricular failure and early death1. PAH can complicate connective tissue disease (CTD) and congenital heart disease, or occur in the context of genetic mutations causing heritable PAH (hPAH), or is considered as idiopathic (iPAH) in the absence of an identified predisposing factor1. The key pathophysiological feature of PAH is remodeling of the pulmonary precapillary vessels, which involves uncontrolled growth of endothelial and smooth-muscle cells and fibroblasts, leading to complete obliteration of small vessels1,2.

Although PAH is seen as a disease restricted to the pulmonary circulation, there is growing evidence of a systemic microangiopathy3–5. Part of this evidence is based on studies using nailfold capillaroscopy (NC). Nailfold-capillaroscopy is a non-invasive tool that enables morphological evaluation of the peripheral microcirculation (‘NC pattern analysis’) and measurement of capillary density6. Its main application is detecting structural microvascular alterations in the preclinical stages of systemic sclerosis (SSc)6,7. In addition, small longitudinal studies suggest that progression to an ‘active’ or ‘late’ NC pattern may represent a warning signal mirroring complications of CTD, such as PAH8. Among patients with SSc, those with PAH have increased capillary width and reduced capillary density8,9. Interestingly, iPAH patients, when compared with healthy controls, show a similarly reduced capillary density4,5,10, albeit to a lesser extent as observed in SSc-PAH. Capillary density was correlated with mPAP in iPAH patients4. This might indicate that vascular disease in the pulmonary circulation is mirrored by NC-detected microvascular alterations in iPAH. However, whether NC may be of prognostic value is presently unknown. Data on NC-detected microvascular alterations in hPAH are currently lacking.

To date, there is no single unifying theory about the pathophysiological background of the systemic microangiopathy in PAH. Mechanisms that may simultaneously affect the microcirculation in the lung and at the nailfold may involve inappropriate angiogenesis, inflammation and neurohumoral activation3,5, whether or not mediated or accelerated by the hemodynamic changes in PAH. Limited data demonstrate other forms of precapillary PH, i.e. CTEPH5,11 and PAH secondary to congenital heart disease11, to be associated with peripheral microvascular alterations comparable to that demonstrated in iPAH. CTEPH is a distinct pulmonary vascular disease caused by chronic obstruction of major pulmonary arteries that is amenable to cure by pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) or balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA)12. It is presently unknown whether NC-detected microvascular alterations resolve after PEA/BPA.

The aim of the present study was to: (a) investigate the presence of systemic microangiopathy as assessed by NC in patients with iPAH, hPAH and CTEPH compared to healthy controls; (b) investigate whether capillary density reflects disease severity and could be of prognostic value in iPAH and hPAH; (c) investigate whether NC-detected microvascular alterations in CTEPH normalize after PEA/BPA, or could predict residual PH after PEA/BPA .

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective single center case–control study which took place at a tertiary center for PH with expertise in nailfold capillaroscopy. Approval was obtained from the institutional ethic committee of the Amsterdam UMC, VU medical center (2017.711), and all patients gave informed consent. Median follow up was 3.7 years (3.5–4.3).

Study subjects

24 patients with iPAH, seven patients with hPAH, 17 patients with CTEPH (diagnosed according to ESC/ERS guidelines1) and 48 age- and gender matched controls with primary Raynaud’s phenomenon not suspected of PH and without connective tissue disease (excluded by normal serology and NC-results; Table S1) were included in this study. Patient files were queried retrospectively to obtain information on medical history, treatment, age, gender and WHO functional class. Pulmonary function tests, including lung uptake of carbon monoxide (DLCO) and oxygen saturation during six minute walking distance (6MWD) testing were collected. In addition, hemodynamic parameters were obtained from right heart catheterization, including mPAP, PVR, mean right atrial pressure (mRAP), PCWP and cardiac index (CI). Cardiac MRI was used to derive RV end diastolic volume (RVEDV), and ejection fraction (RVEF). All measurements were performed within one year from NC Patients with positive auto-immune serology, or signs or symptoms suggestive of auto-immune disease were referred to a rheumatologist for complete evaluation. None of the patients included in the study were diagnosed with an auto-immune disease.

Nailfold capillaroscopy

Capillaroscopy and computerized mosaic of the nailfold was obtained as described previously4. The nailfolds of the 3th and 4th fingers were examined bilaterally in each patient. The collected NVC images were randomly analyzed by a blinded single trained assessor (AS). Capillary density, total capillary, arterial and venous width were measured using ImageJ software (v1.49 NIH Image, Bethesda, Maryland, USA). NC assessment was performed in a standardized manner by applying the capillaroscopic definitions consented by the EULAR Study Group on Microcirculation in Rheumatic Diseases6. The quantitative assessment consisted of examining the following parameters per linear millimeter, per image: ‘capillary density’ (i.e. number of distal row capillaries), ‘capillary dimension’ (more specifically number of giant capillaries, i.e. apical limb width > 50 μm), ‘capillary morphology’ (i.e. number of abnormally shaped capillaries not meeting the following definition of normal: hairpin shaped, crossing or tortuous shape), and presence of ‘microhaemorrhages’6,13. Capillaries were counted in the distal row and measured over a median length of 1.87 mm/digit (0.97–2.78) in the 100 × magnification image of every digit13. The loop dimensions (total capillary, apex, arterial and venous width, Fig. 1) were presented as the mean of three to five capillaries of digit L4 and R4, employing the 300 × magnification image. The qualitative assessment was performed by first determining the overall capillary pattern, by categorizing the images into a ‘(non-)scleroderma pattern’, according to the standardized Fast Track Algorithm. Subsequently, the ‘scleroderma patterns’ were further subcategorized according to Cutolo et al. based on the most dominant/worst pattern as ‘early’, ‘active’ or ‘late’6. Non-specific capillary patterns were defined by the presence of changes in capillary dimension (e.g. “elongated” capillaries, “widened” loops but not giant capillaries), prominence of the subpapillary plexus, haemorrhages, abnormal shapes (e.g. “bushy” capillaries, “bizarre” capillaries)6.

Time to clinical worsening in patients with iPAH and hPAH

Time to clinical worsening (TTCW) was defined as time to add-on therapy (unless within four months after initial therapy), atrial balloon septostomy, lung transplantation or death, and was conducted from the date of NC until the 1st of June 2022. Median follow up was 3.7 years (3.5–4.3).

Measurement of capillary density after PEA or BPA

Fifteen patients underwent PEA (n = 8) or BPA (n = 7). Eleven patients had normalized mPAP after undergoing PEA or BPA, defined as an mPAP < 20 mmHg and PVR ≤ 160 dyn∙s∙cm-5 at least one year after the intervention. In seven patients (n = 6 with normalized mPAP) capillary density was reassessed a median of 3.3 year (2.8–3.9) after PEA or the last BPA.

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of the study: capillary density expressed as number of distal row capillaries/mm. Sample size was calculated adopting α 0.05, β 0.80. Expected mean capillary density for control subjects was estimated at 10.0 ± 1.5, and for patients with iPAH at 8.0 capillaries/mm based on previous publications4,10,14. A minimum of 9 patients per group was necessary to discern this difference.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (v26, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Data are represented as mean with standard deviation (SD), or median with interquartile ranges (IQR). Baseline comparisons between iPAH, hPAH and CTEPH were done using independent student t-tests or Mann–Whitney-U tests depending on distribution, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. One-way ANOVA with post-hoc t-testing and bonferroni correction was employed for comparison between iPAH, hPAH, CTEPH and healthy controls. Univariate linear- or logistic regression analysis were used for investigating associations. We used linear regression when the outcome is continuous (e.g. capillary density) and logistic regression in case of a dichotomous outcome (e.g. medication-use or co-morbidities). p-values of ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. Cox-regression analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of capillary density, and was corrected for age. The proportional hazard assumption was tested by plotting Schoenfelds residuals against time to clinical worsening. Since the plot was reasonably flat, the proportional hazards assumption holds. The fitted cox regression model therefore adequately describes the data15.

Results

Study population

Data from 47 consecutive PH patients were collected. The PAH group consisted of 23 patients with iPAH and 7 patients with confirmed hPAH (BMPR2 mutation, n = 5; GDF2 mutation, n = 1; TBX4 mutation, n = 1), see Fig. 2. Clinical characteristics are described in Table 1. IPAH and hPAH (in this study referred to as ‘PAH’) patients were predominantly female (61%), and received vasodilator treatment including endothelin receptor antagonist (ERA) (n = 17), phosphodiesterase (PDE-5) inhibitors (n = 23) and prostaglandin (PGI-2) in 24 out of 30 cases, while 10 out of 17 patient with CTEPH were treatment naïve, or were administered guanylate cyclase stimulators (sGC; riociguat n = 6). Hemodynamic parameters, right ventricular volumes, peripheral oxygen saturation, NT-proBNP, 6 min walking distance (6MWD) and WHO functional class were comparable among patients with PAH and CTEPH. DLCO was significantly lower in patients with PAH compared to CTEPH. In addition, patients with PAH more often had a history of coronary artery disease. ANA antibodies were detected in 10% of PAH patients16. These patients were referred to a rheumatologist who excluded the presence of a connective tissue disease in all. Other auto-immune serology was negative (Table S1).

Baseline NC evaluation

The median time from NC to right heart catheterization and cardiac MRI was 1 day (IQR 0–1.8). The median time from NVC to cardiac MRI was 12 days (IQR 0.5–32). Compared to asymptomatic controls, capillary density was significantly lower in PAH and CTEPH (10.3 ± 1.0 vs 7.5 ± 1.1 and 8.4 ± 1.5 capillaries/mm, p < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 3). Capillary density did not differ between iPAH and hPAH patients (7.5 ± 1.1 vs. 7.6 ± 1.0, p 0.915; Table S2). In further exploratory analyses, patients with iPAH and hPAH were taken together as one group (PAH-group). Capillary dimensions were increased similarly among PAH and CTEPH compared to healthy controls (Fig. 4, Table S3). None of the patients with PAH or CTEPH had a ‘scleroderma pattern’. 23% of PAH patients and 13% of CTEPH patients showed a non-specific capillary pattern, the rest was classified as ‘normal’ (Table S4).

Correlations between capillaroscopic characteristics and patient characteristics

No statistically significant associations could be detected between cardiopulmonary functional measures (mPAP, PVR, CO, DLCO, lowest SpO2 during exercise, NT-proBNP, systolic blood pressure, age, BMI) and nailfold capillary density. Likewise, administered drugs (ca-antagonists, ERA, PDE-5, PGI-2 or riociguat) and co-morbidities (DM2, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and smoking) were not associated with capillary density (Fig. S1, Table S5).

Time to clinical worsening in patients with PAH

Patients with PAH were followed for a median of 3.7 years (3.5–4.3). 11 out of 30 patients experienced significant clinical worsening resulting in add-on therapy (n = 5), lung transplant (n = 1) or death (n = 5). No patients were lost to follow up. With each decrease of 1 capillary per linear mm in patients with PAH, the hazard of clinical worsening increased by 2.0 (1.3–2.7), p 0.05 when corrected for age. Cox proportional hazard assumption was met, indicating that the risk of clinical worsening was comparable over all time points.

Capillary density after PEA or BPA

In total 15 patients with CTEPH underwent PEA (n = 8) or BPA (n = 7). Eleven patients had normalized mPAP after the intervention. Four CTEPH patients had residual PH, defined as an mPAP > 20 mmHg and PVR ≥ 160 dyn∙s∙cm−5). Baseline capillary density was 8.4 ± 1.5 in the total group. Capillary density in CTEPH patients with residual PH was 8.6 ± 1.4, as opposed to 8.5 ± 1.5 capillaries/mm in those with normalized mPAP (P = 0.863). Capillary density was reassessed in seven patients (n = 6 with normalized mPAP) after a median of 3.3 years (2.8–3.9). There was no statistically difference between nailfold capillary density before (7.9 ± 1.7 capillaries/mm) and after the interventions (7.8 ± 0.5 capillaries/mm, p 0.678) in the seven patient that underwent a repeated NVC measurement. Discussion

The current study confirms that patients with iPAH and CTEPH are characterized by increased capillary dimensions and reduced capillary density, but extends these findings to patients with confirmed hPAH (BMPR2, GDF2, TBX4). Moreover, we could demonstrate that lower baseline capillary density was associated with clinical worsening in patients with PAH during a follow-up of 3.7 years. Thirdly, we show that NC-detected microvascular alterations did not normalize after PEA/BPA in patients with CTEPH, despite normalization of mPAP and PVR.

The reduction in capillary density is in concordance with earlier reports in patients with iPAH4,5,10 and CTEPH5, and confirm that systemic microangiopathy is not unique to PAH-SSc, but seems to characterize most forms of precapillary PH. The significant increase in total capillary diameter in patients with iPAH4,5,10 and CTEPH5 compared to controls is in line with previous studies. We confirm the findings of Arvanitaki et al.5 indicating an additional increase in diameter of the arterial and venous limb with in both patients with iPAH as CTEPH. In PAH-SSc, however, the NC-detected microvascular alterations are much more pronounced with extreme widening of capillaries (40 ± 18 up to 55 ± 7 μm;4,9,10) and lower capillary density (4.2 ± 1.8 up to 5.6 ± 1.9 cap/mm; Refs.4,9,10). A capillary density of ≥ 7 per linear mm is considered normal6 which implies that most patients with precapillary PH have normal capillary density, however on average approximately − 2.5 capillaries per linear mm lower compared to healthy controls. Interestingly, although mean capillary density in patients with SSC is much lower and abnormal (~ 5.4 ± 1.6 capillaries per linear mm; Refs.6,7), the weighted mean differences in capillary density between SSc patients with and without PAH is − 1.2, 95% CI − 2.3 to − 0.19.

We found lower capillary density in all studied disease categories (iPAH, hPAH and CTEPH), which seems to suggest that capillary rarefaction at the nailfold is not specific to the type of pulmonary vascular disease. A low capillary density in precapillary PH is possibly explained by changes in perfusion (low cardiac output) or by the systemic consequences of pulmonary vascular disease, such as upregulated inflammatory signaling and neurhormonal activation. Conversely, a low capillary density may also reflect a generalized microcirculatory disorder causing simultaneous changes in the systemic and pulmonary vasculature. Normalization of capillary density after cure of PH would have strengthened the first hypothesis. However, we did not observe improved capillary density after successful PEA/BPA for CTEPH. The fact that hemodynamic parameters and NC density were not correlated makes this hypothesis even more unlikely. Nevertheless, it cannot be excluded that right heart failure imposes structural changes to the systemic microvasculature that are irreversible. It seems therefore more plausible that shared, multifactorial mechanisms account for a simultaneous development of a systemic microangiopathy and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Inappropriate angiogenesis, genetic mutations, inflammation and impaired vasoreactivity could all be present. An interesting example of such a possible shared mechanism is the role of VEGF signaling17,18. Loss-of-function variants in the kinase insert domain receptor (KDR) gene, which encodes for the VEGFR2 (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2), are associated with PAH. In addition, inhibition of VEGF signaling by Semaxanib (SU5416), a small molecule inhibitor of the tyrosine kinase segment of VEGF receptors, inhibits VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 causing endothelial cell apoptosis and loss of lung capillaries19. Further evidence supporting the role of VEGF inhibition comes from reports of PH in patients treated with bevacizumab, a selective inhibitor of VEGF signaling, and the multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors20,21. Importantly, bevacizumab and sunitinib, a related compound to semaxinib, have both been demonstrated to cause a reduction in skin capillary density measured with nailfold capillaroscopy22,23. Capillary density reversed to normal after discontinuation of VEGF-inhibition22.

In SSc, nailfold capillaroscopy may represent a warning signal mirroring complications and clinical worsening6,7, but whether this is also true in PAH is presently unclear. Cross-sectional analyses could not demonstrate associations between capillary density and markers of cardiopulmonary function. We could not reproduce the inverse association between capillary density and mPAP in iPAH as reported earlier4. Nevertheless, in the present study patients with PAH were followed for a median of 3.7 years and during this follow-up, 11 out of 30 patients experienced significant clinical worsening resulting in add-on therapy, lung transplant or death. Capillary density measured at enrolment was associated with time to clinical worsening suggesting that nailfold capillaroscopy may have prognostic value also in PAH. We explored this further in patients with CTEPH. Persistent or recurrent PH after PEA, which can be observed in 17% to 51% of patients, remains a significant problem and is associated with high morbidity and mortality in patients with CTEPH12. Impaired neovascularisation is considered to be associated with poor survival, start of medical treatment and persistent pulmonary hypertension post-PEA in patients with CETPH24. An increase in VEGFa has been demonstrated post-PEA25, which might be paralleled by an increase in capillary density. In our study, however, NC-detected microvascular alterations in CTEPH did not normalize after PEA/BPA and did not predict residual PH after treatment.

Our study had several strengths. First, the study population was well characterized phenotypically and genotypically, and treated according to the available clinical practice guidelines in a specialized tertiary center for PH- patients. Second, heterogeneity regarding NC image analysis was avoided by having one independent trained assessor rating the images, and by following recently published consensus recommendations. Third, follow-up time of participants was reasonably long to assess clinical outcome. Fourth, we were able to perform NC before and after PEA/BPA in a rather large group of patients with CTEPH.

Limitation were also present. First, since NC was performed only once (with the exception of CTEPH), at the time of enrolment, we may have overlooked associations with the microvascular alterations that usually occur during the natural course of the disease. Second, PAH patients were predominantly female (61%), in contrast to CTEPH patients (35% female). Previous literature, however, indicates gender does not have a significant impact on mean capillary density at the nailfold in healthy volunteers, which suggest gender is less likely a confounder of this data24. Third, this is a single-center, observational study. Generalizability of these results should take into account the size of the study population and the fact that all subjects were seen and followed at the same PH care center. Fourth, power was limited to assess whether a lower capillary density was predictive of residual PH after invasive treatment in CTEPH.

In conclusion, we observed loss of nailfold capillary density in patients with different categories of precapillary pulmonary hypertension: iPAH, hPAH and CTEPH. Although capillary density was associated with time to clinical worsening in patients with PAH, capillary density did not normalize after recovery of pulmonary hemodynamics in patients with CTEPH.
