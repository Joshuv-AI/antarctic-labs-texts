Urban-town-rural disparities in subjective life expectancy among middle-aged and elderly adults in China: evidence from the CHARLS 2020
Authors: Yanlin Leng, Zhitao Cheng, Chenyan Jia, Tingfang Wu, Hao Liu, Fan Li, Junfeng Wang, Yong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubjective life expectancy (SLE) is a critical predictor of health risks and care needs among middle-aged and elderly adults, playing a vital role in guiding social security policies. Despite increasing research attention to SLE, few studies have explicitly investigated how place of residence influences SLE. This study aims to explore the association between place of residence (urban, town, and rural) and SLE among middle-aged and elderly adults.Methods12,907 participants aged 45 to 89 from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2020 were included in this study. Kruskal-Wallis and Chi-square tests were utilized for continuous and categorical variables, respectively, and the multinomial logistic regression was conducted to analyze the association between residence and SLE. The SLE was estimated using the Likert-scale method, while the conditional survival probability was calculated based on data from the China Population Census 2020. The similarities and differences between SLE and the conditional survival probability were systematically compared.ResultsThe majority of participants resided in rural areas (n = 8203, 63.6%), followed by cities (n = 3137, 24.3%) and towns (n = 1567, 12.1%). After adjusting for all covariables, multinomial logistic regression revealed that the likelihood of individuals reporting medium SLE (OR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.79–0.89) and high SLE (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.76–0.86) significantly decreased, as the residence variable changes from urban to town to rural. Stratified multinomial logistic regression further confirmed that rural residents had 16% lower odds of reporting medium SLE and 20% lower odds of reporting high SLE compared to urban residents. SLE in rural areas was consistently low, while it was moderate in towns and high in urban areas. Notably, women reported lower SLE than men residing in the same area, despite having a higher conditional survival probability, which potentially reflects Sex-specific perceptions of health and aging.ConclusionThese findings highlight disparities in SLE across residences, which may contribute to discussions on policy development for healthy aging, particularly regarding equitable access to healthcare services, community-based elderly support programs, and health education initiatives in rural areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24509-y.Keywords: Middle-aged and elderly adults, Residence, Subjective life expectancy, CHARLS

Introduction

By 2030, one in six people worldwide will be over the age of 60, resulting in an increasing proportion of elderly individuals within the global population [1]. In China, the country entered an aging society in 1999. Between 2021 and 2025, the proportion of adults aged 60 and above is projected to exceed 20% of the total population, marking a transition into a moderately aging society [2]. The life expectancy (LE) of older adults in China will continue to rise steadily throughout the period from 2020 to 2050 [3]. Life expectancy is the average remaining lifespan for a specific age cohort [4], assuming homogeneity within that group. However, an individual’s survival probability is influenced by various factors, including genetic, environmental, and behavioral determinants [5]. To accurately capture individual heterogeneity, the concept of subjective life expectancy (SLE) was introduced as an individual’s personal expectation regarding their remaining lifespan [6]. As a self-rated psychological indicator, SLE has gained increasing attention in aging research. It not only reflects a person’s perception of their future longevity but also offers insights into their broader attitudes toward health, aging, and future planning [7, 8]. SLE is a crucial indicator used to predict health risks and care needs among middle-aged and elderly populations [9], and significantly informs the formulation and optimization of social security policies [10, 11].

Three main approaches have been used to measure SLE, including point estimation, probabilistic survival assessment, and Likert-scale evaluation. The point estimation method elicits respondents’ anticipated lifespan in years. This approach has been applied to Hungarian adults aged 50 and above to compare subjective life expectancy with actuarial benchmarks [12]. The probabilistic survival assessment method, widely implemented in surveys such as the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [13], quantifies survival expectations by asking respondents to report percentage probabilities (e.g., 0-100%) of living to specified target ages. The Likert-scale method, adopted in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [14], similarly captures survival expectations in probabilistic terms, but utilizes an ordinal scale with predefined categorical options rather than a continuous probability metric.

Previous studies have identified significant disparities in the trajectory of healthy aging between urban and rural areas [15]. Research from the United States indicates that smoking prevalence is higher in rural areas compared to urban regions (odds ratio [OR] = 0.85 [95% CI, 0.81–0.88; P < 0.001]), with lower rates of smoking cessation (OR = 1.03 [95% CI, 1.03–1.04; P < 0 0.001]) [16]. Based on data from the 2003 and 2011 waves of the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study (WLS), one study has shown that elderly individuals in rural areas participate less frequently in social activities and have a higher prevalence of health issues compared to their urban counterparts [12]. In China, pronounced urban–rural disparities have been identified, with rural older adults experiencing substantially poorer physical and mental health, as well as limited access to medical care [17–20]. Furthermore, urban–rural differences extend to socioeconomic domains, including individual income [21] and educational resources [22]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that place of residence contributes to variations in health behaviors, socioeconomic status, psychological well-being, and other factors that significantly influence SLE in several developed countries. Unlike other developing countries with young populations, China is becoming a severely aging society, especially with 36.11% population in rural areas, facing unprecedented societal and public health challenges [23]. Understanding the health difference between aging populations in different place of residence is critical for the health policy makings to promote the wellbeing of the aging populations. However, there is still a lack of systematic research of the relationship between residence and SLE in China, making it necessary to explore this using dataset of elderly adults in China. However, the conventional binary urban–rural classification widely adopted in existing studies is increasingly insufficient to capture the heterogeneity of China’s spatial and administrative landscape. In practice, China operates under a three-tier administrative hierarchy, including urban, town, and rural, which has been institutionalized in several large-scale national surveys, including the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS). This tripartite structure better reflects the transitional nature and distinct socio-economic characteristics of town areas, which are often overlooked in binary classifications [24]. Therefore, we adopt this three-level residential classification in our analysis to more accurately capture the complexity of residential context in China.

Therefore, this study investigated the association between place of residence and SLE, and compared SLE with conditional survival probabilities across residences using data from CHARLS 2020. It aimed to provide empirical insights that may inform discussions on health inequalities and support efforts toward promoting healthy aging from a residence-based perspective.

Methods

Study reporting

This study is reported as per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline (see Additional file 1).

Data sources

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative longitudinal survey targeting individuals aged 45 and above in mainland China (https://charls.pku.edu.cn/en/). Its primary objective is to develop a high-quality public micro-database to facilitate aging-related research by collecting multidimensional data, including socioeconomic status, health conditions, and other relevant factors. The national baseline survey was conducted between 2011 and 2012, covering 17,708 individuals from 150 counties/districts, 450 villages/urban communities, and 10,257 households, providing a comprehensive representation of China’s middle-aged and elderly population. The fifth wave of the survey, conducted in 2020, comprised data from 19,395 participants. The dataset encompasses detailed information on demographic characteristics, family structure, health status and functioning, employment and retirement, household and personal income, the impacts of COVID-19, and the reasons for survey attrition. Consistent with the standard criteria employed in the CHARLS, the middle-aged and elderly adults were defined as individuals aged 45 years and older. For this study, given that the proportion of participants aged 90 years and above was very small and thus insufficient to represent the characteristics of this age group, we focused on participants aged 45 to 89. A total of 12,907 valid samples after excluding cases with incomplete data for key variables.

The CHARLS survey was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, with each respondent required to sign a consent form prior to participation.

Description of variable

The Likert-scale method was employed to evaluate respondents’ SLE. The question was phrased as follows: “How likely do you think you will survive to [target age]?” Respondents could choose from five options: (1) almost impossible, (2) unlikely, (3) maybe, (4) very likely, and (5) almost certain. The respondents were divided into eight age groups and each assigned a corresponding target age for survival estimation. Specifically, those currently aged under 64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94, and 100 years or older were asked about their perceived likelihood of surviving to ages 75, 80, 85, 90, 95, 100, 105, 110, and 115, respectively. To address potential imbalances in the data distribution, this study recategorized the original five groups into three groups based on respondents’ subjective attitudes toward their life expectancy. “Almost impossible” and “unlikely” were combined as low SLE, reflecting a negative attitude. “Maybe” was categorized as medium SLE, representing a neutral attitude. “Very likely” and “almost certain” were grouped as high SLE, indicating a positive attitude. The place of residence refers to the type of area where the respondent currently lives. In the questionnaire, four types of residential areas were listed: (1) city or town center areas, (2) urban–rural or town–village combined areas, (3) rural areas, and (4) special areas. For analysis, “city or town center areas” and “urban–rural or town–village combined areas” were regarded as the urban and town aeras, respectively. Due to their small sample size and heterogeneous nature, the “special areas” category was excluded from the analysis.

To further explore the relationship between residence and SLE, a total of 14 covariates were selected and categorized into four dimensions based on previous studies, including individual factors, health behaviors, economic factors, and psychological factors [25–27]. Individual factors included age, sex, and marital status. Marital status was grouped into two categories: married and living with a spouse, and all other situations. Health behaviors included smoking, drinking, physical activity, and social activity. Smoking status was classified as “never-smokers” and “smokers”, while drinking was based on whether the respondent had consumed alcohol in the past year. Physical and social activities were assessed using binary (yes/no) responses, indicating whether the respondents had recently engaged in these activities. Economic factors included income and education level. Income was defined as the respondent’s receipt of a salary from employment within the past year. Education levels were divided into three categories: “below primary school”, “primary school”, and “junior high school or higher”. Psychological factors covered satisfaction with life, health self-assessment, chronic disease, pain, and depression. Satisfaction with life and health self-assessment were simplified into two categories: “good” or “bad”. Chronic disease was defined as the presence of at least one medically diagnosed condition. Pain was categorized as “present” (any level of pain) or “absent” (no pain). Depression was measured using the CESD-10 scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 30, lower scores indicated better mental health.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, the linear relationships between each predictor and SLE were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients, which indicated no strong linear correlations (Figure S1, [see Additional file 2]). Subsequently, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed through variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis with all VIF values founded to be below 5 (Figure S2, [see Additional file 32]). These results suggest that the variables included in the analysis did not present problematic multicollinearity and were suitable for inclusion in this study. The continuous variables were summarized using means and standard error (SE), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables due to non-normal distributions, and the Chi-square test was applied to categorical variables to evaluate differences across groups defined by SLE. Given that the brant test indicated a violation of the proportional odds assumption in the ordinal logistic regression model, the multinomial logistic regression was performed to examine the associations between the variables and SLE. Four sets of factors, including individual factors, health behaviors, economic factors, and psychological factors, were sequentially included to adjust the models. To further investigate potential heterogeneity across residential contexts, stratified multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted based on participants’ place of residence. Specifically, separate models were estimated for participants residing in urban areas compared to those in towns, and urban areas compared to those in rural areas. These stratified analyses performed on the fully adjusted models enabled us to examine whether the associations between the covariates and SLE varied by residence. All the results of multinomial logistic regression models were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To compare the differences between SLE and conditional survival probability, SLE values were categorized into low, medium, and high levels, corresponding respectively to subjective survival probabilities of 0%, 50%, and 100%. These probabilities were used to estimate the subjective survival distribution. Additionally, life tables for each region were constructed using data from the 2020 Chinese census to calculate the conditional survival probability. The conditional survival probability (Px) represents the likelihood that an individual aged x will survive to age y. The samples were further stratified by residence and sex, and the probabilities of SLE and conditional survival were analyzed.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.9 and R 4.5.0. Data processing was performed using numpy 1.23.5 and pandas 2.2.3, while statistical analyses were conducted with MASS 7.3.65, brant 0.3.0, scipy 1.15.2, and statsmodels 0.14.4. Data visualization was carried out using matplotlib 3.8.0 and shap 0.46.0. The analyses strictly adhered to established statistical methodologies, with a p-value threshold of 0.05 for determining statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 2 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the participants. The sample had a mean age of 60.4 years (SE = 8.9), with the majority (63.6%) residing in rural areas, a distribution that reflects the cohort’s largely rural background. Only about one-quarter lived in urban areas (24.3%), and a smaller proportion (12.1%) resided in towns. Women and men were almost equally represented (50.7% vs. 49.3%), and most participants (78.6%) were married and living with a spouse, suggesting relatively stable family structures. Fewer than 40% of participants had completed junior high school or higher, while a substantial proportion (37.2%) had not progressed beyond primary school. In addition, only about a quarter (27.1%) reported paid work in the past year, reflecting the older age of the cohort and potential economic inactivity post-retirement. Regarding lifestyle factors, just over half (51.4%) participated in social activities, and most engaged in physical activity (91.2%), though the prevalence of smoking (29.2%) and drinking (38.5%) remained notable. Participants with lower SLE were typically older and had less favorable profiles. They were more likely to live in rural areas, have lower education and income, report chronic disease, experience pain, show higher depression scores, and express dissatisfaction with life and poorer self-rated health. In contrast, those reporting high SLE tended to be younger, more educated, more socially and physically active, and less likely to report chronic disease or depressive symptoms. These patterns highlight the multifaceted social, economic, and health-related contexts associated with subjective life expectancy in this population.

Association between place of residence and subjective life expectancy

Table 3 summarizes the results from adjusted multinomial logistic regression models examining the association between place of residence and SLE, controlling sequentially for individual, behavioral, economic, and psychological factors. Overall, the models demonstrated good fit (see Additional file 4, Table S1). The analysis revealed that individuals living in rural areas had significantly lower odds of reporting both medium and high SLE compared to their urban counterparts. Specifically, the odds of reporting high SLE decreased most markedly (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.76–0.86), followed by medium SLE (OR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.79–0.89), as residence shifted from urban to town to rural areas. These findings indicate a clear gradient in perceived longevity associated with degree of urbanization. Further stratified analyses (Table 4; see Additional file 5, Table S2) provide additional nuance: rural residents consistently exhibited 16% and 20% lower odds of reporting medium and high SLE, respectively, than urban residents. While town residents also showed lower odds ratios, statistical significance was observed only for high SLE. This suggests that the negative association between rural living and SLE is robust, even after accounting for demographic, economic, and psychosocial factors, whereas patterns in towns are comparatively weaker.

Comparison of SLE and conditional survival probability in different residential areas

As illustrated in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b), both SLE and conditional survival probability start to decline around age 61. Before age 70, urban residents generally reported the highest SLE, reflecting possibly better perceived future health and longevity. Beyond this age, town residents’ SLE surpassed that of urban residents, while rural residents consistently reported the lowest SLE across all ages. Interestingly, conditional survival probability showed a different dynamic. It was highest among rural residents before age 65, suggesting a stronger short-term survival outlook in early older age. However, after age 65, this advantage diminished, and urban residents demonstrated higher conditional survival probabilities. By approximately age 81, both SLE and conditional survival probabilities converged across residential groups, indicating that the differences in perceived and short-term expected longevity narrow in advanced age.

SLE and conditional survival probability stratified by place of residence and sex. a SLE under regional models. b Conditional survival probability under regional models. c SLE under regional and sex-specific models. d Conditional survival probability under regional and sex-specific models

Stratifying by sex (Fig. 1c and d) revealed further patterns. Up to age 75, men reported higher SLE than women in each region. After this age, women in urban and town areas overtook men, while rural women consistently had the lowest SLE. For conditional survival probability, women generally showed higher values than men throughout the age span within each residential category. Notably, after age 70, survival probabilities converged among women across regions, suggesting shared perceptions of longevity in later life.

These findings underscore the complex interplay between age, sex, and place of residence in shaping subjective and conditional expectations of longevity among older adults.

Discussion

Main findings

In this cross-sectional study of 12,907 middle-aged and elderly Chinese participants, we found significant association between place of residence and SLE. Compared to urban residents, odds ratios for rural residents were 16% lower for reporting medium SLE and 20% lower for reporting high SLE, while town residents had an 18% lower odds of reporting high SLE. The SLE survival probability curve showed persistently lower levels among rural residents. However, conditional survival probability was highest for rural residents before age 65, and SLE in towns surpassed that in urban areas between ages 70 and 81. Additionally, the sex pattern of SLE remained consistent across regions, with men reporting higher SLE than women, despite women had a lower conditional survival probability.

Residential disparities

Few studies have explicitly examined the association between place of residence and SLE, this study aims to address that gap in existing research. A study investigated factors associated with SLE in relation to actuarial life expectancy (LE), categorizing place of residence into metropolitan, urban, and rural areas as potential influencing factors [26]. The results revealed that residence was not a significant determinant of the gap between an individual’s SLE and LE. However, it is important to note that such conclusions may vary across countries and regions, given differences in sample characteristics and contextual factors. The findings from China, based on CHRLAS 2015, indicated the SLE of urban population was higher than that of rural population [28]. However, this study relied on household registration (hukou) status to define residential categories, which may not accurately capture individuals’ actual living environments, especially given increasing rural–urban migration and the blurred boundaries between settlement types. The findings reveal statistically significant differences in SLE across the three categories, underscoring the importance of incorporating nuanced spatial stratifications in health and aging research. The lower SLE observed in rural areas likely stems from cumulative structural disadvantages. Limited access to healthcare and underdeveloped infrastructure impair rural older adults’ ability to manage chronic conditions and receive preventive services [28]. Rural older adults experience limited access to healthcare, lower health literacy, and socioeconomic disadvantages, all of which hinder disease management and reduce preventive care engagement, contributing to lower SLE [18–20, 29, 30]. Towns, as transitional spaces between urban and rural areas, exhibit more complex patterns. Notably, town residents aged 70–82 reported higher SLE than both urban and rural counterparts, suggesting possible cohort-specific advantages, such as stronger social cohesion or perceived safety [31]. These nonlinear patterns highlight the importance of moving beyond binary urban–rural classifications to account for the nuanced socio-spatial context of aging.

Role of individual factors

The analysis of individual factors—such as age, sex, and marital status—revealed their substantial influence on SLE. Age is a significant determinant of SLE, with levels of SLE peaking at age 62 and gradually declining thereafter. Notably, the lowest SLE values observed in the elderly group were significantly lower than those in the younger group. This pattern demonstrates that age-specific trends in SLE do not follow a continuous decline as age advances [32]. The impact of sex on SLE has been well-documented in numerous studies, with women typically expressing more pessimistic expectations regarding their SLE compared to men [33, 34]. However, this study reveals an interesting deviation from this trend: urban women generally report higher SLE than rural men. This divergence may be explained by regional disparities in socioeconomic status, healthcare accessibility, and social support, which potentially buffer traditional sex-based differences. Urban environments often provide women with better educational and health resources, fostering more positive health perceptions. Marital status also plays a crucial role, as married individuals living with their spouses tend to report higher SLE, possibly due to the psychosocial benefits of companionship, economic stability, and shared health behaviors [35, 36].

Role of health behaviors

When examining health behaviors, we found significant associations between SLE and physical activity, smoking, drinking, and social activity. Social activities are conducive to extending SLE, but due to sex differences in the types of social activities, there are sex differences in the impact of social activities on the subjective healthy life expectancy of the elderly [17]. Physical activity, on the other hand, serves as a causal variable positively associated with SLE [21], while unhealthy behaviors such as alcoholism and smoking demonstrate a bidirectional predictive relationship with reduced SLE [37]. Consistent with previous studies, our findings revealed that smoking was associated with a reduction in SLE, with OR values of 0.80 for medium SLE and 0.85 for high SLE. Conversely, alcohol consumption was linked to an increase in SLE, with OR values of 1.14 for medium SLE and 1.29 for high SLE. It is possible that a portion of the population may not fully recognize the mortality risks associated with these behaviors [6]. This limitation is also present in the sample used in this study, which may partially explain why the impact of alcohol consumption on SLE remains unclear and cannot be fully elucidated.

Role of economic factors

Economic factors, including income and education, play a significant role in shaping SLE. Low education and low income both reduce an individual’s impact on SLE. Individuals with lower educational attainment may be more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviors [38], while lower income levels are often associated with poorer living conditions and reduced access to healthcare resources, and other factors that can collectively lead to lower SLE. However, the effects of income and education are heterogeneous. Other studies have shown that wealth and education have no significant effect on SLE [39], and even people with high education underestimate SLE [9]. This discrepancy may be partly explained by differing definitions of “high education” across studies, for instance, while some define it as a college degree or above, our study categorizes high education as high school and above.

Role of psychological factors

Psychological factors, including life satisfaction, self-rated health, chronic disease, pain, and depression, were found to have a profound impact on SLE. Specifically, higher levels of life satisfaction and better self-rated health are associated with higher SLE, consistent with findings from several prior studies [22]. These individuals who perceive their lives as meaningful and fulfilling are more optimistic about their future. Chronic diseases among middle-aged and elderly individuals are significantly negatively correlation with SLE. Specifically, those with chronic diseases are 36.2% less likely to report high SLE compared to those without such conditions [26]. This finding aligns with the notion that chronic disease introduces not only physical burden but also psychological strain, fostering uncertainty about one’s health trajectory and survival [40]. In the moderate and high SLE groups, the odds ratio for individuals troubled by pain decreased by 25% and 47%, respectively. This finding is consistent with other studies demonstrating that respondents reporting severe and/or interfering pain had significantly lower subjective survival probabilities compared to those without pain (Marginal Effect = − 0.03 to − 0.06, p < 0.05) [41]. This study found the relationship between depression scores and SLE, with higher depression scores associated with lower SLE. Specifically, the mean depression scores were 12.56, 9.06, and 7.83 for the low, medium, and high SLE groups, respectively. This inverse association corroborates prior findings indicating that elevated depressive symptoms significantly predict reduced SLE levels [42]. Beyond the direct effects of symptoms, depression can interfere with health‑promoting behaviors, weaken social support networks, all of which may further diminish individuals’ expectations regarding their future lifespan [43, 44].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Notably, it is the first to specifically investigate the association between residential location and SLE. Secondly, recognizing that town areas possess unique regional characteristics blending urban and rural features, we categorized residential areas into three distinct types, including urban, town, and rural, to provide a more nuanced analysis of how regional differences influence SLE. Thirdly, we conducted a comparative analysis of the disparities between SLE and conditional survival probability, stratified by both place of residence and sex.

Despite its contributions, this study still has limitations. Firstly, the conversion of ordinal values (0, 1, and 2) into percentage probabilities (0%, 50%, and 100%) for SLE lacks a universally accepted standard, potentially introducing inaccuracies. In future surveys of China’s subjective life expectancy, it may be possible to consider using the probabilistic survival assessment method to give a probability of 0–1. Secondly, as SLE is a self-reported measure, it may be subject to various forms of measurement bias [45, 46]. To mitigate this concern, future studies could triangulate self-reported SLE with objective health indicators (e.g., medical records or biomarkers) or compare it against actuarial life tables to validate its plausibility. Thirdly, the cross-sectional design limited the assessment of how long-term experiences affect SLE. Longitudinal data could help explore the effects of residence duration and mobility on SLE. Fourthly, the findings are specific to the context of China based on the selected variables. Considering more abundant data in the future, including different countries and more covariates, will make the conclusion generalizable and persuasive.

Conclusion

This study found a significant association between place of residence and subjective life expectancy among Chinese elderly adults. Compared to urban residents, rural residents have a 16% and 20% lower probability of achieving medium and high SLE, respectively. Moreover, SLE in the rural population remains consistently lower than that in both urban and town populations. These findings call for targeted rural health interventions including mobile geriatric clinics, community elderly programs, and village doctor training to address healthcare access gaps and improve subjective life expectancy disparities.
