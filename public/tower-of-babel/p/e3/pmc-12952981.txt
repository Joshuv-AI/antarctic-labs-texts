The Barriers and Facilitators Healthcare Professionals' Experience When Assessing the Cutaneous Manifestations of Chronic Venous Insufficiency and Peripheral Arterial Disease in People With Dark Skin Tones: A Qualitative Descriptive Study
Authors: Victoria Clemett, Neesha Oozageer Gunowa, Jemell Geraghty, Mubeena Shahid, Rianna John, Lukla Biasi, Sue Woodward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecognising peripheral arterial disease and chronic venous insufficiency early and managing them appropriately improves patient outcomes and prevents ulceration. However, few studies consider the influence patient skin tone has on this assessment. The study aimed to explore the experiences of healthcare professionals when assessing the cutaneous manifestations of chronic venous insufficiency and peripheral arterial disease in people with dark skin tones. We conducted semi‐structured online interviews with healthcare professionals working in the United Kingdom between July and December 2023. The ‘theoretical domains framework of behaviour change’ (TDF) guided the interview schedule and data analysis. 24 healthcare professionals were interviewed (18 nurses, 5 doctors, 1 physiotherapist) with an average experience of 15 years (IQR 9.75–21.75 years). We found the environmental context domain best explains clinical behaviour, which included effective teamworking, referral pathways and training, and adequate lighting. Environmental factors also influenced other aspects of the TDF such as skill development, focus and concentration, and belief about capabilities. Some participants also found personal challenges in asking questions exploring patients' behaviour and skin care practices as they did not want to cause offence. Findings will inform the development of interventions to support lower limb assessment in those with dark skin tones.

Introduction

People living with a leg ulcer experience negative emotional reactions associated with impaired body image, social isolation and depression [1]. They experience a range of other adverse physical symptoms such as swelling, malodour and wound discharge [1] and pain [2]. These negatively impact on people's ability to self‐care and maintain employment, placing a strain on relationships and finances [2].

Recognising peripheral arterial disease and chronic venous insufficiency early and managing them appropriately improves patient outcomes and prevents ulceration [3, 4]. For people with peripheral arterial disease, early intervention minimises limb loss [4], which is associated with an 18.1% mortality rate over 5 years [5]. Therefore, guidelines focus on interventional treatment for chronic venous disease prior to the onset of ulcers [6]. However, current practice in the United Kingdom (UK) highlights a lack of knowledge and training that prevents junior nurses from completing an initial assessment [7]. Front‐line clinical staff also feel unable to action referrals to specialist services [7, 8], subsequently, 34% of people with leg ulcers do not have an aetiology reported [9].

This is not just an issue within the UK. 71% of healthcare professionals and 60% of patients in India believed lower limb conditions in primary care settings were misdiagnosed, with patients reporting their wounds significantly progressed or became infected before they consulted with specialists [10]. In Australia, primary care physicians lack confidence in the diagnosis of leg ulcers, but nurses relied on them to make referrals to specialist services [11]. Subsequently, patients felt their referral to specialists was delayed [11]. Patients in the United States were similarly plagued by delayed diagnosis, with other specialities frequently consulted before an accurate diagnosis of venous disease and definitive treatment were identified [12]. For these individuals, this led to a median delay of 230 days (IQR 86.5–1088 days) [12].

Few studies consider the influence patient skin tone has on lower limb assessment and referral, even though cutaneous manifestations that occur as part of both peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and chronic venous disease (CVI) vary across skin tones [13, 14]. Moreover, studies examining other wound aetiologies have highlighted the impact of patient skin tone on clinical assessment, specifically delaying the identification of pressure ulcers in people with dark skin tones, resulting in a higher prevalence of more severe pressure ulcers in people with dark skin tones [15]. Thereby exemplifying the potential skin tone may have on skin assessment.

The limited evidence available in leg ulcers and lower limb vascular conditions suggests the skin tone of the patient is an important consideration in vascular conditions. Nurse educators in clinical practice and universities have less confidence in their ability of undertaking a skin assessment on people with CVI & PAD in dark skin tones compared to light skin tones [16]. Front‐line clinicians report assessing skin changes in lower limbs is challenging on people with dark skin tones [17]. There is also a lack of clinical representation of people with dark skin tones in both the leg ulcer [18] and vascular guidelines [6], and a dearth of photos of people with dark skin tones in vascular surgery educational resources [19]. Clinical staff therefore acquire skills in assessing patients' skin and wound care on community placements or through experiential learning and personal exposure [20] where there is variation in exposure to diverse skin tones [21]. This may negatively impact healthcare professionals' ability to undertake an accurate lower limb assessment on patients with dark skin tones in practice. However, the facilitators and barriers to undertaking these assessments are unknown.

Aims

This study aims to explore the experiences of healthcare professionals regarding lower limb skin changes in people with dark skin tones with chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD).

Methods

Design

This qualitative descriptive study uses a theory driven interview approach comprising of individual online interviews with registered healthcare professionals. This design enables experiences of healthcare professionals to be explored. This study used a semi‐structured interview guide based on the ‘theoretical domains framework of behaviour change’ (TDF) [22] (Supporting Information, Table A). It was reported using the ‘standards for reporting qualitative research’ (SRQR) guidelines [23].

Theoretical Framework

Understanding factors that determine behaviour were evaluated using the ‘Theoretical Domains Framework of behaviour change’ (TDF) [22] because when developing new complex interventions, it is advised these should be theory‐based [24]. The TDF is a validated, theory‐informed methodological framework developed by behavioural scientists consisting of 14 domains: knowledge, skills, professional role and identity, beliefs about capabilities, optimism, beliefs about consequences, reinforcement, intentions, goals, memory attention and decision processes, environmental context and resources, social influences, emotion, behavioural regulation [22]. With an appreciation that the domains for environmental context and resources, social influences, professional role and identity and behavioural regulation encompass organisational constructs and team interactions as well as individual factors relevant to clinical behaviours [25]. This framework has been used successfully in previous wound care research to explore staff behaviour related to pressure ulcer prevention [26], complex community wound care [20] and the use of venous leg ulcer clinical practice guidelines [27].

Using this TDF enables findings to identify components to be modified or enhanced and therefore can be used as part of a stepped approach to changing clinical behaviour [28]. These theoretical insights can then be developed, defined, and refined with stakeholders to define the mechanisms of change and provide contextual factors to realise how practice may be improved within a future study [24].

This theory informed both the interview schedule and the analysis.

Study Setting and Recruitment

Invitations to participate were shared on social media to recruit healthcare professionals (e.g., doctors, nurses or allied healthcare professionals) working in the UK. The invitations to participate were also shared by several professional organisations that represent healthcare professionals working with patients with CVI or PAD (CommunityHART, Leg Ulcer Forum, Legs Matter, Queen's Nursing Institute, RCN District Nurses Forum, RCN General Practice Nurses Forum, Society of Tissue Viability, Society of Vascular Nurses, TVN2gether).

All participants were offered a £12.50 e‐shopping voucher as a thank‐you for their time.

Inclusion and/or Exclusion Criteria

Any licensed healthcare professional (e.g., doctors, registered nurses or allied healthcare professionals) involved in assessing skin changes in chronic venous insufficiency and peripheral arterial disease in the UK were recruited for this study. Participants self‐certified they had experience working with patients with a range of skin tones (dark to light skin tones) or exclusively dark (black and brown) skin tones, had undertaken some relevant post‐registration continuing professional development in vascular disease or leg ulcers (which may include professional courses, e‐learning, academic qualifications, or attendance at in‐house training), and were practising in the UK. Healthcare professionals could participate regardless of their years of experience but had to have completed some specialist training (either in house or external) on vascular conditions or leg ulcers.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted (July—November 2023) via MS‐teams by [VC] or [RJ] using open ended questions. The interview schedule was based on target behaviours from the TDF. A full definition of the domains included in the TDF and questions relating to these target behaviours can be found in Table A, Supporting Information. During the 1 h, one‐to‐one interviews, the questions in the interview schedule were available for the participant to read on the MS‐teams video feed as well as being verbally asked. The interviewer used additional prompts to encourage the participant to explain their responses in more detail and clarify points made. Automated transcripts were captured by the MS‐teams recordings, checked for accuracy against the recording and amended as required.

Demographic information was captured on participants' age, years of experience, exposure to patients with dark skin tone and their own skin tone and ethnicity. These data were captured to determine whether these variables affected responses.

Data Analysis

A hybrid inductive/deductive data analysis approach based on recommendations for analysing data using the TDF framework [22] was used to determine the facilitators and challenges to assessing skin changes associated with CVI or PAD in people with dark skin tones. Two authors [VC] & [MS] began with a reflective thematic analysis approach [29]. They firstly independently read, familiarised themselves with the data and reflected on the transcripts. Both [VC] & [MS] then started to independently generate codes from the transcripts and met to discuss their thoughts and progress. Codes were discussed and iteratively developed as transcripts were re‐read and discussed. These codes were then grouped to form themes and discussed to ensure themes were consistent and had exclusive content. This ensured both codes and findings related to both TDF and non‐TDF information could emerge [30]. This may enable more contextual information about determinants of behaviour and other relevant information to arise giving a more nuanced understanding of healthcare professionals' experience [30]. Authors chose to either develop these initial codes and themes in NVivo 14 or using a pen/highlighter approach. Throughout this process [VC] & [MS] worked independently and met to discuss findings and challenge perspectives. These themes were then aligned under the over‐arching domain with the 14 items on the TDF [22]. The final classification of codes and findings were presented and discussed with the wider research team.

The process of undertaking the assessment for skin changes was also extracted and tabulated to present the clinical ‘skill’ aspect used and valued in assessing skin changes as part of participants' clinical assessment.

Ethical Considerations

All participants gave their informed consent and completed the consent form electronically. Data were handled according to UK data protection law, including the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) and the Data Protection Act 2018. Ethical approval was obtained from Kings College London ethics committee (Ethical Clearance Reference: RS/DP‐22/23‐36 610).

Rigour and Reflexivity

Research rigour and quality considered several strategies to enhance the credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability of this research. The research team was either registered healthcare professionals (registered nurses with experience in community or hospital settings, and a vascular surgeon) or students on healthcare courses. All worked in areas with diverse multi‐cultural populations seeing patients with a variety of skin tones. The research group came from a range of ethnicities, but the majority of the team had light skin tones.

During the interviews, credibility was maintained through building rapport with participants to enable them to be open and honest with their answers, the use of a theoretical framework to structure the interview, and by the interviewer checking understanding and obtaining clarification with the interviewee during the interview. Furthermore, regular peer debriefing also enhanced the credibility of findings. The interviewers also discussed their potential biases and reflected on these to ensure they did not affect interviewee responses. The final group of interviews (with registered nurses) provided no new data or insights to their experiences not captured in existing findings, suggesting data saturation had been reached for this professional group.

During data analysis and interpretation, further strategies were employed to ensure research rigour. Dependability in findings was ensured by reflective journaling of interview transcripts and researchers reflecting on and becoming self‐aware of their potential biases, as advised by Braun and Clarke [29]. Coding was undertaken independently by [VC] & [MS] but meetings occurred frequently to discuss codes and share ideas from the data. This added dependability and confirmability by challenging each other's interpretations of the data from their own perspectives and experiences. The researchers who undertook the initial coding consisted of one researcher who had undertaken previous research exploring skin tone bias in healthcare and a second researcher who, although new to the topic, felt they could relate to the experience of people from diverse backgrounds and ethnic minority groups. Both researchers approached the topic with a sense of curiosity and openness. However, they were aware they assumed gaps existed in the accurate assessment of people with dark skin tones. This use of multiple coders is considered useful in developing richer and more complex insights into the data through this collaborative process [29]. The use of two researchers working independently in the analysis stage and then periodically meeting throughout to discuss their findings, thoughts and ideas enabled wider perspectives and interpretations of the data. This is a strength of this work.

Themes were related to the domains within the TDF first independently by [VC] & [MS], and final alignment of categorises discussed and agreed. This added rigour by questioning each other's categorisation and discussing findings throughout this process. The inclusion of direct quotes from participants to exemplify themes and the availability of anonymised transcripts on request to King's Open Research Data Storage (KORDS) improve the credibility of the findings.

Findings

Characteristics of Participants

Twenty four healthcare professionals were interviewed (18 nurses, 5 doctors, 1 physiotherapist). Experience ranged from 2 to 40 years (median 15 years, IQR 9.75–21.75 years). Participants with fewer years of clinical experience generally reported seeing people with dark skin tones frequently, but those who had longer clinical experience reported a wide range of frequencies in seeing people with dark skin tones in their case load. However, over their years of experience, they had built up experience of assessing this patient group. The majority of participants identified themselves as ‘Black or Black British (African)’ (n = 11) (Table 1). Skin tone varied across participants, but most participants had skin tones from the top row (1 of 6) of the Ho and Robinson [31]– Skin Tones Tool (n = 11, 45.8%), representing the lightest skin tones (Table 1).

Barriers

Barriers were dominated by subtle cutaneous manifestations of CVI and PAD in people with dark skin tones and ambiguous skin changes. This required healthcare professionals to undertake a comprehensive holistic assessment and rely on patient reported history and symptoms to interpret findings. Often this required healthcare professionals to ask clinically relevant questions in a culturally sensitive way and overcome their own reservations to benefit patient assessment. The barriers identified frequently related to knowledge, behavioural regulation, decision process, and environment domains (Table 2).

Knowledge

Subtle Presentations of CVI and PAD, and Ambiguous Indicators in People With Dark Skin Tones

A subtle presentation was evident throughout the interviews with a recognition that cutaneous manifestations of CVI and PAD are easily observed in people with light skin tones but visualisation may be subtle or absent for people with dark skin tones, even though the underlying pathophysiology causing these presentations would be the same. Participants talked about skin changes being associated with other non‐vascular condition causes. There was therefore a requirement for the healthcare professional to consider this within their assessment and consider other potential differential diagnoses. This included contact eczema, skin bleaching, bruising, external injury and ‘a skin care problem’.

Interpreting these skin changes required healthcare professionals to understand the physiology and pathophysiology of skin for people with dark skin tones in relation to the cutaneous manifestations of vascular conditions and other common skin problems.

Behavioural Regulation

Overcoming Taboos and Having Braver Conversations

Clinician behaviour was often regulated by their desire to approach conversations with patients in a culturally sensitive way. Some participants faced challenges with needing to ask questions during their consultations on tricky or taboo subjects to gather appropriate history and understand the presentations of skin changes they were observing, for example inquiring whether skin bleaching may be causing variations in skin tone. There was often a hesitancy in approaching this. This awareness transcended the skin tones and ethnicity of participants, with some highlighting ‘the difficulty is can you ask such a question?’.

However, participants felt they had been more successful in their consultations when they had what was described as ‘braver conversations’ with some participants reporting commitment to initiating these conversations. Where people didn't talk about this hesitancy themselves, they often reported that this was a challenge for some of their colleagues and felt this might be holding them back from doing an in‐depth assessment. There was a clear acknowledgement that these reservations needed to be overcome to benefit patient assessment. However, participants reported they felt that training to do this was lacking and didn't support them in asking all the questions they wanted to.

Desire to Use Culturally Sensitive Terminology

There was also a desire, specifically among participants with light skin tones, to use appropriate terminology. This appeared to be motivated by not wanting to come over as racist or discriminatory, or causing offence to the patient.

Often this was implied through their hesitancy in the interview and tone of their statements rather than fully acknowledging their limitations in this area. This hesitancy in some terminology was observed for some participants, regardless of skin tone. For example, when a participant from Black African heritage was trying to describe different shades of dark skin in people of African heritage.

Memory, Attention and Decision Process

Reliance on Patient‐Reported Symptoms, History and a Comprehensive Assessment

A range of assessment techniques were reported in the interviews to assist in the recognition of skin changes in people with dark skin tones (Table 4). However, obtaining an accurate patient and symptom history was a key contributor to assist in accurately interpreting visual signs for the majority of participants. This was especially important when informing their decisions when visual signs were subtle or absent. There was an acknowledgement that for patients with dark skin tones, visual assessments alone could not be relied on, and this holistic consideration of patient‐reported symptoms and history was advantageous at guiding differential diagnoses. This inclusion of history taking and patient reporting symptoms was evident across professional groups.

Some participants acknowledged this reliance on narrative and subjective assessments to inform their diagnosis was often challenging in people with language barriers, poor health literacy or poor understanding by patients of their own condition. It was perceived that some of these challenges, although not exclusive to people with dark skin tones, may occur frequently in this group. These factors had an influence on the quality of information healthcare professionals were able to obtain and subsequently their confidence in obtaining an accurate diagnosis. Participants reported this acquisition of patient symptoms and history taking was similar to assessments taken across all skin tones when a comprehensive leg assessment was undertaken, which is considered best practice in all lower limb assessments. However, participants also acknowledged some further details may be required from those with dark skin tones, such as the importance of knowing patients' sickle‐cell status as leg ulcers may be associated with sickle‐cell disease rather than CVI or PAD. There was also a perception these reports were needed more in people with dark skin tones, especially when skin changes were poorly visualised or healthcare professionals were unsure how to interpret the presentations they were seeing.

Focus and Attention

Participants highlighted the importance of being able to focus during their assessments, citing barriers such as noisy wards and being hungry reducing their attention and focus. Less experienced healthcare professionals who felt they needed to concentrate during the assessment were inspired by healthcare professionals who could simultaneously communicate with the patient and undertake their assessment.

There was a fear among some participants that without this focus on the clinical assessment, mistakes would occur and they would not be able to accurately identify what is wrong with the patient.

Environmental Context and Resource's

Insufficient and Poor‐Quality Literature and Skin Photographs Hindering the Effectiveness of Care

There was a clear acknowledgement that the quality of education received to support accurate assessment of people with dark skin tones was poor and there was a large reliance on in‐person training and learning from senior colleagues due to a lack of published resources.

This lack of diversity in healthcare education was important to many participants, with some attributing this skin tone bias leading to a reduced awareness about the presentation in people with dark skin tones. This subsequently has a negative impact on obtaining an accurate diagnosis by non‐specialists and patients being commenced on the correct treatment. To address this disparity in healthcare literature some participants have reported collecting their own images and teaching materials about people with dark skin tones for local use. In several situations practice developed through experiential learning with a lack of evidence supporting skin assessment and interpretation in dark skin tones.

Making Standard Lower Leg Assessment Work for Individuals With Dark Skin Tones

Where participants reported the tools they used to frame their assessment of skin changes, standard history taking proforma or standard tools such as CEAP (Clinical manifestations, Aetiology, Anatomic distribution, Pathology) classification for describing venous disease & WIfI (Wound, Ischemia, Foot infection) tool for amputation risk in PAD were cited. These included, for example, looking for signs of oedema, venous eczema, varicose veins, infection and undertaking ABPI assessments. Some healthcare professionals made modifications to enable skin changes to be better visualised and inclusion of palpation within these assessments was highlighted (Table 4). However, this has led to variations in practice across the people interviewed. Additionally, less experienced colleagues reported everything to ensure they obtained a full picture and would not miss anything. Whether the assessment should be modified based on skin tones was debated, with some believing ‘it's how the tool is implemented by the clinician’ that was important rather than changing the tool itself. However, frequently there was a lack of confidence in the efficacy of some assessments on people with dark skin tones, specifically the Buerger's assessment to identify limb threatening ischaemia which assesses skin changes in response to positioning to determine arterial supply.

Culture and Capabilities of Non‐ Specialists

Across all clinical areas there was a perception that wider healthcare services were not undertaking lower limb assessments accurately and managing patients appropriately. However, it was unclear from the interviews whether this was just isolated to those with dark skin tones. The experiences from the perceptions of non‐specialists also remain unknown.

Facilitators

Facilitators encompassed proficiency developed through cultural exposure and clinical experience, being professionally motivated to facilitate an accurate assessment and obtain an accurate diagnosis, and the importance of multidisciplinary team working. There was also a recognition that recent media coverage and guidelines heightened the significance of skin tones in clinical assessments. These facilitators were predominantly related to domains of skills, beliefs about capabilities, environment, professional role and social influences (Table 3). However, often the environmental context influenced other aspects of the framework and appeared critical to clinical practice.

Knowledge

Participants' knowledge of skin changes in people with dark skin tones was associated with understanding the procedure and task they needed to undertake. This developed with clinical exposure. It was this knowledge that made their assessments and interpretations ‘actually very easy’.

Variations in Dark Skin Tones

Participants emphasised the variation in skin between light and dark skin tones went beyond skin colour, acknowledging ‘skin type, skin texture, everything is different from people with light skin colour’. This knowledge was considered essential to interpret the holistic assessment participants were undertaking. However, there was also an acknowledgement that presentation across people with different dark skin tones differs as well as awareness that skin tone naturally changes across the bodies of people with dark skin tones. These factors all feed into the assessment and have an impact on the number of comparisons across body parts they felt were needed.

Skills

Building Report and Creating Friendly Environment

Several participants reported that taking time to build good rapport with their patients and creating a friendly environment were essential for an effective consultation and assessment. This often related to making the patient feel comfortable or at ease during their assessments, through explaining what they are doing, having general conversations, or joking. This small talk may also start to gain some subjective information from the patient.

Participants with dark skin tones sometimes reported the advantage they had at building these relationships with patients due to shared cultural heritage and understanding. They considered their shared heritage made picking up aspects of the patient reported history and body language cues easier to interpret than they perceived their colleagues with light skin tones could do. However, the importance of building these relationships transcended across the skin tones of all participants. With a recognition building a positive patient‐clinician relationship was advantageous for the assessment; however, this may be especially important for those with dark skin tones, where the subjective assessments from patient history and symptoms were relied on to inform clinical diagnosis (Table 4).

Skill Development

Skills in the skin assessment of people with dark skin tones were sometimes acquired by direct teaching and feedback from seniors. However, where this wasn't available, participants frequently talked about their own self‐study and experiential learning to develop their practice. In both cases, the inclusion of a sound evidence base was infrequently discussed, although several reported they undertook their own research to support their learning. For all participants in this study, there appeared to be a professional commitment and motivation to improve their competence. Many also highlighted shared professional development within their multi‐disciplinary teams and the importance of learning from each other to share knowledge and develop skills.

Beliefs About Capabilities

Professional Confidence

There was an indication among all participants that confidence improved with clinical exposure, both in their ability to undertake the physical assessment, their communication with patients and how to report this. This was enhanced by healthcare professionals checking each other's diagnosis meaning ‘there is literally no way you will be missing anything’. This collaborative approach improved confidence in their own assessments and the unique expertise members of the multi‐disciplinary team gave to support the assessment and diagnosis was valued. However, many were aware of their limitations and acknowledged they were still learning as part of their own continuing professional development.

Memory, Attention and Decision Process

Skin Changes Are Just Part of the Jigsaw

There was an appreciation by participants that a comprehensive holistic assessment was needed to obtain an accurate diagnosis and skin changes would never be used as the sole diagnostic technique. This was considered important regardless of the patient's skin tone. However, there was an appreciation that this was possibly even more important in people with dark skin tones as the visualisation of skin changes could be subtle or absent and therefore couldn't be relied on to form a diagnosis. Participants often reported being more conscious in ensuring they had thoroughly completed all aspects of the full lower limb assessment when assessing patients with dark skin tones. This meant even experienced healthcare professionals needed to spend more time on their assessment to be confident in their diagnosis. This detailed assessment was similar across all skin tones; however, some specific considerations for people with dark skin tones were mentioned (Table 4). It was also acknowledged that due to subtleties in skin changes and variation in skin tone across the same person, some aspects of the assessment may take longer. This use of the holistic lower limb assessment meant the presence or absence of skin changes did not have a direct impact on patient care. Other signs, symptoms, and assessments were, therefore, used to ensure the correct diagnosis was made ahead of commencing treatment.

Environmental Context and Resource's

Lighting Is Key

Lighting was a key component in obtaining an accurate skin assessment in people with dark skin tones and enabled certain skin changes to be distinguished. However, the type of lighting preferred varied, with some people feeling natural light gave a more accurate representation of colour, while others reported the benefit of artificial light. There was an acknowledgement that colour would be distorted under some artificial light (specifically those with a yellow hue) or when light shone through a blue hospital curtain. The use of energy saving lightbulbs in people's homes would also mean getting enough good light to do a visual assessment would be challenging. However, artificial lights that were bright and white may be advantageous. Depending on clinical settings, the use of additional artificial lighting took the form of extended lights, head torches or light from a mobile phone. However, the use of the transillumination technique was also reported, which shines a strong light source behind the skin to visualise vascular structures (Table 4).

Culture of Discussion With, and Referral to Multi‐Disciplinary Team

The hospital‐based teams often spoke about multi‐professional working and peer‐to‐peer support to enhance patient assessment and facilitate a conducive working environment. This awareness of collaborative working appeared to be advantageous in patient assessment and diagnosis. For patients with dark skin tones there appeared to be a lower threshold for referral in some cases, especially when it came to ruling out other diagnoses or when their clinical assessments were inconclusive. However, it was acknowledged that this would not always result in an ultrasound scan if it was not clinically warranted as ‘they're very strict policies in place for who you scan and why you scan’. This referral to multi‐disciplinary colleagues occurred across professional groups to achieve an accurate diagnosis and was considered by participants to be a strength within their teams. These referrals recognised the role and expertise of others within the team and appeared to have a positive impact on the patient journey.

Goals

Participants' rationale for an accurate assessment in people with dark skin tones relates to their desire to provide the best standard of care. Participants often were aware of the implications of not getting their diagnosis correct and the relationship that had to providing the right treatment and ensuring good health outcomes for the patient.

Intentions

Overcome Past Inequity

Frequently participants reported needing to adapt their behaviour in response to overcoming patients' poor experience of care or managing patients who were delayed in being seen by the specialist team. Often this was reported as an unreliable initial health assessment by non‐specialists leading to delayed diagnosis and referral. There was a perception this was exacerbated further during the COVID pandemic due to a reduction in face‐to‐face consultations. However, a clear acknowledgement remained that assessment in non‐specialist services could be improved.

A few participants also talked about patient factors also causing delays. This included ignorance of their condition and poor health literacy. Participants also perceived some patients were delaying seeking healthcare support because of fear or previous experiences of racism within the health service. Participants were conscious of the impact this could have had on patient care. Professionally, participants focused on improving care for individual patients and ensuring they provided them with the most appropriate treatment rather than addressing inequities experienced ahead of reaching their clinical teams.

Social Influences

Heightening Awareness of Recent Guidelines and Media

Participants reported that the existence of generic guidelines on skin tone bias in wound care has ensured this is considered by healthcare professionals. This appears to have heightened awareness of the potential limitations of their assessments. However, these guidelines were only mentioned by the nurses within the interviews. Participants welcomed the discussion of this topic and appreciated its usefulness. A few felt this did not influence their practice directly, although it benefited the healthcare team as a whole.

Discussion

To our knowledge this is the first study to interview health professionals about their experiences of assessing cutaneous manifestations of CVI and PAD in people with dark skin tones. The analysis highlighted several key components that inform clinical behaviours specifically relating to both their individual factors (knowledge, skills, beliefs about capabilities, decision process, and behavioural regulation) and organisational determinants of clinical practice (resources, social influences, and teamworking).

Participants in this study were motivated to accurately assess and interpret lower limb skin changes in people with dark skin tones. However, we found the environmental context best explained clinical behaviour. This not only influenced the organisational climate and resource availability but also influenced other aspects of the TDF such as skill development, focus and concentration, and belief about capabilities. Moreover, the process of assessing and interpreting lower limb skin changes (such as hemosiderin pigmentation or venous eczema) in people with dark skin tones was predominately attributed to experiential learning which relied on both peer‐ and self‐learning. In this motivated group of healthcare professionals, there remained areas where capability and opportunities could be improved specifically relating to the environmental and resource context. Modifying these components could facilitate changes in clinical behaviour [32] and remained crucial to accurately assess and interpret cutaneous manifestations of CVI and PAD by clinical staff.

Participants expressed their capability was limited by a lack of educational resources depicting dark skin tones. The insufficient clinical representation of people with dark skin tones for the skin changes which occur in chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is evident in both the leg ulcer [18] and vascular guidelines [6, 33] and across all vascular surgery educational resources [19]. These gaps were confirmed by healthcare professionals within this study who had an average experience of 15 years. This is consistent with a recent survey highlighting better visual aids and reference images were required [17]. However, findings of this study also established the impact this had on challenges to lower limb assessments, especially for less experienced healthcare professionals and when experienced clinicians reflected on their past experiences. This concern about poor representation is not isolated to skin presentation in vascular conditions and has been found across nurse education [34] and medical journals [35]. But the impact of a lack of representation is evident in this study, with healthcare professionals needing to self‐learn or rely on the experience of colleagues. Thus suggesting, representation of people with dark skin tones in health care literature would enable healthcare professionals to appropriately implement standard lower limb assessments on people with dark skin tones.

Participants within this current study highlighted the extensive clinical exposure needed to interpret physical assessments and confidently obtain an appropriate and detailed patient history. These skills acquired through clinical practice developed knowledge of the appearance of healthy dark skin across the lifespan and across the body. This enabled healthcare professionals to accurately interpret changes in colour, texture, thickness and hydration of the skin in people with dark skin tones. Though participants in this study felt this understanding positively impacted their capability, having knowledge of normal skin is not mentioned in recent international and national guidelines on skin assessment and wound care across skin tones [13, 14]. Moreover, recent research on skin tone bias in healthcare education only considers skin assessment when wounds are present [16, 36].

However, the importance of knowing the appearance of healthy dark skin is supported elsewhere, with healthcare professionals with dark skin tones perceiving they were more effective at assessing skin on patients with dark skin tones due to their familiarity with this skin type [15]. Findings of this study therefore have the potential to drive inclusive healthcare education which includes skin anatomy and physiology across diverse skin tones. However, knowledge of healthy dark skin alone may be insufficient, as people from Black ethnicity (who are likely to have experience of dark skin tones on themselves and their family members) have a similar reduction in confidence in assessing skin as it got darker to people from other ethnicities [37]. This highlights that additional factors also impact effectiveness and confidence in skin assessment.

Alongside undertaking a physical assessment of the skin, participants frequently discussed the importance of obtaining an accurate patient and symptom history. This supports strategies used elsewhere to interpret lower limb skin changes in people with dark skin tones [17], although this superficial evaluation failed to explore how healthcare professionals facilitate this. In the current study, participants felt creating a friendly environment that is conducive to discussing skin changes openly was essential. Building this connectedness at a personal level when working across skin tones may bring additional challenges but supports previous work on enabling culturally competent healthcare [38]. Participants in this study highlighted clinical experience working across culture groups or having a shared cultural background was advantageous, presenting the unique perspectives of experienced clinicians and clinicians with dark skin tones. These skills in components of cultural competence could however be taught, such as building these foundations of knowledge and awareness [38]. Thereby, building capability in obtaining an accurate lower limb assessment in people with dark skin tones regardless of face‐to‐face clinical experience with people with dark skin tones and CVI or PAD.

However, time pressures within healthcare systems may impede the opportunity needed to build relationships with patients. Feeling under time pressures significantly restricted doctors from asking patients questions about their symptoms and from completing a thorough clinical examination [39]. Nursing care is also missed when there are staff shortages and high nurse–patient ratios, with nurses reporting limited opportunity to interact with patients to build therapeutic relationships due to task focus/priority driven care [40]. Therefore, knowing a detailed patient history is needed may not be enough to improve clinical behaviour as the influence organisational structures and workload have on the ability of clinical staff to complete these tasks thoroughly. However, findings from this study indicate the need to allocate sufficient time in the initial assessments of the lower limb in people with dark skin tones to accommodate thorough patient interviews and detailed skin comparisons.

Findings within this study also highlight opportunities for individuals to improve their practice could be facilitated by peer learning and multi‐disciplinary team working. Often the clinical culture of the participants' teams enabled multi‐disciplinary and peer collaboration. However, some participants in this study reported this was limited due to either community areas covering a large geographic area or strict referral protocols limiting access to specialist services. This may prevent expertise being sought and disproportionately disadvantage people with dark skin tones where skin changes may be subtle or absent [41, 42] and obtaining accurate photographs of the skin may be challenging [13]. The strong relationships across multi‐disciplinary teams and between primary–secondary–tertiary care reported by many participants in this study emphasised the importance of organisational structures and multidisciplinary teamwork to improve quality of patient assessment and diagnosis. However, this organisational approach is considered uncommon, with a previous UK study citing poor communication between healthcare professionals negatively affecting patient care in lower limb assessment [7]. Nevertheless, this study suggests communication and collaborative learning across professional groups could enhance patient assessment and subsequently improve outcomes, warranting consideration as an organisational change in clinical practice.

The model of self‐ and peer‐learning within isolated groups is not without its limitations. This has led to variations in practice (Table 4). Often these are not mentioned in evidence‐based guidelines on skin assessments [14]. Some techniques may have developed due to ritualistic practice being distorted. However, as experienced healthcare professionals reported these were useful, these experientially developed techniques warrant further investigation. Therefore, further supporting the need for more evidence to underpin practice in the assessment of people with dark skin tones.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

The use of a validated framework such as the TDF to organise and categorise the barriers and facilitators ensured a robust, theoretical driven approach to analysis and synthesis. However, we did not provide participants a copy of the TDF. This was to prevent participants' comments being influenced by this framework, and instead structured questions around these domains.

The self‐selecting nature of this sample may not be representative of all those who assess people with CVI and/or PAD and dark skin tones. The majority of those who participated had extensive experience in skin assessment and generally belonged to a specialist group. This meant the experiences of those less experienced or without a specialist qualification were not captured. Furthermore, the sample comprised predominantly of participants from Black ethnic groups (58.3%), a proportion substantially higher than their 8% representation among clinically qualified NHS staff [43]. While this limits generalisability, the sample offers valuable insights and perspectives from individuals across light and dark skin tones, which are often underrepresented in research.

This study only collected minimal data on participant characteristics. Some people in the interviews spoke about periods of working overseas, and others had their initial training overseas before working in the UK. Participants also worked in a variety of NHS and private settings. These factors may influence the barriers and facilitators to skin assessment they experienced.

Data saturation appeared to be reached for nursing staff working in specialist roles; however, the absence of doctors and allied health care professionals with light skin tones from the sample may have limited the diversity of insights and experiences from these professional groups.

Recommendations for Further Research

Findings indicate participants were professionally motivated to complete an accurate skin assessment in people with dark skin tones, with several developing their own localised practice to improve their confidence in their assessment findings and diagnosis. However, the efficacy of these experientially developed techniques needs investigating and validating before its use can be advocated in clinical practice. There was also debate about whether standardised tools should be adapted to explicitly include the presentation on people with dark skin tones. This needs to be investigated further. Participants within this study have suggested both non‐specialist healthcare professionals and the patients themselves lack knowledge and skills in skin assessment for people with dark skin tones. Therefore, further research should consider the patient perspective and the experiences of non‐specialist healthcare professionals in skin assessment.

Implications for Policy and Practice

This study expressed the importance of a clinical culture that enables peer collaboration and multi‐disciplinary learning to strengthen assessment skills, interpretation of assessments, and healthcare professionals' confidence. Insights from the interviews also suggest developing an organisational culture that enables referral to specialist teams and mechanisms to lower thresholds for referrals for people with dark skin tones may be advantageous. However, embedding representation in education and local training (covering skin anatomy and physiology across diverse skin tones, clinical presentations in people with dark skin tones, and culturally competent training on patient and symptom history taking) is pivotal to accurately interpreting skin changes in people with dark skin tones and reducing diagnostic disparities.

Conclusion

Through the utilisation of the TDF this study identified several facilitators to undertaking accurate assessment of skin changes associated with CVI and PAD in people with dark skin tones. As part of this, it highlighted the importance of the environmental context and appropriate resources to enable this change. However, several challenges remain, specifically relating to discussing sensitive topics and using appropriate terminology. The validation of some assessment techniques used in practice also requires further investigation.
