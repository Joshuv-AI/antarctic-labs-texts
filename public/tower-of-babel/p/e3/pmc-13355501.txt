Association of an estimated glucose disposal rate-frailty index with incident cardiovascular events in two prospective cohorts
Authors: Yue Liu, Zhengbiao Luo, Zhixiang Xu, Jun Feng, Linlin Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance and frailty are linked and may increase cardiovascular vulnerability. We evaluated whether an aligned index integrating estimated insulin sensitivity and frailty (eGDRFI) predicts incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and improves risk stratification beyond conventional factors.MethodsTwo prospective population-based cohorts free of baseline heart disease or stroke were analyzed: CHARLS (China; n = 3859) and ELSA (England; n = 2486). Estimated glucose disposal rate was derived from waist circumference, hypertension, and HbA1c; frailty was measured using a deficit-accumulation frailty index. eGDRFI was prespecified as 100×FI/eGDR, with higher values indicating greater metabolic-frailty burden. Incident composite CVD comprised self-reported physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke. Cohort-specific Cox models, restricted cubic splines, subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and incremental prediction metrics were applied.ResultseGDRFI was right-skewed, with median values of 1.00 (IQR, 0.47–1.84) in CHARLS and 1.30 (0.62–2.59) in ELSA. In fully adjusted models, higher eGDRFI quartiles were progressively associated with composite CVD in CHARLS (Q4 vs Q1: HR 2.587, 95% CI 2.030–3.298) and ELSA (HR 1.881, 95% CI 1.398–2.529; both P trend<0.001); continuous associations were significant. Associations were consistent for heart disease in both cohorts and stroke in CHARLS, whereas ELSA stroke estimates were imprecise. Adding eGDRFI modestly improved AUCs and yielded significant NRI and IDI in both cohorts.ConclusionsHigher eGDRFI was associated with incident CVD and modestly improved prediction beyond conventional risk factors, supporting further evaluation of metabolic-frailty assessment as a cardiovascular risk marker.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be the leading cause of mortality worldwide, placing a significant burden on global healthcare systems [1,2]. The number of people living with CVD increased substantially, from 271 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019, while annual CVD-related deaths rose from 12.1 million to 18.6 million during the same period [3,4]. This growing burden is particularly evident in aging populations, where CVD is a major contributor to morbidity, disability, and rising healthcare costs. This highlights the importance of improving the early identification of individuals at higher cardiovascular risk [4].

Although advancements in prevention and treatment have been made, CVD remains primarily driven by modifiable cardiometabolic and behavioral factors. Key contributors include hypertension, diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [[5], [6], [7]], while unhealthy diet, smoking, physical inactivity, and obesity further exacerbate risk [8]. Insulin resistance plays a critical role in linking metabolic dysfunction to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events [9,10], yet traditional risk assessments may fail to fully reflect broader, age-related physiological vulnerabilities.

Frailty, which is characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased susceptibility to stress, is common in older adults [11,12]. The frailty index (FI) quantifies frailty by accumulating deficits across various domains [13]. Frailty has been associated with the incidence of CVD and cardiovascular mortality in community-based settings [14,15], and frailty status can change over time, with potential implications for future cardiovascular risk [16]. Notably, insulin resistance and frailty often coexist, potentially sharing common underlying pathways, such as chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired energy metabolism [[17], [18], [19]], suggesting an insulin resistance-frailty axis that may contribute to cardiovascular vulnerability in later life.

Estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) is a pragmatic surrogate of insulin sensitivity derived from routinely available clinical variables, including waist circumference, hypertension status, and HbA1c [20]. Lower eGDR has been associated with higher risks of stroke, coronary artery disease, and mortality [[21], [22], [23], [24]], whereas the FI captures multisystem vulnerability and biological aging through accumulated health deficits [13,25]. Because eGDR is inversely related to risk whereas FI is positively related to frailty burden, the composite index should be interpreted as a directionally aligned risk marker rather than as a directly measured pathophysiological construct.

We therefore constructed the estimated glucose disposal rate-frailty index (eGDRFI) as 100 × FI/eGDR, so that higher values indicate a greater frailty burden in the setting of lower estimated insulin sensitivity. We examined whether eGDRFI was associated with incident composite CVD and whether it added prognostic information beyond eGDR alone, FI alone, and conventional risk factors in two independent prospective ageing cohorts.

Methods

Study design and data sources

The CHARLS and ELSA are two large, ongoing, nationally representative cohorts of community-dwelling adults, with participants aged ≥45 years in CHARLS and ≥50 years in ELSA, conducted in China and the United Kingdom, respectively. Further details on the objectives, design, and methods of these cohorts can be found elsewhere [26,27]. The CHARLS received approval from Peking University's Institutional Review Board, while the ELSA study was approved by the Ethics Review Committees of London Multi-Centre Research Ethics. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in both cohorts, and this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [28]. For this study, baseline data were drawn from CHARLS wave 1, conducted in 2011, and ELSA wave 4, conducted between 2008 and 2009. Participants from each cohort were subsequently followed through three additional waves, culminating in CHARLS wave 4 (2018) and ELSA wave 7 (2014-2015).

The participant selection process, depicted in Fig. 1, involved two distinct cohorts. Initially, 17,139 individuals aged 45 years or older from the first wave of CHARLS and 11,050 individuals aged 50 years or older from the fourth wave of ELSA were included. After applying exclusion criteria, 13,280 participants from CHARLS were removed due to pre-existing cardiovascular disease, non-fasting status, absence of eGDR data, lack of FI data, or loss to follow-up. In a similar vein, 8564 participants were excluded from ELSA for reasons including incomplete nurse visits, pre-existing cardiovascular disease, missing eGDR data, missing FI data, or loss to follow-up. Ultimately, 3859 participants from CHARLS and 2486 participants from ELSA were retained for analysis and stratified into quartiles.Fig. 1Flowchart of the study population.

Flowchart of the study population.

Because CHARLS and ELSA differ substantially in ethnicity, socioeconomic context, healthcare system, baseline cardiometabolic profile, and survey procedures, all primary analyses were performed separately by cohort rather than by simple pooling. This strategy allowed us to assess consistency and transportability of the association across heterogeneous populations while avoiding inappropriate assumptions of a common baseline hazard, identical exposure distribution, and uniform covariate effects.

Calculation of eGDRFI

The eGDR was derived using the following equation: eGDR (mg/kg/min) = 21.158 - (0.09 × WC) - (3.407 × hypertension) - (0.551 × HbA1c), where WC is waist circumference in cm, hypertension is coded as 1 for yes and 0 for no, and HbA1c is expressed as %. In both cohorts, participants were asked to stand and waist circumference (WC) was measured at the navel using a tape measure [29,30]. HbA1c concentrations were measured from venous blood samples: CHARLS used boronate affinity liquid chromatography, whereas ELSA measurements were performed using the Tosoh G7 analyzer [31]. Hypertension was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension, current antihypertensive medication use, systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg.

The FI was constructed using a standardized deficit-accumulation approach covering major health domains. In this study, we constructed the FI following standard procedures described previously [32,33]. Based on the data of CHARLS and ELSA, we selected 32 items spanning comorbidities, physical function, disability, depressive symptoms, and cognition (item definitions are provided in Supplemental Table s1). The first 31 items were coded as binary deficits using established thresholds (0 = no deficit; 1 = deficit present). Cognitive ability (item 32) was retained as a continuous measure scaled from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating poorer cognition. For each participant, FI was calculated as the number of deficits divided by 32, yielding a score from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater frailty. To maximize data use while addressing missingness, we allowed up to 20% of items (≤6) to be missing, as commonly applied in prior studies [33,34]. Participants with >6 missing items were excluded from FI estimation for that wave. Otherwise, FI was computed as observed deficits divided by non-missing items (e.g., 9 deficits/30 items = 0.30, classified as frail).

Because lower eGDR indicates poorer insulin sensitivity whereas higher FI indicates greater frailty, the two components were aligned in the same risk direction by defining eGDRFI as 100 × FI/eGDR. The scaling factor of 100 was used only to improve numerical readability and did not change participant ranking. Higher eGDRFI values therefore indicate a greater frailty burden under lower insulin sensitivity.

Assessment of endpoint events

The primary outcome was incident composite CVD, defined as the first occurrence of heart disease or stroke during follow-up. Heart disease was identified by asking participants whether a physician had ever told them that they had a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems. Stroke was assessed by asking whether a physician had ever told them that they had had a stroke. For secondary endpoint-specific analyses, incident heart disease and incident stroke were evaluated separately using the same follow-up framework. Follow-up continued until the first recorded relevant event, death, loss to follow-up, or the censoring date, whichever occurred earliest. Because outcomes were based on self-reported physician diagnoses, potential recall bias and outcome misclassification were considered in the interpretation of findings. This CVD definition is consistent with prior CHARLS and ELSA studies [23,35].

Covariates

Baseline descriptive variables included age, sex, education, marital status, smoking and drinking status, BMI, lipids, Hs-CRP, grip strength, ADL/IADL limitations, hypertension, and diabetes. The primary fully adjusted Cox model included age, sex, education, smoking, drinking, BMI, grip strength, diabetes, TG, TC, and HDL-C.

To ensure comparability between CHARLS and ELSA, education was harmonized into three categories (below high school, high school, and college or above), and marital status was grouped as married/partnered versus other (separated, divorced, unmarried, or widowed). Smoking and drinking were each classified as never versus ever. BMI was computed as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Grip strength was defined as the mean of the maximum values recorded for each hand. Functional limitations based on Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) were coded as binary variables (1 = limitation; 0 = none). Diabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting blood glucose ≥7.00 mmol/L, or current anti-diabetic treatment.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were compared across eGDRFI quartiles using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Quantitative variables are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate, whereas qualitative variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages). These descriptive analyses were conducted independently for the CHARLS and ELSA cohorts using standardized statistical protocols. Missing data patterns for covariates were documented in Supplemental Fig s1 and s2, and missing values were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations [36].

Cumulative CVD risks across eGDRFI gradients were illustrated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, with differences assessed through log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for both continuous and quartile-based eGDRFI. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, education, smoking, and drinking. Model 3 additionally adjusted for BMI, grip strength, diabetes, TG, TC, and HDL-C. Covariate selection was guided by prior literature and collinearity diagnostics; variables with variance inflation factor (VIF) < 5 were retained (Supplementary Table s2) [37,38].

Nonlinear associations were evaluated using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with three knots, balancing flexibility and avoidance of overfitting [39,40]. Stratified subgroup analyses were conducted according to age, sex, education, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes, with interaction terms used to assess effect modification. Because hypertension is a structural component of eGDR, hypertension-stratified results were interpreted cautiously.

We compared a baseline multivariable-adjusted model with enhanced models incorporating eGDR, FI, and eGDRFI. Discrimination was assessed using ROC analysis and quantified by AUC (Supplemental Fig s4), and reclassification was evaluated using continuous NRI and IDI (Supplemental Table s3). To assess robustness, sensitivity analyses were conducted by 1) excluding participants who developed CVD within the first two years of follow-up; 2) additionally adjusting for antidiabetic medication use; 3) performing complete-case analyses; 4) separately assessing associations of eGDRFI with incident heart disease and incident stroke using Cox proportional hazards models; and 5) calculating E-values based on estimates from the fully adjusted models (Supplemental Fig s5). Analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.5.1), with two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Across both cohorts, participants in higher eGDRFI quartiles were generally older, more likely to be female, and had lower educational attainment. Higher quartiles also showed a less favorable cardiometabolic and functional profile, including higher waist circumference, BMI, systolic blood pressure, prevalences of hypertension and diabetes, and use of related medications. Grip strength was lower, and ADL and IADL limitations were more common in higher eGDRFI quartiles. Higher quartiles were further associated with higher fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, and Hs-CRP, as well as lower HDL-C.

These baseline patterns suggest that a higher eGDRFI quartile identifies a profile in which metabolic dysfunction and frailty-related functional deficits coexist, rather than representing either construct alone.

Predictive performance of eGDRFI for CVD risk

The predictive performance of different indices for CVD risk was assessed via ROC curve analysis (Supplemental Fig s4). In both cohorts, eGDRFI yielded numerically higher AUCs (0.646 in CHARLS; 0.618 in ELSA) than the directionally inverted eGDR index (-eGDR; AUC 0.612 and 0.600) and FI alone (AUC 0.627 and 0.598). Incremental predictive analyses (Supplemental Table s3) showed that adding eGDRFI to a model adjusted for conventional risk factors modestly improved prediction. In CHARLS, AUC increased from 0.589 to 0.646 (P < 0.001), with an NRI of 0.219 (95% CI 0.143–0.295; P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.0068 (95% CI 0.0045–0.0092; P < 0.001). In ELSA, AUC increased from 0.591 to 0.618 (P < 0.001), with an NRI of 0.124 (95% CI 0.031–0.217; P = 0.009) and an IDI of 0.0043 (95% CI 0.0019–0.0067; P < 0.001). These results indicate incremental predictive information, although absolute discrimination remained modest.

This association remained significant across most predefined subgroups, except among participants with college education or above (HR 1.33, 95% CI 0.85–2.08; P = 0.217). In ELSA, higher eGDRFI was also associated with increased CVD risk in the overall population (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03–1.10; P < 0.001). The association was generally consistent across subgroups but did not reach statistical significance among participants with high school education (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.96–1.12; P = 0.316) or never drinkers (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.96–1.18; P = 0.216). Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were generally consistent with the primary findings. Similar associations persisted after excluding participants who developed CVD within the first two years of follow-up (Supplemental Table s4), after additional adjustment for antidiabetic medication use (Supplemental Table s5), and in complete-case analyses excluding participants with missing covariate data (Supplemental Table s6). E-values for the Q4 versus Q1 estimates were 4.61 in CHARLS and 3.17 in ELSA (Supplemental Fig s5), indicating that unmeasured confounding would need to be moderately strong to fully explain the observed associations; nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

In endpoint-specific analyses, eGDRFI was associated with incident heart disease in both cohorts (Supplemental Table s7). In the fully adjusted model, the HRs for Q4 versus Q1 were 2.467 (95% CI 1.886–3.227; P < 0.001) in CHARLS and 1.806 (95% CI 1.207–2.705; P = 0.004) in ELSA. For incident stroke (Supplemental Table s8), associations were significant in CHARLS (Q4 vs Q1: HR 2.404, 95% CI 1.629–3.547; P < 0.001; continuous eGDRFI: HR 1.320, 95% CI 1.169–1.491; P < 0.001). In ELSA, however, stroke-specific estimates were imprecise and not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (Q4 vs Q1: HR 1.371, 95% CI 0.546–3.440; P = 0.502; continuous eGDRFI: HR 1.105, 95% CI 0.752–1.623; P = 0.611), likely in part because of the smaller number of stroke events.

Discussion

In these two prospective aging cohorts, higher eGDRFI, defined as 100 × FI/eGDR, was associated with a higher risk of incident composite CVD. The association was graded and stronger in CHARLS, whereas in ELSA the magnitude was smaller but directionally consistent, with excess risk mainly observed in the higher eGDRFI quartiles. RCS analyses suggested nonlinear associations in both cohorts, although the curvature was attenuated in ELSA after full adjustment. Endpoint-specific analyses supported an association with incident heart disease in both cohorts and with incident stroke in CHARLS; in ELSA, stroke-specific estimates were imprecise and not statistically significant. eGDRFI also provided modest incremental predictive information beyond conventional cardiovascular risk factors and yielded numerically higher AUCs than eGDR or FI alone, although absolute discrimination remained limited. Overall, these findings suggest that eGDRFI may serve as a risk marker reflecting integrated metabolic-frailty burden in middle-aged and older adults rather than as a stand-alone prediction tool.

The rationale for this composite index is clinically plausible. Frailty reflects the accumulation of multisystem health deficits and reduced physiological reserve [11,12], and the deficit-accumulation frailty index provides a standardized approach for quantifying this vulnerability [41]. Lower eGDR reflects lower estimated insulin sensitivity and has been associated with incident CVD in population-based cohorts [23]. By aligning FI and eGDR in the same risk direction, eGDRFI combines frailty-related functional vulnerability with metabolic dysregulation. In the present cohorts, higher eGDRFI quartiles were characterized by higher waist circumference, BMI, systolic blood pressure, hypertension, and diabetes, together with weaker grip strength and more ADL/IADL limitations. Therefore, eGDRFI should be interpreted as an integrated metabolic-frailty burden measure rather than as a specific marker of adiposity, glycemia, hypertension, or frailty alone.

Several biological pathways may underlie the observed associations, but causality cannot be inferred from these observational data. Insulin resistance has been linked to endothelial dysfunction, dyslipidemia, vascular inflammation, oxidative stress, and atherogenesis [17,23]. Frailty is characterized by impaired stress resilience, sarcopenia, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, and reduced functional reserve [18,42]. These processes may converge through shared pathways, including inflammaging, skeletal muscle catabolism, endothelial injury, and adverse cardiometabolic remodeling. The elevated CVD risk associated with eGDRFI may therefore represent the combined burden of metabolic dysfunction and multisystem physiological vulnerability rather than either component alone.

The present findings are consistent with and extend previous evidence. A nationwide prospective study among individuals without diabetes reported that higher eGDR was associated with lower incident CVD risk [23]. In two prospective cohorts, higher eGDR was also associated with lower baseline FI and slower frailty progression, supporting a longitudinal link between insulin sensitivity and frailty accumulation [43]. Evidence from CHARLS has further shown that pre-frailty and frailty are associated with incident CVD [44]. A CHARLS-based study also reported independent and joint associations of frailty and the triglyceride-glucose index with incident CVD [45]. Compared with these studies, the present analysis integrates an insulin-sensitivity surrogate and deficit-accumulation frailty into a directionally aligned index and evaluates its association in two heterogeneous aging cohorts.

Between-cohort and endpoint-specific differences should be interpreted cautiously. The stronger association and more pronounced nonlinearity in CHARLS than in ELSA after full adjustment may reflect differences in baseline frailty burden, cardiometabolic profile, socioeconomic context, healthcare access, follow-up structure, and outcome ascertainment. Measurement error and residual confounding may also contribute. The absence of a statistically significant stroke association in ELSA should not be interpreted as evidence of no relationship, because only 64 stroke events were recorded and confidence intervals were wide. Subgroup analyses showed broadly consistent associations across most demographic and clinical strata. Effect modification was observed by age in CHARLS and by hypertension status in both cohorts; however, hypertension-stratified findings are exploratory because hypertension is incorporated into the eGDR formula. Validation using alternative insulin-resistance indices or eGDR formulations that do not include hypertension would be useful.

These findings also align with the broader emphasis on individualized risk assessment in older adults, in whom multimorbidity, frailty, and clinical heterogeneity may require more refined risk stratification than general-purpose models. Recent studies have shown the value of elderly- or setting-specific scoring systems for predicting myocardial injury after non-elective surgery [46] and mortality after implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation in older patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [47]. Although these studies differ from the present community-based cohort design, they support the concept that metabolic, functional, and comorbidity-related information should be integrated when refining cardiovascular risk assessment in older adults.

The predictive analyses have potential practical implications, but they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. eGDRFI achieved numerically higher AUCs than eGDR or FI alone and improved reclassification metrics when added to conventional risk factors. These results support further evaluation of integrating metabolic and frailty information for cardiovascular risk stratification. Nevertheless, the absolute AUCs were modest, calibration was not assessed, and decision-curve analysis was not performed. Accordingly, eGDRFI should not be considered a stand-alone prediction tool. Future work should assess calibration, time-dependent discrimination, clinical utility, and external validation before considering clinical implementation [39].

Dynamic frailty evidence further highlights the potential value of longitudinal assessment. In three prospective cohorts, frailty progression was associated with increased incident CVD risk, whereas frailty recovery was associated with lower risk [16]. Similarly, frailty progression in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome populations was associated with higher future CVD risk [48]. These findings suggest that repeated eGDRFI assessment may be worth evaluating, but the added prognostic value of trajectories beyond a single baseline measurement remains unknown. Future studies should investigate eGDRFI trajectories, clarify inflammatory and metabolic pathways, and determine whether interventions that improve insulin sensitivity or preserve physical function are associated with lower cardiovascular event rates.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, eGDR is an indirect surrogate of insulin resistance derived from waist circumference, hypertension status, and HbA1c; it is not a clamp-based measure, and residual measurement error is possible [23]. Second, the FI was constructed from variables available in CHARLS and ELSA and may not capture all relevant frailty domains; dynamic changes in frailty over time were not incorporated. Third, incident CVD was based on self-reported physician diagnoses, and the heart disease component included heterogeneous conditions such as myocardial infarction, angina, congestive heart failure, and other heart problems. Although endpoint-specific analyses were performed, these outcomes were still self-reported, and the ELSA stroke analysis was limited by a small number of events. Future studies with adjudicated cardiovascular endpoints are needed. Fourth, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Variables such as diet, physical activity, medication adherence, and socioeconomic factors were not comprehensively measured and may influence both insulin sensitivity and frailty. Fifth, the generalizability of our findings to populations outside China and the UK is uncertain. Participants in CHARLS and ELSA were middle-aged or older adults; thus, extrapolation to younger individuals requires caution. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference regarding whether improving insulin sensitivity or reducing frailty would lower cardiovascular risk.

Conclusion

In two prospective cohorts of middle-aged and older adults, higher eGDRFI was associated with a higher risk of incident composite CVD and provided modest incremental predictive information beyond conventional risk factors. Endpoint-specific analyses suggested more consistent associations with heart disease than with stroke in ELSA, where stroke events were few and estimates were imprecise. These findings support further evaluation of joint metabolic-frailty assessment as a potential cardiovascular risk marker, while emphasizing the need for calibration assessment, adjudicated endpoint-specific validation, external validation, and longitudinal evaluation before clinical application.
