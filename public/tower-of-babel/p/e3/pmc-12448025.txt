Improving fermentation quality of Sichuan radish paocai through co-inoculation of homo- and heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria
Authors: Yiwen Fan, Quanyou Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effects of lactic acid bacteria, including Lactobacillus brevis (B), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (P), and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (R), on the fermentation and quality of Sichuan radish paocai (SRP) through both single and mixed inoculations. The mixed culture PRB (P + R + B) achieved rapid acidification and balanced homo-heterofermentative metabolism. It maintained high LAB viability and modulated yeast populations, potentially enhancing flavor complexity and microbial stability. PRB fermentation resulted in improved lightness, texture, and umami-related amino acids, and a volatile profile resembling that of naturally fermented SRP. Despite higher lactic acid levels, PRB yielded a harmonious flavor compared to the more sour B and PB groups, possibly due to a more balanced acid profile. Nitrite reduction (67%) was also observed. This study demonstrates that PRB co-inoculation optimizes SRP production by integrating efficient fermentation, enhanced sensory quality, and microbial safety providing a practical strategy for standardized, high-quality paocai manufacturing.Keywords: Radish paocai, Homofermentative and heterofermentative fermentation, Mixed-culture fermentation, Flavor profile, Paocai quality, Fermentation metabolites

Introduction

Sichuan paocai is a traditional Chinese fermented vegetable known for its unique flavor and rich nutrition, traditionally made by spontaneous fermentation or using aged brine (Qiu et al., 2023; Zhao, Wu, Miyao, & Zhang, 2022). Common ingredients include radish, mustard tuber, and long beans, combined with spices such as Sichuan pepper and chili. Although spontaneous fermentation often yields richer flavor (Xing et al., 2023), it is influenced by raw material variability, environmental factors, and fermentation conditions. This results in uncontrollable microbial communities, long fermentation cycles, and inconsistent product quality (Tomita, Watanabe, Kuribayashi, Tanaka, & Kawahara, 2021; Liao, Luo, Asif, Luo, & Xia, 2024; Zheng et al., 2023; Song et al., 2021). Industrial production also faces challenges like low productivity, labor intensity, and food safety risks (Xing et al., 2023), driving interest in controlled fermentation using microbial starters.

LAB starter cultures help accelerate fermentation and reduce risk (Jung et al., 2022). While single LAB strains like L. plantarum and L. brevis have been applied (Tomita et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023), they show limited metabolic activity and flavor development, often producing excessive acidity and lacking complexity (Lee et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2020). These strains generally fail to replicate the dynamic microbial and physicochemical characteristics of traditional fermentation (Liao et al., 2024).

Flavor formation in fermented foods is closely linked to microbial community composition (Song et al., 2021). Homofermentative LAB mainly produce lactic acid via glycolysis, enhancing safety, while heterofermentative LAB produce more diverse metabolites through the pentose phosphate pathway, enriching flavor (Liu et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2020). Combining these strains in mixed-culture fermentation can enhance interspecies synergy, mimic natural fermentation ecology, and improve product quality (Luo et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021). Mixed cultures also enhance microbial diversity and metabolic output, support stable fermentation, and promote flavor complexity and safety (Zheng et al., 2023; Lan et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2020).

Mixed-starter cultures comprising multiple LAB strains have shown potential in paocai fermentation, enabling lower salt use and faster processing, while enhancing sensory quality (Luo et al., 2020). Compared to spontaneous fermentation, these cultures increased lactic acid, mannitol, and umami- and sweet-related amino acids, promoted early acid production and LAB growth, and maintained lower final acidity—favorable for flavor balance (Choi, 2023). They also improved nitrite degradation and early-stage safety. Specific strains such as Weissella paramesenteroides and Lactobacillus brevis thrived throughout fermentation, while Leuconostoc mesenteroides was active early, contributing to flavor formation (Luo et al., 2020). Similarly, co-inoculation of L. plantarum and P. pentosaceus in Suancai boosted acidity, reduced nitrite, and significantly modified volatile compounds, amino acids, and organic acids. PCA and cluster analyses showed these samples closely matched traditional Suancai in composition and flavor, with higher bacterial diversity than single-strain fermentations. L. plantarum remained dominant in all groups, supporting improved sensory resemblance to spontaneous Suancai (Song et al., 2021).

However, few studies have applied this mixed-culture approach to Sichuan radish paocai, which differs in substrate composition, microbial ecology, and processing conditions. Our previous studies successfully isolated several dominant LAB from traditionally fermented Sichuan paocai, including Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (homofermentative), and Lactobacillus brevis (heterofermentative) (Akarca, Kilinç, & Denizkara, 2024; Basso et al., 2014; Fan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022). These strains showed strong adaptability and desirable fermentation characteristics in preliminary trials. This study aims to evaluate the fermentation performance and flavor profiles of single versus mixed LAB cultures in Sichuan radish paocai. By analyzing microbial succession, physicochemical parameters, and metabolite changes, we hope to elucidate LAB interaction mechanisms and provide a theoretical basis for developing safe, efficient, and flavorful fermentation systems.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Xinlimei radish (Raphanus sativus L., red flesh with green pericarp) and other ingredients, including salt, white sugar, garlic (Allium sativum L.), ginger (Zingiber officinale), chili pepper (Capsicum frutescens L.), anise (Pimpinella anisum), myrcia (Myrcia glomerata), cassia (Cinnamomum cassia), and Chinese prickly ash (Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim), were purchased from local supermarkets in Shanghai (Table B).

Standard glucose, organic acids, and amino acids were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). The sodium nitrite standard was sourced from Tan-Mo Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Other chemical reagents, including phenol, sulfuric acid, methanol (chromatographic purity), acetonitrile (chromatographic purity), potassium dihydrogen phosphate, sodium chloride, and other laboratory-grade chemicals, were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China).

Fermentation agent cultivation and paocai preparation

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, and Lactobacillus brevis, previously identified in our laboratory, were activated in MRS medium at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacterial cells were then centrifuged at 3024g (5,000 rpm, Beckman Avanti J-301, JA-30.50 rotor, USA) for 10 min at 4 °C, washed, and resuspended in 0.85% (w/v) saline to a final concentration of 109 CFU/mL as LAB stater.

Sterilized fermentation jars (500 mL) were filled with 250 g of radish cubes (1.5 cm), 10 g garlic, 10 g ginger, 7.5 g chili, 0.5 g prickly ash, 1 g each of anise and cassia, 0.5 g myrcia, and 200 mL cooled boiled water containing 5% salt and 1% sugar. Inoculation groups included single-strain (B), double-strain (PB, 1:1), and triple-strain (PRB, 1:1:1), and 1 mL of LAB stater was added per jar. For PB and PRB, equal volumes of each 109 CFU/mL suspension were mixed. This ensured accurate ratios and consistent inoculum levels (Fig. B). The inoculation concentration was based on previous studies (Jung et al., 2022; Song et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). A control group (CK) underwent spontaneous fermentation. All samples were incubated at 25 °C for 7 days in a CO₂ incubator to ensure stable temperature and anaerobic conditions. Fermentations were conducted in sealed jars with one-way airlocks (see Appendix Fig. B). Daily sampling was performed aseptically at a fixed time. Samples were homogenized with sterile water (1:1, w/v) using a household blender for 1 min (two 30-s pulses) to achieve uniform mixing. Then centrifuged at 17,418g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants filtered for further analysis.

Physicochemical measurements

The pH of the samples was measured using a pH meter (PHSJ-3F, Leici, Shanghai, China) on the filtered supernatants obtained as described in Section 2.2. Total sugar content was determined using the phenol‑sulfuric acid method: 1 g of sample was mixed with 5 mL of water and heated in a boiling water bath for 1 h. The supernatant was then collected and diluted to 25 mL. Subsequently, 1 mL of the diluted solution was mixed with 1 mL of phenol solution and 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid. After standing for 10 min, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm. Total sugar content was quantified using a glucose standard calibration curve. Total titratable acidity (TTA), reducing sugar, and nitrite levels were measured according to the National Standards of the People's Republic of China: GB 5009.239–2016, GB 5009.7–2016, and GB 5009.33–2016, respectively.

Microbial analysis

Viable cell count analysis

Homogenized paocai samples were serially diluted in 0.85% (w/v) physiological saline. One milliliter from each dilution was plated onto MRS agar medium and Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium using the pour plate method. MRS agar medium was used to enumerate LAB, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. PDA agar medium was used for the enumeration of yeasts and molds, with incubation times of 48 h and 96 h, respectively.

Carbon source utilization of LAB strains

The carbon source utilization profile of LAB strains was assessed using the PM 1–2 Microplates (Biolog, Hayward, California, USA) and the GEN III MicroPlate (Biolog, Hayward, California, USA). The PM 1–2 Microplates contain 71 pre-filled and dried carbon sources in each well. Changes in color, resulting from the reduction of oxygen-sensitive dyes, indicated the microbial utilization of the carbon sources. LAB strains were first activated on BUG agar plates (Biolog, Hayward, California, USA) using a standard streaking method. The bacterial cultures were then transferred to Inoculating Fluid-C using sterile disposable cotton swabs. The turbidity of the cell suspension was adjusted to 90–98% transmittance using a turbidimeter (Model No. 3532–220V, Biolog, Hayward, California, USA). One hundred microliters of the prepared cell suspension were added to each well of the microplate, which was incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. Results were recorded at intervals of 2 h in the early stage, 4 h in the middle stage, and 8 h in the late stage of the incubation period.

Metabolite analysis

Organic acid measurement

Organic acids were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with slight modifications based on previous studies (Zhang et al., 2023). The HPLC system (Agilent 1260, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was equipped with an Agilent SB-Aq column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm). The organic phase consisted of methanol, while the mobile phase was a mixture of acetonitrile and sodium dihydrogen phosphate solution (10 mL acetonitrile and 3.11 g potassium dihydrogen phosphate dissolved in 1 L of ultrapure water). The chromatographic conditions and detection protocols followed previous research (Fan et al., 2024) for organic acid quantification. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Free amino acid measurement

Free amino acids (FAAs) were quantified using an automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi, la8080, Tokyo, Japan), equipped with a sulfonic acid cation exchange column (4.6 mm × 60 mm, 3 μm). The analysis was performed using the external standard method, with a reaction temperature of 135 °C, a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, and detection wavelengths of 570 nm and 440 nm. The method followed the approach described by Zhao et al. (2023). Taste activity values (TAVs) for FAAs were calculated based on the taste threshold values (TTV) provided in the literature (Teranishi, Buttery, & Shahidi, 1989), using the formula TTV = C/TTV (C represents the actual concentration of free amino acids; TTV is the corresponding taste threshold). The calculation method was adapted from previous studies (Zhang et al., 2023).

Volatile flavor compound measurement

Volatile flavor compounds (VOCs) were analyzed using a headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) system (GAS, Flavoursec, Italy). A 1 g sample was added to a 20 mL headspace glass vial, which was incubated at 40 °C with a rotation speed of 500 rpm for 15 min. Headspace sampling was performed by injecting 500 μL of the headspace into the system, with the syringe maintained at 65 °C in splitless mode. The analysis was conducted using a strong polar chromatographic column (MXT-WAX, 30 m × 0.53 mm, 1 μm), with the column temperature set to 60 °C and nitrogen (≥99.999%) as the carrier gas. The carrier gas flow rate was programmed as follows: initial flow rate of 2 mL/min (held for 2 min), increased to 10 mL/min over 8 min, then further increased to 100 mL/min over the next 10 min, and held for an additional 10 min. The total run time was 30 min, with the transfer tube temperature set to 45 °C, and the analysis duration was 30 min.

Sensory characteristics of paocai

Color measurement of paocai

Color parameters (L*, a*, b*) of the paocai samples were measured using a chromameter (Chroma Meter, CR-400, Osaka, Japan), with each sample tested in triplicate. The total color difference (ΔE), chroma (C*), and hue angle (h) were calculated based on the following (1), (2), (3), respectively, which represent the degree of color difference, color saturation (or purity), and the angle of the color relative to red.

Texture measurement of paocai

The texture profile of the paocai samples, including hardness, springiness, chewiness, and crispness, was measured using a texture analyzer (FTC, TMS-Touch, Franklin, Massachusetts, USA). Each sample was tested in triplicate. Hardness, elasticity, and chewiness were measured using the TA25 probe and TPA program, with a test speed of 50 mm/min and a compression ratio of 50%. Crispness was measured using the T44 probe with a puncture test, under the following parameters: test speed of 50 mm/min, return speed of 50 mm/min, and a puncture depth of 5 mm.

Taste profile of paocai

The taste profile of the paocai samples was measured using an electronic tongue system (Insent, SA-402B, Tokyo, Japan). This system is equipped with six sensors: AAE (umami and richness), CT0 (saltiness), CA0 (sourness), C00 (bitterness and aftertaste-B), AE1 (astringency and aftertaste-B), and GL1 (sweetness). The samples were prepared by diluting the supernatant from centrifuged samples with ultrapure water in a 1:1 ratio.

Data analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's post hoc test was performed using SPSS 25.0 software, with P-values <0.05 considered statistically significant. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis were conducted using SIMCA 14.1 software. Heatmaps and clustering analysis were performed using Origin 2021 software.

Results and discussion

Changes in the physicochemical properties during fermentation

pH and TTA

pH and TTA are key indicators for evaluating fermentation progression (Song et al., 2021). A pH of 4.0, optimal for paocai flavor (Lee et al., 2020). was used as the maturity benchmark. As shown in Fig. 1A, pH in all inoculated groups rapidly dropped to ∼4.5 by day 1, with groups B and PB showing the fastest decline. By day 2, their pH fell below 4.0, while CK reached 4.01 on day 3. The PRB group declined more gradually, likely due to strain interaction dynamics. Overall, inoculated groups maintained lower pH than CK, indicating enhanced acidification by LAB.TTA steadily increased during fermentation (Fig. 1B). CK showed low early TTA, likely due to weak initial LAB activity, but increased later, possibly from natural microbial synergy. The PRB group followed a similar trend, suggesting strain-specific effects on acid accumulation. Notably, B group showed the highest early TTA, reflecting strong acid production (Song et al., 2021).

Changes in physicochemical properties during the fermentation of paocai. (A) pH; (B) TTA; (C) Total sugar content; (D) Glucose content; (E) Nitrite content. Abbreviations: CK, spontaneous fermentation (no inoculation); B, singly inoculated with L. brevis; PB, co-inoculated with L. plantarum + L. brevis at a 1:1 ratio; PRB, co-inoculated with L. plantarum + L. rhamnosus + L. brevis at a 1:1:1 ratio.

Sugars

During paocai fermentation, both total sugars and reducing sugars (expressed as glucose) generally decreased (Fig. 1C and Fig. 1D), consistent with LAB metabolizing sugars as carbon sources to produce lactic acid and other metabolites (Jung et al., 2022). However, a sharp increase in glucose content was observed in all groups on days 1 and 2, likely due to sugar release from plant tissues and sucrose hydrolysis in the low-pH environment (Lee et al., 2020). In contrast, the marked increase in total sugar in B on day 1 may be attributed to acid-induced hydrolysis of plant polysaccharides, facilitated by the strain's relatively weak sugar metabolism. Acidification has been reported to disrupt calcium crosslinking in the plant cell wall and enhance polysaccharide hydration, thereby promoting cell wall loosening and facilitating sugar release (Phyo, Gu, & Hong, 2019). B reached its glucose peak on day 1, while PRB and CK peaked on day 2. This variation may be attributed to differences in acidification rates and TTA accumulation, which influence enzymatic activity and sugar availability. By the end of fermentation, the reducing sugar content in B was the lowest (3.32 mg/mL), indicating more rapid glucose consumption. This suggests that the B strain primarily utilized glucose as its main carbon source, with fewer alternative carbon sources available for metabolism. This limited substrate diversity may have led to lower sources levels, influenced fermentation dynamics, and ultimately affected product quality.

Nitrite

Nitrate, commonly present in vegetables, serves as a precursor to nitrite during fermentation and poses food safety concerns (Huang et al., 2021). In this study, all LAB-inoculated groups maintained lower nitrite levels than the control (CK), with significantly reduced peak values (Fig. 1E). This reduction is likely attributed to the early production of organic acids by LAB, which suppresses the growth of nitrite-producing bacteria and modulates the expression of nitrogen metabolism-related genes (Hang et al., 2022; Hou et al., 2023; Lin, Bakyrbay, Liu, Tang, & Liu, 2023). Specifically, the CK group reached a peak nitrite concentration of 8.39 mg/kg on day 3, whereas inoculated groups peaked earlier on day 2, with a maximum of 4.29 mg/kg. As fermentation progressed and pH dropped below 4.0, nitrite levels declined in all groups, with a more rapid degradation observed in LAB-inoculated samples. Notably, although the PRB group exhibited relatively high nitrite content at the early stage, it achieved more efficient degradation during the later phase, with nitrite reduction rates of 60% in PB and 67% in PRB, consistent with previous reports (Luo et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021). The enhanced nitrite degradation observed in the co-fermentation groups may be attributed to synergistic interactions between homofermentative and heterofermentative LAB. Homofermentative strains rapidly reduce pH through lactic acid production, inhibiting nitrite-producing bacteria and accelerating acid-driven chemical degradation. Meanwhile, heterofermentative strains produce a wider array of metabolites, including acetic acid, CO₂, and ethanol, which further inhibit undesirable microbes and support nitrogen metabolism regulation. These complementary metabolic activities help maintain microbial diversity and stability, optimizing the fermentation microenvironment. Therefore, LAB inoculation, particularly mixed-strain (co-fermentation), enhances the suppression and degradation of nitrite, thereby improving both the safety and overall quality of paocai.

Microbial counts and changes in available energy

Changes in viable microorganisms during paocai fermentation

Freshly harvested vegetables naturally harbor a high microbial load, with yeasts and molds dominating, while LAB are initially less prevalent (Hang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 2A-C, the initial viable counts of yeasts and LAB in the CK group were approximately 4.5 log CFU/g, and molds around 2.0 log CFU/g. In contrast, the LAB-inoculated groups exhibited LAB counts exceeding 6.5 log CFU/g at day 0, allowing them to establish dominance early in fermentation. This early dominance facilitated rapid sugar metabolism and acid production, lowering the pH and thereby inhibiting spoilage organisms and reducing nitrite formation. LAB counts increased sharply during the first three days, followed by stabilization or slight decline, but remained higher in the inoculated groups throughout the process compared to CK, aligning with previous studies (Luo et al., 2020). The absence of significant differences in LAB counts among B, PB, and PRB groups suggests that mixed-strain inoculation did not compromise LAB viability.

Yeasts were detectable throughout fermentation but acid-sensitive strains declined rapidly during early acidification. As fermentation progressed and acid-tolerant yeasts became dominant, their counts rose, particularly in PRB, which had the highest final yeast counts. This suggests that co-inoculation may enhance microbial diversity and support yeast survival, potentially improving flavor development (Song et al., 2021). Although lactic acid generally inhibits yeast growth (Basso et al., 2014), yeasts in turn may help regulate pH by metabolizing organic acids, particularly in mixed-culture environments (Khan et al., 2020). The observed fluctuation in yeast counts corresponded closely to the pH profiles across treatments, reflecting dynamic microbial interactions. In contrast, molds were only observed early in fermentation, likely suppressed by increasing acidity and competition, indicating effective control by LAB. Yeasts also contribute to flavor through production of alcohols, lipids, and aromatic compounds (Wang et al., 2022), suggesting the potential practical advantages of co-fermentation over single-strain fermentation in improving sensory quality.

Carbon source utilization by LAB

The carbon source utilization of the three LAB strains is presented in Fig. 2D. Strains B, P, and R utilized 17, 40, and 46 carbon sources, respectively, each with its own substrate preferences (3, 1, and 7 specific to B, P, and R, respectively). PCA analysis (Fig. 2E) showed clear differences in utilization patterns between heterofermentative strain B and homofermentative strains P and R. Co-fermentation in PB and PRB significantly expanded substrate usage, reaching 40 and 46 types (Fig. 2F and G), thereby enhancing fermentation efficiency. Among the 46 carbon sources utilized by the PRB group were 6 monosaccharides, 7 disaccharides, 1 oligosaccharide, 3 polysaccharides, 9 organic acids, 5 alcohols, 2 glycosides, 3 amino sugars, and 11 others, reflecting broad metabolic versatility. This diversity likely supports improved microbial synergy, with co-cultures re-utilizing metabolic by-products and enhancing both growth and flavor compound formation. These findings align with previous studies showing that single-strain fermentations may suffer from limited substrate scope, leading to over-acidification and weaker flavor profiles (Lee et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021). In contrast, mixed-culture fermentation can optimize flavor complexity and overall product quality in paocai.

Paocai metabolite analysis

Organic acids

Organic acids produced by LAB during fermentation contribute diverse flavor notes such as sourness, astringency, citrus-like, mild, vinegar-like, and bitterness (Chen et al., 2019). They also inhibit non-dominant microbes and enhance paocai's overall flavor complexity (Zheng, Zhou, et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2020). Fig. 3A, A1, and A4 show the organic acid profiles during fermentation, with acid levels rising sharply to peak at day 1 before stabilizing with slight fluctuations. The dominant acids shifted from oxalic, citric, and succinic acids initially to acetic, lactic, malic, and tartaric acids later. Both PRB and CK groups showed similar metabolic patterns, likely due to their higher microbial diversity (Fig. 3F).

Abbreviations: CK, spontaneous fermentation (no inoculation); B, singly inoculated with L. brevis; PB, co-inoculated with L. plantarum + L. brevis at a 1:1 ratio; PRB, co-inoculated with L. plantarum + L. rhamnosus + L. brevis at a 1:1:1 ratio.

Oxalic acid, abundant in radishes (Zheng, Wei, et al., 2023), initially accounted for over 64% of total acids (4.52 mg/mL). It first increased, then declined, likely influenced by pH changes. At acidic pH (<6), oxalate ions fully deprotonate, increasing soluble oxalate and oxalic acid concentration. Later, LAB metabolized oxalic acid, lowering its levels. By fermentation's end, PRB and CK groups had the lowest oxalic acid (3.61 and 3.50 mg/mL), while PB and B groups retained higher amounts (4.11 and 5.27 mg/mL), suggesting that complex microbial communities enhance oxalic acid metabolism. Since high oxalic acid intake risks kidney health, its reduction improves paocai safety (Huynh, Nguyen, & Nguyen, 2022). Thus, co-fermentation not only reduces radish sourness but also enhances food safety.

Lactic acid, the main LAB fermentation product (Chen et al., 2019), started low (0.57 mg/mL) but rose sharply after day 1 and dominated late fermentation, exceeding 30% of total acids. The CK group, with initially fewer LAB, showed lower lactic acid early on. Lactic acid contributes mild sourness and synergizes with other flavors (Zheng, Zhou, et al., 2023), so appropriate levels enrich paocai's taste. Acetic acid, a key LAB carbon source, gradually declined. Lactobacillus brevis (B group) being homofermentative produced both lactic and acetic acids, resulting in higher acetic acid. Conversely, PRB group converts acetic acid into other products, lowering its concentration (Fig. 2G).

Malic acid, comprising 10–20% of total acids in radish paocai fermentation, adds fruity sourness and participates in lactic acid synthesis and energy metabolism (Zhao et al., 2023). It continuously increased, peaking at 2.47 mg/mL in the PRB group on day 7, significantly higher than CK and other inoculated groups. Citric, succinic, and tartaric acids remained low throughout. Tartaric acid was undetectable pre-fermentation but appeared on day 3, with fluctuating levels likely due to microbial consumption (Zhao et al., 2023). Higher tartaric acid in PRB and CK groups on day 7 might relate to elevated yeast activity.

FAAs

FAAs are vital to fermented food flavor, acting as precursors for flavor compounds through transamination and degradation. Seventeen FAAs were detected in paocai (Fig. 3C-E, A3) and categorized as umami, sweet, and bitter based on flavor (Luo et al., 2020). At fermentation's end, umami FAAs accounted for ∼40%, sweet ∼35%, and bitter ∼25%. Despite high bitter FAA levels, their taste thresholds (TTV) were below sensory significance. Glutamate (Glu) combined with nucleotides enhances umami, while high salt and acidity reduce bitterness, improving sensory quality (Chen et al., 2019). Total amino acids increased during fermentation and some FAAs declines may indicate amino acid utilization for metabolism or flavor compound formation. Umami and sweet FAAs increased, especially in the PRB group, which maintained significantly higher FAA levels than other inoculated groups. The CK and PRB differences likely stem from higher LAB counts in PRB, accelerating fermentation. Over time, the richer microbial profile in PRB led to FAA compositions resembling CK (Fig. 3G, A5).

Umami FAAs, primarily from LAB metabolism, rose from 21.36% in raw materials to ∼40%. Glu, initially 6.61 mg/100 g, peaked at day 7. Glu and aspartate (Asp) bind Na+ to boost umami and derive from alanine, Asp, and Glu metabolism (Zheng, Wei, et al., 2023). Sweet FAAs like alanine and proline increased over 2.5 times but had TAVs below 1, limiting flavor impact. Bitter FAA content was higher in CK but negligible in flavor due to low TAVs. Inoculated groups saw umami and sweet FAAs rise while bitter FAAs decreased.

FAA changes mainly result from LAB protease specificity and proteolytic activity. Aminopeptidases, endopeptidases, and dipeptidases secreted by LAB enhance FAA content and paocai quality. Glycine improves flavor and inhibits biogenic amine formation (Zhao et al., 2023). Biogenic amines, produced by Lactobacillus, Pediococcus, and Pichia, pose health risks if excessive. Although glycine was lower than typical addition levels, increased glycine could reduce biogenic amines by downregulating related enzymes and inhibiting decarboxylase activity, enhancing safety.

VOCs

VOCs are critical for fermented vegetable quality and sensory appeal (Xing et al., 2023). Paocai's VOCs arise from raw ingredients and microbial metabolism by LAB and yeasts. LAB, the main microbes in traditional paocai, significantly shape flavor. Sixty-three VOCs were identified, with 59 characterized via GC-IMS (Fig. 3H), including esters (14), ketones (7), aldehydes (5), alkenes (10), alcohols (14), acids (2), heterocycles (5), sulfur compounds (1), and alkanes (1), showing varied signal intensities (Fig. 3, Table A). Some compounds appeared as monomers and dimers (Tian et al., 2020).

Raw radish VOCs were mainly ethyl formate, methyl benzoate, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, and acetone, contributing fresh, fruity, and pungent notes. OPLS-DA and clustering revealed distinct VOC profiles among LAB combinations at the same fermentation stages (Fig. A6). Day 1 saw a notable increase in VOC types and amounts, especially esters, aldehydes, and alkenes, adding fruity and woody aromas. The CK group showed fewer VOC differences but distinct aroma profiles. VOCs derived from raw materials and LAB secondary metabolism or enzymatic reactions on esters and proteins (Liu et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2022), driven by LAB abundance and activity differences. CK's flavor was mainly influenced by plant and spice ingredients (Xing et al., 2023), while inoculated groups produced more VOCs enzymatically (Chen et al., 2019; Qiu et al., 2023).

From days 3 to 7, VOC profiles remained distinct due to microbial differences but converged somewhat as diversity declined. CK maintained a unique VOC profile throughout, differing significantly from inoculated groups. On day 5, PRB diverged from PB and P, with further separation by day 7. VOC peak intensities followed similar trends (Fig. A6), with CK and PRB groups showing lower intensities than PB and P, especially at fermentation’ s end. Microbial diversity thus influences VOC profiles, possibly limiting VOC variety and concentration. This may relate to specific metabolic pathways, such as those of Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, producing undetected metabolites.

Inoculated groups had higher VOC content, notably esters, aldehydes, and alcohols. Compounds like ethyl acetate, isoamyl butyrate, and ethyl benzoate impart floral, sweet, and fruity aromas (Liao et al., 2024), masking undesirable odors and softening radish pungency (Lan et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2023). Alcohols increased during fermentation, enhancing aroma and flavor (Chen et al., 2019). Acetic acid, mainly from LAB metabolism, added sharp acidity (Liao et al., 2024). VOCs like 1,8-cineole, 1-hexanol, and dimethyl disulfide likely originated from spices such as Sichuan pepper, ginger, and garlic, sames as previous research (Liu et al., 2022). Some, like dimethyl disulfide, produced unpleasant odors from sulfur amino acid degradation (Wang et al., 2022). Esters and alcohols such as methyl benzoate, isoamyl butyrate, and 2-ethylhexanol showed variable trends, possibly due to ester-alcohol exchange or further microbial metabolism (Qiu et al., 2023).

VIP analysis highlighted key VOCs (Fig. 5): acetic acid, ethyl acetate, 1-butanol, styrene, acetone, methyl isobutenyl ketone, and 2,3-pentanedione (VIP >1). The B group had the highest VOC content and diversity at fermentation end (Zhang et al., 2023), but co-inoculation, especially in PRB, enhanced VOC profiles early on. PRB also kept undesirable compounds like pyridine and dimethyl disulfide low, reducing off-flavors. These results suggest that introducing diverse strains into single-strain starters accelerates fermentation, improves quality, and better replicates traditional naturally fermented paocai flavor.

Sensory characteristics of SRP

Color analysis

Color critically affects the quality and consumer acceptance of fermented vegetables such as paocai. Anthocyanins, water-soluble pigments residing in plant vacuoles, display colors ranging from red to purple or blue depending on pH conditions (Liu & Chen, 2019). During fermentation, the pH decline leads to enhanced red hues in radishes, stimulating appetite and aligning with the traditional preference for red-fleshed radishes in Sichuan paocai. As Fig. 4A shows, marked color changes occurred within the first three days, primarily due to anthocyanin release and acidification. These changes were more pronounced in inoculated groups, especially PRB, which exhibited the highest redness.

The hue angle (h, Fig. 4D) closely mirrored total color difference (ΔE), shifting rapidly from purple toward red early in fermentation. Chroma (C*, Fig. 4C) differed significantly among treatments, with PRB showing the highest color intensity, suggesting co-fermentation better preserves or enhances anthocyanin stability. Beyond microbial activity, enzymatic browning and oxidation can alter color, as indicated by b* and L* values. Early acidification plays a key role in inhibiting browning enzymes (Cui, Chen, Yu, Hayat, & Zhang, 2023), and maintaining a pH below 4.5 generally improves color quality (Yang et al., 2022). Here, the increasing b* values, especially in PB and PRB, likely reflect chemical transformations of anthocyanins under acidic conditions rather than classical browning. Lightness (L*, Fig. 4B) remained stable initially but decreased later, with B and PRB retaining higher brightness, consistent with quicker acidification and inhibited enzymatic browning. These findings highlight that inoculation, particularly co-fermentation, not only accelerates acid production but also promotes favorable color development in SRP, which has practical significance for consumer appeal.

Texture analysis

Crunchiness is a hallmark of quality in Sichuan paocai, closely tied to consumer preference (Zheng, Zhou, et al., 2023). Key texture attributes affected by fermentation include hardness, springiness, chewiness, and crispness. Fermentation-induced water loss and nutrient depletion typically soften radish tissue (Choi, 2023). Fig. 4E-G illustrate a significant decrease in hardness and chewiness within the first three days across all groups, with the CK group showing a slower decline, likely due to its less vigorous fermentation. Interestingly, a slight rebound in hardness and chewiness occurred at later stages, most notably in PRB, suggesting that co-fermentation may slow softening and help maintain textural integrity. Springiness declined sharply on day 1 but remained stable thereafter without significant differences among groups. Crispness (Fig. 4H) increased sharply on day 1, fluctuating over time, with inoculated groups, especially PRB, maintaining higher crispness levels. This suggests that inoculation delays texture degradation, which could be attributed to early suppression of molds and yeasts and lowered pectinase and cellulase activity, as reported by Zhang et al. (2023). Co-fermentation's ability to preserve hardness and crispness indicates practical benefits for product quality and shelf life, reflecting both statistical and practical significance in texture preservation (Chang & Chang, 2010).

Taste profile analysis

Electronic tongue analysis (Fig. 4I, A7-A9) revealed that all taste parameters, except sweetness (remaining below threshold), were above the no-taste baseline, consistent with prior studies (Zhang et al., 2023). Dominant taste characteristics included acidity, umami, astringency, and richness, largely contributed by sugars, salts, organic acids, and free amino acids (FAAs) (Luo et al., 2020). Inoculated groups exhibited elevated acidity throughout fermentation, corresponding to increased organic acids, particularly lactic and acetic acids. Differences between single- and multi-strain inoculations were subtle, indicating that the type of inoculum influences acid production kinetics more than overall taste profile. Organic acids such as oxalic acid can impart bitterness and astringency (Chen et al., 2019). The PRB group's relatively low astringency likely results from its lower oxalic acid content, demonstrating how microbial interactions can modulate taste compounds. Umami FAAs accumulated over fermentation but experienced a gradual decrease in perceived umami value due to acid and salt interactions (Tian et al., 2020). Nevertheless, the high TAV of umami FAAs maintained a strong umami sensation, with PRB and CK groups showing higher umami scores. Richness increased over time, with the B exhibiting greater richness than co-fermented and control groups in later stages. Other taste indices such as bitterness, bitter aftertaste, astringent aftertaste, and saltiness showed minimal variation and no significant differences among groups. These results underscore that inoculation strategies primarily modulate acidity and umami characteristics, which are critical for consumer acceptability and quality control in paocai production.

Screening of differential compounds and pathway analysis

Differential compounds between inoculated and naturally SRP

To evaluate the metabolic differences induced by inoculation, Euclidean distances were calculated based on metabolite content or peak areas across fermentation stages (Fig. A2). On days 1 and 5, all groups showed comparable profiles, indicating minimal divergence in early fermentation. However, more distinct separation appeared on days 3 and 7, particularly on day 5, where the B deviated notably from the others. Throughout the process, PRB maintained the highest similarity to CK, while B showed the greatest departure, suggesting co-inoculation may better preserve or mimic natural metabolic progression. At the end of fermentation, differential metabolites were analyzed using thresholds of Log2 FC > 2 or < −2 and VIP > 1. Compared to CK, significantly upregulated compounds were 20 (P), 19 (PB), and 7 (PRB), while downregulated ones were 7, 4, and 8, respectively (Fig. 5A, C, E). Ten, eight, and eight key differential substances (KDSs) were further identified in the P, PB, and PRB groups, respectively. These KDSs, including various organic acids, amino acids, and volatile precursors, are associated with sourness, umami, or aromatic notes (Fig. 5B, D, F). Although the differences were statistically significant, their practical relevance lies in the potential flavor enhancement and product quality modulation. For example, the higher accumulation of umami-associated amino acids in PRB not only reflects metabolic shifts but may contribute to a more balanced sensory profile, aligning with earlier sensory evaluations.

Pathway analysis of differential compounds (KEGG)

To explore underlying metabolic changes, identified KDSs were mapped onto KEGG pathways (Fig. 6). Carbon metabolism emerged as the dominant route, consistent with the energy and biosynthetic demands of lactic acid bacteria (LAB). Homofermentative LAB such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus primarily utilize the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway, converting sugars into pyruvate and subsequently lactic acid. In contrast, heterofermentative Lactobacillus brevis favors the hexose monophosphate (HMP) pathway, yielding precursors like ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis and NAD+/NADP+ cofactor generation. Intermediate metabolites such as glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) serve as critical junctions between EMP, HMP, and broader fermentative processes. Pyruvate, central to both aerobic and anaerobic metabolism (Choi, 2023), is a key precursor for free amino acids (FAAs), organic acids, and volatiles, many of which were elevated in co-inoculated samples. Co-inoculation expanded metabolic diversity by combining homo- and heterofermentative pathways. For instance, phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose-4-phosphate were linked to the production of fumaric acid and phenylalanine, compounds tied to sourness and floral notes. Notably, L. rhamnosus appeared to enhance carbon flux efficiency, facilitating broader substrate utilization and metabolite diversity.

Metabolic pathway analysis of SRP-derived metabolites. (This pathway map was reconstructed based on KEGG database annotations).

Beyond primary fermentation, evidence of active citrate metabolism suggests some engagement of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Although LAB derive most ATP from sugar fermentation, the TCA intermediates (e.g., malic acid, oxaloacetate) support FAA synthesis (Jung et al., 2022), potentially contributing to the richer flavor observed in PRB and CK.

Taken together, the metabolite shifts not only demonstrate statistically significant group differences but also suggest practically meaningful impacts on flavor development and microbial adaptability. The PRB group, in particular, shows a favorable balance between microbial diversity, metabolic complexity, and sensory enhancement.

Conclusion

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of single and mixed LAB inoculations on the fermentation, flavor, and safety of SRP, using L. brevis as the base strain, co-inoculated with L. plantarum and L. rhamnosus. Compared to natural fermentation, inoculated treatments, especially the PRB group, accelerated fermentation through faster glycogen consumption and effective nitrite degradation. PRB maintained a more stable pH, moderated excessive acidity, and improved sugar utilization, indicating a more balanced fermentation process. Microbial analysis revealed increased yeast activity in the PRB group during later stages, supporting more diverse microbial dynamics. Metabolite profiling showed higher levels of FAAs (notably umami-enhancing ones) and favorable organic acids—lower oxalic acid but higher citric and tartaric acids—contributing to improved flavor and safety. PRB-treated samples also had brighter color, firmer texture, and richer volatile profiles, with more esters, ketones, and aromatic aldehydes, enhancing sensory quality. Key differential metabolites (KDMs) were identified and mapped via KEGG pathways to explain flavor development mechanisms. However, the study's limitations include the use of a single radish variety, controlled lab conditions, a limited number of LAB strains, and a small sample size. These factors may limit broader applicability. Future research should explore optimization of PRB inoculation, long-term effects on product quality, and industrial-scale validation. In summary, this study highlights co-inoculation, particularly the PRB combination, as a superior strategy to replicate the complex flavor of natural fermentation while enhancing safety, improving fermentation efficiency, and achieving better sensory quality. This provides a solid foundation for optimizing Paocai fermentation practices beyond traditional methods.
