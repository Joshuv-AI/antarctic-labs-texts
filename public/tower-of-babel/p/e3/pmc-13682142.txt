Late-onset cobalamin C defect with a rare MMACHC c.457 C > T variant: a case report and literature review
Authors: Qingmei Huang, Zhenmei Liao, Honghua Li, Xiu Lu, Lingdong Zeng
Published: 2026

Background

Cobalamin C (cblC) defect, also referred to as combined methylmalonic acidemia and homocystinuria, is the most common inborn error of intracellular cobalamin metabolism [1–4]. It is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the MMACHC gene, which impair the intracellular synthesis of methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin, leading to the characteristic biochemical profile of elevated methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine with decreased or inappropriately normal methionine [2–4]. Clinically, cblC defect is highly heterogeneous, ranging from severe early-onset disease in infancy to late-onset forms presenting in childhood, adolescence, or adulthood [1–5].

Unlike early-onset disease, late-onset cblC defect often presents with atypical and nonspecific manifestations, particularly neuropsychiatric and neurological symptoms such as cognitive decline, behavioral abnormalities, psychiatric disturbance, gait instability, myelopathy, peripheral neuropathy, and other progressive neurological deficits [1, 3–8]. Because these presentations are diverse and may mimic autoimmune, psychiatric, or other neurological disorders, late-onset cblC defect is frequently underrecognized or misdiagnosed, resulting in delayed metabolic and genetic evaluation [1, 4, 6–8]. Recent cohort studies have further shown that onset around puberty is not uncommon, and although many patients respond biochemically to hydroxocobalamin-based treatment, delayed diagnosis may still be associated with persistent neurological sequelae [3, 5, 6].

Genotype contributes importantly to the phenotypic variability of cblC defect. In particular, the MMACHC c.482G > A variant has been repeatedly associated with late-onset and relatively milder phenotypes in large cblC cohorts, especially in East Asian patients [9]. Nevertheless, substantial clinical heterogeneity remains even among patients carrying recurrent variants, underscoring the complexity of genotype-phenotype correlations in this disorder [2, 3, 9]. Given the treatable nature of cblC defect and the potentially irreversible consequences of delayed recognition, this diagnosis should be considered in children and adolescents with otherwise unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms, cognitive deterioration, or progressive motor dysfunction. Here, we report a 13-year-old girl with late-onset cblC defect caused by compound heterozygous MMACHC variants c.482G > A and c.457 C > T, who initially presented with progressive neuropsychiatric and motor symptoms and was misdiagnosed before metabolic and genetic confirmation.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old girl was admitted to our hospital with progressive neuropsychiatric symptoms. Since June 2021, she had developed cognitive impairment, incoherent speech, self-talk, inappropriate answers, emotional lability with frequent crying and laughing, irritability, unsteady gait, confusion, staring episodes, sluggish responses, memory decline, and urinary and fecal incontinence. The patient was initially hospitalized elsewhere and diagnosed with “autoimmune encephalitis” and treated with intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy and supportive care including vitamins. The symptoms partially improved, and the patient was switched to oral prednisone for maintenance. However, five months later the patient experienced relapse with worsening cognitive dysfunction, speech disturbance, motor impairment, impaired consciousness, and autonomic dysfunction, prompting referral to our hospital for further evaluation.

Clinical history

The patient was born at term via spontaneous vaginal delivery without perinatal asphyxia. Feeding during infancy was normal. Developmental milestones were age-appropriate: rolling at 4 months, sitting unsupported at 6 months, and independent walking at around 12 months. Language development was also normal, with single words at 1 year and short sentences by 2 years. Academic performance was average. Menarche occurred at age 11, with regular menstrual cycles and normal volume thereafter. The patient denied any history of infectious diseases. Her parents were non-consanguineous. No family history of intellectual disability, epilepsy, or metabolic disorders was reported.

Physical examination

On admission, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.6 °C, pulse 97 beats/min, respiratory rate 22 breaths/min, and blood pressure 112/79 mmHg. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable.

Neurological examination

Neurological examination revealed an alert mental status with a flat affect, accompanied by dysarthria and impaired memory. Cranial nerve examination was normal. Motor testing showed Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 5/5 strength in both upper and lower extremities, with increased muscle tone in all four limbs. Deep tendon reflexes were normal, and no pathological reflexes were elicited. Sensory examination did not reveal definite loss of vibration sense or joint position sense on routine bedside testing; however, the Romberg sign was positive, suggesting possible subtle posterior column dysfunction or sensory ataxia. Coordination testing showed impaired performance on finger-to-nose testing, an abnormal heel-to-knee-to-shin test, and dysdiadochokinesia. Gait assessment demonstrated a scissoring gait, and the patient required assistance to ambulate.

Complete blood count showed mild anemia (hemoglobin 104.8 g/L; reference range, 120–140 g/L). Serum vitamin B12 was decreased (100 pg/mL; reference range, 187–883 pg/mL). Other laboratory results, including blood gas analysis, urinalysis, liver and renal function tests, coagulation profile, thyroid function, iron studies, ferritin, and autoimmune antibody panels, were unremarkable. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis and autoimmune encephalitis antibodies were negative. Electroencephalography (recorded during wakefulness and natural sleep) demonstrated diffuse background slowing with reduced reactivity. A poorly sustained posterior dominant rhythm of 9–11 Hz was intermittently observed over the bilateral occipital leads; intermittent low-to-moderate amplitude 3–5 Hz slow-wave activity was more prominent over the frontal regions and was occasionally asymmetric. Hyperventilation increased the slow-wave activity, whereas photic stimulation did not induce a photoparoxysmal response. No epileptiform discharges were detected. Brain MRI revealed punctate, ill-defined lesions in the supratentorial deep white matter/centrum semiovale (hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR images) without diffusion restriction on DWI. In addition, the cerebral sulci, fissures, and ventricles appeared symmetrically widened, consistent with mild cerebral atrophy (Fig. 1).

Metabolic screening was performed using dried blood spots (DBS). Plasma total homocysteine was markedly elevated (153.63 µmol/L; reference range, 0–15 µmol/L). Tandem mass spectrometry of the DBS (Suzhou PerkinElmer Medical Laboratory, Suzhou, China) demonstrated decreased methionine (Met), mildly elevated propionylcarnitine (C3), and increased C3/C2 and C3/C0 ratios. In addition, urine organic acid analysis by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Guangxi Huayin Medical Laboratory Center, Nanning, China) revealed markedly increased methylmalonic acid and methylcitrate (Table 1), consistent with combined methylmalonic acidemia and homocystinuria (cblC disease) [10].

Genetic testing

Peripheral blood samples (EDTA-anticoagulated) were collected from the patient and her mother (the father was not available for testing) for genomic DNA extraction and whole-exome sequencing (WES). WES was performed with a report issued by The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Nanning, China). Exonic regions were enriched using the IDTv2 capture kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, with > 95% of target regions achieving a depth > 20×.

Variant calling and bioinformatic analysis were performed using GATK (HaplotypeCaller), with alignment to the UCSC hg19/GRCh37 reference genome. Variants were annotated using ANNOVAR, Oncotator, and Variant Effect Predictor, and filtered against population databases (e.g., gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, ESP, and ExAC). In silico prediction tools (e.g., SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and M-CAP) and disease databases (ClinVar, OMIM, and HGMD) were used to support interpretation. Sanger sequencing was performed for familial segregation when applicable.

WES identified two heterozygous variants in MMACHC (NM_015506.3): c.482G > A (p.Arg161Gln; maternally inherited) and c.457 C > T (p.Arg153Ter; paternal origin undetermined) (Fig. 2), consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Both variants have been reported in ClinVar (Variation IDs: 1425 and 550539). According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines, c.482G > A was classified as pathogenic based on PS3, PM2, PM3, PM5, and PP3, and c.457 C > T was classified as pathogenic based on PVS1_strong, PM2, and PM3_strong. The final diagnosis was combined methylmalonic acidemia and homocystinuria, cblC type [2].

Treatment and follow-up

Following confirmation of the diagnosis, the patient was managed with nutritional support aimed at ensuring adequate caloric intake and individualized protein intake. Hydroxocobalamin was administered intramuscularly at 1 mg/day (once daily) to replenish cobalamin stores. Oral folic acid was prescribed at 10 mg/day (divided twice daily) to support the remethylation pathway. Oral L-carnitine was initiated at 55 mg/kg/day in three divided doses as a weight-based starting dose within the commonly used therapeutic range. The dosing decision was based on body weight, the patient’s low-normal baseline free carnitine level, and published therapeutic recommendations [11, 12]. During follow-up, free carnitine transiently decreased at 1 month but normalized thereafter, and therefore no major dose escalation was required; treatment was continued with serial biochemical monitoring and clinical follow-up. Oral betaine capsules were initiated at 250 mg/kg/day (divided three times daily) to reduce plasma total homocysteine. Dose selection was guided by therapeutic recommendations for combined methylmalonic acidemia and homocystinuria [11] and published guidelines for the diagnosis and management of methylmalonic and propionic acidemia [12], with individualized adjustments based on body weight, tolerability, and serial biochemical monitoring.

Following initiation of targeted therapy, the patient’s clinical status improved. Biochemical monitoring showed a progressive decline in plasma total homocysteine with normalization by 2 years; methionine normalized by 1 year. Propionylcarnitine (C3) decreased into the reference range after treatment, whereas the C3/C2 ratio remained mildly elevated during follow-up. Urinary methylmalonic acid and methylcitrate decreased to within reference ranges after treatment and remained stable (Table 1). The patient has been followed regularly since discharge. Her affect and communication improved, and dysarthria became less pronounced. Urinary and fecal incontinence resolved after therapy and did not recur. However, cognitive impairment persisted, characterized by ongoing memory decline, and gait disturbance showed limited recovery; she continued to exhibit a scissoring gait and required assistance to ambulate at the last follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

Cobalamin C defect (cblC disease) is the most common inherited disorder of intracellular cobalamin metabolism and is caused by pathogenic variants in MMACHC [2, 13, 14]. These variants impair the intracellular synthesis of methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin, leading to disruption of methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase pathways, respectively [2, 13, 14]. As a result, patients typically exhibit elevated total homocysteine and methylmalonic acid with reduced methionine, and may develop multisystem involvement, particularly neurological dysfunction [1–4, 13, 14].

Late-onset cblC defect is increasingly recognized as a clinically heterogeneous but treatable remethylation disorder whose onset may range from childhood to adulthood and whose diagnosis is frequently delayed because presenting manifestations are nonspecific and often multisystemic [1, 4, 15]. In Huemer et al.’s review plus three new cases, the interval from first symptoms to diagnosis ranged from 3 months to more than 20 years; in older children and adolescents, psychiatric symptoms, cognitive impairment, ataxia, and myelopathy were frequent, whereas brain atrophy, white-matter lesions, and myelopathy were common neuroimaging findings [4]. Arhip et al.’s systematic review of 199 late-onset patients similarly identified neuropathy/myelopathy, encephalopathy, and psychiatric symptoms among the most frequent manifestations; importantly, although many patients improved after treatment, incomplete recovery and death still occurred, emphasizing the clinical cost of delayed recognition [1]. More recently, a multicenter northern China cohort of 156 patients further underscored this heterogeneity, with a median onset age of 16 years and common manifestations including spastic paralysis, mental and behavioral abnormalities, renal damage, anemia, cognitive decline, and ataxia; the most frequent variants were c.482G > A and c.609G > A [15].

Case-based reports and smaller series show that late-onset cblC defect may initially mimic autoimmune encephalitis, primary psychiatric disease, or other nonspecific neurological disorders [6, 8, 16–18]. Wang et al. described a 29-year-old woman with an encephalitis-like presentation characterized by cognitive decline, delirium, increased muscle tone, and ataxia; MRI showed bilateral cerebellar lesions, biochemical testing revealed marked hyperhomocysteinemia and methylmalonic aciduria, and the patient improved dramatically after intramuscular hydroxocobalamin [16]. Xu et al. reported a 9-year-old girl with fatigue, inattention, nightmares, and macrocytic anemia after adoption of a vegetarian diet; she had marked hyperhomocysteinemia, an elevated C3/C2 ratio, and methylmalonic aciduria/methylcitrate, but no obvious cranial MRI abnormality, and improved significantly within one week after dietary correction and cobalamin-based therapy [17]. In a 26-patient Chinese case series with neuropsychiatric presentations, the most frequent manifestations were lower limb weakness, psychiatric disturbances, and gait instability [6]. A teenage case reported by Cheng et al. first presented with depression before progressing to gait disturbance and lower-limb weakness [8]. Adult-focused reviews likewise emphasize that neurological, psychiatric, renal, ophthalmic, and thromboembolic symptoms may all dominate the initial presentation [18]. Taken together, the available literature strongly supports the view that predominant neuropsychiatric symptoms are a well-established phenotype of late-onset cblC defect and that early metabolic and genetic testing is essential in otherwise unexplained adolescent or adult neuropsychiatric disease (Table 2).

Reported late-onset or atypical cblC defect cases with predominant neuropsychiatric manifestations

An additional clinical consideration in this patient was the markedly decreased serum vitamin B12 level at presentation. Acquired vitamin B12 deficiency itself may cause cognitive impairment, psychiatric symptoms, gait instability, sensory ataxia, and other neurological manifestations, and may be associated with posterior column involvement on clinical and radiological grounds [19–22]. This overlap can create a diagnostic challenge, particularly when patients present predominantly with neuropsychiatric or gait-related symptoms [20, 21]. In the present case, however, the markedly elevated total homocysteine, decreased methionine, increased urinary methylmalonic acid and methylcitrate, and the identification of biallelic pathogenic MMACHC variants strongly supported late-onset cblC defect as the primary diagnosis [1, 3, 6, 12, 23]. Previous studies have shown that late-onset cblC defect may present with heterogeneous neuropsychiatric and neurological phenotypes and can initially mimic vitamin B12 deficiency-related neurological syndromes or other neurological disorders [1, 6, 12, 20, 23]. Therefore, although concomitant vitamin B12 deficiency cannot be excluded and may have aggravated the neurological phenotype, particularly the gait instability and possible sensory ataxia-related manifestations, it is more appropriately interpreted as a modifying factor rather than an alternative explanation for the overall syndrome in this patient [1, 3, 6, 12, 19–21, 23]. This may also be relevant to the patient’s positive Romberg sign, which could reflect sensory pathway involvement potentially exacerbated by vitamin B12 deficiency.

The advancement of genetic testing has greatly improved the diagnostic yield of late-onset cblC defect. The most common MMACHC mutations reported in China include c.482G > A and c.609G > A [24–26]. In particular, the c.482G > A mutation is often associated with late-onset and relatively mild clinical phenotypes [9]. However, our patient, who carried the compound heterozygous mutations c.482G > A and c.457 C > T, exhibited significant neurological sequelae despite treatment, suggesting that the second allele may contribute to disease severity. The c.457 C > T mutation is a nonsense mutation in MMACHC, which introduces a premature stop codon at codon 153, resulting in truncated nonfunctional protein [27, 28]. Previous studies have suggested that c.457 C > T is often found in early-onset patients as part of compound heterozygosity and is associated with increased oxidative stress and apoptosis in patient-derived cells [29]. Vitamin B12 supplementation may partially ameliorate oxidative stress in these cells [29]. In our case, the c.457 C > T mutation occurred in compound heterozygosity with c.482G > A and was associated with a late-onset phenotype but persistent neurological sequelae despite metabolic improvement.

The c.482G > A variant has been frequently reported in patients with late-onset cblC defect and is often associated with relatively milder or delayed clinical presentation. In contrast, c.457 C > T is a truncating variant and has been less commonly reported, with available studies suggesting possible association with more severe functional impairment [29]. In our patient, the coexistence of c.482G > A and c.457 C > T may partly explain the late onset together with persistent neurological sequelae. However, genotype-phenotype correlation should be interpreted cautiously because the number of reported patients carrying c.457 C > T remains limited.

The severity of late-onset cblC phenotypes is likely influenced by both genotype and residual functional activity. While nonsense mutations usually abolish protein function completely, some missense mutations retain partial activity, leading to delayed onset and milder presentation. This mechanism may explain why our patient manifested late in childhood despite harboring a nonsense mutation. In addition, metabolic parameters such as elevated propionylcarnitine (C3) and C3/C2 ratio have been proposed as predictors of poor neurological outcomes and mortality [30]. Our patient’s C3 mildly elevated while the C3/C2 ratio was elevated, combined with delayed diagnosis and treatment due to initial misdiagnosis, may have contributed to the unfavorable neurological outcome.

Even with appropriate therapy including hydroxocobalamin, betaine, folate, and carnitine, many patients with late-onset cblC defect continue to develop long-term neurological complications [31]. Therefore, early recognition and intervention are critical. Our case expands the spectrum of MMACHC mutations in China by documenting the rare c.457 C > T variant and emphasizes the importance of considering inherited metabolic disorders in children and adolescents presenting with unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Conclusions

In summary, we report a rare case of late-onset cobalamin C defect caused by compound heterozygous MMACHC mutations, including the rarely reported c.457 C > T variant in China. The patient presented with atypical neuropsychiatric manifestations and was initially misdiagnosed as autoimmune encephalitis, which delayed appropriate treatment. Although metabolic correction was achieved with hydroxocobalamin, folate, betaine, and carnitine, neurological sequelae persisted. This case expands the genotypic spectrum of cobalamin C defect in China and underscores the importance of metabolic screening and genetic testing in pediatric patients with unexplained psychiatric or neurological symptoms. Early recognition and intervention remain crucial to improving long-term outcomes.
