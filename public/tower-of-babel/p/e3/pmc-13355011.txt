Incidental discovery of persistent Müllerian duct syndrome with transverse testicular ectopia during adult inguinal hernia repair: A case report
Authors: Zubair Mohamed Dahir Sed, Mohamed Omar Osman Filig, Badrudin A. Warsame, Abdulkadir Ali Dahir, Shafie Adan Dirir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) associated with persistent Müllerian duct syndrome (PMDS) is a rare congenital anomaly that may remain undiagnosed until adulthood. We report a 27-year-old man with a right inguinal hernia, non-palpable left testis, and primary infertility. During hernia repair, exploration revealed a uterus, bilateral fallopian tubes, and an atrophic ectopic left testis within the right inguinal canal. Müllerian structures were excised, left orchidectomy performed, and the right testis preserved by orchidopexy. Histopathology showed no malignancy. Postoperative evaluation demonstrated a normal 46,XY karyotype and azoospermia. This case highlights the challenges of adult TTE with PMDS.

Introduction

Transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) is an exceedingly rare congenital anomaly characterized by migration of both testes through a single inguinal canal into the same hemiscrotum. It typically presents during childhood as an inguinal hernia on the side of the palpable testis accompanied by contralateral cryptorchidism. The diagnosis is most often established incidentally during surgical exploration for an inguinal hernia or undescended testis. TTE has been reported in association with inguinal hernia alone in approximately 40–50% of cases and with persistent Müllerian duct structures in nearly 30% of patients.1

Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome (PMDS) is a rare disorder of sexual development in which phenotypically and genotypically normal (46,XY) males retain Müllerian derivatives, including a uterus, fallopian tubes, and upper vaginal structures, due to deficiency of anti-Müllerian hormone or resistance of its receptor. Although affected individuals have normal male external genitalia, the persistence of Müllerian structures is frequently associated with cryptorchidism and infertility. Adult patients with TTE and associated undescended testes are considered to be at increased risk of malignant transformation, with reported rates ranging from 18% to 33%.2

The coexistence of TTE and PMDS in adulthood is particularly uncommon and presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Preoperative diagnosis may be difficult because clinical findings are often nonspecific, and in resource-limited settings the condition frequently remains unrecognized until surgical exploration. Management requires balancing oncological considerations, fertility potential, and preservation of endocrine function.

We report the case of a 27-year-old male who presented with a right inguinal swelling, a non-palpable left testis, and primary infertility. During elective repair of a presumed right inguinal hernia, surgical exploration unexpectedly revealed transverse testicular ectopia associated with persistent Müllerian duct syndrome.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old male presented with a painless right inguinal swelling and an empty left hemiscrotum that had been present since childhood. He also reported primary infertility after three years of unprotected married life but had never previously sought medical evaluation. His medical, surgical, and family histories were unremarkable.

Physical examination demonstrated a phenotypically normal male with well-developed secondary sexual characteristics. The penis was normally developed with a terminal urethral meatus. Examination of the scrotum revealed a hypoplastic left hemiscrotum with underdeveloped rugae and a nonpalpable left testis. On the right side, the testis was palpable near the superficial inguinal ring adjacent to a soft, approximately 5-cm reducible mass extending toward the deep inguinal ring.

Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, and coagulation profile, were within normal limits. Ultrasonography demonstrated a right inguinal hernia and non-visualization of the left testis, while the abdominal viscera appeared normal. Advanced imaging and hormonal assessment were not performed preoperatively. The patient was scheduled for elective right inguinal hernia repair, with further evaluation of the absent testis planned subsequently.

Under spinal anesthesia, a right inguinal incision was made and the inguinal canal was explored. A mass adherent to the spermatic cord was encountered. Gentle delivery of the hernia sac unexpectedly revealed a uterus, bilateral fallopian tubes, and an ectopic atrophic left testis emerging through the internal inguinal ring. Following intraoperative assessment of the unexpected findings, management was individualized based on gonadal viability, spermatic cord length, and the need to preserve endocrine function while minimizing oncological risk. The Müllerian structures and the atrophic left testis were excised en bloc as completely as safely possible, extending distally to the level of the prostate while preserving the right vas deferens and testicular vessels. Excision of the left testis was undertaken because of testicular atrophy (2.5 × 1.5 cm) and inadequate cord length preventing safe transseptal orchidopexy. The right testis was preserved and mobilized by careful elongation of the spermatic cord. The inguinal floor was reconstructed using the Bassini technique, and the right testis was fixed within a right subdartos pouch. All excised specimens were submitted for histopathological examination.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Histopathological analysis confirmed uterine tissue with proliferative endometrium and normal fallopian tube architecture. The excised testis demonstrated mature testicular tissue without evidence of dysplasia or malignancy. Karyotype analysis revealed a normal 46,XY genotype (Fig. 2). Postoperative hormonal investigations, including testosterone, anti-Müllerian hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, and luteinizing hormone, were within normal reference ranges. Tumor markers (AFP, β-hCG, and LDH) were normal. Discussion

Transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) is a rare congenital anomaly in which both testes descend through a single inguinal canal into the same hemiscrotum and is most often identified incidentally during surgery for inguinal hernia or cryptorchidism.1 It was first described by von Lenhossék in 1886.3 Based on associated anomalies, TTE is classified into three types, with type 2 being associated with persistent Müllerian duct syndrome (PMDS) in approximately 30% of cases.4 This classification is clinically useful in anticipating associated anomalies and guiding intraoperative decision-making.

The pathogenesis of TTE is not fully understood but is thought to be multifactorial, including abnormal gubernacular development, defective testicular guidance, and mechanical influence from persistent Müllerian structures.4 In adult cases, delayed diagnosis is common, and the condition is associated with a significant risk of malignant transformation ranging from 18% to 33%, most frequently seminoma arising from undescended or dysgenetic gonads.2 Compared with pediatric cases where diagnosis is typically early, adult presentations such as in our patient reflect prolonged silent progression and limited access to specialist evaluation.

PMDS is a rare disorder of sexual development affecting 46,XY individuals, caused by anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) deficiency or AMH receptor type II (AMHR2) resistance, resulting in persistence of Müllerian structures such as the uterus and fallopian tubes.2,5 While AMH normally induces regression of these structures during embryogenesis, failure of this pathway leads to coexistence of Müllerian and Wolffian derivatives.5 Although most cases are diagnosed in childhood, adult presentation is typically incidental or related to infertility evaluation.6

Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging. Ultrasonography is commonly used as the first-line imaging modality; however, its sensitivity is limited, particularly in detecting non-palpable or ectopic testes.7 Cross-sectional imaging with MRI or CT improves anatomical delineation, but diagnostic laparoscopy remains the most accurate modality for definitive diagnosis and simultaneous surgical management.6, 7, 8 In the present case, reliance on ultrasonography alone resulted in failure to identify both the ectopic gonad and Müllerian structures preoperatively, highlighting a key diagnostic limitation in resource-constrained settings and reinforcing the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients with inguinal hernia and contralateral cryptorchidism.

Surgical management of TTE with PMDS is individualized and depends on gonadal viability, spermatic cord length, and malignancy risk. When anatomically feasible, transseptal orchidopexy is preferred to preserve endocrine and reproductive function.3,8 However, intraoperative findings ultimately determine management strategy. In the present case, the left testis was atrophic with inadequate cord length, making orchidopexy unsafe; therefore, orchidectomy was performed, consistent with management recommendations for non-viable or dysplastic gonads.8,9 This reflects the principle that functional preservation must be balanced against oncological and technical feasibility.

Management of Müllerian duct remnants remains controversial. Earlier approaches favored preservation to avoid injury to the vas deferens and testicular vessels; however, more recent evidence supports excision due to the risk of malignant transformation in both Müllerian remnants and associated gonadal tissue.9,10 Reported malignancy risk ranges from 3.1% to 8.4% in Müllerian remnants, with seminoma being the most commonly reported tumor.9,10 In this case, near-complete excision was achieved while preserving the right vas deferens and vascular supply, thereby achieving a balance between oncological safety and functional preservation. An important finding in this case was azoospermia despite normal postoperative hormonal profiles, reflecting irreversible germ cell loss due to prolonged cryptorchidism and testicular atrophy. This highlights the dissociation between preserved endocrine function and impaired spermatogenesis in PMDS-associated TTE. Reported paternity rates in such patients remain low, ranging from 11% to 19%,4,10 underscoring the long-term reproductive impact even after anatomically successful surgery.

To the best of our knowledge at the time of writing, this represents only the second reported case of TTE with PMDS in an adult Somali male. Compared with the previously reported staged surgical approach,11 our single-stage management was feasible due to intraoperative anatomy and preserved testicular viability. This underscores the importance of individualized surgical decision-making rather than a standardized protocol. The availability of karyotype analysis in this case reflects improving diagnostic capacity in our setting. The main limitations of this report are the short follow-up period and the lack of molecular genetic testing, which is not currently available in our setting.

Conclusion

Transverse testicular ectopia associated with persistent Müllerian duct syndrome is a rare congenital anomaly that may remain undiagnosed until adulthood. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of the condition, particularly in resource-limited settings where advanced imaging and genetic testing may not be readily available.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for TTE and PMDS in patients presenting with inguinal hernia and contralateral cryptorchidism. Management should be individualized according to gonadal viability, anatomical feasibility, fertility considerations, and oncological risk. In our patient, excision of Müllerian remnants and orchidectomy of the severely atrophic ectopic testis, while preserving the viable contralateral testis, provided an appropriate balance between oncological safety and preservation of endocrine function.

Long-term surveillance remains essential because of the recognized risk of malignancy associated with cryptorchid testes and persistent Müllerian remnants.
