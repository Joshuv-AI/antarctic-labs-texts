Ladder diagrams and cardiac arrhythmias
Authors: Eduardo Back Sternick, Jeronimo Farre
Published: 2025

Ladder diagrams are specialized schematics commonly used to document industrial control logic systems. They are called “ladder” diagrams because they resemble a ladder, with two vertical rails and as many “rungs” (horizontal lines) as there are control circuits to represent. However, what matters here is its use in another sort of environments: those concerned with the generation and propagation of cardiac electrical activity.

Ladder-like diagrams illustrating the formation and conduction of the cardiac impulse have existed since 1885 [1]. In 1889, Karl Wenckebach described atrioventricular block using venous and arterial waveform tracings, which he portrayed in the form of a ladder diagram, in the absence of an electrocardiogram, as the latter was not yet available (Fig. 1A) [2]. The so-called “Lewis lines”—a designation now confined to historical references—have traditionally implied that Thomas Lewis should be credited with the development of ladder diagrams. Nevertheless, it has become clear that a number of authors in different countries already worked with similar diagrams [4]. Although the expression “ladder diagram” did not appear in the literature until as late as 1969, the canonical form of the diagram, essentially identical to that employed today, was first presented in Lewis's 1920 monograph [3].

The ladder diagram has primarily been employed as a schematic tool to represent re-entrant mechanisms of tachycardia and the conduction of the cardiac impulse at the sinoatrial and atrioventricular levels. It has likewise served to propose novel mechanisms of arrhythmias, which were subsequently validated by intracardiac recordings. In 1950, Pick and Langendorf postulated that dual atrioventricular nodal pathways were responsible for a tachycardia observed in a 76-year-old woman—six years before this phenomenon was described in animal models and two decades prior to its demonstration in humans [5]. The schematics of dual atrioventricular nodal tachycardia were later refined after the advent of intracardiac catheter–electrode recordings and programmed electrical stimulation, developments that heralded the beginning of the modern era of cardiac electrophysiology. Lines representing fast and slow pathways used different angles to account for slow or fast impulse conduction. The subsequent step in the evolution of the ladder diagram was its application to accessory pathway–mediated re-entrant circuits and AV conduction, a development that emerged during the 1970s. At that time, it was customary to designate accessory pathways with the letter K, in reference to the so-called “Kent pathway”; however, this terminology was inappropriate, as the structure originally described by Stanley Kent was more likely an accessory atrioventricular node (i.e. an atriofascicular pathway). In such schematics, the QRS complex was represented as a rectangle of variable width, corresponding to the duration of the QRS complex. Depiction of atrial flutter in a ladder diagram evolved as the mechanism of macro-reentry became clear. In the early diagrams as early as 1912, current understanding was that atrial flutter was caused by enhanced automaticity in the atria. In 1950 the diagram evolved to incorporate Langendorf's concealed conduction concept [5].

Ladder diagrams work best with reentrant tachyarrhythmias and sino-atrial and atrioventricular conduction disorders. Depiction of triggered activity or abnormal automaticity are not well represented by means of these diagrams (Fig. 5). Torsade de Points tachycardia in Long-QT syndrome caused by early after-depolarizations has never been shown in a ladder diagram.Fig. 5Spectrum of arrhythmias from SVT, saw tooth flutter to random fibrillation. 2001 [10].Fig. 5

With the advent of electroanatomic intracardiac mapping, high density catheters with multiple electrodes, reentrant tachycardias could be mapped (both, endocardial and epicardial) in detail, and critical slow conduction areas identified in sophisticated propagation maps. This was a terrible blow to the bidimensional ladder diagram as a teaching tool.

The ladder diagram may still retain value as a pedagogical tool for students, as illustrated in the following definition derived from an artificial intelligence source: “A ladder diagram is a graphical tool in cardiology that illustrates the sequence and timing of electrical conduction through the heart to explain the mechanism of an arrhythmia, helping clinicians and students visualize complex electrical activity in the atria and ventricles and understand how abnormal conduction creates arrhythmias”.
