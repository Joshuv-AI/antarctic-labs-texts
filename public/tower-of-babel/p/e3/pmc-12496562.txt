A New Minimally Invasive Technique for Anterior Resin‐Bonded Fixed Dental Prostheses: A 5‐Year Clinical Report
Authors: Abdulaziz Samran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis clinical report describes the use of a new design for resin‐bonded fixed dental prostheses (RBFDP) to replace a missing maxillary right central incisor. This report illustrates a conservative, functional, and esthetic RBFDP made of a metal‐ceramic framework. Using RBFDP allowed for achieving acceptable esthetics with a conservative and functional result.Keywords: fixed dental prostheses, metal‐ceramic, resin cement, resin‐bonded, retainer

Introduction

One of the most challenging aspects of mouth rehabilitation, especially in the esthetic zone, is replacing a single missing tooth. There are several treatment modalities available to replace individual missing teeth, which include: implants, traditional fixed dental prostheses (FDPs), and removable partial dentures. Although all treatment options provide reliable results and patient satisfaction, they each present different advantages and disadvantages, have varying costs, and different success and survival rates. Implant‐supported FDP is a well‐documented treatment option that has a high survival rate [1]. However, the disadvantages of this treatment option include the relatively high cost of the treatment, the number of treatments and visits needed, and the bone volume in the current region, which is important and must be carefully analyzed [2]. Therefore, resin‐bonded FDPs (RBFDPs) could be considered an alternative option with relatively few serious complications and a high survival rate, which makes this treatment option more attractive and affordable than implant‐supported FDPs [3].

Traditional FDPs usually involve removing 50% to 70% of healthy teeth structures [4, 5], which has been shown to cause damage to their vitality in 10% [6, 7]. As a result, they can be recommended when the adjacent teeth are restored and used as abutments. For these reasons, RBFDPs may be considered an alternative treatment option to replace a single anterior missing tooth with less invasive preparation and better esthetic appearance [8, 9, 10]. The “best solution” in each individual case must be defined individually after weighing up all the advantages and disadvantages and taking the patient's wishes into account.

In 1970, Rochette et al. introduced the first RBFDP as a temporary, minimally invasive option for replacing lost anterior teeth [11]. After that, RBFDPs became a very popular treatment modality for replacing both anterior and posterior missing teeth [12, 13]. RBFDPs, which involve a minimally invasive preparation design, are now a reliable and esthetic tooth replacement alternative solution.

A recent clinical study revealed an estimated survival rate of conventional metal‐ceramic RBFDPs of about 100% after 5 years and 89.8% at 10 years [14]. In addition, according to clinical data, zirconia RBFDPs can last up to 5 years [15] while alumina ceramic RBFDPs can last up to 10 years [16]. Continuous advancements in dental material sciences have allowed adhesive techniques which require less invasive tooth preparation to be used [17]. Fiber‐reinforced composite RBFDP is an alternative treatment and is considered a permanent treatment, as the survival rates were only 73% after 4.5 years of follow‐up [18]. These RBFPDs can be formulated using porcelain fused to metal [19], glass ceramic [20], and zirconia [21]. Metal‐ceramic and all‐ceramic RBFDPs have been recommended as an alternative conservative option to traditional FDPs [11, 22, 23, 24]. They showed successful outcomes after a 7.5‐year follow‐up [19]. One or two retainers (wings) may be used in the design of metal RBFDPs [25, 26, 27].

Today the use of Co‐Cr alloys is recommended for metal‐ceramic work [28, 29]. The advantage of these alloys lies mainly in their high elasticity modulus. This allows a more delicate design of the frameworks while maintaining the same stability. The risk of framework deformation, which leads to stress on the cement joint, is thus reduced. In addition, Co‐Cr alloys can be used to achieve a good adhesive bond to the luting composite [29]. With the correct material combination and processing, a secure metal‐ceramic bond can also be achieved [30].

The goal of this clinical report was to represent a new design of metal‐ceramic RBFDP to replace a missing maxillary right central incisor with a previous diastema.

Case History/Examination

A healthy 36‐year‐old Arabic man came to the Department of Restorative and Prosthetic Dental Sciences in Dar Al‐Uloom University with a missing maxillary right central incisor that had been extracted several years ago due to a sports trauma (Figure 1). He expressed concern regarding the esthetics and functionality of the affected area.

Preoperative intraoral view from facial aspect.

At the first appointment, a comprehensive clinical examination was carried out to check his medical and dental histories. Medical History: No significant medical history, non‐smoker, no known allergies. Dental History: The patient presented with a missing maxillary right central incisor due to sports trauma. Extraoral examination reveals no facial asymmetry or swelling. Intraoral examination shows: Missing tooth #11 accompanied by mild bone resorption. Both the maxillary left central incisor and maxillary right lateral incisor were sound. Gingival health is acceptable with no signs of inflammation.

His dental history revealed a few dental restorations that had been done in the last few years. The clinical investigation revealed a wide maxillary space in the missing tooth area. When the patient was asked if there was a previous diastema between maxillary central incisors, he replied positively. The patient was made aware of the various treatment options such as implant‐supported FDPs, RBDFPs, conventional FDPs, and removable partial dentures. He refused any surgical solutions and preferred conservative work rather than a treatment including removal of sound teeth structures. The patient chose the treatment with RBFDP. However, the problem was the presence of a diastema between the central incisors. Therefore, an RBFDP with a modified design which includes a proximal space and a palatal metal loop was suggested. This was done by transferring the proximal connectors to the palatal as a metal loop. The patient accepted the idea and wrote a consent form for the treatment plan.

Methods (Investigations and Treatment)

After that, the primary impressions and diagnostic casts were made [31]. Then, abutments were prepared in a minimally invasive manner, involving the enamel only, and following earlier literature descriptions with slight modification [19]. The minimal palatal preparation was done (within 0.5 mm), and the finish line in the proximal area did not extend beyond the proximal contact. All sharp edges and surfaces were meticulously smoothed. After the minimally invasive preparation, the polyvinyl siloxane impression material was used for the final impression (Variotime, Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) in a stock tray. Then, the impression was poured with Type IV dental stone (Fujirock, GC Corp). A bite registration was also obtained to ensure proper occlusion (Variotime Bite, Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany).

The framework was cast from cobalt‐chromium (CoCr) alloy, and feldspathic ceramic was used for veneering (Figure 2). After making minor corrections, the framework was seated on the cast. Then, the marginal and internal fittings were checked intraorally using an explorer and a silicone indicator paste (Fit Checker, GC America Inc., Alsip, IL, USA).

The metal‐ceramic RBFDP was fabricated.

The silicone indicator paste exhibited a uniformly thin layer which was accepted. Following the try‐in phase and the removal of any remaining blood or saliva (Figure 3), the bonding surfaces of the RBFDP were cleaned using a hot water steamer and airborne‐particle abraded with 50 μm Al2O3 for 10 s with 0.25 MPa pressure [32]. The abrasive residues were then removed from the RBFDP by ultrasonically cleaning for 3 min in an alcohol path (99% isopropanol). Consequently, the RBFDP was kept in a special container while the abutments' preparation surfaces were cleaned and treated for the next cementation procedures.

Try‐in stage.

During the cementation procedures, a rubber dam was placed, and the pumice was used to clean the abutment teeth. The prepared surfaces were then etched for 30 s using a 37% phosphoric acid gel (Cica, Promedica, Neumünster, Germany). Then, the acid etch gel was removed with water spray for 15 s, and the teeth were gently air‐dried. Then, the matching primer (Compobond LCM Primer; Promedica, Neumünster, Germany) was applied to the prepared surfaces in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations, conditioned for 30 s, and then any excess was removed with a soft, oil‐free air stream. The adhesive material (Compobond LCM Adhesive; Promedica, Neumünster, Germany) was then mixed in accordance with the manufacturer's directions, applied for 15 s, and then the excess was removed using a gentle, oil‐free air stream before it was light‐cured for an additional 15 s using an LED light‐cure unit with a full‐power mode (1500 mW/cm2 Slimax‐C Plus, Beyes, Toronto, Canada).

Finally, a ceramic primer (Aureocem DC Ceramic Primer; Promedica, Neumünster, Germany) was applied with a microbrush to the prosthesis bonding surfaces. After 60 s, the excess of the primer was removed using a gentle, oil‐free air stream. Thereafter, the prosthesis was cemented using adhesive resin cement (Aureocem DC Automix, Promedica, Neumünster, Germany) (Figure 4). During the setting time, steady finger pressure was applied for 2 min, and the cement was light cured for 20 s from all directions. After that, articulating papers were used to verify dynamic and static occlusion.

Labial view of the RBFPD directly after insertion.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient was given post‐operative care instructions, emphasizing oral hygiene and the importance of regular follow‐up appointments. Follow‐up visits were scheduled (after 6 months and after that annually for 5 years) to check the integrity of the bond and patient satisfaction with esthetics and function. After 5 years of follow‐up, a functionally and aesthetically pleasing outcome was achieved (Figure 5).

Appearance of the patient's anterior dentition at a recall visit 5 years after completion of the treatment.

The patient expressed satisfaction with the treatment both in terms of appearance and comfort. The use of an anterior RBFDP in this case allowed for a conservative yet effective restoration of the missing central incisor, achieving the desired outcomes in terms of function and esthetics. Regular monitoring will continue to ensure long‐term success.

Discussion

Midline diastema has a multifactorial etiology and is more common in the maxilla [33]. Different strategies for closing diastemas are suggested by the existing literature, including restorative treatment, orthodontic treatment, or a combination of the two. The choice is made after considering the patient's preferences, the economics, and the etiology [34].

RBFDPs have emerged as a valuable option in restorative dentistry, particularly for anterior diastema, due to their conservative nature and favorable esthetics. Unlike traditional fixed dental prostheses, which often require extensive tooth preparations and potentially compromise adjacent teeth, RBFDPs rely on minimally invasive techniques, favoring the preservation of tooth structure. The adhesive bonding mechanism enhances the biomechanical performance of these prostheses, allowing for effective load distribution and improved retention compared to conventional crowns. Additionally, advancements in resin materials and bonding agents have significantly increased their strength and durability, making RBFDPs suitable for various clinical situations. Aesthetically, RBFDPs can closely mimic the appearance of natural teeth, which is especially important in the anterior region where esthetic concerns are paramount. However, the success of RBFDPs is contingent upon precise tooth preparation, optimal material selection, and meticulous laboratory work. Long‐term stability and maintenance of periodontal health surrounding the bonded teeth can also present challenges that require careful monitoring. Despite these considerations, the growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness and success of RBFDPs highlights their role as a key tool in modern restorative practices, providing patients with an effective solution that aligns with contemporary demands for both functionality and esthetics.

In this clinical report, the large space left by the lost maxillary right central incisor is solved using a conservative and esthetic RBFDP made of porcelain fused to metal materials. While this method has only been recommended as an interim solution, it can be considered as a final solution in some situations [3, 10, 16, 22, 35]. The framework of the current RBFDP was made of cobalt chromium based on laboratory studies that reported higher fracture load for zirconia‐based RBFDPs [36, 37, 38]. This RBFDP design was developed to overcome diastema in the anterior teeth area. The presence of palatal metal connectors in this design, with a 0.5‐mm thickness, reduces stress caused by torsion forces applied non‐axially.
