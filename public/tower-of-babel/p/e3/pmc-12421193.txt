Evaluating the effectiveness of a One-Stop PrEP Care model to enhance HIV prevention: a protocol for a cluster-randomised trial in Kisumu County, Kenya
Authors: Benn Kwach, Josephine B Odoyo, Amber R Lauff, Victor Omollo, Bernard Rono, Vallery Ogello, Victoria Mann, Zachary Kwena, Katherine Thomas, Monisha Sharma, Jennifer F Morton, Kenneth Ngure, Elizabeth Bukusi, Kenneth K Mugwanya, One-Stop PrEP Care Team
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTIntroductionOral HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective biomedical intervention for HIV prevention, but its access and utilisation are challenging, especially in high-burden settings such as Kenya. For potential PrEP users, long delays and repeated consultations with several providers are obstacles to both PrEP uptake and continuation. The One-Stop PrEP Care project aims to promote the use of PrEP among clients in the health system and enhance client satisfaction by reducing the waiting time.Methods and analysisWe are conducting a 1:1 cluster-randomised trial to evaluate whether One-Stop PrEP Care achieves equivalent or better PrEP outcomes compared with the standard of care model in 12 high-volume HIV clinics in Kisumu County, Kenya. In the One-Stop model, all core PrEP components, including HIV risk evaluation, HIV testing and PrEP dispensing, are provided by one provider in a single consultation room. Programme data from ≥2400 new PrEP clients will be abstracted for 12 months each to obtain primary endpoints of PrEP initiation and continuation. Adherence will be assessed via blood drug level testing. A nested cohort of up to 300 PrEP clients will be enrolled and followed every 3 months to provide in-depth data on individual HIV prevention behaviour, risk perception and how they align PrEP use with perceived risk. Introduction

The past decade has resulted in great progress in developing effective HIV prevention interventions, notably antiretroviral therapy (ART) to reduce the risk of transmission and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)1,4 in diverse populations.5,9 The approval of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine for use as HIV PrEP by the US Foods and Drugs Administration in 2012 and the global recommendation by the WHO in 2015 marked a pivotal step for HIV prevention.10 Since then, PrEP delivery has been gradually increasing,7 including in Kenya, which rolled out its national PrEP programme in 2017.11 Despite the gradual increase in PrEP delivery, it has not been adequate to meaningfully accelerate the progress towards HIV epidemic control. On a global scale, the aim is to reach 3 million individuals on PrEP by the year 2025. As of the latest global estimates, approximately 1.6 million people are using PrEP—well below the 2025 target of 3 million—highlighting that a substantial portion of the target population remains unreached.12 In Kenya, PrEP has been integrated into public health services since 2017, with over 300 000 individuals having initiated PrEP.11 13 Despite this, many discontinue due to structural barriers such as long wait times, stigma and fragmented service delivery.14,16 Given Kenya’s high HIV burden (16% in Kisumu County) and strong national PrEP infrastructure, this study offers an opportunity to evaluate a scalable service delivery model in a real-world programmatic setting.

PrEP has high effectiveness when used with high adherence, but uptake and persistence of PrEP depend not only on client-level factors but also on how well potential PrEP clients can effectively navigate opportunity costs, health systems and structural barriers.13,30

Structural barriers such as long wait times, clients seeing multiple providers and congestion at the facility all play a role in why many do not seek out or continue getting PrEP at healthcare facilities.1315 16 24,26 These challenges are not unique to oral PrEP but may also affect the roll-out of the emerging long-acting PrEP option, and there is a need to rethink and adopt efficient user-friendly delivery models. Differentiated care models have been successfully employed in the management of people on ART and provide a way forward in PrEP provision.31,39 One promising approach is One-Stop PrEP services, in which clients receive all services from a single provider. This model has previously been tested in Kenya and Uganda and has been associated with improved ART and tuberculosis care outcomes, reduced wait times and improved retention.2940,43 The overall goal of this project is to test the effectiveness of one-stop to improve PrEP continuation and to study health system, provider and client determinants of continuation at a larger scale in a more rigorous cluster-randomised clinical trial design accounting for variations across facilities. Secondary outcomes include investigating health system, provider and client factors that influence PrEP continuation and programme costs.

Rationale

While Kenya has made significant progress in scaling up PrEP, many clients discontinue due to long wait times, multiple provider encounters and fragmented care. These barriers highlight the need for simplified service delivery models. The One-Stop PrEP Care model offers a streamlined approach by providing all PrEP components through a single provider in one consultation room. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of this model in improving PrEP uptake and continuation within high-volume public clinics in Kenya.

Objectives

The primary objective of the project is to evaluate whether the One-Stop PrEP Care model results in equivalent or better PrEP initiation and continuation in high-volume public health clinics in Kisumu County, Kenya. Secondary objectives include assessing adherence by drug levels, client and provider satisfaction, barriers and facilitators, the quality-of-service delivery and the cost of the model from both the healthcare system and user perspectives.

Significance

By conducting a cluster-randomised trial, this project seeks to provide robust evidence on whether the One-Stop PrEP model can be scaled up as a cost-saving and efficient strategy for PrEP delivery in resource-limited settings. In addition to measuring effectiveness, the study will generate implementation and cost data to inform national policy decisions. The findings will also be relevant to other low-resource settings beyond Kenya, offering insights into how streamlined service delivery models can improve HIV prevention outcomes globally.

Methods and analysis

Project design

This is a cluster-randomised non-inferiority-controlled trial of 12 high-volume PrEP facilities in Kisumu County, Kenya. The clinics were randomised in a 1:1 fashion to One-Stop PrEP delivery and usual PrEP Care. At all 12 clinics, direct routine clinical provision of PrEP to clients is conducted by the existing public health staff as part of the standard of care services. A nested cohort of up to 300 individuals will be enrolled and followed quarterly for 1 year to provide detailed complementary HIV prevention behaviour and clinical data (figure 1). The Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials reporting guidelines were used to guide the inclusion of all essential protocol components.44

Project setting

This project is being conducted in 12 high-volume HIV clinics in Kisumu County, which is in western Kenya. Kisumu is a major urban centre along Lake Victoria, a large fishing and farming area and is home to both urban and rural populations with an HIV prevalence of 16%—the second highest of all counties in Kenya.45

Population and inclusion criteria

We will recruit HIV-uninfected men and women eligible for and newly initiating PrEP at 12 public sector facilities in the large cluster-randomised project. Consecutive clients newly initiating PrEP services at the participating facilities will be eligible for this project. Kenya Ministry of Health (MoH) has defined specific characteristics for HIV risk that determine PrEP eligibility for its national PrEP programme that we will use. PrEP is the standard of care at these clinics, recommended by Kenyan guidelines,46 and available for free for all who are interested.

Eligibility for data abstraction includes HIV-uninfected men and women aged 15 and above who initiate PrEP, per Kenya’s national guidelines.

The nested observational cohort study is a research subsample of up to 300 individuals initiating PrEP as part of the larger trial but who consent to additional study procedures such as phone call surveys. The care received is consistent with the Kenya PrEP guidelines; however, additional research procedures are conducted separately.

The qualitative component will involve in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with PrEP clients and key stakeholders, including health providers, clinic leaders and policymakers. Clients will be purposively selected to represent various PrEP users, including those in serodifferent relationships and women at risk for HIV. In addition, focus group discussions and interviews with health providers will assess workload, acceptance and barriers to PrEP delivery, ultimately aiming to improve implementation and inform future service scale-up.

Intervention

We will test a One-Stop PrEP delivery model to streamline client flow and reduce the burden of PrEP provision. In the one-stop arm (intervention), the number of client consultation rooms in the PrEP care pathway will be reduced to a single consultation room. All core PrEP services that include HIV testing, risk assessment, clinical review, PrEP initiation, prescription, dispensing and follow-up will be performed in a one-stop consultation with a single provider. Demand creation and retention strategies will be the same as those implemented during standard practice. The core component of the one-stop model is the delivery of all PrEP services in a single location–independent of the provider cadre. We will systematically study clinic adaptations and their influence on key outcomes, exploring how different clinics implement the one-stop approach while ensuring quality and efficiency in PrEP service delivery. Clinic adaptations will be studied using data from quarterly technical assistance reports, time and motion studies and implementation logs. Adaptations will be categorised using the FRAME framework and analysed for associations with performance outcomes. Strategies to improve adherence to the One-Stop PrEP delivery model include providing tailored client education about the model and streamlining follow-up visits.

Clinics randomly assigned to usual care will serve as the comparison group (control arm). All eligible persons at control clinics will receive PrEP services according to the usual clinic flow, which typically involves multiple room and provider consultations.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes: the primary outcomes of the project are PrEP initiation and the continuation of PrEP use, assessed by abstracting data from clinic records of client’s initial PrEP visits, client return visits and pharmacy records of client PrEP refills. While refill records provide pragmatic measures of continuation, they may not capture intermittent use. To improve accuracy, adherence data from dried blood spots (DBS) and self-reported use from nested cohort surveys will complement these measures.

Secondary outcomes: secondary outcomes will include measures of PrEP adherence, as indicated by tenofovir drug levels in DBS collected at a random subset of PrEP visits. Specifically, a computer-generated algorithm will randomly select two study clinics each week for DBS collection. At these sites, all routine PrEP clients seen that week will be offered to consent to provide DBS for tenofovir-level testing. This sampling approach is designed to complement the nested cohort data while capturing adherence measures across the broader programme population. Up to 200 such PrEP visits will be sampled in total. Additionally, we will assess client and provider satisfaction, acceptability and experience with the one-stop model using surveys and interviews. We will also evaluate the impact on clinic efficiency by analysing waiting times, the number of services delivered in each visit and health system costs associated with PrEP delivery.

Follow up: clients on PrEP will be followed according to national PrEP guidelines, which recommend visits at month 1 and then quarterly (every 3 months) for PrEP refills and HIV testing. Data collection for outcome assessment will be via programme data abstraction on clients initiating PrEP for 12 months after initiation.

Sample size and rationale for non-inferiority

The project is abstracting client records of up to 2400 individuals newly initiating PrEP across 12 clinics (six were randomly assigned to each arm).

The client abstraction sample size was selected to ensure the power to compare the proportions of continuing PrEP between one-stop and usual care clinics in both non-inferiority and superiority analyses. For our non-inferiority comparison, with six clinics per arm and a 4% non-inferiority margin, we require a minimum cluster size of 141 per clinic, totalling 1692 individuals overall, to provide >80% power to find one-stop to be non-inferior to usual care. The 4% margin reflects clinical relevance based on stakeholder consensus that even modest differences in continuation are meaningful for policy adoption. It aligns with similar pragmatic trials in HIV prevention settings. We acknowledge programmatic heterogeneity and will interpret findings in that context. Our power calculation assumes PrEP continuation at month 6 of 34% in the usual care arm (as reported in the pre-COVID period of the Partners Scale Up project)47 and 40% in the one-stop arm, coefficient of variation between clusters of κ = 0.1, and a one-sided 95% CI (common for non-inferiority trials). Power calculations are based on prepandemic PrEP continuation rates (Partners Scale-Up study); postpandemic dynamics may have shifted. However, this allows conservative estimates and strengthens our ability to detect meaningful programme effects under variable conditions. For superiority analysis, we require a minimum cluster size of 144 per clinic, totalling 1728 individuals overall, to provide >80% power to detect a 10% improvement in continuation on PrEP compared with usual care (from 34% to 44%) with κ = 0.1 and a two-sided 95% CI. These calculations are informed by real-world PrEP programme data, aiming to assess programmatic efficiency and cost reduction while ensuring statistical robustness in the project’s findings.

The nested cohort (n=up to 300) is not powered for definitive subgroup comparisons. However, descriptive and exploratory subgroup analyses by age, gender and serostatus will be conducted to inform hypothesis generation for future studies.

Facility selection and randomisation

We conducted a comprehensive assessment of 36 public health facilities providing PrEP services in Kisumu, Kenya to evaluate client volume and willingness to engage in quality improvement (QI) activities, a prerequisite for project participation. Twenty-six facilities that met these criteria were purposively selected for further consideration. Before randomisation into either Project intervention arm or control arm, facilities were categorised into three operational levels—county hospitals, subcounty hospitals and health centres—and the top four PrEP-enrolling facilities within each level, totalling 12 facilities. Within these three categories, we further stratified facilities into high-enrolling and low-enrolling groups, forming six randomisation blocks with two facilities each going head-to-head. The randomisation process occurred publicly during a PrEP stakeholders’ meeting on 12 September 2023, where opaque bags containing red and blue balls were used. Facility representatives each selected a ball from the bag for their respective block, with red and blue groups formed accordingly to represent either the control arm or the intervention arm. A neutral individual, representative of the Director of Public Health in Kisumu County, drew a ball from the final bag to determine the arm assignment, that is, which colour group would represent the control arm, and which would be the intervention arm, ensuring an unbiased and systematic assignment of interventions across the participating facilities.

To mitigate contamination, separate technical assistance visits and feedback loops are maintained for each clinic. We will discourage direct facility-to-facility communication about implementation processes during the intervention phase. Shared learning sessions will only occur after the primary data collection period ends.

Blinding (masking)

This trial will be open-label, meaning that both participants and project personnel will be aware of the allocation to either the One-Stop PrEP delivery model (intervention) or usual care (control). Given the nature of the intervention, blinding providers or participants is not feasible, as the restructured clinic workflows in the one-stop arm will be obvious to both groups.

To minimise potential bias, although data collectors will be aware of the clinic allocations, they will follow standardised data collection protocols to ensure consistency across all sites. Additionally, efforts will be made to reduce bias through the use of objective outcome measures, such as PrEP refill data and tenofovir drug levels, which are less subjected to subjective interpretation. While blinding of participants and providers is not feasible, the data analysts conducting the primary statistical analyses will not be blinded to clinic allocation. However, the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) will be finalised prior to unblinding outcomes by study arm, and any subsequent changes to the analysis plan will be documented to ensure transparency and integrity of the analyses.

Consenting to research procedures

For all research-related activities (eg, cohort studies, interviews, questionnaires and blood draws), informed consent will be obtained by project (research) staff before any procedures begin. Both paper-based and electronic consenting methods will be available. For electronic consent, participants will review the consent form on a tablet, sign electronically and receive a paper copy if desired. This approach minimises paper storage needs.

A paper-based option will remain available for those who prefer it or in case of failed digital or network technical issues. For provider surveys, exit surveys or clients discontinuing PrEP, oral consent—an approach being used in public health facilities for PrEP delivery—will be used to reduce clinic time. These clients may also be contacted by phone for brief surveys and interviews. Oral consent will involve a clear explanation of procedures, and clients can ask questions. Participation in research is voluntary, and opting out will not affect access to PrEP or other services. Oral consent will be used for low-risk phone interviews following ethics committee approval. Participants will receive verbal explanations of study procedures, confidentiality and their rights. All oral consents will be documented in secure Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) logs, in accordance with national ethical guidelines.

Confidentiality will be prioritised, and only authorised personnel will have access to records. Participants in interviews will be compensated as per local ethical guidelines.

Data collection and management

Clinical records and programme data will be documented using standardised PrEP delivery tools approved by the Kenya MoH by existing facility staff as part of the standard of care services. Trained project staff will abstract individual medical and programme data, including demographics, HIV risk factors, PrEP initiation, follow-up visits, pharmacy pickup and adherence records. All data are entered into password-protected tablets daily and uploaded to web-based encrypted REDCap servers. We will utilise multiple data sources (table 1), including qualitative interviews, quantitative questionnaires and observation reports, to comprehensively understand PrEP delivery processes and identify implementation strategies. Additional details on study instruments are provided in online (online supplemental file 1).

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Science Research (CFIR)48 guided the development of data collection instruments and will further guide data analysis and reporting of findings. We will investigate relevant constructs within the five domains of CFIR: intervention characteristics (eg, adaptability, cost), outer setting (eg, MOH and county policies and resources), inner setting (eg, structural characteristics of the clinic, training needs, clinic culture and implementation climate, relative priority, clinic goals and feedback, leadership engagement, available resources), characteristics of individuals (eg, staff self-efficacy to deliver PrEP) and process (eg, planning, engaging, executing and reflecting, the emergence of champions). All data are securely stored, with regular quality control checks to monitor programme progress and facilitate continuous improvement. Evaluation of implementation outcomes is framed within the Proctor framework, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of the public health impact across multiple dimensions.

To minimise potential for measurement bias since clinic staff know the study arm, trained project staff—not clinic staff—will abstract data using standardised tools, and outcomes will be based on objective measures such as pharmacy refills and electronic medical records.

Project procedures and activities

Project activities are outlined in table 2. The project is comprised of two complementary components: (1) programme activities and (2) research activities. The programme component involves continuous QI and is performed entirely by existing Kenya MoH healthcare providers as part of standard-of-care PrEP services. The research project staff have no direct contact with clients participating in the programme but only abstract programme records to document PrEP initiations and refills. The research component includes the nested observational cohort, objective assessment of adherence and administration of quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews. This component will require written informed consent for study participation in the research procedures and is conducted by project-dedicated staff.

PrEP programme delivery optimisation

Within the large cluster-randomised trial, standard PrEP services—counselling, HIV testing, prescription, dispensing, safety assessment and follow-up—will be provided by the existing public health staff at all 12 clinics as part of the standard of care. Guided by Proctor et al’s guidelines for operationalising implementation strategies,49 we will use multifaceted implementation strategies to optimise one-stop and continuous QI. The core implementation strategies to optimise one-stop intervention will include training of frontline providers, ongoing technical assistance, and stakeholder engagement. Project-hired staff will implement all these procedures (ie, actors) and clinic managers, providers and MOH/county health officials will be the respective target of action.

This study is best classified as a type 2 hybrid effectiveness-implementation design, as we evaluate both intervention outcomes (eg, PrEP continuation) and implementation processes (eg, fidelity, acceptability) concurrently. We will use implementation outcome frameworks (eg, Proctor and CFIR) to assess the independent contribution of implementation strategies.

Training of healthcare providers

The success of the project will hinge on comprehensive training and technical support for healthcare providers. Project staff, in collaboration with MoH, National AIDS and STI Control Programme (NASCOP) and the Kisumu County Health Team, will facilitate NASCOP-approved facility-based PrEP training sessions at each participating clinic to ensure alignment with MoH National guidelines. These 3-day, 9-hour training sessions will cover key topics such as PrEP introduction, service provider toolkits, clinical case management, PrEP commodity management and monitoring and evaluation tools. Pretests and post-tests will be administered to assess knowledge gains, with scores shared to foster QI discussions.

Technical assistance

Clinic-specific PrEP delivery models will be documented, and frontline providers will engage in system analysis and improvement, including clinic flow mapping. Ongoing training and technical assistance will be provided, with technical assistants conducting structured visits to mentor and observe PrEP implementation. Rapid-cycle analysis will inform summary reports, which will highlight changes and outcomes, facilitating the sharing of best practices among clinics for potential adoption by MoH policymakers.

Stakeholder engagement

Engagement with stakeholders—including facility staff, in-charges, MoH, county leadership and PrEP implementing partners—will continue throughout implementation to optimise the One-Stop PrEP Model. Regular stakeholder meetings with clinics, county health officials and partners, as well as participation in national PrEP technical working groups, will guide the refinement of the one-stop model and its implementation outcomes. This approach will leverage extensive experience with the Kenya health system and previous successful PrEP implementation studies.47

Clinic flow mapping and QI

We will conduct a comprehensive review of existing PrEP delivery systems at all 12 clinics to identify bottlenecks and opportunities for improvement.50 51 During the preparatory phase, providers involved in PrEP delivery will participate in clinic flow mapping and QI activities, including system analysis and strategy implementation. These QI strategies, informed by the Partner Scale-up project,47 52 will be repeated every 6 months. An HIV prevention cascade tool for PrEP will also be utilised to assess system performance.51 Data abstracted from clinics and quarterly technical assistant reports will allow for monitoring progress and identifying high and low-performing facilities.

Health economic evaluation

We will conduct activity-based microcosting of One-Stop PrEP delivery in the intervention arm and the ones in the control arm to assess costs associated with both delivery strategies. Programme costs are collected from the project budget, public health clinic budgets, government reports and the health economics literature, as we have done in the previous work.1353,56 Client costs associated with attending clinic visits will be estimated using client questionnaires, and time and motion studies will be conducted annually to assess client wait time and staff time spent on PrEP provision activities (table 3). Indirect costs will include client productivity losses (eg, time off work), transport costs and waiting time, estimated via user exit surveys and time-motion data. These will be incorporated into cost-effectiveness analyses from a societal perspective.

We will input incremental costs and project effectiveness outcomes into an HIV dynamic transmission model to simulate the health and budget impact of One-Stop PrEP provision on HIV incidence. The HIV transmission simulation model will use previously validated parameters for transmission risk, ART coverage and PrEP effectiveness, adapted from prior models used in Kenya.13 53 57 Key assumptions and sensitivity ranges will be provided in supplementary materials on publication.

We will estimate the intervention’s undiscounted incremental cost and budget impact on the Kenya MoH and Kisumu County budgets annually over a 5-year time horizon. We will follow standard practices,57 including the guidelines by the Panel of Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine.58

Research procedures

Beyond PrEP programme delivery optimisation, additional research will be conducted to understand PrEP users’ and providers’ perspectives on the one-stop intervention and service satisfaction. Research components will include (1) a nested cohort of PrEP users, (2) quantitative surveys with users and providers, (3) blood draws at a subset of visits and (4) qualitative interviews with users, providers and key informants to identify implementation facilitators and barriers. All research activities will be conducted by project staff.

The nested observational cohort

Up to 300 newly initiated PrEP clients will be enrolled into a nested observational cohort at selected clinics for detailed, longitudinal data collection on HIV prevention behaviours. Recruitment will be stratified by age, gender and serodiscordant status, allowing for comprehensive evaluation and subgroup analysis. Participants will undergo regular visits for HIV and STI testing, blood collection for tenofovir testing and surveys on sexual behaviour and PrEP adherence. Weekly phone follow-ups will collect data on prevention behaviours. In-depth interviews with a subset of participants will provide insights into motivations for PrEP use. All procedures will be conducted by project staff, and participants will be compensated for their time.

Provider surveys

Brief surveys will be administered to clinic managers and providers to assess clinic-level factors influencing implementation readiness and processes, including workload, quality of care, service impacts and job satisfaction. Intervention acceptability and appropriateness among providers will be measured using Wiener’s psychometric tool.59

Programme user surveys

Quantitative surveys will be conducted with a subset of clients as they exit clinics to gather data on user satisfaction, demographics, risk behaviour, risk perception and opportunity cost. This information will be used to inform continuous QI activities and enhance service delivery.

Blood drug levels for PrEP adherence

A key measure of PrEP programme success will be adherence. A subset of PrEP users, randomly selected during visits, will provide DBSs for tenofovir testing to assess adherence. Written consent will be obtained before sample collection. Adherence will be objectively measured by drug detection in only 200 DBS samples among only those returning for refills in the programme, which limits generalisability to those not returning or frequently cycle on or off PrEP. However, random selection visits for DBS sampling will help minimise any potential for selection for those who come for refills. Additionally, we will triangulate findings from surveys in the nested cohort to explore reasons for discontinuation and explore representativeness across important subgroups.

Qualitative component

Up to 60 qualitative interviews will be conducted with PrEP clients, focusing on their motivations and behaviours related to healthcare access, PrEP use and discontinuation. The sample will include diverse user profiles, and interviews will be conducted in the participants’ preferred language. Additionally, up to 120 key stakeholders—including providers, clinic leaders and policymakers—will participate in interviews or focus group discussions to assess implementation barriers and facilitators. Providers will discuss workload, self-efficacy and opportunities for efficiency. All qualitative data will be transcribed and analysed to inform adjustments and final reporting.

Costing and budget impact analysis

Time and motion observation of intervention activities will be conducted to inform staff time necessary for the One-Stop PrEP intervention. A research assistant (RA) will collect data on the time and motion of patient/provider interactions to inform cost estimates. The RA will also conduct a survey with healthcare providers to assess daily responsibilities associated with the intervention. Research time (eg, administering project questionnaires) and other research costs will be removed from programmatic costs. We will estimate incremental programme costs for PrEP provision through a one-stop service pathway. Using the intervention effectiveness and programme cost estimates, we will model the budget impact of implementing the One-Stop PrEP into the health system over a 5-year time horizon.

Data analysis

We will use a convergent mixed-methods approach to integrate qualitative and quantitative findings. Themes from interviews will be mapped onto quantitative results (eg, triangulating reasons for discontinuation), with findings synthesised during interpretation using a joint display matrix.

Quantitative data

Primary quantitative outcomes will be PrEP initiation and continuation as measured by PrEP refill. Secondary outcomes include adherence as measured by drug levels in the blood, and acceptability and related Likert scale measures in provider and client exit surveys.

The statistical analysis of primary outcomes will be intention-to-treat comparisons between intervention and control arms using individual-level data, performing both non-inferiority and superiority comparisons. Binary outcomes of PrEP continuation and drug detection in the blood (adherence) will be described as proportions and compared between arms using relative risk regression through modified Poisson models, which use generalised estimating equations robust SE methods to modify the Poisson model for binary outcomes as well as accounting for clustering by clinic.60 Time-to-event outcomes, such as time not missing any refill, will be analysed using survival analysis via Cox proportional hazards model. Any adjustment of comparisons between groups will be outlined in the study’s SAP (SAP) prior to unblinding. Key potential covariates will include age, gender, facility type and baseline PrEP uptake rates. A descriptive analysis of adaptations to fit one-stop to context will be performed. A descriptive analysis of HIV prevention behaviours will be performed for the nested cohort, overall, and by key subgroups. SAS or R will be utilised for analysis.

Qualitative data

Interviews will be recorded, transcribed and translated into English, ensuring accuracy by cross-checking a sample of the translated transcripts. Qualitative analysis will focus on identifying key system factors, operational aspects of one-stop clinics and implementation outcomes within the CFIR framework.48 61 Themes will be identified using a blend of conventional and directed thematic analysis.62 An iterative process involving multiple readings, coding and comparison of transcripts will be employed, facilitated by Dedoose (www.dedoose.com),63 a secure cloud-based qualitative analysis platform. Intervention mapping will be utilised to map strategies to determinants, and descriptive information will be collected to characterise facility-level innovations and opportunities for improvement.

The project is overseen by an independent Data Monitoring Committee that meets every 6 months, with the flexibility for ad hoc meetings when needed.

Patient and public involvement

PrEP clients and the public played a significant role in shaping the design of the One-Stop PrEP Care project, building on insights from the pilot study. During the pilot, PrEP clients and healthcare providers were engaged through qualitative interviews and surveys, which provided crucial feedback on the feasibility and acceptability of the one-stop model. PrEP clients expressed a preference for reducing clinic wait times and minimising the number of consultations across multiple rooms, which directly informed the trial’s approach to streamlining PrEP services. Providers also contributed by identifying operational bottlenecks and suggesting improvements to enhance service delivery, making the One-Stop PrEP Model more patient-centred and efficient.

In terms of dissemination, the trial will involve PrEP clients and the broader public in sharing the results. Community meetings, annual forums with healthcare providers and collaborations with local health departments will ensure that findings are communicated effectively. This participatory approach will ensure that the trial’s impact is felt not only at the policy level but also by the individuals it aims to serve.

The project was activated on 26 September 2023, and implementation is currently ongoing and expected to end in July 2027.

Discussion

This project aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the One-Stop PrEP Care pathway to improve PrEP service delivery in Kenya’s public health facilities. Previous research supports the use of integrated service delivery models in HIV prevention and care. Studies from Uganda and Kenya have demonstrated the efficacy of one-stop shop models in improving retention in ART programmes and reducing clinic wait times.29 43 Our project builds on these findings by exploring their application in PrEP delivery. We will also rigorously assess whether the model enhances client satisfaction, improves adherence and reduces programme and personal opportunity costs—key indicators of successful health interventions.40

This work has several strengths: first, the pragmatic, real-world setting will allow for a robust evaluation of the model’s scalability and sustainability. Second, we utilise different data sources and frameworks that will help discern pathways and mechanisms that enable implementation. The project also has limitations. Key outcomes of PrEP continuation and initiation are based on abstracted medical records data, and we recognise this as an important limitation. However, we posit that a robust study of the effective integration and sustainability of PrEP programs in African settings can only be tested within and using existing staff and systems. Despite this limitation, the integration of technical assistance and rapid-cycle evaluation will help mitigate such risks, ensuring data integrity and consistent implementation across sites.64 The current focus for the one-stop model is oral PrEP. However, our approach is robust and if during the project period, injectable PrEP becomes available as Kenya’s standard of care, we will adapt to add evaluation of how one-stop fits its delivery. Additionally, frequent provider turnover and retention may likely impact adaptation across the assessed clinics.

In conclusion, this work will advance client-centred PrEP delivery in innovative ways, aiming for greater efficiency, reduced workload, diminished client burden and better PrEP outcomes—allowing for human and financial resources to be directed to other key health areas. Upon completion of this project, we will have implemented and optimised the One-Stop PrEP Model and documented its successes and challenges.
