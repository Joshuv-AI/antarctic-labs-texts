Electrocatalytic performance of PdNi bimetallic alloys on CNFs for glucose oxidation
Authors: Muhammed Bekmezci, Mehmet Selcuk Erdogan, Ramazan Bayat, Fatih Sen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
It is imperative to utilize nanotechnology in body-integrated sensors and fuel cell technologies. In accordance with these two purposes, this study focused on developing a suitable nanocatalyst structure to contribute to both glucose sensors and direct glucose fuel cell research. We primarily produced carbon nanofiber (CNF) structures using the electrospinning method. Carbon nanofiber-supported bimetallic palladium/nickel nanoalloy (PdNi@CNF) with high electrocatalytic activity was produced by the chemical reduction method to replace platinum-based nanostructures. CNF-supported PdNi structures were revealed by scanning electron microscope (SEM), and the average fiber diameter was calculated as 35.86 nm. In this study, this PdNi@CNF nanostructure was produced as an electrocatalyst for non-enzymatic glucose sensing (NEGS) and direct glucose fuel cell (DGFC) applications. It was modified by the drop-casting method on a glassy carbon electrode (GCE). The (Limit of Detection) LOD value for the PdNi@CNF structure was 5.93 mM, and the (Limit of Quantification) LOQ value was 17.95 mM. The PdNi@CNF electrode exhibits a low detection limit, high selectivity, short response time for NEGS, long-term stability, and high current value for DGFC applications. Briefly, an ideal catalyst that can operate in low alkaline environments and that can be used in NEGS and DGFC structures has been synthesized in this work. The results have shown that it can be used as a body-integrated sensor structure and a long-term stable catalyst in future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22335-1.

Introduction

Although glucose is the most basic energy source for living organisms, it is also known that abnormal glucose levels in the blood can cause serious harm to human health, such as diabetes mellitus and heart disease1. Consequently, glucose detection is critical in managing hyperglycemia-related disorders such as diabetes. Electrochemical glucose sensors are among the most accurate techniques to measure blood glucose because they can measure glucose concentration directly based on the oxidation currents of the glucose flowing through the electrodes2.

Sensors made of bimetallic alloys for the electrochemical determination of glucose are considered promising nanomaterials due to their high sensitivity, better performance in glucose oxidation, stability, and cost-effectiveness3. Compared with platinum-based catalysts, cheaper and widely available palladium has been utilized as a substitute for platinum in various electrochemical sensor applications4. Moreover, palladium bimetallic alloys, instead of pure palladium, possess enhanced electrochemical activities because the bimetallic alloys can facilitate the desorption of molecule species, resulting in more surface site availability and thus higher catalytic activity5.

Obtaining clean and renewable energy from biomass has also been the main subject of many studies conducted in recent decades6. Additionally, among a wide range of biomasses, glucose can be considered a good candidate for use in Glucose/O2 biofuel cells, due to its non-toxicity, non-flammability, easily availability, theoretical high energy density, and stability7. Direct glucose fuel cells (DGFCs) can be used as implantable power and power sources in many devices by producing electricity by oxidizing glucose8. Electrode catalysts affect the efficiency of the glucose oxidation reaction (GOR). The GOR is essential for glucose sensors and fuel cells1. Generally, two types of electrode catalysts are used for GOR, namely, enzymatic and non-enzymatic9. Enzymes are used as catalysts in conventional glucose biosensors and biofuel cells, but their stability is low, and their operation is complicated due to the nature of enzymes10. Enzymatic fuel cells also have disadvantages such as short life span, low energy density, and high enzyme cost11. To overcome the disadvantages of enzymatic electrodes, various non-enzymatic glucose sensors and fuel cells using metal nanomaterials as catalysts have been designed12.

Several approaches have been used to improve the electrochemical performance of catalysts in NEGS and DGFC applications. One of them is the use of bimetallic alloy nanoparticle (NP) electrocatalysts, which have recently attracted much interest due to their demonstrated superior catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability over monometallic NPs13. Among these pairs, PdNi stands out because it exhibits excellent electrocatalytic performance in GOR, is stable, and has a lower cost than Pt14. Various approaches have been employed to enhance the electrochemical performance of catalytic materials in NEGS and DGFC applications. One of them is the use of bimetallic alloy nanoparticle (NP) electrocatalysts, which have recently attracted much interest due to their demonstrated superior catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability over monometallic NPs15.

A fascinating development in nanoscience that has been receiving continuous attention in science and technology is carbon nanomaterials (CNM)16. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene quantum dots (GQDs), graphene oxide (GO), fullerenes, carbon nanofibers (CNFs), and carbon sheets (CS) are examples of CNMs that can be easily explored for biological applications due to their wide range17. Sumio Iijima realized in 1991 that carbon arc discharge forms multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs). Later, Sumio Iijima and Donald Bethune created single-walled CNTs18. Graphene, fullerenes, CNFs, amorphous carbon, and carbon nanobulbs are examples of CNMs with nanoscale dimensions that represent different structures and are allotropes of a single carbon element19. According to studies, carbon structures can be used for a range of biological applications, including drug administration, biosensors, cancer treatment, tissue engineering, infection and disease diagnosis, energy release and conservation, and diagnostic imaging20–22.

Also, in such electrocatalyst applications, CNMs such as CNFs23, CNTs24, and graphene (GR)25,26 are used to support these platforms consisting of metal NPs, due to their ability to provide NP dispersion with their large surface areas, mass transfer with their porous structures, and good charge transfer with their high electrical conductivity27. Recently, CNFs have attracted significant attention due to their desirable mechanical properties, outstanding physical and chemical resistance, and superior electrical and thermal conductivity28.

Herein, we synthesized a stable and durable CNF-supported bimetallic PdNi catalyst with high electrocatalytic activity for NEGS and DGFC applications. The PdNi@CNF modified electrode exhibited excellent performance, showing high sensitivity, stability, and durability in the NEGS application and an improved power density in the DGFC application. At the same time, the electrochemical sensor and anodic reaction results obtained have yielded important insights for future studies to demonstrate the sensitivity of glucose in different environments and to highlight its energization from environments, including wastewater, where glucose is present.

Materials & method

Materials

All chemicals are analytical grade. Palladium chloride (PdCl2), nickel(II) acetate tetrahydrate (Ni(OCOCH3)2.4H2O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), polyacrylonitrile, glucose, dopamine, oxalic acid, uric acid, fructose and dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Nafion D-125 was purchased from Alfa Aesar and used as received without any purification.

Synthesis of CNF

CNFs were synthesized using the electrospinning method, which is a facile and fast method29. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and lignin were mixed in DMF at a 1:1 (w/w) ratio for 24 h to form a 10% solution. The resulting polymer solution filled a medical syringe (10 mL 0.80 × 38 mm 21G). The fully automated electrospinning machine (Nanoweb 101) was set to 20 kV voltage and 1 mL/hour pump speed to obtain nanofibres. An aluminium-coated plate was connected to the electrospinner to create an electric field. The resulting fibers were deposited on this foil. The produced nanofibers were pre-annealed in a muffle furnace at 250 °C for 90 min at an increasing rate of 1 °C/min. The pre-annealed nanofibers were then heated in a tube furnace (Protherm ASP) under an N2 atmosphere to 1000 °C for 60 min. at a rate of 2 °C/min, carbonized, and then gradually cooled. The resulting CNFs were used without any external cleaning process.

Synthesis of PdNi@CNF

The electrocatalysts used in NEGS and DGFC studies were obtained by the NaBH4 chemical reduction method, which is easy, fast, and low-cost30. For the solution; 10 mM PdCl2 and 10 mM Ni(OCOCH3)2.4H2O in purified water were mixed on a magnetic stirrer for 30 min. Subsequently, 40 mg of CNF was added onto the metal salts mixture. Then, 10 mM NaBH4 is added, and the mixture is stirred for another 60 min at room temperature. A visible color change occurred in the solution. Then, after the synthesis, it was washed by centrifuging at 4000 rpm for 15 min to eliminate unreduced particles and remove unwanted structures. This process was repeated three times. The final pellet obtained was dried for 24 hours in a protected environment at 45 °C.

Characterization

The prepared materials were characterized by using Raman Spectroscopy (Renishaw inVia Raman Microscope), X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku Miniflex), scanning electron microscopy (JEOL JEM-1220), and electrochemical characterization Gamry Reference 3000.

Electrochemical measurements

Cyclic voltammetry (CV), chronoamperometry, differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) methods were used to calculate the electrochemical performance of the prepared materials. At the same time, 500-cycles and scan rate (SR) tests were also performed to assess stability and durability. Glassy carbon electrodes (GCE) modified with the prepared catalyst were used as working electrodes, Ag/AgCl as reference electrode (RE), and a platinum plate as counter electrode (CE). We modified the GCEs by dropping 10 µL of the active material onto them, which had been previously polished using 1.0, 0.3, and 0.05-micron alumina, respectively. The GCEs were then rinsed with deionized water and ethanol and dried at room temperature. GCE modification solutions were created by adding 37.5 µL of DMF and 75 µL of Nafion D125 solution to 1 mg of each of PdNi@CNF and then sonicated for 30 min. Before each dripping, the solution was vortexed, and then the GCEs were placed in a protected oven and dried at 45 °C.

Results and discussion

Materials characterizations

Firstly, material characterization analyses were performed for the study. Raman, XRD, and SEM were used in the analyses. Analyses were conducted to examine the material’s chemical structure and morphological properties.

PdNi@CNF nanostructure was obtained using the chemical reduction method as stated. Diffraction peaks for Pd, Ni, and CNF structures in the resulting nanoarchitecture were examined. The diffraction peaks obtained are shown in Fig. 1a. XRD patterns reveal the bulk structure of the catalyst and CNF support. Approximate 2θ values corresponding to Pd(111), Pd(200), (220), and (311) crystal planes were observed as 39.65⁰, 45.40⁰, 67.06⁰, and 80.53⁰31,32. These diffraction peaks are characteristic of a face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure of PdNi (PDF# 46–1043). In the XRD analysis, a partial shift to the right occurred for palladium due to alloy structures. The peak values ​​of the shifts for Ni remained quite limited. The result shows that Pd and Ni metals were deposited on a carbon carrier. These peaks appear in both metal phase reflections and CNF, C (002) diffraction values around 2θ = 25° at a significant rate, and the broad slope obtained can be attributed to the presence of CNF structure33,34. This value, also known as the CNF structure, was confirmed by Raman analysis and SEM analysis. Using the Debye-Scherrer formula, the average size of the PdNi crystallites is estimated to be 3.8 nm. This value can be expressed as a substantially good crystal size compared to the literature. As another analysis, Raman spectroscopy was performed. Raman measurements of the PdNi@CNF nanostructure were further used to infer the structural composition, as shown in Fig. 1b. The characteristic D and G bands of CNF are seen at 1344 cm−1 and 1592 cm−1 these peak values can be attributed to the defective structure of carbon materials. The D band is thought to be one of the disordered sp3 defect regions. Additionally, the D band can be attributed to defects and irregularities in the carbon structure. The ratio of the peak values of the D and G bands can determine the degree of graphitization of carbon materials. In this case, these defects will act as active sites, strengthening the interaction between Pd particles and the support, thereby increasing the catalyst loading and particle dispersion. The ID/IG ratio of CNF and PdNi@CNF is 0.98 and 1.15, respectively. The increase in the D/G ratio indicates that the amorphous degree of PdNi-modified CNF increases, thus increasing the defects on the CNF surface35,36.

We carried out electrochemical analyses at appropriate scanning intervals following the reaction. We started with Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis as the first analysis. CV measurements were performed to evaluate the performance of GOR for CNF-supported PdNi nanostructure. As shown in Figs. 4a and b, due to the low hydroxide concentration and high oxidation peak resulting from water partitioning, there is no obvious limiting current value in the potential range of − 0.2 to 1 V, representing the oxidation of the CNF-supported PdNi nanostructure. Additionally, a significant current increase is observed compared to the blank electrode (no catalyst coating). In the study, it can be said that the potential onset begins around 0.6 V. In GOR reactions, it can be said that the more the potential onset shifts to the left, the more significant a catalyst is obtained. At the same time, the considerable loading/sensing range of the catalyst is also very important. From the CV cycle obtained, it was seen that a significant value of 5.27 mA.cm−2 was obtained in the forward scan current (If). Similarly, the back current (Ib) was measured as −6.197 mA.cm−2 in the mechanism that occurs as a reversible reaction. A measure of catalyst poisoning was measured as If/Ib −0.850. This value can be interpreted as indicating that the poisoning of the catalyst is limited. Significant current increases were observed when 1 mM Glucose was added, indicating that the PdNi@CNF sensor detected GOR.

However, it is essential to note that non-enzymatic glucose sensors that utilize the electrocatalytic oxidation of glucose are currently in intense research, as mentioned before. The responses of GCE electrodes modified with PdNi@CNF to glucose at a potential of 0.4 V in 0.1 M NaOH aqueous medium are given. As shown in Fig. 4c, there is an increase in constant increasing or changing concentrations. These results were necessary for the sensitivity of glucose in weakly alkaline environments. It was also promising for reducing platinum-related costs used in sensors and fuel cells. There are several important benefits to the electrochemical determination of glucose in an alkaline environment. The easier oxidation of glucose molecules in alkaline solutions (usually using bases such as NaOH or KOH) provides a greater current response at the electrode surface. This is primarily due to OH⁻ ions, which promote the electrochemical reaction mechanism and the development of active sites on the electrode surface. Furthermore, significant catalytic activity for glucose oxidation is provided by alkaline conditions, which favor the growth of oxide/hydroxide phases, especially on the surface of nickel and cobalt-based electrode materials. In developing body surface sensor structures, neutral applications are preferred, but current densities are generally low. An important analysis for sensor applications is chronoamperometric methods. These methods provide very important information about the stability of the material and its meaningful sensing ability. CA analysis of the PdNi@CNF anode we used in our study was measured by sequentially adding 50 µL of glucose in 0.1 M NaOH buffer solution at an applied potential of 100 mV. Figure 5a shows stepwise ladder programs upon sequential glucose addition. At the beginning of the analysis, measurements were started after waiting a certain period for the intracellular environment to be stable. This process was very important to avoid unnecessary noise and a meaningless sense of competence. Following the increasing and sequential addition of Glucose, the oxidation current increased almost immediately, reaching a partially steady-state oxidation current within 30 s. The fast sensor response is attributed to the catalytic activity of PdNi@CNF, where electrons are immediately transported from the glucose electrolyte to the electrode surface. Moreover, oxidation currents increased with increasing glucose concentration. Furthermore, Fig. 5b shows the correlation obtained from the concentration versus current values obtained in the respective CA analysis. According to the calibration curve, R2 = 0.96, and the slope of the line was approximately 0.04638. The calibration curve showed that a sensor structure was obtained that revealed a linear relationship in current in response to increasing glucose rates. It can be said that the obtained current values and concentration rates are quite ideal compared to the literature.

Another important issue for sensor studies is the improved sensing ability against the target marker. Accordingly, it is necessary to define the target marker as a non-competitor and the non-target marker as a competitor and examine the oxidation reactions in the resulting sensor application. However, mixed analytes in biological fluids are at least 10 times lower than glucose. Accordingly, a selectivity map for PdNi@CNF was created, revealing a significant sensing structure, as shown in Fig. 7a. In the selectivity map, glucose ((Glu) 100 µl, 0.33 mM) non-competitive analyte and (50 µl, 0.17 mM) competing interfering analytes (uric acid (UA), ascorbic acid (AA), dopamine (DA), oxalic acid (OA)) at a potential of −0.1 V.) and fructose (Fru)) shows the current response curve upon addition. The response current increased sharply and reached a steady state for glucose. The addition of every other competing analyte remains at the noise level. Another point that we must emphasize in our study is that an important target, such as glucose, is evaluated as an electron-donating oxidizing analyte in fuel cells because of the target redox reaction. It has been shown that a stable formation for the PdNi@CNF structure has been demonstrated by utilizing CA methods, which is one of the most important elements in fuel cells. Accordingly, the CA analysis obtained in Fig. 7b, started with a high current value decreased rapidly. However, the PdNi@CNF catalyst never reached the zero-threshold value during the scanning period and remained at approximately 2.5 mA.cm−2 at the end of the period. This revealed an important result on the long-term stability of the obtained catalyst. In addition, chronoamperometry experiments performed on all catalysts showed that the 1:1 mol ratio catalyst has a lower poisoning rate and thus a greater tolerance to intermediate species formed during GOR compared to all other mole ratio catalysts. Incorporating well-dispersed PdNi particles in CNF can be interpreted as an acceleration of the GOR process in a lower potential region, which is evidence of the relatively larger GOR current of the catalyst and increased tolerance to intermediate species40.

Conclusions

The production of body-integrated sensor structures is becoming increasingly important. For these devices, glucose from body fluids in the device systems has reached a very important level. This study investigated both glucose sensing and the resulting sensor’s behavior in fuel cell-like structures. In the study, the CNF platform decorated with PdNi nanoparticles was characterized by SEM, XRD, and Raman spectroscopy methods. Particles dispersed on the CNF structure were observed in the obtained SEM images. XRD and Raman spectroscopy results showed crystalline and amorphous structural diffraction peaks in PdNi and CNF. In the second step, the electrocatalytic activity of PdNi@CNF nanomaterial for glucose oxidation was investigated. The PdNi@CNF catalyst showed better properties in both NEGS and DGFC applications. The linear range of the sensor was calculated to be 1.66 mM − 11 mM. The catalyst continued to function in the oxidation process in long-term tests without disrupting its stable structure. The results identified PdNi@CNF as a promising catalyst for NEGS and DGFC applications. This study provided important insights into the search for cost-effective and effective catalysts to replace platinum-based electrochemical catalysts. The study has provided essential insights into the usability of the resulting structure in both sensor applications and fuel cells. At the same time, the manufacturability of the CNF structure has been demonstrated, and its usability in sensor and fuel cell studies has been emphasized. In this direction, contributions have been made to the literature on developing body-integrated sensor and implant structures.
