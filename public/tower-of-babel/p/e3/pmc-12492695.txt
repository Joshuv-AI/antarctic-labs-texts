Polycythemia and its determinants among children with unoperated cyanotic congenital heart disease at Tikur Anbessa specialized hospital, Ethiopia: observational cross-sectional study
Authors: Lelise Kaba, Henok Tadele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycythemia is a physiologic adaptive response to hypoxia seen in children with cyanotic congenital heart disease (Cyanotic CHD). Globally, due to timely cyanotic CHD interventions, polycythemia is underreported or understudied. Timely identification and treatment of polycythemia among unoperated cyanotic CHDs avoids serious hematologic and related complications. This study aimed to determine the burden and factors associated with polycythemia among patients with cyanotic CHD enrolled in chronic care.MethodsThis observational cross-sectional study used a semi-structured questionnaire to collect relevant sociodemographic and clinical data. Children with cyanotic CHD aged 1 month to 18 years were included. Polycythemia was considered when the hemoglobin was ≥ 17 g/dl on the complete blood count report of an automated hematology analyzer. Bivariate and multivariable binary logistic regression models were used to identify associated variables.ResultsIn this study, 384 patients with cyanotic CHD were included, 55.2% males. The median age of study participants was 42 months (Interquartile range/IQR: 20.3–91.5). The median altitude of residence and age at cyanotic CHD diagnosis were 2355 m (IQR: 1826–2470) and 4 months (IQR: 1–12), respectively. Polycythemia was documented in 38% (146) [95% CI: 33.1–43.1] of participants. Males had almost doubled odds of polycythemia, aOR = 1.66 (95% CI: 1.01–2.70). Children with oxygen saturation < 70 and 70–90 had almost tripled odds of polycythemia, aOR 2.71 (95% CI: 1.25–5.88) and aOR 2.79 (1.46–5.33), respectively. Moreover, children with tricuspid atresia had tripled odds of polycythemia, aOR 3.20 (95% CI: 1.19–8.55). Additionally, children who had no phlebotomies in the last six months had reduced odds of polycythemia, aOR 0.07(0.02–0.22).ConclusionThe burden of polycythemia among patients with cyanotic CHD is huge. Male sex, saturation level, diagnosis of tricuspid atresia and phlebotomy frequency were associated with polycythemia among patients with cyanotic CHD. Background

Cyanotic congenital heart disease (Cyanotic CHD) is defined as heart lesions resulting in cyanosis owing to right-sided heart obstructive lesions, left heart obstructive lesions, and mixing lesions [1]. According to the 2017 Global Burden of Diseases report, congenital heart disease (CHD) affects close to 12 million people worldwide, with 261,247 deaths. CHD mortality was highest in low-income countries with high fertility rates and poor resources to care for these patients [2]. Tetralogy of Fallot(TOF) and transposition of great arteries are the two most common cyanotic CHDs [1].In Ethiopia, CHD is the second most common cardiac disease among cardiac patients enrolled in chronic care, with TOF being the most common cyanotic CHD [3].

Polycythemia, secondary erythrocytosis evidenced by increased red blood cell mass, is usually caused by increased erythropoietin levels among children with cyanotic CHD [4]. It is a physiologic adaptive response to chronic hypoxia among patients with cyanotic CHD [5]. Whereas polycythemia helps in improving the oxygen-carrying capacity, it also has deleterious effects, including hyperviscosity, hyperuricemia, iron deficiency anemia, bleeding disorders and thrombosis. Phlebotomy, which aims at decreasing the red blood cell mass and hence these complications, is not routinely recommended. Presentation clinical symptoms, hydration status and iron deficiency should be assessed before doing phlebotomy [5–9]. Though there is no defined optimal hematocrit level for children with cyanotic CHD, phlebotomy targets a hematocrit below 65% and blood transfusion for associated iron-deficiency anemia hemoglobin of greater than 14 g/dl [10].

As polycythemia is a complication owing to years lived with untreated cyanotic CHD, it is not expected among cyanotic CHDs living in high-income countries [11–14]. Access to definitive cardiac care is not usually possible in Africa, particularly in Ethiopia. With a shortage of human resources and infrastructure, children with cyanotic CHD in these settings often develop polycythemia [15–19]. Data on polycythemia are scarce from Africa and related settings. Identification and timely treatment of polycythemia among unoperated cyanotic CHD avoids serious thromboembolic and related hematologic complications. Moreover, it will also help policymakers to design a strategy for health service access for patients with cyanotic CHD. Hence, this study aimed to identify the burden and determinants of polycythemia among majorly unoperated patients with cyanotic CHD enrolled in chronic cardiac care at the National Cardiac Referral Hospital of Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and study period

A cross-sectional study design with observational data collection was used between August 2023 and January 2024.

Study area

This study was conducted in Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), a national cardiac referral hospital, in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Pediatric Cardiologists confirm the diagnosis of cyanotic CHD using echocardiography. Additional imaging, like a CT scan, is considered when the complex cardiac anatomy necessitates that. TASH provides inpatient and outpatient pediatric cardiac services. The inpatient services include admissions in the emergency room and wards, general and cardiac intensive care units, cardiac catheterization rooms, and operating theatres. In Ethiopia, definitive healthcare access for cyanotic CHDs takes three routes: overseas mission-based, local team and abroad referral. Ethiopia, a country of over 120 million, has four local cardiac surgical centers. TASH is the only national public cardiac referral center, while the others are private or charity-based [16]. All children with cyanotic CHD receive an outpatient cardiac follow-up in TASH. The outpatient cardiac clinic is attended by pediatric cardiologists, pediatric cardiology fellows, pediatric residents, and trained nurses. There is no pulse oximetry screening practice for congenital heart disease in the study setup, and generally in Ethiopia.

Study population

The study population included all parents or guardians and their children with cyanotic CHD between the ages of one month and 18 years and enrolled in cardiac follow-up.

Sample size and sampling procedure

Using the assumptions of a 50% polycythemia among children with cyanotic CHD, 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and a power of 80, a sample size of 384 was calculated. A proportion of 50% was used due to the lack of powered studies on polycythemia among children with cyanotic CHD from similar settings. Due to the nature of the illness, cyanotic CHD, limiting the survival of children, all children coming to their cardiac follow-up were approached for inclusion in this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All consenting parents or guardians and their children with cyanotic CHD between the ages of one month and 18years with confirmed echocardiographic diagnosis, and enrolled in follow-up, were included. Study participants with no consent and incomplete initial echocardiographic data were excluded from the study.

Data collection

A semi-structured questionnaire was prepared to collect sociodemographic and clinical data (see Supplement 1). Sociodemographic and some clinical variables were collected from parents/guardians of children. Detailed clinical information was retrieved from the medical records of study participants. Venous blood samples were collected, and a complete blood count was analyzed using an automated Beckman Coulter hematology analyzer with UniCel DxH 800 Coulter Cellular System, USA. The laboratory reports were conducted and released following the standard operating procedures and quality checks of the hospital. Two BSc nurses, who were not working staff of the cardiac clinic and received one-day training, collected the sociodemographic and clinical data. The quality of data was checked daily by the investigator, and details of medical information were cross-checked from the electronic medical records of the patient.

Variables

Polycythemia was the dependent variable. The independent variables include age, sex, altitude of residence, family monthly income, age at cyanotic CHD diagnosis, history of any cyanotic CHD intervention, hospital admission history, any comorbidity, history of iron intake, frequency of phlebotomy, developmental delay, severe acute malnutrition, presenting symptom, pulse rate at presentation, respiratory rate at presentation, oxygen saturation at presentation, type of cyanotic CHD, white blood cell count at presentation, mean corpuscular volume(MCV) at presentation and platelet count at presentation.

Operational definitions

Though there is no agreed hemoglobin level for polycythemia among patients with cyanotic CHD, polycythemia was considered in this study when hemoglobin was 17gm/dl or more (19, 20). In line with the recommended practice [10], therapeutic phlebotomy is considered in the study center when the hematocrit is 65% or more with symptoms or when the hematocrit is 70% or more regardless of symptoms of polycythemia. The altitude of residence above sea level was classified as low when it is 2399.9 m or less, and high when it is 2400 m or more [21]. Delayed cyanotic CHD diagnosis was considered when the diagnosis was made after a hospital discharge of a neonate or any time after that [22]. Vital signs were assessed based on age and sex and were grouped as normal or elevated during analysis.

Data analysis

Analysis was done on Statistical Software for Social Sciences Version 25. A median with an interquartile range was used to report continuous variables. Bivariate and multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify variables associated with polycythemia. Variables with a p value less than 0.05 on bivariate analysis were fitted into the final multivariable binary logistic regression analysis model to check for confounders. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Variables associated with polycythemia were assessed using an odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical data of the study participants

Demographic characteristics

A total of 396 study participants were approached, and 12 were excluded (6 due to non-consent and 6 due to incomplete initial echocardiographic data). Finally, a total of 384 study participants with cyanotic CHD, with a slight male predominance, 212(55.2%), were included in this study. The median age was 42 months (IQR: 20.3–91.5). The median altitude of residence was 2355.5 m (IQR: 1826.3–2470). The lowest altitude of residence was 385 m from an Afar region, while the highest altitude of residence, 3900 m, was from the Gurage administration. The majority had a monthly family income between 5,000 and 10,000 Ethiopian Birr, 229 (59.6%) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study on children with majorly unoperated cyanotic CHD documented a high burden of polycythemia, 38%. There was a delayed cyanotic CHD diagnosis. Males had doubled odds of polycythemia. Children with low saturation of oxygen levels, 70–90%, had tripled odds of polycythemia. Moreover, children with tricuspid atresia had tripled odds of polycythemia. Cyanotic CHD children with no phlebotomies in the last 6 months had reduced odds of polycythemia. The altitude of residence didn’t show any association with polycythemia.

In this study, the age at cyanotic CHD diagnosis is delayed with a median age of 4 months. Other studies from low-and middle-income countries documented a high burden of delayed CHD diagnosis; delay is most common with cyanotic CHD [23, 24]. As any diagnosis of cyanotic CHD after a hospital discharge is considered a delayed diagnosis [22], due attention and institution of focused antenatal care surveillance, newborn screening and further expert evaluation are recommended to avoid delayed cyanotic CHD diagnosis [25–27]. Hence, prevention of polycythemia and other cyanotic CHD complications is crucial.

In the current study, polycythemia was documented in 38% of patients with cyanotic CHD. This is lower than a single report from Ethiopia, 63% [19]. However, the latter study included only small study participants. In totality, both studies have documented a significant burden of polycythemia among unoperated children with cyanotic CHD. An adaptive response for cyanosis with the release of erythropoietin is a postulated reason for polycythemia [4]. The median age of our study participants was 3.5 years. The burden could accumulate and increase if follow-up studies are designed. Whereas conducting further mixed studies, uncovering reasons for delayed presentation and delayed diagnosis are recommended, timely polycythemia consideration and management are recommended. The ultimate solution of definitive service access awaits the attention of key stakeholders [15, 16].

Males with cyanotic CHD had increased odds of polycythemia in this study. A consistent pattern was documented in a review that showed a high occurrence of cyanotic CHD or severe CHD among males [28]. We didn’t anticipate sex to have a direct effect on the noted hemoglobin difference, as our study participants are relatively younger with a median age of 3.5 years. The noted hemoglobin difference is usually expected in children 8 years or older [29]. The effect of testosterone with increased erythropoietin can be considered as the main mechanism for increased hemoglobin in males [30]. The effect of sex on polycythemia among cyanotic CHD is an area that requires further detailed study.

The level of hypoxemia is directly associated with polycythemia among cyanotic CHD in this study. This is an anticipated finding as polycythemia is an adaptive response to hypoxemia. Moreover, our study participants are mainly unoperated cyanotic CHD cases with years lived with it. The only means to avoid hypoxemia among patients with cyanotic CHD is to do either palliative or definitive interventions [11–14]. This calls for routine availability of these services and hence prevention of polycythemia [15–17].

Tricuspid atresia cases had tripled odds of polycythemia. Tricuspid atresia is a cyanotic CHD with a complex anatomy requiring multiple staged palliative surgical interventions. The majority of tricuspid atresia cases will die in the first one-year of life without these palliative interventions. Survival is dependent on obligatory and adequate right-to-left and left-to-right shunts or adequate mixing [10, 11, 31]. Most tricuspid cases are aged 12 months or older in the current study. Adequate mixing and survival are associated with compensatory polycythemia for the degree of hypoxemia seen in these complex patients with cyanotic CHD. Further studies are recommended to better understand the pathophysiologic, hematologic and other predisposing abnormalities among children with tricuspid atresia.

Phlebotomy frequency and polycythemia showed a direct association in the current study. Though the hematocrit cutoff point for polycythemia is not at the level indicated for phlebotomy, the finding could point out an improved and recommended watchful practice of phlebotomy among patients with cyanotic CHD in the study setup [5, 10, 19]. Moreover, given the higher hemoglobin cutoff point for phlebotomy, study participants undergoing phlebotomy are likely to have polycythemia. No association was documented between the altitude of residence and polycythemia in this study. A relatively higher proportion of low-altitude landers in this study should be considered when interpreting this finding. Further studies focusing only on highlanders may be considered to assess the effect of high altitude on patients with cyanotic CHD.

The strengths of the study include a large number of participants, assessment of diverse variables including the altitude of residence, and a study on unoperated patients with cyanotic CHD. Some of the limitations include the lack of qualitative, continuous family income and serum iron studies data, and potential selection bias as children with cyanotic CHD who survived and showed up for their chronic follow-up were included in the study, limiting external generalizability and the cross-sectional nature of the study. However, the study documented a significant burden of polycythemia with associated variables among majorly unoperated patients with cyanotic CHD. This will help clinicians and healthcare system leadership in this and related settings to design means for timely evaluation, management and prevention of polycythemia among patients with cyanotic CHD.

In conclusion, this study documented a significant burden of polycythemia among cyanotic CHD patients. Male sex, saturation level, diagnosis of tricuspid atresia and phlebotomy frequency were associated with polycythemia among cyanotic CHD patients. Males with cyanotic CHD, children with tricuspid atresia, and cyanotic CHDs with saturation of oxygen below ninety should receive targeted screening for polycythemia during chronic follow-up.
