Choroidal vascularity index changes in proliferative and nonproliferative macular telangiectasia type 2
Authors: Zeynep Eylul Ercan, Gulsah Gokgoz, İmren Akkoyun, Gursel Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:This study aimed to evaluate the choroidal vascularity index (CVI) in patients with nonproliferative and proliferative macular telangiectasia type 2 (MacTel-2) compared to healthy controls, assess the impact of ellipsoid zone (EZ) disruption on CVI, and determine whether CVI measurements vary with different binarization areas.MATERIALS AND METHODS:CVIs from MacTel-2 eyes were compared to those of healthy controls. MacTel-2 eyes were categorized as proliferative or nonproliferative and further subdivided by the presence or absence of EZ disruption. CVI was measured in three regions: 1500 µm, 4000 µm, and the total macular choroidal area (TCA). Statistical analyses included paired samples t-tests, independent samples t-tests, and Mann–Whitney U-tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.RESULTS:This study included 90 eyes each from MacTel-2 patients and healthy controls. CVIs did not significantly differ between eyes with and without EZ disruption in either MacTel-2 group (P > 0.05). CVIs beneath EZ-disrupted areas were also comparable to intact zones (P > 0.05). No significant CVI difference was observed between nonproliferative and proliferative MacTel-2 eyes (P > 0.1). However, both the groups had significantly lower CVIs than healthy controls across all measured areas (P < 0.001 for nonproliferative and P < 0.05 for proliferative). CVI values were consistent across all binarization areas (P > 0.1).CONCLUSION:EZ disruption does not affect CVI. CVIs were similar in nonproliferative and proliferative MacTel-2, both lower than in healthy eyes. CVI also remained consistent across different binarization areas, confirming its reliability regardless of area size.

Introduction

Macular telangiectasia type 2 (MacTel-2) is a bilateral retinal disorder characterized by capillary abnormalities in the macula and progressive neurosensory atrophy. It typically manifests in individuals in their fifth to sixth decades of life, initially affecting the temporal area of the foveal center. As the disease advances, it may extend up to one disc diameter from the fovea, with a greater horizontal than vertical spread. Although generally bilateral, MacTel-2 may display asymmetry, with one eye showing milder involvement. Early stages are often marked by reduced retinal transparency on fundus examination. Clinical signs include crystalline deposits at the internal limiting membrane, ectatic capillaries, and blunted venules, followed by retinal pigment plaques and foveal atrophy.[1]

Neovascularization may develop, often following the appearance of blunted venules and intraretinal pigment plaques temporal to the foveola, though not necessarily in this order. These subretinal neovascular membranes are thought to originate from retinal vessels but may access the subretinal space and form chorioretinal shunts.[2] In later stages, thinning of the photoreceptor layer leads to decreased central neurosensory retinal thickness.[3] In the neovascularization phase, choroidal vessel communications have been observed.[4] As the disease progresses, it may lead to the formation of lamellar or full-thickness macular holes, accompanied by outer neurosensory atrophy, which is nourished by the choroid.[1] Optical coherence tomography (OCT) typically reveals inner and outer neurosensory retinal hyporeflective cavities, disruption of the ellipsoid zone (EZ), and increased inner retinal reflectivity. Although MacTel-2 is considered primarily a neurodegenerative retinal disease, changes in the choroid have been reported, including hyporeflective cavities in Sattler’s layer and choriocapillaris alterations on OCT angiography.[56]

The choroidal vascularity index (CVI) – a more nuanced metric than choroidal thickness – quantifies vascular and stromal components through image binarization of OCT scans. It evaluates vascular and stromal layers separately by employing image binarization of OCT images to compute vascular and stromal area ratios.[7] Previous MacTel-2 studies have shown reduced CVIs.[89]

This study aims to: (a) assess whether EZ disruption influences CVI, (b) compare CVIs between nonproliferative and proliferative MacTel-2, (c) evaluate differences between MacTel-2 and healthy eyes, and (d) determine whether varying the binarization area affects CVI measurements.

Materials and Methods

The study included 54 individuals diagnosed with MacTel-2, accounting for a total of 90 eyes, along with an age- and sex-matched control group with healthy eyes. Participants were retrospectively selected from patients who visited the Baskent University Ophthalmology Clinic between 2016 and January 2022. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the institutional review board of Baskent University Medicine and Health Sciences Research Board (approval number: KA18/201) and the authors confirm that they have obtained appropriate consent from the patient. All MacTel-2 cases were diagnosed and staged by the same examiner (GY). MacTel-2 eyes exhibiting significant scarring that could interfere with accurate CVI calculation were excluded. The MacTel-2 group was further divided into proliferative and nonproliferative subgroups. In addition, eyes were categorized based on the presence or absence of EZ loss, as observed in horizontal OCT scans centered on the fovea. A comprehensive ocular examination was performed for all participants. The control group consisted of individuals with no known ocular or systemic diseases. Exclusion criteria were the presence of any ocular disease other than MacTel-2, history of vitreomacular surgery, cataract surgery within the past year, or laser treatment, systemic diseases known to affect ocular tissues, including diabetes mellitus and MacTel-2 eyes with macular holes, as these may independently influence choroidal measurements. Refractive errors exceeding ± 4D were also not included since CVI decreases changes with major refractive error changes.[10]

Image acquisition and processing

The Heidelberg Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) system’s enhanced depth imaging mode was used to examine all eyes. The macular region was scanned with seven horizontal line scans centered at the fovea, averaging 100 frames per B-scans previously described by Agrawal et al.[7] All images taken for the study had a signal-to-noise ratio >20. Horizontal OCT scans centered on the central foveal region were selected for measurement. Image processing was performed using the Fiji image processing package and the method described by Agrawal et al.[7] The measurements included choroidal areas at distances of 1500 and 4000 microns (µ) from the fovea’s center, along with the total macular choroidal area (TCA). In cases with EZ loss, the length of the loss and the choroidal vascular index beneath the loss were calculated [Figure 1].

Interrater agreement and reliability

Two evaluators (ZEE, GG) evaluated and segmented the MacTel-2 images. Interrater agreement was assessed using the absolute agreement model of the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) through regression analysis. Interrater correlation was assessed using regression analysis, confirming the insignificance of differences (P = 0.857). The interexaminer ICC was 0.998, with a confidence interval of 0.997–0.999. Following the validation of ICC, one author (ZEE) performed all measurements.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows version 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Parametric tests were utilized for comparisons when normality tests (Shapiro–Wilk for the proliferative and Kolmogorov–Smirnov for nonproliferative) yielded P > 0.05. Repeated paired samples t-tests were used for CVI comparisons within the same group, and Levene’s test confirmed equal variances. For comparisons between EZ loss subgroups, the Mann–Whitney U-test was used due to significant Shapiro–Wilk test results. Independent samples t-tests were conducted for comparisons between MacTel-2 groups and the healthy group. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Comparison of the combined macular telangiectasia type 2 group with the control group

The mean age of the combined MacTel-2 group was 65.40 ± 9.62 years, with 30 male (30 eyes) and 60 female patients (60 eyes). The ages and sex ratio were statistically similar to the control group with P = 0.51 and 0.87, respectively. Spherical equivalence was 0.44 ± 1.48, which was statistically similar to the control group (P = 0.49). In the combined MacTel-2 group, 23 eyes had intraocular lenses, and there was no statistically significant difference in intraocular lens presence between the groups (P = 0.40). Visual acuity was 0.34 ± 0.33 LogMAR in the combined MacTel-2 group, which was significantly worse than the control group (P < 0.0001).

CVI values at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and total choroidal area were 62.83 ± 2.59%, 62.70 ± 3.14%, and 62.99 ± 2.90% respectively. In all cases, these CVIs were significantly reduced in the MacTel-2 group compared to the control group (P = 0.002, P = 0.003, and P = 0.004, respectively).

Comparison of nonproliferative and proliferative macular telangiectasia type 2 groups

Both the groups demonstrated similar sex distribution (P = 0.17), age (P = 0.06), spherical equivalence (P = 0.67), and intraocular lens presence (P = 0.54). Visual acuity was significantly worse in the proliferative group (P < 0.001).

No significant differences were seen in CVI at 1500 µ (P = 0.56), 4000 µ (P = 0.60), or for total choroidal area (P = 0.80) between these groups.

Comparison of nonproliferative macular telangiectasia type 2 group with the control group

Both groups displayed similar distribution of sex (P = 0.78), age (P = 0.20), spherical equivalence (P = 0.06), and intraocular lens presence (P = 0.38). Visual acuity was higher in the healthy group, as expected (P < 0.0001).

CVI values at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and total choroidal area were all significantly lower in the nonproliferative MacTel-2 group compared to healthy eyes (P = 0.006, P = 0.008, and P = 0.004, respectively).

Comparison of proliferative macular telangiectasia type 2 group with the control group

Both the groups had similar distributions of sex (P = 0.21), age (P = 0.21), spherical equivalence (P = 0.67), and intraocular lens presence (P = 0.61). Visual acuity was higher in the healthy group, as expected (P < 0.0001).

CVI values at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and total choroidal area were all significantly lower in the proliferative MacTel-2 group compared to healthy eyes (P = 0.02, P = 0.01, and P = 0.01, respectively).

Comparison of ellipsoid zone loss subgroups in nonproliferative macular telangiectasia type 2 group

Among the eyes in this group, 49 exhibited EZ loss, while 25 had intact zones. Patients with EZ loss had lower visual acuity compared to those with intact zones (P = 0.005). Sex and intraocular lens presence, assessed using the Chi-square tests, showed no significant differences (P = 0.11 and P = 0.59, respectively). The ages of the two subgroups were statistically similar (P = 0.35), but spherical equivalence was significantly myopic in the EZ loss subgroup (P = 0.007).

No statistically significant differences were observed between eyes with EZ loss and those with intact zones in terms of CVI at 1500 µ (P = 0.18), 4000 µ (P = 0.16), and TCA (P = 0.11).

Comparison of ellipsoid zone loss subgroups in proliferative macular telangiectasia type 2 group

Within this group, 10 eyes had EZ loss, while 6 did not. Patients with EZ loss had lower visual acuity compared to those with intact zones (P = 0.047). The Chi-square test for sex indicated a female predominance in the EZ loss group (P = 0.013), while intraocular lens presence showed no significant difference (P = 0.88). Spherical equivalence was similar between the two subgroups (P = 0.18).

There were no statistically significant differences between these subgroups in terms of CVI at 1500 µ (P = 0.3), 4000 µ (P = 0.95), and TCA (P = 0.82).

Comparison of choroidal vascularity index under ellipsoid zone loss

In the nonproliferative MacTel-2 group, CVI under the EZ loss area was statistically similar to CVI at 1500 µ (P = 0.22), 4000 µ (P = 0.27), and total choroidal area (P = 0.26) of the same eye. The CVI under the EZ loss area also had no statistically significant difference from CVI at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and TCA of eyes with intact zones (P = 0.09, P = 0.16, and P = 0.06, respectively).

In the proliferative MacTel-2 group, CVI under the EZ loss area was statistically similar to CVI at 1500 µ (P = 0.60), 4000 µ (P = 0.72), and total choroidal area (P = 0.39) of the same eye. The CVI under the EZ loss area also had no statistically significant difference from CVI at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and TCA of eyes with intact zones (P = 0.84, P = 0.86, and P = 0.55, respectively).

The combined EZ loss CVI of both nonproliferative and proliferative MacTel-2 CVI was 61.57% ± 5.35% which was significantly lower than CVI at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and TCA of healthy eyes (P = 0.004, P = 0.005, and P = 0.003, respectively).

Comparison of choroidal vascularity index according to area size

In the healthy group, CVI 1500 and CVI 4000 were statistically similar (P = 0.21). CVI 1500 was statistically similar to CVI TCA (P = 0.06), and CVI 4000 was statistically similar to CVI TCA (P = 0.051). In all MacTel-2 subgroups and groups, CVIs at 1500 µ, 4000 µ, and total choroidal area were also compared with each other, and no statistically significant differences were found. Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the CVI to evaluate potential structural complications within the choroid.

The inner border of the choroid corresponds to the lower border of the hyperreflective band of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/Bruch’s membrane layer, while the outer border is located at the chorioscleral junction. CVI refers to the proportion of the choroidal luminal area relative to the total choroidal area. CVI values can vary depending on factors such as retinal length, the imaging area, and the type of raster scan used.[1112] The significance of CVI lies in its ability to quantify choroidal vascularity. As a ratio, it is less dependent on the absolute area of the choroid, making it a potentially more reliable biomarker compared to choroidal volume.[11] However, the calculation of CVI is sensitive to the quality of the images obtained. If the choroidal–scleral interface cannot be clearly defined, CVI calculations may be unreliable. In addition, retinal blood vessels in OCT imaging may cast shadows, complicating the visualization of the choroid. Other limitations of CVI may arise from suboptimal image acquisition, particularly in highly myopic eyes, reduced image resolution due to media opacities, and eye movements.[13] Furthermore, it is important to consider the assumptions underlying CVI calculation. In addition, the OCT magnification effect seen in axial length changes was reported to have an effect on choroid measurements, especially in hyperopes in the parafoveal sectors.[14] For example, in our study, overtly hyperopic or myopic retinas were not included in the study. Finally, in CVI, the binarized image’s dark and white pixels are assumed to represent the vascular and stromal areas, respectively. This is considered to be true due to previous empirical observations but has not definitively been proven.[15] Recent studies suggest that choroidal vascular changes may contribute to the primary pathology of photoreceptor and RPE degeneration in inherited retinal disorders. CVI has been proposed as a parameter to quantify structural changes in eyes with retinal dystrophies.[16]

The impact of MacTel-2 on the choroid remains a subject of ongoing debate, with various studies presenting differing perspectives on its effects. In retinochoroidal investigations, Breazzano et al. reported the formation of de novo retinal–choroidal anastomoses in longitudinal follow-ups of MacTel-2 patients.[17] Similarly, Spaide et al. used OCT angiography to visualize retinal–choroidal anastomoses, indicating vascular remodeling.[4] These anastomoses were observed to cluster in the temporal juxtafoveal macula.[18]

Hess et al. suggested that such vascular changes may precede the development of exudative neovascularization.[19] In addition, Tzaridis et al. demonstrated a progressive decline in retinal microvasculature using OCT angiography, potentially linked to compromised choriocapillaris perfusion.[20] Findings regarding choroidal thickness in MacTel-2 have been inconsistent. Some studies reported increased choroidal thickness in MacTel-2 eyes.[21] Demir et al. observed this in both proliferative and nonproliferative stages compared to healthy controls, while Nunes et al. hypothesized that Müller cell dysfunction and retinal vascular abnormalities could induce compensatory thickening of the choroid.[2223] In contrast, studies by Chhablani et al. and Wang et al. found no significant differences in choroidal thickness between MacTel-2 and control eyes.[2425] Furthermore, long-term follow-up studies have reported choroidal thinning in proliferative MacTel-2, possibly reflecting the neurodegenerative aspect of the disease.[26] However, these results may have been confounded by antivascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy initiated during extended follow-up.

Given the variability in findings, it is likely that MacTel-2 does affect the choroid, though the exact nature and extent of this impact remain uncertain. The current study offers several findings.

Choroidal vascularity index is lower in macular telangiectasia type 2 eyes compared with healthy eyes

Karasu and Celebi found that only nonproliferative MacTel-2 eyes had lower CVIs compared to healthy eyes, which aligns with our findings.[9] However, our study showed that both proliferative and nonproliferative MacTel-2 eyes had lower CVIs compared to healthy eyes. The difference in patient numbers between our study (42 eyes) and Karasu et al.’s study (21 eyes) might explain this variation.[9] Another study investigated the long-term effects on CVI in 43 eyes.[8] The baseline CVI measurements in that study did not show any difference from the control group, but they observed a significant decrease in CVI from baseline to 2 years when measured yearly. Although their baseline results are different from our study’s findings, since a decreasing trend in CVI over time was found, this discrepancy may be the result of images taken at different times in disease progression. In summary, all these results collectively suggest that changes in MacTel-2 involve alterations in the choroid.

There are no choroidal vascularity index differences between nonproliferative and proliferative macular telangiectasia type 2

It has been suggested that neovascularization in MacTel-2 may originate from retinal blood vessels, as seen in indocyanine green angiography showing intraretinal anastomosis.[2728] This might explain why we did not observe significant changes in CVI. Demir et al. found that both proliferative and nonproliferative MacTel-2 cases had similar choroidal thicknesses, but their study included individuals who had already received anti-VEGF injections in the proliferative group.[22] Similarly, Çoban Karataş et al. observed no difference between nonproliferative and proliferative MacTel-2 eyes during long-term follow-up, but again, the proliferative group was undergoing anti-VEGF treatment.[26] In our study, we intentionally selected patients who were not given any treatment to eliminate this factor, and our findings showed no significant difference in CVI between these groups. Karasu and Celebi reported lower CVIs in nonproliferative MacTel-2 eyes compared to proliferative ones.[9] However, their study had 42 nonproliferative and 32 proliferative eyes, and this difference between our studies may be due to the varying number of patients involved.

Ellipsoid zone loss does not affect choroidal vascularity index in macular telangiectasia type 2

The main accepted hypothesis for the pathophysiology of MacTel-2 is progressive Müller cell dysfunction, leading to photoreceptor loss, which causes EZ disruption and juxtafoveal capillary proliferation.[29] EZ disruption often starts temporal to the foveola and is one of the most common findings in MacTel-2.[1] It has been proposed that the extent of EZ loss, particularly when assessed in horizontal scans of the central fovea, can be used as a parameter for gauging the severity of photoreceptor loss.[30]

Although the choroid plays a critical role in nourishing the retinal photoreceptor layers, there is no clear evidence of significant choroidal changes associated with EZ disruptions in various retinal diseases. For instance, in retinitis pigmentosa, patients with EZ loss, a greater loss in this zone was associated with a lower CVI during a 1-year follow-up period.[31] In contrast, Cetin et al. showed a CVI increase in retinitis pigmentosa patients with EZ disruption.[32] Nunes et al. discovered that in the case of MacTel-2, EZ disruption is associated with vision loss but does not consistently correlate with choroidal thickness.[23] Our research supports this because CVIs in patients with EZ disruption were similar to those with undisturbed zones. We also calculated the CVI beneath the disrupted EZ to determine if there were any compensatory vascular changes in this area, but found that the CVI in this region was not significantly different from other areas in the choroid. The choroid is responsible for the high oxygen demand of photoreceptors, so we initially expected the CVI to be lower in the disturbed EZ regions due to reduced nourishment requirements, such as seen in geographic atrophy.[33] However, our findings did not support this.

Choroidal vascularity index measurements do not change by expanding or narrowing the binarization zone in macular telangiectasia type 2 or healthy eyes

In CVI calculations, typically, a region measuring 1.5 mm in length with foveal centrality in a horizontal raster scan is considered.[1534] Agrawal et al. conducted an analysis of choroidal vascularity, comparing CVI obtained from (i) a single scan passing through the fovea, (ii) the mean values from scans within a 1000-µ area based on the inner circle of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study grid, and (iii) the mean value from macular scans of a 30° ×25° raster. They reported no significant differences between the three scanning areas. These findings suggest that in healthy controls, a single scan passing through the fovea is a reliable representation of the whole posterior pole choroidal vascularity.[35] Goud et al. similarly found no significant difference in CVI across various rings, subfields, or quadrants within the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study grid.[12] Most studies have used either the 1500-µ zone or the entire choroidal area to represent the CVI. In contrast, Koçak et al. discovered that the CVI within the 1500-µ zone was higher than the CVI of the entire choroidal area, attributing this finding to the higher density of choriocapillaris in the macular choroid.[36] However, our current study does not support this result. We found that not only were the CVIs within the 1500-µ zone and the total choroidal area statistically similar, but the CVI within the 4000-µ zone was also similar to both of these ratios. This shows that the choroid is affected regardless of the defect area and needs to be investigated further. However, it must be noted that the similarity between the CVI in the 4000-µ zone and the TCA was associated with a very low P value margin. Kakiuchi et al., in their research, defined a “macular zone” as starting from 3000 µ away from the fovea.[37] They observed that this area had larger blood vessel lumens compared to the total choroidal area, with significantly larger luminal ratios than the 8000-µ zone it was compared to. It is worth noting that they used wide-field OCT, which has a much broader imaging range compared to the OCT imaging used in our present study. Hence, further research may be needed to explore this area in future CVI studies.

The limitations of our current study include its retrospective design and the absence of follow-up data. In addition, the sample size could be expanded in future studies, with adding more covariates like axial length into the study. Nevertheless, our study contributes valuable data regarding the influence of EZ loss and binarization area on CVI. CVI can potentially be utilized in clinical settings as an OCT-based marker for assessing the choroid in MacTel-2 eyes, regardless of the specific binarization area used.
