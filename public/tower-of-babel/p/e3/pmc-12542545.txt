Association between waterpipe smoking, polycythaemia and cardiac risk factors among Palestinian university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Taima Younis, Haya Hodali, Motaz Saifi, Sima Gharabeh, Basma Damiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the relationship between waterpipe smoking, polycythaemia and cardiac risk factors, including blood pressure and obesity.DesignCross-sectional Study conducted in 2022.SettingAn-Najah National University (ANNU) in the West Bank, Palestine.ParticipantsA total of 954 university students participated in the study.Primary and secondary outcome measuresA questionnaire used to assess smoking behaviours and other substance practices and patterns. Anthropometric and clinical measurements were measured and recorded, and complete blood counts (CBC) were obtained using an automated haematology analyser. Descriptive statistics were used to characterise the sample. Logistic regression models were applied to assess associations between smoking and polycythaemia. Also, there is a link between polycythaemia and high systolic and diastolic blood pressure.ResultsOut of 954 participants, 60% were females, 18.5% smoked cigarettes (57.1% daily), 31.1% smoked waterpipes (20.5% daily), 6.5% smoked e-cigarettes (18.2% daily), 18.8% had polycythaemia and 17.6% were obese. After adjusting for gender, obesity and other confounders, waterpipe smoking was associated with polycythaemia (OR=1.70, p=0.023), male gender (OR=87.89, p<0.001) and obesity (OR=3.82, p=0.014). Polycythaemia was associated with higher systolic blood pressure (OR=2.00, p=0.016) and higher diastolic blood pressure (OR=1.98, p=0.007).ConclusionThe study findings indicated significant associations between high blood pressure and polycythaemia, tobacco smoking, obesity and male gender. These findings underscore the importance of addressing tobacco hazards and their potential impact on cardiometabolic health, particularly among young adults.

Introduction

Smoking is a global public health challenge and the most preventable risk factor for morbidity and mortality, even in developed countries.1 The well-established link between tobacco smoking and cardiovascular disease is well established and not questionable.2 3 This connection can be attributed to tobacco smoking’s association with secondary polycythaemia, a condition caused by interrupted gas exchange and carbon monoxide inhalation.4 5 Polycythaemia or erythrocytosis is an elevated red blood cell (RBC) mass, reflected by increased haemoglobin (Hgb) levels or haematocrit (HCT) beyond the normal range for a specific age and gender.2 Primary polycythaemia, also known as Polycythaemia Vera (PV), is caused by a mutation in the JAK2 gene. In contrast, secondary polycythaemia is linked to tissue hypoxia or inappropriate erythropoietin secretion and is more common in smokers, individuals with chronic lung conditions or those living in high altitudes.6

Polycythaemia has proven to be associated with higher cardiometabolic risk factors.7 About one-third of deaths resulting from polycythaemia occur due to thrombotic complications.8 These complications can present in the form of cardiovascular disease, pulmonary embolisms or ischaemic strokes.9 10 They could be effectively prevented if detected and managed early with widely available drugs.11 12 However, the relationship between high Hgb levels and blood pressure is subject to debate. Some studies suggest that Hgb levels are linked to hypertension (HTN),1315 and elevated Hgb levels might raise both systolic and diastolic blood pressure by causing blood vessels to contract, thereby increasing blood pressure. However, other studies have indicated that blood pressure is not correlated with blood viscosity in healthy individuals.14 16 Smoking has been identified as a risk factor for both HTN and polycythaemia, and therefore, studying the relationship between smoking, HTN and polycythaemia can provide valuable insights into the interrelation of these health conditions and help in developing targeted interventions for individuals at risk.

Smoking in the form of waterpipes and electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) is becoming more common among new generations, including adolescents and young adults,17 with limited studies addressing its effect on their health.18 Some waterpipe smokers have a false belief that it is less harmful than cigarette smoking, as the vapour is being filtered by water, making it more socially acceptable.5 19 20 However, using a waterpipe can lead to higher exposure to toxins compared with smoking cigarettes.21 This is because a waterpipe smoking session can last for hours, while a typical cigarette smoking session only lasts for about 5 min.22 Due to limited monitoring of waterpipe smoking and polycythaemia in the region, there is a growing global concern about the effects of waterpipe smoking on secondary polycythaemia.

Waterpipe smoking is becoming increasingly popular among young Palestinians, especially among females and university students.2326 It has become more socially accepted than cigarettes.27 However, there has been limited research on the health effects of waterpipe smoking. Over the past decade, research indicated that many Palestinians start smoking waterpipes at a young age, often as a substitute for cigarette smoking.28 Others have associated waterpipe smoking with obesity, central obesity and eating habits.29

Different studies have reported that Palestine has a high smoking prevalence compared with other Arab and European countries.30 31 In 2021, the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS) announced that 31.3% of Palestinian adults are smokers, with a higher percentage being from the West Bank (40.1%).32 The prevalence varies based on the type of tobacco smoking, age, gender and location. Moreover, it is concerning that most Palestinians start smoking at an early age, during adolescence, increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases.2 33 Notably, in the occupied Palestinian territory, cardiac disease was reported as the leading cause of death, constituting 21.0% of all fatalities. At the same time, HTN was ranked eighth, accounting for approximately 5% of all deaths.34

In the occupied Palestinian territory, evidence regarding the prevalence of polycythaemia and associated risk factors is limited.2 This study aimed to determine the prevalence of polycythaemia among university students in ANNU, Palestine, and to establish the association between tobacco smoking and polycythaemia and cardiometabolic risk factors, particularly obesity and increased blood pressure. With the increasing popularity of waterpipe smoking among Palestinian young adults in general and university students in particular, it is crucial to implement regulatory measures to address this rapidly growing public health concern.

Research Methodology

Population and ethical considerations

The study was a cross-sectional study conducted among students at An-Najah National University (ANNU), a major university in the West Bank, Palestine. Students from ANNU were invited to participate in this study from March to June 2022. A male-to-female ratio of 1:1 was implied. Then, students were stratified according to their curricular year. One eligible student out of three was chosen to complete the questionnaire and provide a blood sample. The exclusion criteria for the study included individuals with hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, haemophilia, psychiatric disorders, cognitive disorders, developmental disorders, taking iron as a supplement and pregnant students. The required sample size was 371 for a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error with a 43.6% proportion of students. To minimise bias, 371 students from each gender were recruited. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at An-Najah National University (Ref. (16) Nov. 2019). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study. The confidentiality of participants' personal information has been strictly maintained throughout the research process.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Study tools and validity

The self-administered questionnaire (online supplemental file 1), used to assess tobacco smoking and other substance use patterns, was adapted from a previous publication.35 The frequency of substance use was categorised as follows: daily, several times weekly, several times monthly, several times yearly, rarely or ex-user. A participant was considered a current active user if they used the substance daily, several times weekly or several times monthly. Non-active users included those who used the substance rarely, several times per year, ex-users and non-users. Consumption of tea, coffee and energy drinks was assessed because their caffeine content may affect iron absorption and Hgb levels, serving as potential confounders.36

According to previous research, anthropometric measurements, weight and height were taken by trained medical staff.37 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in metres. Categories were as follows: underweight (less than 18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), obese (30.0–34.9) and morbidly obese (35.0 and above). Blood pressure measurements were also taken by trained medical staff twice, 5 min apart, with the participant seated and the arm at heart level using a standardised mercury sphygmomanometer (TXJ-10, China, measuring to the nearest 1 mm Hg) with an appropriate cuff size. The average of the two readings was used to record the individual’s systolic and diastolic blood pressures. High blood pressure: if systolic BP ≥ 130 mm Hg or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mm Hg.37

According to WHO,38 polycythaemia is defined as Hgb levels greater than 16.5 g/dL in men and greater than 16.0 g/dL in women, or haematocrit levels higher than 49% in men and over 48% in women.

Venous blood samples were obtained by trained staff using EDTA tubes, and analyses were performed within 30 min of collection to ensure sample stability. Complete blood count (CBC) test conducted using the Automated Haematology Analyzer XN series (XN-1000), manufactured by Sysmex Corporation, stands as a benchmark for precision and accuracy in clinical haematology and is the chosen instrument for CBC analysis at the An-Najah National University Hospital. The reference CBC values were as follows: HCT (37–47%), Hgb (12–16 g/L), white blood cells (WBC; 4.5–10.0 x 10∧9 /L), RBC (4.2–5.4×10∧12 /L), mean corpuscular volume (MCV; 76–99 fL) and platelets (140–450×10∧9 /L).

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using IBM’s Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) programme for Windows, V. 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Descriptive analysis included the mean, SD or median, and IQR where appropriate for continuous variables. Categorical variables were described using frequency and percentages. A logistic regression model was used to examine the association between polycythaemia (yes, no) and cigarettes, waterpipe and e-cigarettes (yes, no) (Model 1). The model was adjusted for gender (male, female), consumption of tea, energy drinks and coffee (yes, no), employment status (yes, no) and obesity status (obese, normal). The study also used logistic regression models to investigate the relationship between high systolic blood pressure (Model 2) or high diastolic blood pressure (Model 3), and polycythaemia (yes, no). Model 2 and Model 3 were adjusted for cigarettes, waterpipes and e-cigarette smoking (yes, no), gender (male, female), consumption of tea, energy drinks and coffee (yes, no), employment status (yes, no) and obesity status (obese, normal). ORs were calculated with a 95% CI, and any p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 954 participants fulfilled the entire research process. Among them, 60.1% were females. Prevalence and pattern of tobacco smoking

The prevalence of tobacco smoking was as follows: 18.5% of participants smoked cigarettes, with 57.1% of them smoking daily. Similarly, 31.1% of participants smoke waterpipes, with 20.5% of them smoking it daily. Additionally, 6.5% of participants use e-cigarettes, with 18.2% of them using them daily. In general, 22.3% of participants smoked one type and 23.1% of them were dual smokers. Moreover, 86.5% of participants consume coffee, with 50.1% drinking it daily. Similarly, 58.4% of participants consume tea and 42.9% consume energy drinks. The prevalence of cigarette smoking (35.8%) and waterpipe smoking (36%) was higher among males than females (1.7% and 13.8%, respectively). In addition, 50.1% of the males and 20.9% of the females reported energy drink usage. The majority of males (84.1%) and females (82.3%) consumed coffee (table 2).

Univariate analysis for risk factors associated with polycythaemia

Univariate analysis revealed that the following factors are significantly associated with polycythaemia: being male (OR=95.49, P-value<0.001), being a worker (OR=1.8, P-value=0.009), and being obese (OR=2.923, P-value<0.001). Additionally, the use of cigarettes (OR=4.7, P-value<0.001), waterpipes (OR=3, P-value<0.001), e-cigarettes (OR=3.3, P-value<0.001), and energy drinks (OR=2.9, P-value<0.001) was also found to be significantly associated with polycythaemia. High systolic (OR=4.884, P-value<0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (OR=4.558, P-value<0.001) were also more prevalent among students with polycythaemia than among those without polycythaemia. No significant association was found between polycythaemia and consumption of tea and coffee (P-value>0.05) (table 4).

Discussion

The study revealed some significant findings. A high prevalence of polycythaemia (18.8%) was observed among Palestinian university students. This figure is notably higher than that observed in specific groups known to be at risk for polycythaemia, such as male (9.2%) and female (3.5%) participants with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and obstructive sleep apnoea (36,37). This prevalence also contrasts sharply with the rate in countries with similar cultural and ethnic backgrounds, like Iraq (4%).39

The growing perception that waterpipe smoking is less harmful has contributed to its increased popularity.40 Previous beliefs suggested that waterpipe smoking was not associated with polycythaemia due to its less frequent use compared with traditional smoking.41 The univariate analysis results of this study demonstrated a significant relationship between polycythaemia and the three forms of tobacco: cigarettes (42%), waterpipe (31.1%) and e-cigarettes (40%). Nevertheless, the adjusted logistic regression findings indicated that waterpipe smokers were approximately two times more likely to develop polycythaemia compared with non-smokers. This could be attributed to the hypoxia resulting from a significant increase in carboxyhaemoglobin levels associated with waterpipe smoking in comparison to cigarette smoking.42 This notion aligns with prior research demonstrating similar outcomes concerning waterpipe smoking and the elevation of Hgb levels.43

Moreover, the majority of smoking students were waterpipe smokers, with most being dual smokers. Additionally, 44.2% of waterpipe smokers reported daily or frequent use. The prolonged duration of waterpipe smoking sessions compared with cigarette smoking sessions indicates that waterpipe smokers may face a higher risk of hypoxia, potentially contributing to the higher likelihood of developing polycythaemia.44 These factors may have contributed to the higher likelihood of waterpipe smokers developing polycythaemia.

In agreement with other research, polycythaemia is more prevalent among males (45.7%) than females (0.9%).45 Males were also found to be more likely to smoke tobacco and be dual smokers, with a male-to-female cigarette smoking ratio of 21:1 and a waterpipe smoking ratio of 2.6:1. These significant gender differences in both polycythaemia prevalence and smoking habits highlight the crucial need for considering gender-specific factors in understanding and addressing these health issues among Palestinians. Moreover, obesity was associated with the risk of polycythaemia, and male students were more likely to be obese than female students. Obesity is prevalent among Palestinians, particularly among male students compared with female students.46 This prevalence is lower than the overall population.47 48

Consequently, we recommend paying more attention to obesity as a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases among the Palestinian population in general, given its associated consequences, particularly polycythaemia. Surprisingly, polycythaemia was more prevalent in working students than non-working students. This could be attributed to other lifestyle factors rather than their employment status. In this study, working students were more likely to be male, smokers and obese. Further research is required in this area. Importantly, polycythaemia in men was associated with a higher risk of major cardiovascular events compared with women.49 This highlights the importance of gender-specific healthcare and the need for increased attention to men in the context of polycythaemia.

The relationship between high Hgb levels and blood pressure is a topic of debate. The results of this study provide evidence supporting the relationship between high Hgb levels and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. The results also support the relation between cigarettes and high blood pressure. Both HTN and polycythaemia are linked to obesity and smoking and can pose health risks for young adults, potentially leading to cardiovascular diseases and stroke, if not properly managed. These findings underscore the importance of addressing these health concerns and implementing targeted interventions to mitigate the potential risks associated with high Hgb levels, blood pressure, obesity and smoking, particularly among young adults.

Additionally, HTN is a silent disease, as it often exhibits no obvious clinical symptoms. People with secondary polycythaemia may be asymptomatic, but if symptoms do occur, they can include headache, blurry vision, weakness and fatigue, trouble sleeping, pain in the chest or abdomen, dizziness, loss of appetite and confusion.50 Therefore, both polycythaemia and HTN can also affect students' cognitive function and overall well-being, potentially impacting academic performance and daily life.

Based on the study findings indicating significant associations between high blood pressure and polycythaemia, tobacco smoking, obesity and male gender, it is crucial to implement targeted interventions to mitigate these risk factors among young adults, particularly Palestinian university students. These interventions may include educational campaigns and programmes aimed at raising awareness about the harmful effects of waterpipe smoking and the risks associated with polycythaemia and cardiac diseases. Additionally, promoting healthy lifestyle choices and providing access to comprehensive health screenings and early detection measures can be beneficial in improving the overall health outcomes in this population. Furthermore, healthcare providers and educational institutions can collaborate to support smoking cessation programmes and provide resources for managing polycythaemia and addressing cardiac risk factors. By prioritising preventive measures and fostering a supportive environment for healthy behaviours, the potential impact of tobacco hazards and associated health risks can be effectively mitigated among young adults.

This study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between waterpipe smoking, polycythaemia and cardiac risk factors. The study focused on Palestinian undergraduate students, which may not fully represent the general population and limits the generalisability of the findings. The research did not explore the influence of other potential confounding variables, such as dietary habits and physical activity, which could affect the outcomes under investigation. Further studies are required to investigate these factors. Despite these limitations, this research is the first to establish an association between polycythaemia and waterpipe smoking, which is important in future risk predictors for these patients.

The strength of this study lies in its comprehensive investigation of the relationship between waterpipe smoking, polycythaemia and cardiac risk factors among young adults. The study included a large sample size, enabling a comprehensive association analysis. The findings of the study provide important insights into the potential impact of tobacco hazards on cardiometabolic health, particularly among the young adult population. Furthermore, the study’s focus on an understudied population—Palestinian undergraduate students—adds to the significance of the research findings.

Conclusion

The study findings indicate significant associations between high blood pressure and polycythaemia, tobacco smoking, obesity and male gender. The research suggests that waterpipe smoking and polycythaemia are correlated, emphasising the need for early detection of these risk factors in young adults. The high prevalence of waterpipe smoking among Palestinian university students raises concerns about the potential risk of polycythaemia and cardiac diseases. These results underscore the importance of addressing tobacco hazards and their potential impact on cardiometabolic health, particularly among young adults. This suggests a need for targeted interventions to mitigate these risk factors and improve the overall health outcomes in this population.
