Cerebral Hypoperfusion Causes Behavioral Changes and Impairs the Rod Photoreceptor Pathway in the Retina of Aged Mice
Authors: Spencer Talmage Barney, Daniela Becerril, Sheelu Monga, Samantha Flores, Audrey Lee, Marlon Fraga Mattos, Ross M Perez, Frank W Blixt, Michael Maniskas, Shuning Huang, Hongyu Wu, Chunfeng Tan, Louise D McCullough, Elizabeth Zuniga-Sanchez, Jose Felix Moruno-Manchon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGradual reduction of cerebral blood flow occurs with aging, and it is a major cause of vascular dementia, a group of dementias with a cerebrovascular component. Interestingly, patients who have suffered a vascular insult may develop retinopathy, which may occur as an early symptom of vascular dementia. Several rodent models using young animals have been generated to mimic retinopathy caused by cerebral hypoperfusion; however, given that aging is an important factor in developing vascular dementia and hypoperfusion retinopathy, we propose to use aged (17-month-old) mice in a model of cerebral hypoperfusion. In this model, we implant two metallic micro-coils (0.16 mm inner diameter) around both common carotid arteries. We found that two months after surgery, aged mice with bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) showed hyperactivity-like behavior, cognitive deficits, and demyelination, which are pathological features in patients with vascular dementia. We also found that the retina of BCAS mice showed important morphological changes, such as reduced area of the outer synaptic layer, retraction of rod photoreceptor terminals, and increased sprouting of rod bipolar cells. BCAS mice also showed reduced in vivo retinal responses to different light intensities. Our study is the first to propose using aged mice in a model of hypoperfusion retinopathy, which is relevant to identifying the molecular mechanisms underlying vision loss with cerebral hypoperfusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10571-026-01698-7.Keywords: Cerebral hypoperfusion, Behavior, Photoreceptors, Retinopathy, Aging, Vascular dementia

Introduction

As we age, our brains gradually receive a limited supply of oxygen and nutrients due to a sustained reduction in blood flow, leading to white matter loss (Stamenkovic et al. 2025). The reduction of the cerebral blood flow not only affects the brain itself, but also dramatically affects the retina function, as it is the tissue with the highest energy demand relative to its size in the body (Kaynezhad et al. 2023). Indeed, it has been reported that changes in the retina and visual deficits have been associated with vascular dementia (Marquie et al. 2019). Vascular dementia is a group of dementias caused by different types of insults in the brain vasculature, such as microinfarcts, small vessel disease, and atherosclerosis (Ng et al. 2025). Changes in the retina induced by reduced blood flow are manifested by reduced visual clarity and binocular integration, disruptions in eye movement dynamics, decreased contrast sensitivity and dark adaptation, and impairments in color discrimination and visual processing efficiency (Moarefi et al. 2017; Saftari and Kwon 2018; Wolffsohn and Davies 2019). This decline in visual abilities negatively influences the quality of life and the performance of common everyday tasks. Furthermore, untreated or advanced cases of carotid stenosis may progress to severe vision loss and neovascular glaucoma (Patel and Chih 2017).

Several animal models have been generated to identify the mechanisms underlying retinopathy caused by reduced brain perfusion, and to prevent patients from having more serious ocular damage. One model is the ophthalmic artery ligation, which is technically difficult as during the surgery, the optic nerves can be accidentally compressed (D’Onofrio and Koeberle 2013). The middle cerebral artery occlusion has been commonly used to model retinal ischemia (Ritzel et al. 2016); however, the reduction in retinal function is transient, and there are no detectable changes in retinal structure (Allen et al. 2014). Bilateral common carotid artery occlusion in rats induces damage in retinal ganglion cells, neurons of the inner nuclear layer, and photoreceptors (Yamamoto et al. 2006). However, the use of bilateral common carotid artery occlusion in mice causes high mortality, given that they have an incomplete circle of Willis (Lee et al. 2020). The advantages of using mice instead of rats in research are well-known: shorter lifespan than rats, lower housing costs, and a wider variety of human disease models. Thus, the use of mice can accelerate research in the field of hypoperfusion retinopathy. The bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) mouse model was developed as a model of vascular dementia induced by cerebral hypoperfusion in mice, a milder and more chronic model of cerebral hypoperfusion compared to carotid occlusion (Tuo et al. 2021; Dinh and Arumugam 2024). In this model, metallic micro-coils are implanted bilaterally in the common carotid arteries of mice. Thus far, BCAS is considered the most feasible and reliable model of cerebral hypoperfusion (Ishikawa et al. 2023). However, published studies using different animal models to study ischemic retinopathies have been conducted exclusively in young animals. Particularly, those studies reporting retina degeneration in the mouse BCAS model have used young (2–5 month old, m/o) mice (Foddis et al. 2020; Li et al. 2023; Shu et al. 2024). Given that cerebral hypoperfusion mostly occurs during aging, the use of aged mice is highly relevant.

In our laboratory, we perform BCAS using 0.16 mm diameter micro-coils in aged (17-m/o) mice, and house them for two months after surgery. This model of cerebral hypoperfusion exhibits demyelination, hyperactivity-like behavior, and cognitive dysfunction in aged mice, which are pathological features in patients with vascular dementia (Alber et al. 2019; Iadecola et al. 2019; Chun et al. 2023). We have found that aged BCAS mice showed important changes in the retina, such as reduced area of the outer synaptic layer, retraction of rod photoreceptor terminals, sprouting of dendrites from rod bipolar cells, but not cone bipolar cells, and impaired retinal responses to light stimuli, compared with sham mice. In this study, we propose a novel aged animal model to study retinopathy induced by carotid artery stenosis that overcomes the limitations in other animal models, and that, in addition, occurs in the physiological and systemic status of aging.

Materials and Methods

Animals

17-m/o C57BL/6J male and female mice were obtained from the National Institutes on Aging and were housed in the installations of the Center for Laboratory Medicine and Care (CLAMC) at the University of Texas McGovern Medical School. Mice were maintained on a 12 h light/12 h dark schedule with constant temperature and humidity and were fed with mouse lab pellets. Access to food and water was ad libitum. Investigators and CLAMC veterinarians warranted that those mice were not exposed to stress, pain, or injury.

BCAS/Sham Surgery

Mice were subjected to BCAS surgery, as previously described (Shibata et al. 2004). The mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane by inhalation (4% for induction, and 2% for maintenance during the procedure) and placed in a supine position for subsequent surgery. The mouse was subcutaneously injected with Bupivacaine (0.25%) at the site of the incision. A midline incision was made in the ventral cervical region to expose the common carotid arteries. Two silk sutures per artery were placed under the proximal and distal portions of each artery. The arteries were lifted by pulling up the sutures, and then the micro-coils (0.16 mm inner diameter, 2.5 mm length) were placed around each common carotid artery. Once both micro-coils were placed, the skin incisions were sutured. Surgical shams underwent the same procedure for anesthesia, neck dissection, identification of the common carotid arteries, and suture placement, but the arteries were not ligated with micro-coils. Buprenorphine-ER (3.25 mg/kg) was subcutaneously injected after surgery, and sutures were removed 10–14 days after surgery.

Arterial Spin Labeling Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed on a Bruker Biospec 70/30 USR scanner with a 30 cm magnet bore, operating at a field strength of 7 T. An investigator blinded to the experimental groups placed an anesthetized mouse into a mouse holder, which includes a bite bar, nose pad, and ear bars. We used three mice per type of surgery. A respiratory probe and EKG probes were attached to the mouse, and the respiratory and heart rates were monitored utilizing a physiological monitoring system. The temperature within the magnet was set at 37 °C. Images were visualized and analyzed with the ITK-SNAP 4.0.2 software.

Behavior

Two months after BCAS/sham surgery, mice underwent a battery of behavior tests, as previously described (Noh et al. 2023; Monga et al. 2025):

Open Field

A mouse was placed in the middle of an arena (45 cm x 45 cm) and allowed to explore for 10 min. The mouse was videorecorded, and its movements and ambulatory and fine movements in the outer periphery and the center of the arena were analyzed.

Novel Object Recognition

A mouse was placed in an arena (45 cm x 45 cm) with two identical objects, and it was allowed to explore the objects for 10 min. After a 24 h interval, one of these objects was replaced with a novel object, which was different in shape and color from the familiar object, and again the mouse was allowed to explore the objects for 10 min. The differences in exploration time with novel and familiar objects were analyzed. The recognition index was calculated by dividing the time spent exploring the novel object by the total time exploring both objects x 100.

Elevated Plus Maze

A mouse was placed in the center of the intersection of two open arms and two closed arms (25 cm long x 5 cm wide in each arm). The mouse was allowed to explore for 10 min. The mouse was video recorded, and the time spent in each arm was analyzed.

Fear Conditioning

A mouse was placed in the arena (a novel environment) for 10 min to allow for exploration. Then, a beep sound (neutral stimulus) was followed by a 0.1 mA electric foot shock (aversive stimulus) for 2 s. The mouse was returned to its cage. 24 h after the aversive stimulus, the animal was placed again in the arena and exposed to the beep sound without shock for 2 min. The mouse was video recorded, and the percentage of the inactive time was analyzed.

Animals were videorecorded in accordance with the AWC Image Capturing Policy. The arenas were illuminated with 120 V light bulbs during the time of the test. The same investigator manipulated the animals for the tests and was blinded to the groups.

We performed a pilot study to determine the number of mice in the behavior test. We used our preliminary data from NORT (sham(mean ± SD) = 56.3 ± 8.5, n1 = 6; BCAS(mean ± SD) = 38.6 ± 13.9, n2 = 9)) to calculate the power analysis (2 tails, effect size d = 1.54, alpha error probability = 0.05%). 13 mice/group gives 96% power.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse Retinas

After in vivo retinal recordings, mice were euthanized, and the eyes were collected and fixed for 60 min in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at 4℃. Eye cups were then dissected and stored overnight in a 30% sucrose/PBS solution. They were then placed in Optimal Cutting Temperature Compound and frozen at -80℃. Frozen eyes were then sectioned with a cryostat at 20 μm as previously described (Pourhoseini et al. 2021; Goswami-Sewell et al. 2023). Slides were washed with PBS for 10 min at room temperature three times before antibody staining. Retinal sections were incubated with blocking buffer (10% normal donkey serum, 1% BSA, 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h, followed by primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight. Slides were washed 3 times with PBS for 10 min each and then incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies at 4℃ overnight. Slides were then washed with PBS three times for 10 min and then stained with DAPI (1:1000) for 40 min at room temperature. Slides were then sealed with Vectashield (Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope using a 40X objective and analyzed using the Imaris 9.6 confocal software.

Fluorescence intensities were analyzed with the Imaris software and FIJI. An investigator blinded to the experimental groups performed the quantification of the retinal layers, number of cell counts, and localization of fluorescent and intensity was done using the plot profile features on FIJI. All measurements were normalized to 0.01 mm² by computing the surface area of each retinal section. This was done by measuring the height and width of all retinal layers using DAPI staining. Retinal thickness was measured using both Imaris and FIJI.

Mouse Brains

After collecting the eyes, the mouse was transcardially perfused with cold PBS. The brain was collected and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS for 24 h. Then, the brain was washed with PBS and immersed in 30% sucrose/PBS for 72 h. Then, the brain was sectioned with a frozen microtome (20 μm thickness), and the brain slices were stored in PBS at 4 °C. For immunostaining with anti-myelin-binding protein (MBP), brain slices were mounted on glass slides and left to dry overnight. Brain tissue was incubated with blocking solution (5% BSA, 0.5% Triton-X in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. Then, brain slices were incubated with anti-MBP in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. Then, brain slices were washed with PBS containing 1% Tween three times for 5 min. A solution of secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa-488 diluted in blocking solution was added to the samples and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Brain slices were washed again and mounted with a glass coverslip with DAPI solution mounting media. An investigator blinded to the experimental groups took the images with a Leica DMi8 fluorescence stereo microscope system at a 40X objective. MBP fluorescence intensities were analyzed with the ImageJ 1.53t software.

Using data from a pilot study for immunohistochemistry using anti-MBP (sham(mean ± SD) = 11,268 ± 1,801, n1 = 3; BCAS(mean ± SD) = 7,667 ± 1,052, n2=)), we performed the power analysis (2 tails, effect size d = 2.44, alpha error probability = 0.05%), and indicated that 6 mice/group gives 96% power for immunobiological studies. The information of the antibodies and the dilution of each one used in this study can be found in the Supp. Table 1.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Mouse eyes were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Eyes were then dissected and washed with 1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.3), fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, and finally dehydrated by undergoing multiple rounds of graded alcohol. Tissue samples were hardened with acetone and polybed 812 plastic resin and placed in plastic molds with 100% polybed 812 as previously described (Pourhoseini et al. 2021; Goswami-Sewell et al. 2023). After complete solidification of the tissue samples, sections of 80 nm were achieved using a Leica EMUC ultra microtome. Sections were then mounted on 100 mesh copper grids and stained with 2% uranyl acetate and Reynold’s lead stain. Samples were then imaged using a JEOL JEM 1230 electron microscope and an AMTV600 digital camera. TEM images were then analyzed and classified based on synaptic composition by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

Electroretinography (ERG)

Mice were dark-adapted overnight and anesthetized using a combination anesthetic comprised of ketamine (37.5 mg/mL), xylazine (1.9 mg/mL), and acepromazine (0.37 mg/mL). The combination anesthetic was given intraperitoneally at 0.75–1.5 mL/kg of body weight. The pupils of the mouse were dilated using drops of 1% tropicamide and 2.5% phenylephrine. Then, the mouse was placed on a heating pad, and its body temperature was monitored, and drops of 2.5% methylcellulose gel were also applied to their eyes. An investigator blinded to the animal groups placed platinum electrodes at the base of the tail of the mouse, one in the center of each eye, and another between the ears, which served as the ground and reference electrodes. Then, the mouse was maintained in complete darkness for 5 min before the scotopic measurements. Flashes of light were produced by cyan light-emitting diodes of 503-nm peak wavelength for half-millisecond instances. The output of the flashes was calibrated using a radiometer (ILT1700 International Light) with a photodiode sensor and a scotopic filter that provided readouts in scot cd*s/m2 as previously reported (Abd-El-Barr et al. 2009; Tse et al. 2015). These readouts were converted into photoisomerizations/rod (R*/rod), where 1 scot cd*s/m2 is about 581 photoisomerizations/rod/s.

Statistics

Statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10). The normal distribution of the data was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. To compare means from two experimental groups, we used the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for data with a Gaussian distribution, and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney test for data with no Gaussian distribution. To compare data with two factors, such as type of surgery and sex, we used two-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD for multiple comparisons test. Sample size estimates were calculated a priori using G*Power (version 3.1) for all experiments, assuming a significance level of 0.05 and 95% power. Assumptions regarding effect size and variance were based on previously published data or from pilot experiments. ERG data were analyzed using the Holm-Sidak method for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was determined as p < 0.05 and listed in the figure legends. Data are represented as mean ± SD, and data from male and female mice were pulled together per type of surgery, unless indicated in the corresponding figure.

Results

BCAS Mice Exhibited Increased Locomotor Activity, Reduced Recognition Memory, and Enhanced Demyelination Compared to Sham Mice

We used aged (17-m/o) C57BL/6J mice to induce cerebral hypoperfusion by ligating one metallic micro-coil in each common carotid artery (Fig. 1A). To confirm that the implantation of the micro-coils in aged mice reduced cerebral blood flow, BCAS and sham mice were subjected to Arterial Spin Labeling MRI one day before surgery, one day after surgery, and fourteen days after surgery. One day after surgery, the cerebral blood flow in BCAS mice drastically dropped relative to the basal blood flow one day before surgery, and it was 30% reduced compared with the cerebral blood flow in sham mice one day after surgery. Two weeks after surgery, cerebral blood flow in BCAS mice had recovered to ~ 90% of its initial level (Fig. 1B, C). This data aligns with that observed in young mice with BCAS, which also show a dramatic reduction of the cerebral blood flow immediately after surgery with a subsequent gradual recovery (Wang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2021).

Data are represented as mean ± SD; n = 3 mice/surgery. Thus far, most of the studies using BCAS mice have reported changes in behavior and cognitive deficits in mice one month after surgery (Ishikawa et al. 2023). However, the pathology of vascular dementia in humans may appear years to decades later after these initial vascular insults appear. Thus, we extended the mouse housing up to two months after surgery. We used mice of both sexes and found that males with sham surgery had 100% survival, and females with sham and BCAS surgery had 85% and 81% survival, respectively (Supp. Figure 2A). However, males that underwent BCAS surgery had a 50% mortality. Most of the deaths occurred within the first week after the surgery. Neither male nor female mice showed significant changes in their body weight during the two months with sham or BCAS surgery (Supp. Figure 2B, C).

Patients with vascular dementia manifest changes in behavior, anxiety, agitation, hyperactivity, and cognitive decline (Chun et al. 2023; Yang 2025). Thus, to characterize our BCAS model using aged mice, BCAS and sham mice were tested for different behavioral assays. In the fear conditioning test, we did not observe significant changes in the percentage of inactive time between sham and BCAS mice (Fig. 2A). We used the novel object recognition test to assess recognition memory, and found that BCAS showed a significant reduction of the recognition index, compared with sham mice (Fig. 2B), indicating that the recognition memory is affected by cerebral hypoperfusion. To determine whether BCAS mice show anxiety-like behavior, mice were tested in the elevated plus maze, and found no significant differences in the time that the mice spent in the open arms (Fig. 2C). In the open field test, we observed that BCAS mice showed increased velocity, and enhanced distance moved (Fig. 2D), suggesting that cerebral hypoperfusion altered locomotor function or contributed to hyperactivity. The number of visits to the borders in the arena in an open field was also analyzed, and found no differences between sham and BCAS mice (Fig. 2D), supporting the data from the elevated plus maze that BCAS mice do not show anxiety-like behavior. In addition, we also considered sex as a variable and performed a two-way ANOVA (Supp. Figure 3, Supp. Table 2). We only found significant differences in the novel recognition index by “sex”: female mice showed lower recognition indices than males, independently of the type of surgery.

A major characteristic of patients with vascular dementia is loss of white matter in their brains (Luo et al. 2025). We processed the brains of the experimental mice for immunohistochemistry to evaluate demyelination. For this, we used antibodies against MBP. While sham mice showed a uniform distribution of MBP in the corpus callosum, the brain area with higher content of myelin in mice, the distribution of the MBP signal was significantly reduced in the corpus callosum in BCAS mice (Fig. 2 E). This indicates that BCAS induces demyelination.

Overall, the use of aged mice for the BCAS model mimics the pathology associated with vascular dementia, and it is a reliable model of cerebral hypoperfusion.

BCAS Induces Morphological Changes in the Retina of Aged Mice

Prior studies on young BCAS mice have largely focused on the changes in the inner retina, even though the most significant functional deficits are seen occurring in the outer retina (Shu et al. 2024). However, a comprehensive analysis of the synaptic changes in the outer retina in the BCAS mouse model has not been reported. Thus, we set out to perform a detailed analysis of the retinal changes that occur in aged BCAS mice. The mammalian outer retina is comprised of two distinct visual pathways: the rod and cone pathways (Fig. 3 A). The rod pathway consists of rod photoreceptors that form synaptic connections to the dendrites of rod bipolars, whereas the cone pathway is comprised of cone photoreceptors making synapses with the dendrites of cone bipolars (Euler et al. 2014; Behrens et al. 2016). Horizontal cells are a class of interneurons that form connections to rod and cone photoreceptors to mediate lateral inhibition between the two pathways (Hirano et al. 2018). The mouse retina is a highly organized and laminated structure comprised of three nuclear layers (outer nuclear layer, ONL; inner nuclear layer, INL; and ganglion cell layer, GCL), and two synaptic layers (outer plexiform layer, OPL; and inner plexiform layer, IPL) (Fig. 3 A). Cell bodies of photoreceptors are confined to the ONL, and they send their axon terminal to the OPL to form synapses to their respective targets. Similarly, cell bodies of bipolar neurons and horizontal cells are localized to the INL, and their neuronal processes, along with synaptic connections, are confined to the OPL. In the inner retina, cell bodies of amacrine cells are largely found in the INL, whereas ganglion cells are confined to the GCL.

Due to the well-organized structure of the retina, we first examined the different retinal layers for gross morphological defects in both BCAS and sham mice. By staining retina sections from sham and BCAS mice with the fluorescence nuclear dye DAPI, we were able to label the different nuclear layers of the retina. We found no gross abnormalities in the overall structure and organization of the retina in BCAS mice compared to sham (Fig. 3 B). Measurements of the nuclear layers (ONL, INL, GCL) showed no significant differences between BCAS and sham animals (Fig. 3 C). However, the area of the OPL showed a significant reduction between aged BCAS and sham mice (Fig. 3 B’, C). Changes in the area of the IPL were not observed either (Fig. 3 C). We also separated the data based on sex (Supp. Figure 4, Supp. Table 3). Interestingly, we found that the normalized area of INL, IPL, and GCL was significantly reduced by the “sex” factor, being male the sex with the lower area of these layers, independently of the type of surgery. This data reveals important differences in the structure of the retina in aged mice between sexes.

In this study, we focused on the effect of BCAS using 0.16 mm micro-coils; however, most of the studies with BCAS mice have used a wider variation of these micro-coils, 0.18 mm inner diameter. Thus, we wondered if implanting 0.18 mm micro-coils could also affect the structure of the retina in aged mice. To test this, we performed BCAS surgery in 17-m/o C57BL/6J mice using 0.18 mm, and housed them for 2 months. Their retinas were processed for IHC and stained with DAPI, and we found that none of the retina layers (ONL, OPL, INL, IPL, and GCL) showed significant changes in the area with BCAS using 0.18 mm micro-coils, compared with the retinas of sham mice (Supp. Figure 5).

Synaptic Alterations and Impaired in Vivo Functional Responses in the Rod Pathway of Aged BCAS Mice

A decrease in the OPL area has been associated with synaptic defects in different mouse models with visual impairments (Keeley et al. 2013; Goswami-Sewell et al. 2023). Therefore, we hypothesized that cerebral hypoperfusion induced by BCAS can negatively affect synaptic connectivity in the outer retina. To test this, we used available antibodies of pre- and post-synaptic neurons to assess synaptic changes in aged BCAS mice. In the adult retina, neuronal processes from pre- and post-synaptic neurons are largely confined to the OPL, where changes in the positioning of their processes are seen in animal models with synaptic connectivity defects in aging (Samuel et al. 2011; Zabouri and Haverkamp 2013; Williams et al. 2022; Goswami-Sewell et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2023). Mispositioning of neuronal processes is largely associated with retraction of photoreceptor axon terminals and sprouting of dendrites from bipolar neurons (Keeley et al. 2013; Zabouri and Haverkamp 2013; Goswami-Sewell et al. 2023). Thus, we stained retina sections with antibodies against PSD-95, a marker of axon terminals of rod photoreceptors (Sarin et al. 2018). We found an increased number of retractions from PSD-95-positive rod photoreceptors in BCAS mice compared to sham (Fig. 3 D). Although some rod retractions were also observed in the sham mice, these were far less than in BCAS mice and more likely due to age-related deficits as described previously (Samuel et al. 2011; Zhu et al. 2023). We found a shift in PSD-95 protein localization from the OPL to ONL in BCAS mice compared to sham (Fig. 3 D). To quantify this observation, we measured the PSD-95 fluorescence intensity across the different retinal layers, and observed an increase in the ONL of BCAS mice compared to sham (arrow in Fig. 3 D’). Our results demonstrate a change in the positioning of axon terminals from rod photoreceptors, which may disrupt synaptic connectivity of the rod pathway.

To further investigate the possibility that there exists synaptic disruption in the rod pathway, we examined rod bipolar cells, which are the known synaptic partner of rod photoreceptors (Euler et al. 2014; Behrens et al. 2016). We stained retina sections from sham and BCAS with antibodies against protein kinase C alpha (PKCα), a marker for rod bipolar cells (Euler et al. 2014). We found no significant difference in the number of rod bipolar cells between BCAS and sham mice (Fig. 3 E-E’). However, we observed an increased in the number of rod bipolar sprouts into the ONL in aged BCAS mice compared to sham (Fig. 3 E-E’). We also observed that the length of rod bipolar sprouts was longer in the retina of BCAS mice, compared with those from sham mice (Fig. 3 E’). This indicates that rod bipolar cells similar to rod photoreceptors display changes in their positioning in response to carotid artery stenosis.

Next, we examined cone photoreceptors, which send their axon terminals to the OPL in wild-type retinas. Staining with the cone marker, cone arrestin (CAR), we found a reduction in the overall number of cone terminals localized to the OPL (Supp. Figure 6A, A’). However, we did not observe mispositioned cone terminals in the ONL as seen with rods in aged BCAS mice. We also considered whether BCAS affects cone bipolars, which are the neurons that establish contacts with cone photoreceptors in the OPL, and their cell bodies are located in the INL. Interestingly, we found no significant differences in the number of cone bipolars, nor in the number and length of cone bipolar sprouts between BCAS and sham animals (Supp. Figure 6B, B’). Horizontal cells are interneurons whose cell bodies are confined to the INL, and they extend their processes to the OPL to establish communication with the different types of photoreceptors. We found no significant differences in the number of horizontal cells between sham and BCAS mice, and no differences in the number and length of horizontal cell sprouts (Supp. Figure 6 C, C’). This indicates that cone photoreceptors, but not cone bipolar or horizontal cells, are altered in aged BCAS mice.

Then, we further investigated the synaptic changes associated with aged BCAS mice by performing transmission electron microscopy (TEM). We used TEM to examine the ultrastructure of the rod photoreceptor synapse in the outer retina of sham and BCAS mice. We noticed that rod terminals were ectopically positioned in the ONL (yellow arrows in Fig. 4 A). Indeed, the number of rod terminals in the OPL was significantly reduced in BCAS mice, compared to sham mice (Fig. 4 B). As the rod synapse is a 3D structure, TEM sectioning results in four characteristic structures termed: triad, dyad, monad, and unclassified (Tsukamoto and Omi 2014). The triad structure is considered the bona fide wild-type synapse, which consists of a ribbon and three invaginating processes (Fig. 4 C). The dyad consists of a ribbon and two invaginating processes, whereas the monad only contains one invaginating process. Unclassified rod terminals are those without any noticeable invaginating processes. Thus, we classified rod terminals into these four categories and found a 3% reduction in the number of triads from BCAS compared to sham, and that the total number rod synapses considering all the three categories (triad, dyad, and monad) together, was reduced by 7% in BCAS mice compared to sham (Fig. 4 D). This data supports that BCAS negatively affects the rod pathway, and lead to retraction of rod terminals from the OPL.

Morphological changes in rod photoreceptors negatively affect vision in dim light, perception of shapes, and peripheral vision. Thus, we wondered whether BCAS mice actually have impaired visual responses. We performed in vivo full-field electroretinograms (ERG) to measure retinal responses in dark-adapted BCAS and sham mice as described previously (Abd-El-Barr et al. 2009). Mice were exposed to different light stimuli of varying intensities to measure rod-driven responses, also known as scotopic ERG. Average ERG traces at various light intensities from sham and BCAS were represented and merged (Fig. 4 E). We found ERG responses to different light intensities were reduced in BCAS mice, compared with sham mice. Importantly, both a-wave (photoreceptor response) and b-wave (bipolar response) amplitudes were significantly reduced in BCAS mice, compared to sham (Fig. 4 F).

Overall, our findings demonstrate that BCAS induces cognitive deficits, behavioral changes, demyelination, and profound changes in the retina of aged mice. Particularly in the retina, brain hypoperfusion induces visual impairment in the rod pathway, which further supports synaptic connectivity defects in the outer retina of aged BCAS mice.

Discussion

In this study, the use of aged mice in the BCAS model recapitulates changes in behavior, cognitive decline, and demyelination, which are major pathological features observed in patients with vascular dementia (Alber et al. 2019; Iadecola et al. 2019; Chun et al. 2023). We also found that aged BCAS mice showed retinal changes and impairments in the rod pathway. Indeed, morphological and functional changes in the retina occur early in the development of the pathology of vascular dementia (Zheng et al. 2024). Importantly, we demonstrate that BCAS mice show a reduction in the OPL area, rod photoreceptor terminal retraction, loss of cone terminals, increased number and length of rod bipolar sprouts, and impaired retinal response to light stimuli, compared with sham mice.

Patients with vascular dementia show changes in behavior, such as agitation, disinhibition, and compulsive behavior (Chun et al. 2023). We observed that aged BCAS mice show increased velocity and increased distance moved, suggesting a hyperactivity-like behavior. Another study using the 2VO rat model in 9-11-m/o female rats reported an increased number of grid crosses and time exploring in the open field, suggesting a hyperlocomotion behavior (De Butte and Gieseking 2020). This supports our findings about the hyperactivity behavior induced by cerebral hypoperfusion. However, this phenotype is not recapitulated in different cerebral hypoperfusion models using young mice. Different studies using the 2VO model in 2-4-m/o rats did not observe a reduction in distance moved or velocity one or two months after surgery (Chen et al. 2018; Tiang et al. 2020; Meftahi et al. 2021). No signs of hyperactivity were noted in 2-m/o rats with unilateral common carotid artery occlusion 2.5 months after surgery (Kim et al. 2023). Furthermore, studies using the BCAS mouse model in 2-m/o mice reported no changes in distance moved and velocity (Suzuki et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2025) one month after surgery. This suggests that the age of the animals influences the phenotype induced by cerebral hypoperfusion, and that using aged mice can recapitulate some features observed in patients with dementia associated with reduced cerebral blood flow, which are not observed by using young mice.

The age in the rodent models of BCAS is also relevant, not only in the behavioral outcomes, but also in how the retina is affected. Thus far, three studies have reported retina degeneration and visual impairments in young (2–5 m/o) mice with BCAS (Foddis et al. 2020; Li et al. 2023; Shu et al. 2024). In these studies, mice were maintained with the micro-coils up to four weeks after surgery, and only two of these studies reported the diameter of the micro-coils used (0.18 mm). In our laboratory, we have observed that aged (17-m/o) mice with 0.18 mm micro-coils do not show changes in the retina structure two months after surgery; however, we found that aged mice with 0.16 mm micro-coils manifest a significant reduction of the OPL compared with sham mice. This suggests that the mechanisms that govern changes in the retina induced by cerebral hypoperfusion are different between young and aged mice.

Aged BCAS mice show notable demyelination two months after BCAS surgery, compared with sham mice. This finding is in agreement with human cases: cerebral hypoperfusion causes white matter hyperintensities in the brains of patients with cerebrovascular diseases (Alosco et al. 2013; Duncombe et al. 2017; Alber et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2025). In young BCAS mice, white matter rarefaction occurs in the corpus callosum, striatum, and internal capsule two weeks after surgery (Shibata et al. 2004). Demyelination induced by cerebral hypoperfusion can be caused by microglia activation and astrocyte proliferation (Shibata et al. 2004), and by activation of matrix metalloproteinases that degrade MBP (Ihara et al. 2001). The activation of microglia and metalloproteinases may occur from seven or three days after BCAS, respectively (Ihara et al. 2001; Shibata et al. 2004). Moreover, the presence of white matter hyperintensities positively correlates with morphological changes in the retina of patients with cerebral small vessel disease (Song et al. 2024). These patients present pathological retinopathy features, abnormalities of the retinal vasculature (Cheung et al. 2010; Geerling et al. 2022), thinning of the choroid (Kwapong et al. 2021), decreased complexity of the retinal vascular network, and reduced thickness of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (Zhou et al. 2022). Young BCAS mice show that the reduction of the capillary red-blood-cell flux is more dramatic, up to 60%, in the retina than in the white matter area (around 30% reduction) seven days after surgery (Li et al. 2023). This abrupt drop in the blood flow in the retina may change its structure and morphology. Studies using unilateral common carotid artery occlusion or BCAS in young rodents reported reduced thickness of the inner retinal layer (Lee et al. 2020) (Lavinsky et al. 2006; Yamamoto et al. 2006; Kalesnykas et al. 2008; Crespo-Garcia et al. 2018; Qin et al. 2019). Thus, the initial reduction of brain perfusion caused by BCAS surgery may trigger molecular and cellular changes in the brain and the eyes at an early stage of the model. While the brain perfusion is being restored during the subsequent weeks, the brain and retina tissues are still exposed to a partially hypoxic and hypoperfused environment during that period, which may aggravate the initial changes in their cells. Thus, these early and middle-stage changes could contribute to the progression of more profound changes in the structure and neuronal connectivity of these organs, which may importantly affect their function at a later stage. Thus, the molecular and cellular mechanisms that occur in BCAS mice may differ from those that occur in acute models, as BCAS mice may show a combination of mechanisms triggered by an acute response with a long-term response. Important changes induced by brain hypoperfusion have also been observed in the retinal vasculature (Cheung et al. 2010; Geerling et al. 2022). We are aware that our study lacks direct measurements of the vasculature in the retina. Further studies will determine whether BCAS induces changes in anatomical branching patterns, vascular density, and vessel length. Taken together, rodent models of cerebral hypoperfusion recapitulate both white matter degeneration and retinal pathology, thereby offering a robust experimental platform to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving vision dysfunction in hypoperfusion conditions.

We found a higher abundance of rod terminals and an elevated number of rod bipolar sprouts in the ONL in the retina of BCAS mice, compared with those from sham mice. An increased presence of rod terminals and dendritic processes from rod bipolars in the ONL has been observed in the retinas of aged mice and human donors compared to those of young animals and donors (Liets et al. 2006; Eliasieh et al. 2007; Terzibasi et al. 2009; Samuel et al. 2011; Zhu et al. 2023). Rod photoreceptor terminal retractions toward cell bodies in the ONL also occur in the retina of patients with proliferative vitreoretinopathy (Sethi et al. 2005). Accumulation of rod photoreceptor terminals and pronounced dendritic sprouting of bipolar cells within the ONL has been associated with progressive photoreceptor degeneration and severe visual deficits, culminating in blindness, in experimental models of retinitis pigmentosa (McGill et al. 2012; Fu et al. 2017), Leber congenital amaurosis (Hernandez et al. 2010), ocular hypertension (Cuenca et al. 2010), congenital stationary night blindness (Clemons et al. 2023), and retinal detachment (Linberg et al. 2006; Kaneko et al. 2008). Rod terminal retraction compromises synaptic transmission, and this lack of input from rod photoreceptors leads to bipolar sprouting, which is an early response to the degeneration of photoreceptors. An example of how a lack of synaptic transmission leads to retinal degeneration is the downregulation of the gene Emc3, which contributes to the synthesis of rhodopsin in rod photoreceptors. Rhodopsin triggers a signaling pathway to release the neurotransmitter glutamate from rod photoreceptors. An exacerbated decrease in glutamate causes photoreceptor death. Thus, Emc3 downregulation leads to degeneration of rod bipolar cells and visual deficits in mice (Zhu et al. 2020). Interestingly, we did not observe differences in the number of cone bipolar cells and horizontal cells in the INL of BCAS mice. The length of sprouts of cone bipolar and horizontal cells was not different between BCAS and sham mice either. In retinal detachment (Erickson et al. 1983; Lewis et al. 1998) and in congenital stationary night blindness (Bayley and Morgans 2007), ectopic contact of cone photoreceptor terminals and bipolar cells in the ONL is also less frequent, supporting our findings. This evidence suggests that there may exist different sensitivities to cerebral hypoperfusion between cones and horizontal cells and rod and bipolar cells. Indeed, rods are more vulnerable to stress than cones due to their higher sensitivity to light brightness, longer light responses, and less efficient retinoid metabolism (Matthews et al. 1990; Burkhardt 1994; Lamb and Pugh 2004; Kawamura and Tachibanaki 2008; Nikonov et al. 2008; Ingram et al. 2016).

In our study, we found that aged BCAS mice exhibit visual impairments compared with sham mice, particularly, they show increased retraction of rod photoreceptors and sprouting of rod bipolars. Our findings suggest that BCAS mice may have impaired dim-light (night) and reduced peripheral vision, compared with sham mice. This could affect the performance of visual-based behavior tests, such as NORT. In NORT, the mouse is exposed to two identical objects (same shape and color), and 24 h later, one of these objects is replaced by a different one (novel), which differs in shape and color. We may have solved this limitation by illuminating the arena with 120 V light bulbs. In addition, having cone bipolars unaffected in BCAS mice suggests that these mice can identify shapes and colors; thus, differences in the recognition index that we found between BCAS and sham mice may actually indicate a degree of cognitive decline induced by brain hypoperfusion. Nevertheless, we are aware that our study lacks a non-visual clue cognitive test to further confirm that BCAS mice show memory deficits. A possible non-visual clue test is assessing olfactory behavior, in which a mouse in an arena is presented with different odors in a temporal sequence or in several phases. With this test, we can measure temporal odor memory, novel odor recognition, and habituation (Arbuckle et al. 2015; Aqrabawi and Kim 2018). Indeed, the olfactory function is of major importance in mice, and their olfactory bulb is prominent, compared with that in humans, and it is importantly irrigated by the olfactory artery, which drains from the internal carotid artery and the middle cerebral artery (Okuyama et al. 2004). Given the stenosis of the carotid arteries, the blood flow in the olfactory bulbs may be dramatically affected, and consequently, may negatively alter the olfactory function.

We also observed that the mortality in aged male BCAS is 50%; however, female BCAS mice showed ~ 18% mortality. Other studies have found that the use of 0.16 mm micro-coils may cause high mortality (up to 75%) in male mice, compared with using larger micro-coils, such as 0.20–0.18 mm (Ishikawa et al. 2023). The occlusion of the carotid arteries is lethal for mice, as it has been proposed that their circle of Willis is not complete in this rodent species (Lee et al. 2020). Thus, the use of narrower coils may compromise the blood flow in the brains of mice and lead to an increased mortality rate. However, there exists important variability in the architecture of the circle of Willis and in the diameter of the cerebral arteries between mouse strains and between the individuals of the same strain (Ward et al. 1990; Qian et al. 2018). This variability may influence the survival of mice after the implantation of 0.16 mm coils. Besides our study, no one has compared the mortality in BCAS mice of both sexes. The different mortality rates between male and female BCAS mice indicate differences in how brain hypoperfusion affects each sex. The reduced survival rate in aged male BCAS mice may be partially explained by sex-dependent differences in baseline cerebral blood flow, which is commonly higher in females than in males (Kastrup et al. 1999; Wang et al. 2020; Alisch et al. 2021; Mazzucco et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024). Thus, the abrupt drop of the cerebral blood flow caused by 0.16 mm micro-coils may be more detrimental to male mice than to females. These results emphasize the difference in the brain vasculature between the sexes and how the manipulation of the cerebral blood flow can lead to different outcomes in a sex-dependent manner.

In summary, this study is important for several aspects: it is the first study using aged mice to model hypoperfusion retinopathy; it includes both sexes of mice; it determines that the rod pathway, but not the cone pathway, is negatively affected by BCAS; and BCAS reduces the retinal response to light stimuli. This study set the foundation to use aged BCAS females and male mice to determine the molecular mechanisms of vascular dementia and hypoperfusion retinopathy.
