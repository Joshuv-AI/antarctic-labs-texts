Pharmacologically stabilizing RNA G-quadruplexes in coronavirus genome reduces infectivity
Authors: Yong Woo Lee, Roy Blum, Tyler Mrozowich, Bojan Bujisic, James E. Kirby, Jeannie T. Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
RNA G-quadruplexes (rG4s) are unusual RNA secondary structures formed by stacking arrays of guanine tetrads. Although thousands of potential rG4-forming motifs occur throughout the mammalian transcriptome, many single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) viruses are thought to be depleted of rG4-forming sequences. Using in silico methods, we examine rG4-forming potential in single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) viruses and observe that, while canonical rG4 motifs are depleted, noncanonical rG4 motifs occur at comparable or higher frequencies relative to the mammalian transcriptome. We ask if the noncanonical rG4's can be leveraged to block viral replication and control infection using OC43, the coronavirus believed to be responsible for the 1889 “Russian flu” pandemic. Profiling with “d-rG4-seq” confirms a dearth of folded rG4 in the OC43 RNA genome during natural infection. Intriguingly, rG4 ligands induce synthetic rG4 structures of a noncanonical nature. Significantly, induced rG4 inhibits viral replication and reduces infectivity. We show that the rG4 ligands act by disrupting the unique pattern of OC43 “discontinuous transcription.” Thus, rG4-targeting compounds present a potential therapeutic approach for targeting ssRNA viruses.

INTRODUCTION

The recent coronavirus pandemic, caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has led to over 700 million cases and 7 million deaths globally (https://covid19.who.int/). The fast-evolving nature of this RNA virus continues to spawn alarming variants that undermine and evade established vaccines (Drake and Holland 1999; Elena et al. 2000; Cyranoski 2021; Callaway 2022). Some new variants also spread more rapidly, making preventative measures and therapeutic management increasingly challenging (Araf et al. 2022; El-Shabasy et al. 2022). Although the threat of SARS-CoV-2 peaked in 2020–2021, other coronavirus strains are also clinically significant and may emerge in a pandemic without warning. One strain of historical significance is OC43, which is now believed to be responsible for the 1889–1894 “Russian flu” pandemic that killed >1 million people among a world population of 1.5 billion (Vijgen et al. 2005; Brussow and Brussow 2021). Thus, continuing waves of SARS-CoV-2 infections and the threat of additional coronavirus strains underscore the necessity for a deeper understanding of RNA virus biology and for new approaches to treatment.

The single-stranded positive-sense genomes of betacoronaviruses are exceptionally large—approximately 30 kilobases (kb) in length. This genome includes two open reading frames (ORFs) that encode nonstructural proteins and nine small ORFs for structural and accessory proteins. Functional structured regions of coronavirus RNA encompass the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) and the ORF1 and ORF2 ribosomal frameshifting sites (Plant et al. 2005; Yang and Leibowitz 2015). These regions perform essential roles in the virus life cycle, such as genome replication, subgenomic RNA synthesis, and translation initiation (Brierley et al. 1989; Plant et al. 2005; Liu et al. 2007; Li et al. 2008; Yang and Leibowitz 2015). Previous RNA structure probing studies have offered full-length structure profiles of the SARS-CoV-2 genome using high-throughput sequencing-based methods, including DMS-MaP-seq, SHAPE-MaP-seq, and COMRADES (Manfredonia et al. 2020; Ziv et al. 2020; Cao et al. 2021; Huston et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2021; Lan et al. 2022). Other RNA viral genomes have been profiled as well for their relevance to therapeutic development (Li et al. 2018; Simon et al. 2019; Wan et al. 2022; Huston et al. 2024). These genomic RNA structure maps have revealed evolutionary conservation and highlighted their importance in the virus infection process (Bassett et al. 2022).

RNA structure maps may be helpful to guide design of antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) drugs targeting the RNA virus genome. For instance, a previous study successfully reduced SARS-CoV-2 infection using ASOs that target structured viral RNA domains (Holden et al. 2006; Tuplin et al. 2015; Huston et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021; Wan et al. 2022). Though effective and highly specific, ASOs generally do not have good biodistribution without the aid of delivery vehicles, and may also be difficult to produce, distribute, and administer at scale during a global pandemic. On the other hand, small molecule viral inhibitors would have more favorable manufacturing, distribution, bioavailability, and compliance profiles. With an accurate viral structure profile, small molecules could be screened or designed to target specific 3D structures in the RNA genome (Parsons et al. 2009; Fedorova et al. 2018; Warner et al. 2018; Hewitt et al. 2019; Haniff et al. 2020).

RNA secondary structure of special interest is an RNA G-quadruplex (rG4). rG4s are a family of ribonucleic acid structures comprised of stacked guanine (G) tetrads generated by Hoogsteen base-pairing between the guanines (Bochman et al. 2012; Rhodes and Lipps 2015). Interestingly, while the mammalian genome harbors many motifs that can fold into rG4 in vitro and in vivo (Kwok et al. 2016a; Garant et al. 2017; Puig Lombardi and Londono-Vallejo 2020), prior reports have noted that bacterial DNA genomes and viral RNA genomes may be depleted of rG4 motifs (Guo and Bartel 2016; Bohalova et al. 2021), potentially due to their in vivo toxicity (Supplemental Fig. S1). On the other hand, affinity-based approaches using G4-specific antibodies or small-molecule probes have argued for folding of rG4 in vivo in bacteria and viruses (Shao et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022a, 2024; Qin et al. 2022; Schult et al. 2024). However, small-molecule probes may artificially stabilize or induce rG4 folding (Biffi et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2018). Furthermore, antibody specificities have been called into question, and the limited resolution of RNA pulldown methods has also complicated interpretation, as the purified fragments tend to be hundreds to thousands of nucleotides (Varshney et al. 2021; Feng et al. 2023).

Here we apply our previously established method, d-rG4-seq, to investigate rG4 structures in viral genomes at high resolution (Lee et al. 2024a,b). rG4s generally require the K+ cation to promote their folding and stabilization. With the intracellular environment being high in K+, rG4 folding may be common inside cells, although folding may occur only transiently (Xu et al. 2010; Laguerre et al. 2015; Guo and Bartel 2016; Fay et al. 2017; Cho et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2018, 2020, 2022; Varshney et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022b; Lee et al. 2024b). Consistent with this, our recent work using X-chromosome inactivation as a model demonstrated significant rG4 folding in the noncoding Xist RNA, with mutagenesis resulting in compromised Polycomb recruitment and gene silencing on the X-chromosome (Lee et al. 2024b). Given the depletion of rG4s in RNA viruses, we now use d-rG4-seq to ask if pharmacologically inducing rG4 formation in the coronavirus RNA genome could reduce viral fitness and infectivity. Various chemical compounds can bind and stabilize rG4 structures (Moore et al. 2006; De Cian et al. 2007; Rodriguez et al. 2008; Yang et al. 2018; Santos et al. 2021; Frasson et al. 2022a), and an antiviral effect was previously reported for SARS-CoV-2, though mechanisms have not been reported (Vijgen et al. 2005; Liu et al. 2022a; Qin et al. 2022). Here we use the other pandemic coronavirus strain, OC43, to address the potential for inhibition and mechanisms of action. Our findings indicate that targeting rG4 may have a therapeutic promise in future antiviral applications.

RESULTS

ssRNA virus genomes are depleted of canonical rG4 but enriched for noncanonical rG4 motifs

The coronavirus RNA genome is thought to have low G-quadruplex potential (Guo and Bartel 2016; Bohalova et al. 2021). To revisit this question, we used the G-quadruplex prediction module in rG4Seeker (Kwok et al. 2016a) to calculate the potential G-quadruplex motif density among the virus species with different genome classes. rG4Seeker categorizes G-quadruplexes into four classes: (i) classical three-quartet rG4s denoted “canonical/G3L1–7,” where L indicates 1–7 nt in the short loops between three consecutive guanines, (ii) “long-loops” where loops involve >7 nt, (iii) “bulges” in which bulge sequences occur within the G-track, and (iv) “two-quartet” where the RNA contains runs of two consecutive Gs to form a two-planar rG4 stack (Fig. 1A, left panel; Kwok et al. 2016a; Chow et al. 2020). All share the stacked G-quartets, but differ in the number of G-stacks, the number of bulges, and the length of the loop comprising the bulge.

The left panel shows a schematic representation of four rG4 subclasses. rG4s are classified into four categories as indicated in Kwok et al. (2016a). The right panel represents the locations of predicted rG4 motifs in the OC43 reference genome. Only two-quartets are predicted with OC43 reference genome sequence. (B) Comparison of in silico rG4 motif density (determined by rG4seeker) in the mouse and human genome and transcriptome relative to viral RNAs.

We analyzed NCBI sequence data sets, a list of sequences from dsDNA, dsRNA, and ssRNA viruses and multiple mammalian genomes and transcriptomes. G-quadruplex motif prediction indicates a greater availability of potential motifs ordered from two-quartet, bulges, long-loop, to canonical (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. S1). In mammals, both DNA and RNA sequences indicated consistent G-quadruplex motif densities among species, ranging from 0.03 to 0.05 motifs/kb for canonical motifs, 0.04–0.1 motifs/kb for long-loop motifs, 0.4–1 motifs/kb for bulges, and 1–3 motifs/kb for two-quartet (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. S1). On the other hand, G-quadruplex frequencies among virus species are highly variable. In dsDNA viruses, genome sequences that demonstrated a higher density of canonical G-quadruplex motifs also showed greater frequencies of noncanonical G-quadruplex motifs than mammals (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. S1: cytomegaloviruses, Epstein–Barr viruses, and human adenoviruses). Conversely, other dsDNA and dsRNA viruses depleted in the canonical G-quadruplex motifs also showed lower than mammalian-level noncanonical G-quadruplex density (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. S1: monkeypox viruses, vaccinia viruses, variola viruses, and rotaviruses). Interestingly, ssRNA viruses generally exhibited a higher or comparable density of nonconventional two-quartet and bulge motifs than mammalian sequences (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. S1), while canonical G-quadruplex motifs were nearly absent among the ssRNA virus species examined here. Interestingly, this pattern was also evident in several emerging ssRNA viruses, including dengue virus and avian influenza viruses (Fig. 1B). Consistent with the rG4 motif density prediction in ssRNA viruses, OC43 RNA genome sequence contained only noncanonical two-quartet motifs (Fig. 1A, right panel). Overall, ssRNA virus genomes were depleted of canonical rG4 motifs but were enriched for the less stable, noncanonical rG4 motifs.

rG4 ligands stabilize noncanonical rG4 motifs in the OC43 genome

We asked if the noncanonical rG4's can be used as a lever to control viral infection and inhibit viral replication to control infection. To test this idea, we used an established host–pathogen system consisting of the pathogenic OC43 virus model and HCT8, a human adenocarcinoma cell line widely used to study coronavirus infection (Gorse et al. 2009; Lai et al. 2014). First, we queried the OC43 RNA structure by performing our recently developed d-rG4-seq method (Rouskin et al. 2014; Guo and Bartel 2016; Lee et al. 2024b). The “d-rG4-seq” method is based on differential sensitivity of various RNA structures to methylation by dimethyl sulfate (DMS) and read-through by reverse transcriptase (RT), coupled to the ability of RNA to refold after denaturation in the presence of monovalent cations Li+ versus K+ (Fig. 2A; Lee et al. 2024b). DMS can methylate three of four nucleosides in RNA: adenosine (A) at the N1 position, cytosine (C) at the N3 position, and guanine at the N7 position. However, when RNA is duplexed via Watson–Crick or wobble base-pairing, the N1-A and N3-C bases are protected from DMS, because N1-A and N3-C participate in hydrogen bonding for base-pairing. In contrast, the N7-G of RNA duplexes is not protected, because the N7 base is not involved in hydrogen bonding during Watson–Crick or wobble base-pairing. On the other hand, when RNA is in rG4 conformation, N7-G is protected from DMS because of its involvement in G:G hydrogen bonding within the tetrad, whereas N1-A and N3-C are not protected because they are not involved in Hoogsteen base-pairing.

Cells were cultured with or without OC43 infection for 48 h and subsequently treated with rG4 ligands for 6 h prior to DMS treatment for the “in vivo” rG4 profile. For in vitro analysis, we do not include a DMS treatment step prior to RNA isolation. We purified total poly(A)+ RNA fractions and performed d-rG4-seq. We fragment the RNA, ligate adaptors, and denature the RNA to eliminate secondary structures. We then renatured the RNA in the presence of K+ (which enables rG4 to form stably) or Li+ (which does not stabilize rG4). We next reverse-transcribed the RNA using Superscript III RT, performed size-selection, and cloned the products for sequencing. In principle, rG4s that reform in vitro following denaturation would occur only in K+, but not Li+. Because RT cannot process past a folded rG4, we can infer the formation of rG4 structures by observing a chain-termination event (RT-Stop) following sequencing (Guo and Bartel 2016). Thus, following a round of denaturation and renaturation, if the G-rich region did not fold into rG4 after denaturation, we infer that the N7-G was DMS-methylated in cells and therefore did not adopt an rG4 conformation in vivo. If a G-rich region could fold into rG4 following denaturation, the N7-G must have been protected from DMS in vivo, and we could therefore infer the occurrence of an rG4 in vivo. In this case, the rG4 would refold following denaturation in the presence of K+ only, not Li+. The in vivo rG4 would be inferred from the sequencing of RT primer extension profiles at nucleotide resolution. RT can read through methylated N7-G, but stalls and chain-terminates (RT-stop) when there is a folded rG4 structure, a methylated N1-A, or methylated N3-C. In this way, RT-stop patterns enable us to infer the presence not only of rG4 but also Watson–Crick or wobble-based RNA duplexes.

With 0.5–2.0 million reads, we obtained at least 100 reads per nucleotide coverage all across the 30 kb coronavirus genome (Supplemental Fig. S2). OC43 reads accounted for 2%–4% of the total reads, with the remaining 96%–98% reads allocated to HCT8 reference genome (Supplemental Table S1). Consistent with the previous report (Guo and Bartel 2016), we observed K+-dependent strong (>50% stop frequency) RT-stop sites at the G-rich regions (Supplemental Fig. S3A). For instance, the noncoding RNA MALAT1 shows 10 strong K+ RT-stop sequences, located at the 3′ end of G-rich rG4 motifs (Supplemental Fig. S3B). In infected cells without rG4 stabilizers (DMSO-treated), in vitro analysis showed a depletion of K+-dependent RT-stops across the OC43 transcript (Fig. 2B, gray bars), consistent with the predicted dearth of rG4s in the coronavirus RNA genome. Only 10 RT-stop sites were detected in vitro, and none folded into rG4 in vivo, as DMS treatment eliminated all K+-dependent RT-stops (Fig. 2B,C; Supplemental Tables S2, S3).

We then analyzed infected cells that have been treated with one of several compounds known to stabilize the G-quadruplex structure: (i) the exceptionally high-affinity rG4 binder, PhenDC3, (ii) the relatively lower-affinity rG4 binder, pyridostatin (PDS), and (iii) CX-5461, a DNA G-quadruplex binder that selectively inhibits POL-I transcription of rRNA (Sanchez-Martin et al. 2021). rG4 ligands are characterized by multiple polycyclic aromatic rings, which interact with the planar G-stacks of the G-quadruplex (Frasson et al. 2022a). Consistent with its higher affinity for rG4, PhenDC3 strongly induced RT-stops in the rG4-seq, while PDS resulted in fewer RT-stops (Fig. 2B). PhenDC3 treatment led to the induction of 88 in vitro rG4 sites, of which 38 (43%) folded significantly into rG4 structures in vivo (Fig. 2C; Supplemental Tables S4, S5). PDS treatment promotes significant rG4 folding but is less effective than PhenDC3; it generates 12 in vitro rG4 sites with four sites demonstrating in vivo rG4 folding (Fig. 2C; Supplemental Tables S6, S7). In contrast, DNA G-quadruplex binding CX-5461 compound yielded only four in vitro rG4 sites, none of which folded into rG4 inside cells (Fig. 2C; Supplemental Tables S8, S9). Therefore, despite the depletion of rG4 motifs in the OC43 genome, it is possible to induce synthetic rG4 formations using stabilizing compounds. Compound-induced rG4s were not restricted to the predicted rG4 motif regions. Seventy-six rG4 formations with PhenDC3 and seven with PDS were detected in G-rich sequences. Interestingly, these were not predicted by rG4Seeker. Although these empirically determined rG4 structures did not conform to previously defined rG4 motifs, they were still characterized by consecutive G-rich sequences (Fig 2C, bottom panel).

To verify that the noncanonical motifs fold into rG4s, we used a UV/FRET)–based assay using RNA oligonucleotides derived from one of the identified synthetic rG4 sequences in the OC43 genome (Ying et al. 2003; Wang et al. 2019). This RNA oligo was dually labeled with a 5′ fluorophore (FAM) and a 3′ quencher (BHQ-1), enabling us to track rG4 folding via fluorescence changes. When the rG4 folds, the distance between FAM and BHQ-1 decreases, resulting in fluorescence quenching. Using this system, we observed strong quenching in the presence of PhenDC3 and PDS at room temperature (Fig. 2D), consistent with formation and stabilization of the rG4. Quenching was observed only in the presence of K+, not Li+, further consistent with rG4 stabilization. Melting temperatures of 49.5°C and 50.8°C were observed in the presence of PhenDC3 and PDS, respectively, indicating moderate thermal stabilities. Notably, reducing the temperature to as low as 4°C did not induce rG4 formation without both the G4 ligand and K+ ions, suggesting that the sequences are weak rG4 folders without the small-molecule stabilizers and fold stably only in the presence of the ligands. For an orthogonal method of detection, we also performed a native gel mobility shift assay to examine the binding of G4 ligands to OC43 rG4s. Small rG4 fragments identified by d-rG4-seq showed mobility shifts in the presence of G4 ligands, while the negative control RNA showed no change in gel migration (Supplemental Fig. S3C). The first tested rG4 fragment bound PhenDC3, whereas the second fragment bound both PDS and PhenDC3. These mobility shifts occurred in a K+-dependent manner, consistent with the shifts being mediated by rG4 formation. Thus, the noncanonical G-motifs fold into rG4s in the presence of the G4 ligands, and our d-rG4-seq method effectively captures such structures.

The extent of synthetic rG4 formation was comparable to that observed in HCT8 host transcripts in both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 2E). Consistent with previously reported in vitro rG4-seq data (Kwok et al. 2016a), PhenDC3 and PDS treatments show an increased relative ratio of detected noncanonical rG4 sites, such as two-quartet and bulges, compared to canonical and long-loop motifs (Fig. 2F; Supplemental Tables S4, S6). This preference for noncanonical rG4s of the ligands may explain the similar effects observed with rG4 ligands in both OC43 and HCT8 rG4s, despite the substantial depletion of canonical rG4 motifs in OC43 viral RNA genome. Thus, treatment with rG4 ligands can induce formation and/or stabilization of noncanonical rG4 structures in the OC43 genome (Fig. 1A, right panel; Fig. 2C).

Ligand-induced RNA duplex structures in OC43 coronavirus genome

Because DMS can also modify N1-A and N3-C when they are in un-duplexed form, the d-rG4-seq profile also reveals the presence of duplex RNA in OC43 when the N1-A and N3-C positions are protected from DMS methylation (Supplemental Fig. S4A; Rouskin et al. 2014). Agreeing with previous studies (Yang and Leibowitz 2015), high DMS reactivity was detected in exposed nucleotides in the stem-loops SL5A, SL5B, SL5C, SL6, and SL7 at the 5′ terminus (Fig. 3A). These sequences at the 5′ end are known to be crucial for virus replication (Liu et al. 2007; Li et al. 2008; Yang and Leibowitz 2015). Interestingly, DMS reactivity profile of the 5′ end indicated significant changes in RNA duplex structures after PhenDC3 treatment, while PDS and CX-5461 treatment did not show significant effects (Fig. 3B). We then calculated a Gini index from the DMS reactivity patterns. A high Gini index, indicating an uneven distribution of DMS methylation across the RNA, suggests the presence of duplex-structure RNAs, reflecting the protection of base-paired N1-A and N3-C nucleotides from DMS methylation. Notably, analysis of both replicates showed a low Gini index—i.e., the genomic RNA of OC43 is relatively unstructured compared to host transcripts, ranking 345th out of 392 transcripts (Supplemental Fig. S4B). Treatment with various rG4 ligands results in distinct changes in the global RNA structure patterns. Most prominent changes are observed with PDS treatment, with a significant increase in Gini value indicating the PDS-induced RNA duplex formations in both HCT8 and OC43 RNA (Fig. 3C). Significant but smaller Gini index differences were also detected in PhenDC3 and CX-5416 treated cells (Fig. 3C).

We plotted the DMS reactivity pattern on the OC43 RNA genome to examine the effects of rG4 ligands in greater detail (Fig. 3D). Notably, the 5′ end of the genomic RNA showed distinctively low DMS reactivity, consistent with prior reports (Liu et al. 2007; Li et al. 2008; Yang and Leibowitz 2015). Although the Gini index indicated no substantial difference in the genome-wide RNA folding profile, DMS reactivity plots revealed long RNA regions of several hundred nucleotides that showed deviation from the DMS reactivity patterns in the DMSO control (Fig. 3D). The difference was more pronounced in samples treated with rG4 ligand PhenDC3 and PDS than in those treated with CX-5461, a compound that does not stabilize rG4 structures. Agreeing with the Gini index calculations, PDS treatment led to decreased DMS reactivity across the OC43 genome indicating the general increase of RNA duplex structures (Fig. 3D). We therefore conclude that rG4 ligands disrupt RNA duplexes to varying degrees. This disruption could be an indirect consequence of stabilizing rG4.

rG4 induction alters pattern of discontinuous transcription

In all viruses of the Coronaviridae family, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) can undergo discontinuous transcription to generate an impressive array of subgenomic RNAs (sgRNAs) (Sawicki and Sawicki 1998; Miller and Koev 2000). This virus-specific mechanism involves template switching between the 5′-UTR leader sequence and various coding regions of the viral genome (Long 2021), involving hybridization of the TRS-B element in negative-strand RNA with the TRS-L region of the Leader element at the 5′ end of the genome (Sola et al. 2015). This mechanism enables shifting viral gene expression patterns throughout the coronavirus life cycle. sgRNAs may also enhance the virus evolution by increasing RNA recombination as a byproduct of template switching (Simon-Loriere and Holmes 2011).

We asked if the induction of synthetic rG4 structures in the viral genome could interfere with discontinuous transcription, thereby reducing overall viral fitness during infection. To test this idea, we performed transcriptomic analysis in two biological replicates of PhenDC3, PDS, and CX-5461-treated samples. RNA alignment patterns in d-rG4-seq indicated substantial changes in the OC43 genomic RNA (Fig. 4A,B; Supplemental Figs. S2, S5). Without drug treatment (Mock), the RNA-seq reads accumulated homogeneously across the genomic region, though with a modest 3′ bias due to poly(A) selection (Supplemental Figs. S2, S5). Treatment with CX-5461—which does not stabilize rG4 (Fig. 2B–D)—resulted in no significant change in transcript patterns. On the other hand, treatment with either PhenDC3 or PDS—both capable of stabilizing rG4s across the OC43 RNA—resulted in significant disruption of transcription pattern across the OC43 genome suggestive of RdRP stalling due to the de novo induced RNA structures (Fig. 4B). These data indicate that drug-induced rG4 in OC43 can alter viral RNA synthesis.

To directly assess the impact of rG4 ligand treatment on discontinuous transcription, we quantified template-switching products by displaying the OC43 genome in a 2D heat map (Fig. 5A). Using computational tools in HOMER (Heinz et al. 2010)—originally designed for chromosome conformation capture experiments—we generated a matrix to quantify the frequency of pairwise covalently “spliced” viral transcripts resulting from template switching. The heat map revealed a marked increase in sporadic template-switching events across the viral genome, particularly in PhenDC3-treated samples (Fig. 5A,B). While similar patterns were also observed with PDS and CX-5461 treatments, the effects were considerably less pronounced than those observed with PhenDC3, as quantified in the violin plot (Fig. 5C).

This irregular pattern of discontinuous transcription correlated with the extent of rG4 stabilization induced by the compounds (Figs. 2B–D, 5C). PhenDC3, which showed the strongest rG4 stabilization effect among the tested substances, also had the strongest impact on increasing template-switching frequency. In contrast, poor rG4 ligand CX-5461 showed the least impact on template-switching. Interestingly, despite PDS inducing greater changes in RNA duplex structures than PhenDC3, it had a less pronounced effect on template-switching (Figs. 3C,D, 5C). This observation suggested that rG4 structures are more directly involved in the aberrant template-switching observed during the transcription of ssRNA viruses than general RNA duplex structures. Our findings indicate that rG4 stabilization at noncanonical rG4 sites disrupts the discontinuous pattern of OC43 viral RNA synthesis during infection.

rG4 binding drug treatment alleviates coronavirus-induced cell death

Previous reports indicate that PDS can inhibit SARS-CoV-2 infection by stabilizing an rG4 within a host transcript, TMPRSS2, thereby blocking the translation of a critical host factor (Liu et al. 2022a). Here, we investigate whether stabilizing rG4s in the RNA genome could block virus propagation. We expanded the list of rG4-binding compounds by testing three more G-quadruplex ligands: 5,10,15,20-tetrakis-(N-methyl-4-pyridyl)porphyrin (TMPyP4), N-methyl mesoporphyrin IX (NMM), and N,N′-(9-((4-(dimethylamino)phenyl)amino)acridine-3,6-diyl)bis(3-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)propanamide) (BRACO19). We infected HCT8 cells for 16 h with OC43, added the drugs to the media for 48 h, and then harvested cells for the quantitative analysis of virus infection. The amount of virus in cells was determined by the RT-qPCR analysis of OC43 genomic RNA (Supplemental Table S11: RT-qPCR primers). Intriguingly, in a dose–response assay (3, 6, and 12 μM), all tested G-quadruplex ligands significantly reduced virus infection, with the exception of CX-5461—the DNA G4 ligand that did not induce rG4's in OC43 RNA in vivo (Fig. 2B–D, 6A). Instead, CX-5461 seemed to enhance infection by twofold. Among the effective rG4 ligands, PhenDC3 was nearly as effective as the current gold standard, remdesivir—an inhibitor of viral RdRP and an FDA-approved drug for treating active infections (Fig. 6A; Agostini et al. 2018; Jordan et al. 2018). PDS was also almost as effective. Therefore, there was a correlation between the ability to induce rG4 in the OC43 RNA and inhibition of viral infection.

We then used a luciferase-based cell viability assay to examine the overall effect of OC43 infection and rG4 ligand treatment on cell health. Time-course analysis revealed that neither PhenDC3 nor PDS affected HCT8 cell viability when applied for two days in the absence of OC43 infection (Fig. 6B, top panel). Consistent with this observation, RNA-seq analysis indicated no substantial gene expression changes, as volcano plots demonstrated similar gene expression profiles between rG4 ligand-treated and remdesivir-treated samples (Fig. 6C). Comparable numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the HCT80 transcriptome were observed between PDS (216 genes), PhenDC4 (233 genes), and remdesivir (127 genes) treated cells (Fig. 6C; Supplemental Table S10). Furthermore, 2 days of PDS and PhenDC3 treatment did not significantly induce DNA damage response genes (Supplemental Fig. S6). Thus, short-term treatment with rG4 stabilizers did not significantly affect cellular gene expression patterns in comparison to those of remdesivir-treated cells.

On the other hand, OC43 infection significantly impacted HCT8 cell viability, starting at day 4 of infection, with viability dropping to 18% of uninfected controls by day 6 (Fig. 6B, bottom panel). Cytopathic effects, indicated by cell rounding and detachment from the plates, were evident as early as day 3 (Fig. 6D). However, these morphologic changes and cell lethality were effectively rescued by applying rG4 ligand PhenDC3, resulting in cell viability and morphology similar to uninfected controls (Fig. 6B,D). The morphology of PhenDC3-treated cells was comparable to both the controls and infected cells treated with remdesivir (Fig. 6D). In contrast, the weaker rG4 inducer PDS provided only partial rescue of cell lethality and cytopathic effects (Fig. 6B,D), while CX-5461, which does not induce rG4s, was completely ineffective (Fig. 6D). Overall, these findings support the role of synthetic rG4 formation in disrupting viral function and highlight a potential pharmacological approach to mitigating active RNA virus infections.

DISCUSSION

Here we have shown that RNA genomes of betacoronaviruses are nearly fully depleted of canonical rG4 motifs but harbor a number of noncanonical motifs that can fold into stable rG4s in OC43 when pharmacologically induced. Intriguingly, for OC43, the 76 PhenDC3-induced and seven PDS-induced rG4s were not predicted by rG4Seeker, and they do not conform to previously defined canonical or noncanonical motifs, but are nevertheless characterized by G-tracts. We show that synthetically inducing the noncanonical rG4s can block viral RNA replication during cellular infection by disrupting the virus's normal pattern of discontinuous transcription. In doing so, viral infectivity is significantly reduced, and cells are protected from lysis. We therefore conclude that rG4-targeting compounds may offer a potential therapeutic to control coronavirus infection in the future.

The mammalian and viral differences in rG4 content are intriguing. Mammalian species typically harbor 0.03–0.05 motifs/kb for canonical rG4 motifs, and 0.04–0.1 motifs/kb for long-loop motifs, 0.4–1 motifs/kb for bulges, and 1–3 motifs/kb for two-quartet. For viruses, G-quadruplex frequencies are highly variable, with some dsDNA viruses demonstrating a high density of canonical and noncanonical G4 motifs (e.g., cytomegaloviruses, Epstein–Barr viruses), while other dsDNA and dsRNA viruses show near absence of both canonical and noncanonical G-quadruplex motifs (e.g., vaccinia viruses, variola viruses). ssRNA viruses show nearly absent canonical rG4 motifs, but can have a higher or comparable density of nonconventional two-quartet and bulge motifs relative to mammalian sequences. As previously suggested with rG4 motif depletion in bacterial RNAs (Guo and Bartel 2016), the significant lack of rG4 motifs in some viral RNAs could suggest toxicity of folded rG4 structures during the virus life cycle due to their inability to resolve the rG4 structure. This observation suggests that while mammalian RNA can tolerate and use rG4 structures for regulatory functions (Kumari et al. 2007; Marcel et al. 2011; Rhodes and Lipps 2015; Simone et al. 2015; Cammas and Millevoi 2017; Rosenberg et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2024b), viruses may be highly susceptible to the disruptive effects of these motifs. The depletion of rG4 motifs in coronaviral RNA, for example, may result from evolutionary adaptation to prevent the rG4's detrimental impact on genome stability and transcription.

In this study, we exploited this presumptive sensitivity to rG4 structures and leveraged known rG4 ligands (Moore et al. 2006; De Cian et al. 2007; Rodriguez et al. 2008; Santos et al. 2021; Frasson et al. 2022a) to fold and/or stabilize the noncanonical rG4s that occur naturally in OC43. This synthetic introduction of foreign structures had been proposed previously as a strategy to thwart normal viral RNA metabolic processes (Ruggiero and Richter 2023). Here we successfully exploited the rG4s to disrupt OC43's transcription and genome replication, likely by stalling its RdRP. Polymerases are known to be sensitive to nucleic acid structures (Edenberg et al. 2014; Perrone et al. 2015; Luo et al. 2019; Abiri et al. 2021; Ruggiero et al. 2021; Sarkar and Armitage 2021; Frasson et al. 2022b). Notably, this strategy is used in rG4-seq methods of detecting folded structures in vitro and in vivo, as folded rG4s cause a stalling of reverse transcriptase (so-called “RT-Stop”) (Guo and Bartel 2016; Kwok et al. 2016a; Lee et al. 2024b). Interestingly, rG4 ligands exert a more pronounced effect on stabilizing noncanonical rG4 structures than canonical rG4s (Fig. 2F; Kwok et al. 2016a). As previously reported, this selective effect may arise from the relatively weak thermodynamic stability of noncanonical rG4s, as canonical rG4s can exist at least briefly in vivo without the assistance of rG4 ligands (Kwok et al. 2016a). Hence, given the high density of noncanonical rG4 motifs in RNA virus genomes, ligand-assisted stabilization of rG4 structures could be a promising therapeutic approach for RNA virus diseases.

Our study is premised on the induction of intramolecular rG4s by the ligands. Previous studies have suggested that two-quartet rG4s can also form through intermolecular Hoogsteen base-pairing between G-rich motifs (Viglasky et al. 2010; Liano et al. 2021). Under our experimental conditions, intermolecular G-quadruplex formation is highly unlikely due to the limited G-rich motifs in the RNA genome (2–12 rG4 motifs per kb) and the low RNA concentrations (∼20 ng/μL), which do not favor intermolecular interactions. Furthermore, in our d-rG4-seq analysis, all observed K+-dependent RT-stops occurred at sites containing at least four consecutive G-tracts, consistent with intramolecular rG4 formation. Though intermolecular rG4s may still form in vivo, our profiling approach is optimized to capture intramolecular rG4 folding.

Chemical probing of in vivo RNA structures has revealed that intracellular RNAs are substantially less structured compared to their in vitro counterparts (Rouskin et al. 2014; Guo and Bartel 2016). This global RNA unfolding is ATP-dependent, indicating that active RNA remodeling enzymes, particularly RNA helicases, play a critical role in regulating RNA structures within cells (Rouskin et al. 2014). A similar trend of limited in vivo folding was observed for rG4s (Guo and Bartel 2016). Several RNA helicases, including DHX36, DHX21, DHX9, DHX1, DHX5, and MOV10/L, have been implicated in resolving rG4 structures (Chakraborty and Grosse 2011; Thandapani et al. 2015; Vourekas et al. 2015; McRae et al. 2017; Murat et al. 2018; Ribeiro de Almeida et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2019). Additional work will be necessary to determine which helicases may be involved in stabilizing or removing the viral rG4s. rG4 regulation is linked to multiple stages of RNA metabolism, including pre-mRNA splicing, microRNA processing, and mRNA stability (Dumas et al. 2021), and it is known that several RNA-binding proteins influence rG4 structural formation and contribute to the control of translation and RNA turnover (Herviou et al. 2020; Kharel et al. 2020; Dumas et al. 2025). Therefore, although our RNA-seq data reveals lack of significant changes in overall RNA expression levels, host gene translation may still be impacted by synthetic stabilization of rG4 structures on the host transcripts. Effects on the proteome will be examined in future work.

Our d-rG4-seq analysis also has provided insight into duplex structures in OC43. Interestingly, OC43 genomic RNA appears highly DMS-reactive at N1-A and N3-C nucleotides, as compared to host transcripts. The high reactivity at these positions indicates that a significant portion of the OC43 RNA genome is not engaged in Watson–Crick or wobble base-pairing (Fig. 3D; Supplemental Fig. S4B). This unfolded nature contrasts with recent studies suggesting that SARS-CoV-2 RNA is highly structured based on mutation rate profiling (Tavares et al. 2020; Huston et al. 2021). This discrepancy could be originated from inherent differences between OC43 and SARS-CoV-2, or methodological differences (RT-stop vs. mutation rate profiling). Furthermore, RNA structure profiles may change during the virus life cycle. We profiled RNA structure early during infection when OC43 accounted for 5% of total RNA, whereas prior studies used later-stage infections when SARS-CoV-2 accounted for 65% of total RNA (Supplemental Table S1; Kim et al. 2020). Given that coronavirus replication hijacks host machinery to produce a vast amount of viral RNA (de Wilde et al. 2018), this can lead to high RNA folding at later infection stages due to the depletion of active RNA unfolding machinery.

Notably, the ligands also affect the host genome (Fig. 2E; Supplemental Tables S2–S9), preferentially stabilizing noncanonical rG4 structures over canonical rG4s. This observation could raise concerns about potential toxic side effects of using rG4 stabilizers in pharmacotherapy. rG4 stabilizers are also known to bind DNA G-quadruplexes, potentially destabilizing the DNA genome (Muller et al. 2012; Rodriguez et al. 2012; De Magis et al. 2019). Identifying RNA G4-specific ligands that do not bind DNA G4s would be a valuable step in the development of rG4-targeting compounds. Furthermore, although we used the d-rG4-seq pipeline to identify ligand-stabilized rG4 structures, other nucleotide resolution rG4 detection methods developed in the field could reveal additional rG4 motifs that may be too weak to generate detectable RT-stops (Kwok et al. 2016b; Weng et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2020). Regardless, if used in a therapeutic application, the rG4 ligand would likely be administered on a short-term basis—long enough to reduce infection of the virus without permanent effects on the host transcriptome. Indeed, over the time course of infection reduction, cell viability was uncompromised (Fig. 6). Finally, our rG4 motif prediction indicates that other clinically significant viruses exhibit an even higher density of noncanonical rG4 motifs than coronaviruses, examples of which include Cytomegaloviruses and Epstein–Barr viruses. This enrichment indicates a potential vulnerability across a broad spectrum of ssRNA viruses that could be exploited pharmacologically. Thus, development of rG4-binding drugs may lead to identification of new treatment approaches to combat viral pathogens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

OC43 coronavirus culture

HCT8 cells are maintained in RPMI-1640 (Sigma, R8758) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× Pen/Strep. HCT8 host cell line was prepared as a 50% confluent monolayer for virus infection. OC43 virus stock for the experiments was collected from the media supernatant when the severe cytopathic effect was visible among the host cells. Prepared virus stock was aliquoted and frozen at −80°C deep freezer until use. The medium was changed with low serum media (2% FBS) before performing the infection, and thawed virus stock was applied to the media at the multiplicity of infection (MOI) 0.1. MOI was determined by counting the number of cells stained with antinucleocapsid antibody (Sino Biological, 40643-T62) after 12 h of incubation with the virus stock. Media was replaced with media containing drugs after 16 h of initial virus infection, and cells were kept in culture until the indicated time points. For DMS-seq analysis, after initial infection and following 48 h of incubation, drugs were treated 6 h before DMS treatment. Cell vitality was measured using CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega, PR-G7570) following the manufacturer's instructions.

DMS treatment

A confluent 15 cm dish (≈8 × 106 cells) was prepared for each sample. OC43-infected HCT8 cells were trypsinized and resuspended in 5 mL cell culture medium. DMS was applied to the cells at 4% final concentration in the fume hood. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 7 min with gentle agitation. 1.3 mL βME (Sigma, M3148) was added to the reaction to stop the DMS modification. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold wash buffer (1× PBS and 25% βME) to remove the remaining DMS. Recovered cell pellets were snap-frozen with dry ice and stored at −80°C until use.

DMS-seq library preparation

RNA purification was performed with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) following the manufacturer's instructions. To enrich poly(A)+ OC43 genomic RNA for downstream DMS-seq preparation that requires relatively large amount of input RNA, poly(A)+ RNA fraction was isolated using the Oligotex Direct mRNA Kit (QIAGEN, 70022). RT-stop product enrichment was performed following the previous DMS-seq methods (Rouskin et al. 2014; Guo and Bartel 2016; Lee et al. 2024b) with minor modifications. One to five micrograms of poly(A)+ RNA was randomly fragmented with treatment at 95°C for 8 min in RNA fragmentation buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8 and 2 mM MgCl2). RNA fragments were size selected on denaturing urea 8% acrylamide gel; 60–80 nt RNA fragments were excised referring to the low range RNA size marker (NEB, N0364). RNA in gel pieces was recovered using the ZR small-RNA PAGE Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, R1070). RNA 3′ end was dephosphorylated with polynucleotide kinase (PNK) (NEB, M0201S) in 10 μL solution containing 1 μL 10× T4 RNA ligase buffer (NEB, B0216), 1.5 μL T4 PNK, and 0.5 mL SUPERase•In RNase Inhibitor (Invitrogen, AM2696) at 37°C for 5 h. Adaptor ligation was performed by incubating the mixture above with 6 μL 50% PEG8000, 1 μL 10× T4 RNA ligase buffer, 2 μL 20 μM adenylated 3 DNA adaptor (Supplemental Table S11), and 1 μL T4 RNA Ligase 2 truncated K227Q (NEB, M0373), at 16°C for 16 h. Adaptor-ligated product was purified with denaturing urea 8% acrylamide gel purification, by excising the 85–105 nt fragment. RT reaction was performed in the reaction mix including 13 mL adaptor-ligated RNA, 4 μL 5× RT buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 750 mM KCl [or LiCl], 15 mM MgCl2), 1 μL 10 mM dNTP mix, 2 μL 10 μM phosphorylated iSp18 PE Reverse oligo (Supplemental Table S11), and 0.1 μL SUPERase•In RNase Inhibitor. Where indicated, rG4 binding compounds were applied at a concentration of 12 μM PhenDC3, 12 μM PDS, or 12 μM CX-5461. After heat denaturing the RNA at 80°C for 2 min, RNA was renatured at 42°C for 5 min, and then 1 μL 0.1 m DTT and 1 μL SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18080085) were added to the mixture. cDNA synthesis was performed by incubating the reaction at 42°C for 10 min. Immediately after the RT reaction, 2 mL 1 M NaOH was added to the reaction mixture, incubated at 95°C for 15 min to hydrolyze the remaining RNA, and 2 μL 1 M HCl was applied to neutralize the solution. RT-stop product was selected by gel purifying the cDNA fragment at the 78–105 nt length. Recovered cDNA was circularized with 50 units of CircLigase (Epicentre, CL4111K) at 60°C for 12 h, followed by amplification using RT-stop amp primer (Supplemental Table S11) and KAPA HiFi PCR master mix (Roche, KK2601) for 8–11 cycles of PCR. Amplified RT-stop products corresponding to length of 78–105 bp were isolated to further enrich the RT-stop product. Additional PCR with four to five cycles was performed with NEBNext multiplex oligos (NEB, E7335) to introduce Illumina library sequences. The resulting libraries were sequenced on the single-end 150 cycles and 110 G output HiSeq 2000 or NovaSeq Illumina sequencing platforms.

DMS-seq read preprocessing

3′ end adaptor (-a AGATCGGAAGAGC) was clipped from the sequencing reads using Cutadapt (Marcel 2011), followed by deduplication using prinseq (prinseq-lite -derep 1) (Schmieder and Edwards 2011). After deduplication, 6 nt molecular barcodes were removed from the reads (cutadapt -g ^NNNNNN). Reads outside the RT-stop product length range of 18–45 nt were excluded from the data.

rG4 RT-stop counting

Adaptor removed sequencing reads were aligned with GRCh38.p13 and OC43 concatenated genome assembly using STAR aligner (Dobin et al. 2013) with allowing one mismatch (‐‐alignIntronMax 1 ‐‐outFilterMismatchNmax 1 ‐‐outSAMattributes NH HI AS nM MD). Subsequent output files were sorted and indexed by SAMtools (Li et al. 2009). Aligned reads were computed through rG4seeker pipeline (Chow et al. 2020); this program identified in vitro rG4 regions from two biological replicates. Unknown and G ≥ 40% classes were omitted from the analysis due to their lack of consecutive G arrays. DMS sensitivity on in vivo samples was addressed by calculating the binomial distribution of RT-stop frequencies at the identified in vitro rG4 RT-stop sites. In vivo rG4 regions were determined from the rG4 sites showing binomial probability of <0.05 in both biological replicates. The difference in RT-stop ratio (dRSR) was calculated by subtracting the RT-stop frequency in the Li+ samples from the K+ samples.

DMS reactivity calculation

Processed FASTQ sequencing read files from K+ and Li+ samples were combined into files sorted by DMS treated and untreated conditions. Adaptor-processed sequencing reads were mapped on the human genecode v39 and an OC43 concatenated transcript index file by using bowtie (Langmead et al. 2009) with allowing 1 mismatch (-v 1). DMS reactivity was calculated with StructureFold2 (Tack et al. 2018) from the mapped reads files. Gini index and DMS reactivity values were analyzed using StructureFold2 module react_statistics.py and react_bins.py.

Statistical tests

Initial in vitro rG4 site lists were obtained by analyzing non-DMS-treated RT-stop sites with rG4seeker pipeline (Chow et al. 2020). The RT-stop frequency in DMS-treated samples was obtained from rG4seeker, and in vivo rG4 folding regions were determined by calculating the statistical significance (P < 0.05) of DMS-treated K+ RT-stop frequencies compared to Li+ samples (Lee et al. 2024b). Binomial distribution analysis was performed using the BINOM.DIST function in Microsoft Excel. When Li+ samples had 0 RT-stops, a +1 pseudocount was applied to the calculations. Significant rG4 folding sites were identified from motifs showing statistical significance in both biological replicates. Wilcoxon test and two-way ANOVA were performed with GraphPad Prism.

rG4 motif density calculation across the species using rG4seeker

G-quadruplex motif prediction module of rG4seeker (Chow et al. 2020) was used to identify regions in DNA or RNA sequences with the potential to form G-quadruplex structures. rG4seeker predicted and located regions from the reference sequences that exhibited motif characteristics of G4-forming sequences. To run rG4seeker, all viral genomes of each viral family (e.g., dengue) were combined into one FASTA file, and a corresponding fai index file was generated using the samtools faidx command (Li et al. 2009). A combined GFF file was generated for each viral family using the names and genomic lengths of all viruses within that family. To analyze DNA G-quadruplex motifs in mammalian genomes, we created a placeholder GFF file. The corresponding GFF file was obtained from the NCBI Nucleotide database for RNA sequences of mammalian genomes. An INI file was assembled to specify all parameters required for executing rG4seeker. The output files from rG4seeker, which categorize rG4 motifs into four types—bulges, canonical, long loop, and two-quartet—were used to generate summary files. These summary files indicate the number of rG4 motifs of each type identified in each viral or mammalian genome. Using the length of each genome, the density of motifs per 1 kb of the genome was calculated and presented in boxplots.

Differential gene expression analysis

Total RNA samples were isolated from the HCT8 cells treated with 0.12% DMSO, 12 μM PDS, 12 μM PhenDC3, and 3 μM remdesivir for 48 h. rRNA was removed using NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (NEB, E6310S). RNA libraries were prepared from 20 ng of rRNA-depleted RNA using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, E7530), following the product manual. RNA-seq library was deep sequenced as paired-end 150 cycles by NovaSeq system (Illumina). Between 50 and 150 million reads per sample were coordinated to the human GRCh38.p13 genome assembly observing splicing junctions using STAR aligner. Two biological replicates were analyzed with Cufflinks (Trapnell et al. 2012) to plot gene expression pattern changes of rG4 ligand-treated cells.

Template-switching frequency calculation

Template-switching frequency in discontinuous transcription was quantified by examining the ratio of sequencing reads that harbor sequences from distant regions (>100 nt) of the OC43 coronavirus genome. cDNA sequences from d-rG4-seq libraries were analyzed to assess the effects of rG4 ligands on the virus's discontinuous transcription. FASTQ files were filtered using cutadapt (Marcel 2011) to remove duplicated sequences and reads shorter than 18 nt. Paired-end RNA reads were aligned separately to the concatenated GRCh38.p13 and OC43 genomes using the STAR aligner (Dobin et al. 2013), and sequencing reads aligned to the opposite strands were removed using samtools (Li et al. 2009). Subsequent SAM files were provided to the Hi-C analyzing modules of HOMER (Heinz et al. 2010). Genome coordination of read 1 and read 2 were processed with the makeTagDirectory module to record the aligned positions of reads 1 and 2 from the same DNA fragment. Hybrid read matrices were generated using the analyzeHiC command with the flags -res 100 and -window 100 to measure the frequency of cDNA reads harboring sequences 100 nt apart. Matrix generation was limited to OC43 genomic regions to minimize the use of computational resources. Interaction heat maps were generated using TreeView3 (Keil et al. 2016).

UV/FRET melting curve analysis

Labeled rG4 RNA oligonucleotide (/56-FAM/rUrUrGrGrUrGrGrUrGrGrArGrArArArUrGrUrUrArArArArCrUrUrGrGrArA/3BHQ_1/) was synthesized with the sequence identified from one of the OC43 rG4 sites stabilized by both PhenDC3 and PDS (IDT DNA). UV/FRET melting curve experiments were performed using a CFX96 C1000 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad). FAM fluorescence intensity was measured to monitor rG4 structural dynamics during temperature shifts. One micromolar of the labeled RNA fragment was mixed with the K+ or Li+ containing primer extension buffer and 1 μM of indicated rG4 ligands. To measure the synthetic rG4 melting temperature, the PCR cycler program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 80°C for 2 min, renaturation of the structure at 42°C for 5 min, and the FAM signal was recorded during gradual temperature increase from 25°C to 95°C in 0.5°C increments, with real-time FAM signal recording at each step. Results were analyzed and graphed using CFX Maestro (Bio-Rad) and GraphPad Prism. The melting temperature (T1/2) was defined as the temperature at which 50% of fluorescence quenching had occurred. Average melting temperatures and statistical significance were calculated from three replicates.

Native gel electrophoresis assay

One hundred nanograms of in vitro transcribed RNA fragments were mixed with 5× RT buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 750 mM KCl or LiCl; 15 mM MgCl2) and 10 μM G4 ligands in a total reaction volume of 10 μL. The reaction was performed in a PCR machine programmed for incubation at 80°C for 2 min, followed by 5 min at 37°C. RNA fragments were then fractionated on a 12% acrylamide gel containing 0.5× TBE. Where indicated, 50 mM K+ was added to both the gel and the running buffer. Electrophoresis was carried out on ice to minimize excess heat during the run, and the gels were run at 70 V for 90 min. After fractionation, the gel was stained with EtBr for visualization.
