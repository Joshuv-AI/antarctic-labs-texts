Adapting Species Risk Assessments to a Changing Climate: The Underestimated Vulnerability of Foundational Trees
Authors: Blair C. McLaughlin, Morgan M. Abbott, Suzanne Lipton, David D. Ackerly, M. Brooke Rose, Holly V. Moeller, Erika S. Zavaleta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClimate change is one of the top threats to biodiversity. However, many species risk assessment frameworks, including the globally authoritative International Union for Conservation of Nature Red List, do not consistently and comprehensively incorporate loss of climatically suitable habitat into listing or threat categorization decisions. Using California's foundational, endemic and near‐endemic trees as an exemplary set of high biodiversity and cultural value species (N = 27), we reviewed and synthesized the literature on species' model‐projected changes in suitable habitat, and on field‐observed spatial and temporal demographic responses to climate change. We demonstrate multiple approaches to using this existing literature to integrate climate threats into species risk assessments, with a focus on the Red List criteria. Over the next century, our focal species are projected to lose between half and three‐quarters of their existing suitable habitat, which would lead to substantial impacts on ecosystem structure and function in forests, woodlands and savannas. Overall, species are more vulnerable than is reflected by their current Red List status. Even under conservative estimates of climate change impacts, two‐thirds of our focal species could now meet the Red List A3 Criterion for Endangered or Critically Endangered status, because of projected loss of climatically suitable habitat (a six‐fold increase). We also found a positive relationship between species' model‐projected risk and observed demographic responses to climate change, indicating that in many cases, projected responses are already evident on the ground. To inform conservation prioritization, we visualized the geographic patterns of species' suitable habitat loss using heatmaps. Our results illustrate the need to revise species risk assessments in light of climate change to better align research and conservation priorities with species' vulnerability. They also highlight the conservation needs of currently widespread but high climate‐risk species, and the regional threats to ecosystem structure and function from foundational species loss.

Introduction

During this century, climate change is likely to meet or surpass most other threats to biodiversity (Arneth et al. 2020, Bellard et al. 2012, Hoegh‐Guldberg et al. 2019, Shin et al. 2019, Urban et al. 2016), leading to extirpations and declines globally, across taxa (Bellard et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2011, Pinsky et al. 2025, Shin et al. 2019). Yet, many long‐standing species risk assessment frameworks, including the highly influential International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List (Red List, hereafter), do not consistently and systematically include projected declines in species' climatically suitable habitat in threat categorizations and listing decisions (Mancini et al. 2024). This is likely leading to erroneous estimates of vulnerability and missed opportunities for conservation. Conservation and research funding can be directly tied to Red List high‐risk threat designations (e.g., International Oak Society 2024). A threat designation even in lower but emerging risk categories, such as the Red List's “Vulnerable” category, can act as an early‐warning system, alerting managers to the need for increased action before a species reaches a critical state (Keith et al. 2014). A more comprehensive and formalized inclusion of projected climate change impacts could promote a general shift in prioritization of limited state, federal, or global resources to better reflect species' vulnerability.

Foundational species are an often overlooked target for climate change conservation because of their current widespread distributions, despite their disproportionate importance for ecosystem structure and function (Dayton 1972; Ellison et al. 2005). In recognition of their constitutive role, place or ecosystem names often include their foundational species (e.g., “Joshua Tree National Park”). Foundational species loss can shift an ecosystem to an entirely different state, for example, from endemic oak woodlands to exotic annual grasslands. Furthermore, foundational species can confer climate change resilience to their ecosystems by moderating microenvironmental conditions (McLaughlin et al. 2017), and they often sequester large amounts of carbon (Hudiburg et al. 2009), thus playing a critical role in nature‐based solutions to climate change. While adult populations of many foundational trees may be currently widespread, emerging changes in recruitment and mortality can signal vulnerability (Young et al. 2020; Huesca et al. 2021; Mclaughlin and Zavaleta 2012), and threshold mortality events (Allen et al. 2010) may cause nonlinear declines (Trisos et al. 2020).

Here, we use California's endemic and near‐endemic foundational trees as a set of exemplary high biodiversity‐ and cultural‐value (Pavlik 1991; Anderson 2005) species (Table 2, Figure S1, N = 27) to explore the incorporation of climate change into species risk assessments. These focal species are likely relatively vulnerable to climate change due to long generation times, limited dispersal, and endemism (Manes et al. 2021). They are also frequently water‐limited species in a seasonally dry regional climate where drought and temperatures are increasing (Diffenbaugh et al. 2015; Flint et al. 2013). These species have relatively well‐studied distributions. Long‐lived trees in this region, including some of our focal species, have already shown climate‐related stress and die‐offs at large scales (U.S. Forest Service 2017, McLaughlin et al. 2020, Baguskas et al. 2014, Asner et al. 2016, Brodrick and Asner 2017).

We reviewed and synthesized literature on model‐projected loss of climatically suitable habitat and spatial and temporal demographic responses to climate change for our focal species (Table 2). From these data, we explored three methods to integrate climate threats into species risk assessments. We focused heavily on the IUCN Red List criteria, because of its international authority, its extensive influence on research, funding, conservation, policy, and management (Betts et al. 2020, Rodrigues et al. 2006), and its detailed, quantitative listing criteria (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee 2024) against which projected loss of climatically suitable habitat can be empirically assessed. However, because of the importance of integrating climate change threats into species risk assessments more generally (e.g., the US Federal or State Endangered Species Acts; the California Western Joshua Tree Conservation Act) we also explored additional ways to evaluate climate risk.

First, we used the Red List A3 Criterion (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee 2024) (Table 1) to assess species' appropriate “climate‐informed” Red List status given projected declines in climatically suitable habitat (hereafter, “suitable habitat”) (Method 1). This approach presumes that projected declines in suitable habitat fall squarely within the A3 Criterion's definition of projected habitat decline, which can be used independently to qualify species for listing (Mancini et al. 2024). Second, we developed an index of modeled climate risk, based on projected rates of suitable habitat loss (Red List Criterion A3) and projected area of remaining suitable habitat, using thresholds identified in Red List Criterion B1 (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee 2024) (Method 2) (Table 1). This climate risk index allowed us to account for vulnerability in species with broader distributions but high rates of projected loss, as well as those with smaller distributions for which even moderate losses may confer substantial risk. Third, we used field studies on species' spatial and temporal demographic shifts in relation to ongoing climate change to validate modeled climate risk (Method 3). Where climate change is already underway, for example, the increasing temperatures and drought in our study region (Diffenbaugh et al. 2015; Flint et al. 2013), field studies on recent shifts in recruitment, mortality and density can help discern whether species are already responding to climate change. Where empirical demographic data are consistent with modeled risk, we can have increased confidence in the models' reliability, and in pursuing climate‐forward conservation decisions (Sweet et al. 2019).

Methods

Species Selection

This study's focal species included all foundational tree species that grow in dominant or co‐dominant stands; are endemic or near‐endemic to the state of California, USA (CA), with distributions in CA and possibly extending into Oregon, eastern Nevada, eastern Arizona, Baja CA, or Southern Washington; with current distributions larger than 1500 km2 (to avoid highly range restricted species) (N = 31); and for which our literature search (below) returned a species distribution model with projected impacts of climate change (N = 27 species, Table 2). This approach excluded a small number of other foundational tree species with continental distributions that play important ecological roles across the state, such as ponderosa pine ( Pinus ponderosa ) and Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii).

We used ArcGIS Pro to identify the area and extent of focal species' current ranges with range maps developed by Pawlak et al. (2023) for species' California ranges. Where species' ranges extended outside of California, we also used digitized tree extent maps from www.databasin.org derived from Thompson et al. (1999). To establish each species' current Red List status as of Fall 2024, we used the IUCN Red List webtool, located at https://www.iucnredlist.org.

Literature Search Terms and Paper Inclusion Criteria

In Fall of 2023, we searched Google Scholar and Web of Science using the following search string: (“climate change” OR “global warming” OR “range shift” OR “climate adaptation” OR “vulnerability assessment” OR “drought” OR “recruitment” OR “mortality” OR “refugia” OR “assisted migration” OR “assisted colonization” OR “assisted gene flow” OR “assisted evolution” OR “assisted adaptation” OR “managed relocation” OR “species distribution model” OR “fire severity” OR “fire seasonality” OR “fire frequency” OR “warming”) AND (“[focal species Latin name](s)*” OR “[focal species common name](s)*”). This search string was also used to generate papers for a broader literature review on climate change impacts (Abbott et al. in press).

From the papers returned from this search, we selected all peer‐reviewed studies that included a species' future distribution model that combined observations of species distributions with environmental associations, projected a species future distribution, and met criteria for minimum currently acceptable practices for distribution models to be included in species risk assessments (Araújo et al. 2019) (N = 27 species, 18 papers, 68 individual species analyses, Table S1). We also selected studies that included observational spatial/temporal demographic analyses related to climate (N = 18 species, 10 papers, 76 species demography × stressor analyses, Table S2). For inclusion, demographic studies had to evaluate changes in natural juvenile recruitment or species density (number of individuals or canopy cover) or mortality across space (relatively wet versus dry or warm versus cool environments), and over time with at least 2 sampling dates (except for mortality), or using a time proxy based on the local distributions of juvenile versus adult populations. For mortality studies in response to a particular climate event, we allowed a space for time proxy across relatively wet versus dry or warm versus cool environments, and did not require 2 sampling dates for inclusion.

Loss of Suitable Habitat and Climate‐Informed Red List Status (Method 1)

The Red List designates species' risk using a range of criteria (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee 2024). For Method 1, we focused on Red List Criterion A3, because it can be used independently to designate current listing status, and it aligns directly with available data on projected loss of species' suitable habitat (Table 1). Under this criterion, a species that is projected to lose over half of its population over the shorter of three generations or 100 years because of a decline in habitat quality can qualify as endangered. Like Mancini et al. (2024), we assumed a linear relationship between population decline and suitable habitat loss, which, in the absence of species‐specific information, is a permitted assumption in listing criteria (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee 2024).

To generate estimates of projected rates of decline in existing suitable habitat, for each species future distribution model, we used the authors' projected current suitable habitat divided by projected contracting suitable habitat. When these values were not reported in papers that otherwise met review criteria, we requested the data from the authors. We excluded studies for which we were not able to ascertain these values. When the same species distributional and climate model data were used across multiple papers, we only included the most recent paper in our analyses. Our final results included data from 9 papers and 50 individual species analyses (Table S1). For each species analysis, we recorded the climate model(s), emissions scenario(s), modeling approach(es), spatial resolution, and occurrence data type (presence/absence vs. presence only) (Table S1). Most species analyses presented a range of results from various emissions scenarios and climate models, from which we recorded the lowest and highest loss estimates.

The models all projected species' future suitable habitat to a time around the end of the 21st century, and approximately 60% also included a time around the middle of the 21st century. To create common projection timeframes across the analyses, we calculated a per‐year rate of change based on the model's baseline climate data and the year of the projection (when projections were given for spans of multiple years, we used the midpoint of the span). For consistency, for models run to approximate mid‐century, we extrapolated or interpolated results to the year 2055; for models run to approximate end‐of‐century, we extrapolated results to the year 2100. For all models run to approximate end‐of‐century, we also extrapolated results to the year 2125 to align with the Red List A3 Criterion specification of decline over a 100‐year period from the present. For each species, to generate overall “high‐loss” and “low‐loss” estimates, we averaged the high estimates, and separately averaged the low estimates, across all analyses for each focal time period (2055, 2100, and 2125). We used the 2125 low‐ and high‐loss estimates to establish species' “climate‐informed” Red List status, based on the Red List A3 Criterion (Table 1). We then compared the species' high‐loss and low‐loss climate‐informed status to its current Red List status. To evaluate the potential influence of extrapolation to 2125 on our change in Red List status results, we also calculated species' climate‐informed Red List status using the 2100 values.

Most of the studies we evaluated did not include biologically realistic limits on species dispersal into newly suitable habitat (Prasad et al. 2025), and some did not evaluate newly suitable habitat at all. Therefore, we did not include projections of newly suitable habitat in our main analyses. Our focal species generally have slow reproductive rates and low dispersal distances, limiting their capacity to establish new populations in newly climatically suitable areas. For example, a recent meta‐analysis found that oaks (Quercus spp.) disperse an average of 0.2 km and a maximum of 1.5 km per generation (1 generation = 50+ years [Swiecki and Bernhardt 1998]) (Seagle et al. in prep). However, to explore the robustness of our results on change in Red List status to the inclusion of potential newly suitable habitat, we reran our analyses with an estimate of newly suitable habitat equal to 10% of the species' current range (a generous estimate of true potential range expansion) and calculated species' climate‐informed Red List status with these results.

Climate Risk Scores (Method 2)

Because range size as well as rates of decline contribute to species risk, we developed a climate risk scoring system (Method 2) based on projected rates of decline by 2125 (Red List Criterion A3) and projected range size in 2125, using the threshold values from Red List Criterion B1 (Table 1). Red List Criterion B1 currently links total current, but not projected, range size with threat status (Ocampo‐Peñuela et al. 2016), however the range size thresholds specified by Criterion B1 provided useful benchmarks for our climate risk index. Using the % loss of existing suitable habitat projected to 2055, 2100 and 2125 for each species (as described above), and the absolute area of species' ranges as reported by expert range maps (Little and Viereck 1971; Pawlak et al. 2023), we estimated the area of suitable habitat within species' existing ranges likely to remain in 2055, 2100, and 2125.

We developed two sets of species' climate risk scores, one using all analyses, to make use of our full data set, and the second using only analyses from Rose et al. (2023), which provided models for 25 of our 27 focal species and allowed us to evaluate relative rankings among species with internally consistent modeling methods from a single recent study. For the Rose et al. (2023) scores, the low‐end estimate was derived from the CNRM‐CM5 model, emissions scenario RCP 4.5; and the high‐end estimate was derived from the Hadley Centre Global Environment Model version 2, emissions scenario RCP 8.5.

A species' climate risk score incorporated the low‐ and high‐end estimates of its projected rates of decline of suitable habitat within its existing range to 2125, and its projected area of suitable habitat remaining in 2125, using a points‐based system. Points were added according to Red List Criterion A3 for rate of species' decline (although there are no specific designations, we included the 20%–29% declines as Near Threatened), and Criterion B1 area thresholds for projected extent of occurrence (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee 2024) (Table 3). The climate risk score summed the points associated with projected % loss of suitable habitat (low‐loss estimate) + projected % loss of suitable habitat (high‐loss estimate) + projected area of suitable habitat persistence (low‐loss estimate) + projected area of suitable habitat persistence (high‐loss estimate) (Table 3). Possible climate risk scores ranged from 0 to 16, with higher scores representing higher risk.

Alignment of Climate Risk Scores and Field Observations (Method 3)

For all spatial/temporal field demographic studies (Table S2), we recorded whether there was a reported change in recruitment, mortality, or density related to climate; whether the change was related to water (precipitation metrics, climate water deficit, subsurface water availability, or fog inputs) or temperature (any metrics related to temperature including seasonal, maximums/minimums, or annual averages); and the direction of the change. Because of ongoing and projected warming and increasing drought in the study region (Diffenbaugh et al. 2015; Flint et al. 2013), we categorized demographic responses as “consistent with climate change” if they showed lower recruitment, lower density, or higher mortality over time in areas of lower water availability or higher temperatures, relative to areas with higher water availability or lower temperatures; as “inconsistent with climate change” if they showed lower recruitment, lower density, or higher mortality over time in areas of higher water availability or lower temperatures relative to areas with lower water availability or higher temperatures; or as showing no change (Abbott et al. in press). For example, a species showing increasing recruitment in wetter locations relative to drier locations over time was categorized as having a response consistent with climate change. For each species, we integrated these results into an additive response score by adding +1 for each analysis showing results consistent with climate change, −1 for each analysis showing results inconsistent with climate change, and 0 for each analysis that indicated no change; and dividing this number by the total number of analyses.

To explore whether species' spatial/temporal demographic trends aligned with projected climate risk, we rescaled the species demographic scores to between 0.001 and 0.999 and ran a beta regression (Cribari‐Neto and Zeileis 2010) with a Cauchit link function, relating the Rose et al. (2023) climate risk scores (N = 14 species) and the All Studies climate risk scores (N = 18 species) to the species demographic response scores, weighted by the total number of studies per species. The Rose models allowed us to compare climate risk scores derived from values that were generated with the same modeling methods across species, while the All Studies scores allowed us to explore the relationship using all of our data. For both models, we also ran the individual component data scores (B1 and A3) separately (Tables S5 and S7). We ran all appropriate checks on the models. All available data were included in these analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.2; R Core Team 2024) and the betareg package (Cribari‐Neto and Zeileis 2010). Data visualization was done using the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

Hotspots of Loss and Persistence

To identify emergent hotspots of suitable habitat loss and persistence for high‐climate‐risk species (those with a Rose et al. (2023) climate risk score of 8 or above), we used the habitat suitability maps available from Rose et al. (2023) projected to 2055 and 2085. For each species, we computed the difference between the current and future rasters to identify areas where climatic suitability was projected to decline or to persist under future scenarios. We then layered these maps using the terra (Hijmans, Barbosa, et al. 2025), raster (Hijmans, van Etten, et al. 2025), and fs (Hester et al. 2024) packages in R. We created the heatmaps in ArcGIS Pro (ESRI 2023). In the main text, we present heat maps projected to 2055 using the RCP 8.5 scenario, and to year 2085 using the RCP 4.5 scenario, based on reports that the 8.5 scenario is closer to the current global emissions trajectory, but that the 4.5 scenario may be more likely over the longer time period (Hausfather and Peters 2020; Schwalm et al. 2020). In the Supporting Information, we present hotspot maps for additional time periods, scenarios, and climate models (Figure S6).

Results

Loss of Suitable Habitat and Climate‐Informed Red List Status (Method 1)

Over the next century, regionally endemic, foundational tree species were projected, on average, to lose between 54% (low‐loss estimates) and 80% (high‐loss estimates) of the suitable habitat within their current distributions, with a wide range of loss estimates (from 0 to 100%) across species (Figure 1B). Even by mid‐century, models projected substantial loss of suitable habitat, with an average of 28% (low‐loss estimates) to 41% (high‐loss estimates) of species' current distributions (Figure 1A), again with high variation among species. Four species (blue oak, Engelmann oak, Western Joshua tree, and foxtail pine) show losses of over 50% of suitable habitat by 2055 under low‐loss estimates (Figure 1A), indicating high imminent climate risk. For projections to the year 2100, see Figure S2.

Based on projections of suitable habitat loss over the next century, over half (63%) of our focal species increased in Red List status by at least one category under the low‐loss estimates, and the vast majority (89%) did so under high‐loss estimates. Even considering only the low‐loss estimates, 18 species (66%) could now meet the Red List Criterion A3 for Endangered or Critically Endangered status, while only three species currently have these designations (Figure 1B, Table 4). Under the low‐loss estimates, 40% of species shifted from “Least Concern” or “Near Threatened” (the two lowest categories of concern) to “Endangered” or “Critically Endangered” (the two highest categories of concern). Under high‐loss estimates, 40% of species are projected to face complete loss of suitable habitat within their current distributions over the next century (Figure 1, Table 4). We found that these patterns were generally robust to perturbations of the analyses that projected the addition of newly suitable habitats or reduced the timespan of the analyses. When using projections to 2100 (to limit influence of model extrapolation and evaluate a shorter timespan), 55% of species increased at least one rank level under low‐loss and 85% under high‐loss estimates; when using projections that included an addition of 10% of current habitat as newly suitable habitat, 59% of species increased at least one rank level under low‐loss and 85% under high‐loss estimates (Figure S3, Table S3).

Climate Risk Scores (Method 2)

Species' climate risk scores ranged from 0 (coast live oak and California bay laurel) to 16 (western Joshua tree and foxtail pine) (Figure 2). Scores calculated from all studies (N species = 27) show all available data, and scores calculated from Rose et al. (2023) (N species = 25) provide a methodologically consistent comparison from a recent study. Alignment of Climate Risk Scores and Field Observations (Method 3)

Species with higher Rose et al. (2023) climate risk scores were more likely to show demographic field responses consistent with climate change, as evidenced by a positive relationship between climate risk scores and demographic response scores (𝛽 = 0.15, z = 4.3, p < 0.001, pseudo R 2 = 0.05) (Figure 3, Table S4). We found similar results for the all studies climate risk scores (𝛽 = 0.11, z = 5.2, p = <0.001, pseudo. R 2 = 0.21) (Figure S4, Table S6). When analyzed separately as predictors of the demographic response score, both the B1 and A3 components of the climate risk scores were both highly significant, with B1 explaining a slightly higher percentage of the data (Tables S5 and S7).

Hotspots of Loss and Persistence

To visualize hotspots of projected persistence and loss of suitable habitat for high climate risk species (those with a Rose climate risk score ≥ 8), heat maps (derived from Rose et al. (2023), HadGEM2‐ES model) show the number of species for which climatic suitability is projected to be lost (Figure 4A,B,D,E), and the number (Figure 4A,C,D,F) and percent (Figure 4C,F) of species for which climatic suitability is expected to persist, by 2055 (RCP 8.5) and 2085 (RCP 4.5). Percent loss can be interpreted as the inverse of percent persistence.

Hotspots of persistence, where species continue to experience climatic suitability, include, by species number of species, the upper elevations of parts of the Central Coast Ranges, the North San Francisco Bay Area and some mid‐elevation sections of the Sierra Nevada (Figure 4A,C,D,F), and by percent of species, many coastal regions. Hotspots of loss, where many species are projected to lose climatically suitability habitat, include, by number, mid‐elevations of the Sierra Nevada, the East San Francisco Bay Area and parts of the Transverse Range north of Los Angeles (Figure 4B,E), and by percent, the foothills of the Sierra Nevada and interior Coast Ranges (Figure 4C,F). Hotspot maps for all species and for other time periods and emissions scenarios can be found in the Figures S5 and S6.

Discussion

Loss of Suitable Habitat and Climate‐Informed Red List Status (Method 1)

Our results demonstrate the need for Red List up‐listing of many of California's foundational, endemic and near‐endemic trees, based on projected declines in suitable habitat across their existing distributions. Even under the most conservative, low‐loss estimates of climate change, nearly two‐thirds of the species that we analyzed (17) are at higher risk than is reflected by their current Red List status. Under high‐loss estimates (more plausible given current climate change trajectories and rates of change in global net emissions [IPCC 2023]), the vast majority of species (24) would shift to a higher‐risk status. Of particular concern are the many species currently categorized as Least Concern that jump to Endangered or Critically Endangered status under even the low‐loss estimates: these include Western Joshua tree, interior live oak, gray pine, California buckeye, tan oak, Pacific madrone, and blue oak. Equally concerning are the more than half of species that shift their Red List status to critically endangered and/or complete loss of suitable habitat from their current distributions under the high‐loss estimates: these include Engelmann oak, giant sequoia, foxtail pine, Coulter pine, valley oak, blue oak, Oregon oak, big cone Douglas fir, Western Joshua tree, interior live oak, gray pine, tan oak, Pacific madrone and California buckeye. The general patterns of these results were robust to both a reduction in the timeframe of the analyses by 25 years, and the addition of 10% newly suitable habitat.

We found a much higher percent of under‐listed species than Mancini et al. (2024), who reported that 13% of all nonvolant tetrapods would increase in Red List status if climate change projections were considered. This difference suggests that incorporation of climate projections into species risk assessments may be more important for certain taxa than others. Inclusion of climate change could affect listing status most strongly for sessile species that have long generation times, are directly climate‐limited, and/or occur in regions predicted to experience relatively rapid change, such as our focal species. Notably, because the Red List A3 Criterion incorporates projected habitat declines that occur over the shorter of 100 years or three generations, species with short generation times may be less subject to change in status based on climate change projections (Akçakaya et al. 2006). Furthermore, for highly mobile species, a more comprehensive incorporation of projected newly suitable habitat than we took would be necessary.

Climate Risk Scores (Method 2)

We initially focused on species' projected rates of suitable habitat decline to align with the Red List A3 Criterion, since this criterion can be used independently to determine a species' Red List status. However, to further refine climate risk estimates, we created a scoring metric that considered both the projected rates of decline and the geographic extent of species' projected remaining suitable habitat (Method 2). This allowed us to differentiate risk among species with similar decline rates but different range sizes. The climate risk scores generated from all studies and from just the Rose et al. (2023) projections were similar, with a few shifts in species' relative rankings due to the variation in models used across all studies.

As expected, species with high climate risk scores were often the same species that shifted into Endangered/Critically Endangered Red List categories using just the A3 Criterion in Method 1. However, the climate risk scores also highlighted the potential vulnerability of species that were less clearly at risk when just using the A3 Criterion, including Bishop pine, Oregon oak, and red fir, which had moderate projected rates of suitable habitat decline but small projected absolute area of suitable habitat remaining. A few species stood out as having consistently low risk from climate change across both methods, including coast live oak and California bay laurel. These species may be buffered by their coastal distributions, where climate change is projected to be less severe. However, they may be vulnerable to indirect biotic effects of climate change that we did not analyze in this paper, such as a potential interaction between sudden oak death and increasing temperatures (Weed et al. 2013).

Limitations to Methods 1 and 2

Limitations of modelling species' climate futures projections have been discussed extensively elsewhere (Santini et al. 2021, Hijmans and Graham 2006, Beaumont et al. 2008, Franklin 2013, Sinclair et al. 2010, Thuiller et al. 2008); however, these models remain our best guess at the implications of climate change for species' climatic habitat suitability. Such models perform best for species with comprehensive distribution data, making species in our well‐studied region good candidates. However, the models do not take into account indirect biotic impacts of climate change (e.g., potential increases in pest or pathogenic species or invasives competitors [Curtis and Bradley 2015]), synergistic effects with other abiotic stressors (e.g., reductions in groundwater caused by increasing drought), or the effects of increasingly severe and intense wildfires (Backus et al. 2023), all of which make our estimates of habitat suitability declines conservative.

A modeling issue relevant to long‐lived, sessile species with distinct life stages, including our focal species, is the absence of information on life‐stage specific climate tolerance and the possibility of climate niche differentiation across life stages. Most climate futures models (including all of those synthesized in this study) identify species' climate envelopes by their current adult distributions (representing the places where seedlings were able to recruit in the past). However, seedlings can be more climate‐sensitive than adults and therefore have a narrower climate niche (Jackson et al. 2009). This could have various impacts on how projected declines in suitable habitat translate into species range loss. If a species' recruitment envelope has already begun to constrict with ongoing climate change, projections based on the current adult climate envelope may over‐represent suitable habitat. At the same time, if seedlings are more climatically limited than adults, we would expect climate change to act on recruitment first, while relictual adult populations could remain until climate exceeds adult climate tolerance, or they reach the end of their natural lifespan (so called “zombie forests,” Hill et al. 2023). In this case, where the recruitment climate niche is narrower than the adult niche, projections may overestimate adult loss, particularly in the short term or for species with life spans that commonly far exceed the 100‐year time frame associated with the Red List A3 Criterion (e.g., redwoods, foxtail pines, some of the oaks). However, even these projections would remain suitable proxies for unviable, relict populations. Such forests should be considered in decline for conservation purposes, with species range size ideally calculated through the niche of the most limited life stage.

Moreover, adults may be highly sensitive to climate change, as has already been observed in climate change‐related, large‐scale adult tree mortality events globally (Allen et al. 2010; Anderegg et al. 2013), and in our region, including in our focal species, where patterns of die‐off patterns reflect climatic vulnerability (Baguskas et al. 2014, Young et al. 2017, McLaughlin et al. 2020, Lemmo et al. 2024). The unprecedented 2010–16 California drought culminated in over 120 million dead trees—roughly 10% of adult trees in the Sierra Nevada, orders of magnitude over background mortality rates (U.S. Forest Service 2017). This suggests that the overall magnitude of climate change may swamp more minor differences in adult versus juvenile climate envelopes. Nonetheless, models that consider life‐stage–specific climate niches (Mclaughlin and Zavaleta 2012; Doak and Morris 2010) could help improve projections and risk assessments.

We did not include projected newly suitable habitat in our main analyses because the models we synthesized did not consistently treat dispersal constraints when estimating newly suitable habitat, and some did not include projections of newly suitable habitat at all. The Red List A3 Criterion does not require consideration of potentially increasing habitat. It is unclear whether our focal species would be able to track their climatic niches given their generally low dispersal distances, long generation times, as well as the high habitat fragmentation in the region and other recruitment limitations. Nevertheless, the addition of 10% newly suitable habitat to our analyses for all species (a generous estimation of potential range expansion over a 100‐year time frame) yielded qualitatively similar results to our main analyses, with only a 4% reduction in the number of species that increased at least one Red List rank level. Where possible, a consideration of species‐specific dispersal distance and likely recruitment potential in newly suitable habitat could help refine future risk assessments.

The future climate suitability models did not take into account potential local‐scale refugia, where small populations of species may persist within areas broadly projected to lose climatic suitability. In many California species, hydrologic refugia (e.g., where subsurface water persistently accumulates and mitigates climatic drought stress) may be particularly important refuges (McLaughlin et al. 2017). However, potential local refugia are relatively small compared to the full extent of species' ranges (e.g., riparian areas where groundwater may be persistently high comprise less than 2% of the vegetation cover in the state Warner and Hendrix 2023). While such refugia represent important conservation targets, they would likely have negligible influence on the overall, range‐wide declines in climatic habitat suitability.

Alignment of Climate Risk Scores and Field Observations (Method 3)

We used a growing body of field studies on species' recently observed spatial/temporal demographic responses to ongoing climate change to ground‐truth modeled projections of change in habitat suitability. This ground‐truthing is particularly important for narrow‐ranged species, or those that have lost substantial historical range and may not currently be climate‐limited (e.g., in our study: Joshua tree, Foxtail pine, Englemann oak). Since our study region is already warming and increasing in drought frequency and severity (Flint et al. 2013; Diffenbaugh et al. 2015), we expected that if species are indeed sensitive to climate change, those projected to lose climatic suitability should already be experiencing observable demographic shifts. Specifically, we would expect decreasing recruitment and/or higher mortality in the warmer and/or drier relative to the cooler and/or wetter parts of these species' distributions over time. Exploration of the relationship between modeled climate risk and observed climate response allowed us to evaluate whether the modeled changes were already becoming apparent on the ground, increasing confidence in the climate risk scores, and enhancing their usefulness for threat assessments. The overall positive relationship between species' demographic response scores and climate risk scores (Rose et al. 2023), indicated that what the models project is often already observable on the ground. The predictive value of both the A3 and B1 risk score component data to the demographic response scores indicates the value of both of these variables, and the slightly stronger influence of the B1 component underscores the importance of range size to climate vulnerability.

Strong departures from the curve that generally characterized the relationship between projections and observations may indicate species for which models are under‐ or over‐projecting climate change risks. For example, species in the top left quadrant of the graph in Figure 3 with high demographic response scores but low modeled climate risk, such as Bishop pine, may be more vulnerable to climate change than the models predict. Conversely, species in the lower right quadrant with low demographic response scores but high modeled risk, such as giant sequoia, may be less vulnerable to the direct effects of climate change than projected. However, a position off the curve might also indicate a species for which climate change will act through non‐linear threshold effects. For example, for giant sequoia, the most urgent threat of climate change relates to increased high‐severity fire (Soderberg et al. 2024), which was not reflected in the models we analyzed. Alternatively, a lack of comprehensive climate/demographic studies on a species (e.g., Bishop pine, giant sequoia) may limit our understanding of ongoing demographic responses.

Hotspots of Loss and Persistence

The non‐random spatial patterns of species losses with climate change are an important guide for climate‐adaptive conservation. While the loss of climatic suitability matters for individual species risk, in the case of foundational species, it also matters for regional ecosystem‐level structure and function. Therefore, in addition to our individual species analyses, we present heat maps that show areas where a high number or percentage of high‐risk foundational species are likely to lose or maintain suitable habitat (Figure 4, for all species see Figures S5 and S6). These maps provide a starting point to inform landscape‐scale climate‐adaptive conservation priorities. Loss hotspots were located in parts of the east San Francisco Bay Area, the lower and mid‐elevation Sierra Nevada, and the foothills north of the Central Valley. Persistence hotspots included parts of the Central Coast region, the north San Francisco Bay Area, and the higher elevation Sierra Nevada. Interestingly, some places with high tree diversity may be both persistence and loss hotspots in terms of absolute numbers of species. In these areas, existing species that are close to their distributional trailing edge may lose climatic suitability, while others at their mid‐range or leading edges may maintain climatic suitability. Hotspot maps based on numbers of species may miss the importance of regions with a single dominant or co‐dominant foundational species, so we also included hotspot maps based on percent of species. The percent hotspot maps showed many similar overall patterns to the numerical hotspot maps, with the Central Coast and the north San Francisco Bay regions showing even more pronounced persistence—these regions may be an important climate refuge for some of California's highest‐risk foundational species. The percent based maps also identified loss hotspots in the Sierra Nevada foothills, where mono‐ or co‐dominant oaks are projected to decline.

While the hotspot maps represent our best approximations, they are limited, like all future species distribution models, by imperfect current distribution data, the spatial resolution of environmental data, and the uncertainties around climate futures. Because of these limitations, field observations, demographic studies, and local and traditional knowledge are needed to complement these projections and refine spatial conservation priorities. Furthermore, loss hotspots (or other areas of individual species projected loss) do not necessarily indicate places to give up on conservation, but places to monitor closely and conserve strategically. For example, many of our focal species are deep‐rooted and can persist in local groundwater refugia during drought (McLaughlin et al. 2017; Miller et al. 2010), making groundwater conservation in these areas a potential way to buffer climatic impacts. Loss hotspots are also places with elevated risk of losing the important ecosystem functions of their foundational species, with cascading impacts across other taxa. Therefore restoration in loss hotspots might foster other native foundational species likely to thrive with climate change to fulfill these roles. Such species might already exist within or just beyond the local landscape, including non‐endemic trees with wider distributions (not included in our analyses), or in “climatic nuclei” (McLaughlin et al. 2025), currently marginal environments with species expected to thrive and expand under future conditions. Areas identified as persistence hotspots could be targets for land acquisition or restoration to conserve the diminishing places where highly climate‐vulnerable species can thrive longer.

Operationalizing Climate‐Informed Risk Assessment

The need to integrate loss of suitable habitat into species risk assessments is well‐supported and can proceed with existing projections for many taxa. Mancini et al. (2024) proposed a new modeling framework using the average of multiple model algorithms, future climate scenarios, and global circulation models. However, those individuals performing risk assessments might not have the resources to build and run new models for each species under consideration. Eventually, species distribution data, climate data, and modeling capacity may become more accessible, making a standardized modeling approach more possible. But for now, many assessors can and will need to make use of existing projections, as we have done in this study. This approach would work well for relatively well‐studied species, which fortunately include many foundational species because of their widespread visibility and value.

In evaluating a species' climate risk, assessors must contend with a range of possible modeled outcomes. Ideally, assessors could consider the full range of low to high estimates of loss of suitable habitat, as we have done here. In a highly conservative step, assessors could use only low‐loss estimates. While this would be overly optimistic given current emissions trajectories, this approach could still capture the increased climate vulnerability of many high‐risk species, as evidenced by our results. However, this approach could forego opportunities to identify management needs in time to assist less rapidly declining species. Alternatively, assessors could build an average of the results. Or, with more nuanced methods, assessments could weight individual models based on various metrics of validity, including more recently developed global climate data or emissions scenarios. Beyond just using projected declines in suitable habitat, assessors could use additional lines of evidence (Araújo et al. 2019), such as range size (Method 2) and observed demographic responses (Method 3). Assessors could also consider species life histories and the likelihood of a species' movement into projected, newly suitable habitat based on dispersal and recruitment capacity.

An important approach to protecting species based on projected climate change impacts was the recent Western Joshua Tree Conservation Act (Western Joshua Tree Conservation Act 2023), passed in California despite the species' lack of official listing on the Red List or under state or federal Endangered Species Acts. The WJTCA creates precedent for conserving currently widespread but highly climate‐threatened species. A novel aspect of the law is its enhanced protection for areas of climate refugia. The WJTCA was proposed in part based on large, projected declines in suitable habitat for western Joshua tree alongside field evidence of climate‐related demographic changes (Sweet et al. 2019, Sirchia et al. 2019). Our results indicate that multiple other climate‐threatened species in the region could benefit from similar, nuanced, and increased protections under these criteria.

To better support climate‐informed risk assessments, researchers and modelers could attend to the usability of their model outputs for assessors. This includes publication of up‐to‐date future species climate suitability models that (1) follow best modeling practices for inclusion in species assessments (Araújo et al. 2019); (2) are accessible to non‐specialists (published in user‐friendly formats, including GIS map layers, KML files observable in open source platforms like Google Earth, and PDFs); and/or (3) are in alignment with the Red List A3 Criterion (e.g., include projections on time frames that represent the shorter of three generations of a species or 100 years), limiting the need for additional calculations by assessors. Gaining familiarity with criteria, timeframes and other requirements of other regulatory or policy protections in the jurisdictions where species occur—including national or state‐level instruments—can also guide researchers' inclusion of elements required for decision‐making. Furthermore, to increase confidence in projections, modelers can include validation, either through remotely sensed stress or die‐off data (Brown et al. 2018; Brodrick and Asner 2017) or demographic field data (Mclaughlin and Zavaleta 2012, Merow et al. 2014, Sweet et al. 2019). Finally, researchers can be responsive to broader data needs–for example, by prioritizing high ecological or cultural‐value species in areas of high projected climate change, but without published future climate suitability models or spatial/temporal demographic studies.

Conclusions

For California's foundational, endemic and near‐endemic trees—those that underlie ecosystem structure and function in forest, woodland, and savanna ecosystems across the region—our findings indicate a future that is more grave than current risk assessments reflect. We show that some currently widespread, dominant species may be at sufficient risk to warrant some of the protections we typically associate with rare species. Without strategic intervention, much of this unique and constitutive biodiversity will likely be lost over the next century.

Despite high climate threats, most of our focal species lack the prioritization, visibility and resources for management associated with “vulnerable” or higher threat categorization on the Red List (Betts et al. 2020; Rodrigues et al. 2006), and none have the habitat protections and limits on take conferred by the U.S. or California Endangered Species Acts (16 U.S.C. 1531–1544) (CNDDB 2025, Cal. Fish & G. Code § 2050–2115.5). To continue to effectively guide conservation, species risk assessments need to consistently, empirically and comprehensively incorporate climate‐change threats. Furthermore, laws and policies need to enable climate‐informed management that anticipates directional loss, not just uniform protection of current occurrences, to achieve widespread, effective species‐ and ecosystem‐scale conservation.
