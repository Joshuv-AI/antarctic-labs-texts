Effects of individualized rTMS on functional connectivity related to the default mode network and frontal-parietal network in major depressive disorder: exploratory analysis of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jing Jin, Yun Wang, Sixiang Liang, Qingchen Fan, Meiling Li, Ling Zhang, Yanxiang Cao, Zhimin Wang, Rena Li, Hesheng Liu, Yuan Zhou, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•rTMS of DLPFC or DMPFC improved clinical symptoms and modulated inFC with DMN/FPN, aligning with symptom-specific circuits.•Target-dependent inFC changes correlated with symptom improvement, offering insight into neural mechanisms of rTMS.•Individualized targeting highlights the value of inFC for future studies on rTMS treatment mechanisms.

Introduction

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has been a well-established treatment for major depressive disorder (MDD) (McClintock et al., 2018). In the context of MDD, depressive and anxiety symptoms are believed to be linked to two dissociable neural circuits (Williams, 2017), implying that interventions targeting these different circuits might preferentially ameliorate the corresponding symptomatology. A retrospective study (Siddiqi et al., 2020) identified distinct neural circuits in depression: a left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) −connected circuit associated with anhedonia/sadness (depressive symptoms) improved with DLPFC-rTMS, while a dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DMPFC) − connected circuit linked to worry/insomnia (anxiety symptoms) responded better to DMPFC stimulation. However, empirical evidences also indicates that rTMS, when applied to the left DLPFC, can alleviate anxiety symptoms, as evidenced by meta-analyses (Kan et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023), and that stimulating the left DMPFC can alleviate depression symptoms (Bakker et al., 2015, Kreuzer et al., 2019, Miron et al., 2019). These findings hint at the possibility that targeting varying brain regions may yield comparable clinical outcomes. Nonetheless, this speculation warrants further investigation to substantiate it, and then the underlying neural basis needs to be elucidated.

Existing studies have explored the effects of rTMS targeting the DLPFC or DMPFC on brain functional networks. Among these, the default mode network (DMN) and the frontal-parietal network (FPN) stand out as the two primary networks investigated due to their involvement in the etiology and treatment of MDD. The DMN, comprising the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and predominantly medial areas of the posterior parietal cortex (Fox and Raichle, 2007; Gusnard et al., 2001), plays acritical role in self-reflection (Gusnard et al., 2001; Zhou et al., 2020) and emotional regulation (Ochsner and Gross, 2005, Sheline et al., 2009). The FPN, consisting of the DLPFC and lateral areas of posterior parietal cortex, supports cognitive tasks that require intense attention and exhibits enhanced activity during the top-down modulation of attention and working memory processes (Corbetta and Shulman, 2002). Accumulating evidence suggests abnormal functional connectivity (FC) within the DMN or FPN and between these two networks in patients with MDD (Kaiser et al., 2015, Scalabrini et al., 2020, Yan et al., 2019). For instance, patients with MDD were found to be characterized by hyperconnectivity within the DMN, hypoconnectivity within the FPN, and disrupted connectivity between the DMN and the FPN, with some regions showing increased and others decreased connectivity (Kaiser et al., 2015, Liston et al., 2014). Additionally, FC within the DMN was positively related to the depression symptom severity in the recurrent MDD (Yan et al., 2019). More importantly, previous studies have found that rTMS directed at the left DLPFC has resulted in the normalization of within-network DMN hyperconnectivity (Liston et al., 2014), increased hypoconnectivity between the DLPFC in the FPN and the medial prefrontal DMN nodes (Liston et al., 2014), and further revealed the ability to predict antidepressant outcomes of rTMS based on DLPFC-anterior cingulate cortex connectivity (Fox et al., 2012, Taylor et al., 2018) or anterior cingulate-parietal connectivity (Taylor et al., 2018). However, targeting DMPFC was found to predict antidepressant efficacy based on the baseline FC between the DMN and the FPN (Fettes et al., 2024). Additionally, stimulation of the DMPFC in healthy individuals can enhance cognitive function, which might be potentially related to the activation of the FPN (Christian et al., 2024, Gamond et al., 2017). These findings suggest that both the DMN and the FPN could potentially undergo alterations following DLPFC or DMPFC stimulation. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that rTMS targeting either the left DLPFC or DMPFC may modulate the FC between the stimulated region and both the DMN and FPN but showed distinct connectivity profiles. Specifically, (1) stimulating the DLPFC will modulate its connectivity profile, characterized by enhanced positive connectivity within the FPN and reduced negative connectivity with the DMN (reflecting network interaction changes). (2) Stimulating the DMPFC will modulate its connectivity profile, characterized by enhanced positive connectivity within the DMN and reduced negative connectivity with the FPN.

In this study, we employed individualized targeting techniques (Mueller et al., 2013, Wang et al., 2015) to stimulate the DLPFC and DMPFC, aiming to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of rTMS by accounting for individual structural and functional variances (Cash and Zalesky, 2024, Cocchi and Zalesky, 2018, Sale et al., 2015). This approach, known for their high precision (Cui et al., 2022), has also received FDA clearance as part of the SAINT (Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy) protocol.

This study implements the pre-registered secondary objective (ChiCTR2100045748) to analyze the effects of individualized rTMS (DLPFC vs. DMPFC targets) on connectivity related to the DMN and FPN. While the pre-registered primary objective (clinical efficacy comparison between targets) is reported elsewhere and demonstrated comparable symptom improvement (Liu et al., under review), the neuroimaging analyses presented here were designed to quantify within-group, target-specific network modulation, employing independent analyses per condition. Inspired by the pre-registered primary finding, we further proposed that DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation might share common circuit-level mechanisms, although connectivity profiles differ, and we explored this possibility. As a complementary analysis, we replicated the examination using group-based seed (DLPFC/DMPFC) to investigate changes in FC between the targets and the DMN/FPN networks, along with their associations with clinical improvements. Additionally, a complementary normative connectivity analysis was conducted as an exploratory approach to provide convergent evidence.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This study was pre-registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100045748, https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj = 125699) and was conducted at Beijing Anding Hospital, Capital Medical University, China, with Institutional Review Board approval. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Only patients with MDD scoring ≥ 17 on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) and diagnosed with MDD by experienced clinicians utilizing the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.) 5.0.0 were recruited. In addition, patients with treatment-resistant depression (TRD) were excluded from this study. TRD was defined as having inadequate efficacy following adequate treatment with at least two different antidepressants. From June 2021 to September 2022, 78 patients with MDD were recruited from both outpatient and inpatient settings and randomized to receive either left DLPFC-rTMS (n = 40) or left DMPFC-rTMS intervention (n = 38). After a 21-day intervention (3 weeks), 69 patients (DLPFC-rTMS: 36; DMPFC-rTMS: 33) completed treatment. Following rigorous quality inspection of imaging data, 4 patients with missing post-rTMS imaging data and 1 with inconsistent timepoint in the DMPFC-rTMS group were excluded. A total of 64 patients (DLPFC-rTMS: 36; DMPFC-rTMS: 28) were included in the statistical analysis (Fig. S1).

All patients were required to maintain a stable antidepressant medication regimen throughout the 21-day intervention period, although they were allowed to change their medication during the one-month follow-up period.

Precision individualized repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)

Individualized rTMS stimulation were guided by subject-specific network-based parcellation following (Wang et al., 2015), which divides the cerebral cortex into 92 functionally distinct parcels belonging to different large-scale networks. Consistent with our previous investigation (Li et al., 2025), individual-level functional networks including DMN and FPN were delineated by referencing established network templates (Yeo et al., 2011). Individualized targets were then performed as follows: (1) DLPFC target: a cortical parcel within the anatomical boundaries of left DLPFC that demonstrates the strongest functional connectivity to the FPN; (2) DMPFC target: a medial prefrontal parcel that shows the strongest connectivity to the DMN template; (3) The selected target parcels were then individually coregistered to each participant’s high-resolution structural cortical surface to enable precise localization of rTMS stimulation coordinates; (4) The final target coordinates were further optimized by constraining selection to gyral crowns (avoiding sulcal regions) and by prioritizing the most superficial cortical vertex within the target parcel; (5) Ultimately, a cortical vertex within the target parcel on the cerebral gyrus was selected as the stimulation site for neuronavigation-guided TMS targeting of the DLPFC or DMPFC.

Two P50 magnetic stimulators paired with a real-time MRI-guided neuronavigation system (Neural Galaxy Inc., Beijing, CN) and equipped with a figure-of-eight coil were used. All patients underwent 21 days of intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS), with 2 sessions per day. Each session lasted approximately 9 min, delivering 1800 pulses at 100 % of the resting motor threshold (RMT). There was a 50-minute interval between the two sessions. This protocol was adapted with reference to the FDA-approved iTBS protocol for depression, which delivers 3,000 pulses per day over 4–6 weeks (typically 20–30 treatment days) (Blumberger et al., 2018).

Clinical assessment

Symptom severity was measured using HAMD, Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) at baseline, week 1, week 2, week 3, and during the follow-up at week 4, week 6 and week 8 (Fig. 1A). MRI image data acquisition and preprocessing

The MRI data, including T1-weighted structural and resting-state fMRI data, were both collected at baseline and week 3 using a Siemens Prisma 3.0 T MRI scanner at Beijing Anding Hospital. The T1-weighted structural scans were conducted using the following parameters: TR = 2530 ms, TE = 1.85 ms, FOV = 216 × 216 mm, FA = 85°, Matrix = 256, slice thickness = 1 mm. The resting-state fMRI scans were collected using the following parameters: TR = 3000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 216 × 216 mm, FA = 85°, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, 47 slices, 120 frames, no gap. Participants were required to rest, relax and keep eyes closed during the scanning. Four resting-state fMRI runs (6 min per run) were collected during the scan.

Resting-state fMRI data from each run were preprocessed separately using DPABI (version 6.2, https://rfmri.org/DPABI), which was selected for its flexibility and reliability in handling standard preprocessing steps. Preprocessing included: deletion of first 5 time points; slice timing correction; motion correction; nuisance covariance regression; functional image normalization (using T1 images); resampling to 2 × 2 × 2 mm3; smoothing (4 mm kernel); and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). Preprocessed data from four runs were averaged for subsequent analysis. For patients with inconsistent timepoint, excessive head motion, or numerous covariables, certain runs were excluded. In these cases, averaging was performed only on the remaining valid runs (Table S2). To ensure head motion did not confound the results, we compared the mean framewise displacement (mean FD) across groups before and after the intervention. No significant differences in mean FD were oberserved between the groups neither in the baseline (t = -1.291, p = 0.201) nor after the intervention (t = -0.589, p = 0.558).

Validation for individualized functional connectivity (inFC) patterns of individualized targets

To validate whether the target regions showed predicted individualized functional connectivity (inFC) patterns, aligns with our localization method, we generated the voxel-level inFC map for each of the target region (i.e., region of interest (ROI)). Firstly, we employed single-subject independent component analysis (ICA) method through the NeuroMark software (version 6.0, https://yuhuidu.com/), by temporally concatenating the pre- and post-treatment fMRI data from all participants. This allowed us to extract a set of stable intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs) and their time courses (TCs). To be noted, although the commonly used GIFT toolbox (Calhoun et al., 2001) can perform ICA, we did not adopt it in this study because it does not incorporate population-derived spatial priors or provide automated network labeling, which are essential for ensuring reproducibility and consistency in clinical populations. Instead, we employed the NeuroMark pipeline, developed by the same research group as GIFT. The NeuroMark can automatically extract 53 large-scale brain ICNs, based on reliable functional network templates in a data driven manner (Du et al., 2020). Considering the target localization and etiology of MDD, we only focused on the ICNs related to the FPN or DMN and thus obtained a total of 17 ICNs related to the FPN and 7 ICNs related to the DMN (Table S1, Fig. S2). Secondly, for each subject, the ROI was defined as a sphere with 6 mm radius centered on the individualized coordinate of the stimulation target. Finally, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between the average time series of the ROI and those of all the voxels throughout all the DMN or FPN ICNs to obtain the voxel-level inFC maps.

Individualized functional connectivity via single-subject independent component analysis

The TCs obtained from the NeuroMark were also used for calculating inFC between the target region and each of the interested ICNs based on the following steps: (1) extraction of TCs from each subject’s ROI; (2) extraction of TCs from each subject’s ICNs; (3) calculation of Pearson correlation between the above two TCs, and transformation of the correlation coefficient to Z-score.

Complementary analyses

Group seed-based FC

Group seed-based FC was calculated to validate whether our finding of inFC alterations can be found in the group-level FC. The group DLPFC seed was localized using the coordinate from one previous study of rTMS localization (MNI: −45, 45, 35) (Fitzgerald et al., 2009). Similarly, the group DMPFC seed was localized using the coordinate (−2, 41, 37) from previous literatures (Brass and Haggard, 2007, Kühn et al., 2011). The group seed-based FC was calculated by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between the TCs of the group ROI, created by a sphere with 6 mm radius centered on the coordinate of the group seed, and the TCs from each subjects’ ICNs. Then we transformed the correlation coefficient to Z-score.

Normative connectivity and symptom-specific circuit maps

Additionally, normative connectivity analysis was utilized to construct symptom-specific circuit maps, enabling direct comparison of the similarities between the DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation targets to the DMN/FPN circuits and their associated clinical symptom changes. This approach offers a highly reliable estimate of expected connectivity profile for each stimulation site (Siddiqi et al., 2020; Siddiqi et al., 2021, Weigand et al., 2018). Normative connectivity for stimulation site was calculated using resting-state fMRI data from another independent dataset of healthy participants (n = 180), collected at Beijing Anding Hospital. To compute the symptom-specific circuit maps, we conducted the following steps: (1) calculated FC maps from the stimulation target (DLPFC/DMPFC) to the whole brain within the healthy dataset, (2) averaged the FC maps for each target (DLPFC/DMPFC) across healthy individuals to obtain the target’s (DLPFC/DMPFC) normative connectivity maps, and (3) within the FPN/DMN mask, correlated each target’s normative connectivity map with the corresponding HAMD/HAMA improvement scores obtained in the MDD patients using partial Pearson correlation at each voxel, controlling for baseline depression severity, to generate the symptom-specific circuit maps (Fig. 1B).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (https://www.ibm.com/cn-zh/spss), DPABI 6.2 (http://rfmri.org/dpabi) and Python 3.10 (scipy toolkit, https://projects.scipy.org/scikits).

To validate inFC patterns of individualized targets, we performed a one-sample t-test on the baseline voxel-wise inFC maps of all patients to examine whether the inFC maps between the target ROI and the interested networks are compatible with the localization method (GRF correction with voxel-wise p < 0.001, cluster-wise p < 0.05, two-tailed test). To investigate whether the inFC between the target region and the interested ICNs was altered after treatment, we conduct within-group paired t-test comparing inFC before and after rTMS treatment (Week 3) separately for each group. Paired t-tests were performed using the difference between baseline and post-treatment values (i.e., baseline inFC − post inFC). False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (p < 0.05) was applied separately for each stimulation condition (DLPFC vs. DMPFC). This approach aligns with our objective to independently characterize condition-specified network effects.

Furthermore, CCA was conducted separately for the DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation groups to investigate the multivariate association between changes in inFC changes (baseline inFC − post inFC) and symptom improvement (baseline score − post score) of HAMD and HAMA scale. Prior to CCA, all inFC and symptom variables were standardized using z-transformation. CCA was then applied using two canonical dimensions, resulting in pairs of canonical variates that capture maximal correlations between linear combinations of inFC and symptom change variables. Due to the exploratory nature and limited sample size, we did not perform formal statistic inference on the canonical correlations. Instead, we interpret the strength of the multivariate associations based on the magnitude of the correlation coefficients. According to conventional guidelines, canonical correlation coefficients of approximately 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 are considered to reflect small, moderate, and large effect size, respectively (Sherry and Henson, 2005). Canonical correlations were computed for each variate pair, and canonical weights were examined to identify the variables contributing most strongly to the observed association. Analyses were conducted using Python (scikit-learn library).

In the complementary analyses, differences in the group seed-based FC between the group seed region and the interested ICNs between before and after rTMS treatment for each group were also explored through paired t-test. Then, CCA were performed to explore the association between alterations in the group seed-based FC and improvement score of HAMD scale and HAMA scale, respectively. Finally, to confirm similarity of symptom-specific circuit maps corresponding to each target (DLPFC/DMPFC), we computed the spatial correlation between the circuit maps. The whole analysis framework was presented in Fig. 1B.

The connectivity patterns observed in the DLPFC-rTMS and DMPFC-rTMS groups were in line with our localization method. Specifically, in the DLPFC-rTMS group, the target region was negatively correlated with the DMN while positively correlated with the FPN. On the other hand, in the DMPFC-rTMS group, the target region was positively correlated with the DMN but negatively correlated with the FPN (Fig. S4).

Complementary analysis: Comparing symptom-specific circuit maps

After comparing symptom-specific circuit maps, we found significant correlations in depression circuit maps for both the DMN (r = 0.456, p < 0.001) and the FPN (r = 0.507, p < 0.001) when contrasting DLPFC with DMPFC targets. Similarly, anxiety circuit maps showed significant correlations in both the DMN (r = 0.287, p < 0.001) and the FPN (r = 0.516, p < 0.001) (Fig. S6).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that rTMS targeting either the left DLPFC or the left DMPFC alleviated depressive and anxiety symptoms and altered inFC related to the DMN and FPN. Our exploratory symptom-specific circuit mapping revealed that the large-scale neural networks predicting clinical improvement was substantially similar for both targets. This convergence indicates that despite targeting different prefrontal nodes, DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation produce therapeutic benefits through the engagement of overlapping large-scale functional networks, providing complementary evidence for shared network-level mechanisms underlying rTMS efficacy in MDD. However, distinct target-specific connectivity changes and their associations with clinical improvement were also observed, suggesting that DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation may engage partially divergent neural pathways underlying therapeutic response.

rTMS alleviating clinical symptoms regardless of whether the DLPFC or DMPFC targeted

In our study, we observed that rTMS effectively reduces depressive and anxiety symptoms when targeting either the DLPFC or the DMPFC, aligning with previous findings. A recent meta-analysis of 174 studies on rTMS targeting the left DLPFC indicated a medium effect size for alleviating depressive symptoms and a small, yet still significant, effect size for anxiety symptoms (Kan et al., 2023). Beyond the left DLPFC, the DMPFC exhibited notable efficacy in addressing anxiety symptoms (Siddiqi et al., 2020) mainly due to its location involvement in neural circuits related to “worry/insomnia/sex” (anxiety symptoms). Furthermore, previous studies have shown that stimulating the left DMPFC can also alleviate depressive symptoms (Bakker et al., 2015, Kreuzer et al., 2019, Miron et al., 2019).

Individualized FC (inFC) changes of DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation targets and the correlation with clinical symptoms

inFC changes between the DMN and DLPFC/DMPFC stimulation target and correlation with clinical symptoms

Our study found that stimulating the left DMPFC increased inFC between DMPFC and DMN regions, specifically the precuneus and PCC. Increases in DMPFC-precuneus inFC were associated with improvements in depressive symptoms. Both the precuneus and PCC are considered major connectivity hubs, along with the DMPFC, forming the midline core of the DMN, which is involved in spontaneous cognition, self-referential processing, and affective decision-making (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2010, Tomasi and Volkow, 2011). Decreased activities within the DMN has been linked to MDD (Bluhm et al., 2009, Kenny et al., 2010, Sheline et al., 2010, Zhou et al., 2010), including lower signal intensities in the DMPFC and precuneus (Grimm et al., 2009), decreased FC between the DMPFC and PCC (Chen et al., 2015) and reduced FC within the DMPFC subsystem of the DMN (Cui et al., 2021). Moreover, following rTMS treatment to the DMPFC, a prior study noted an increase in FC between the DMPFC and DMN regions (precuneus and PCC), suggesting that therapeutic effects may stem from enhanced connectivity within DMN (Persson et al., 2020). Our findings corroborate this observation and furthermore indicate that the increased inFC between the DMPFC and precuneus correlates with reductions in HAMD scores, in line with prior evidence suggesting that strengthened DMN connectivity is linked to alleviation of depressive (Persson et al., 2020, Salomons et al., 2014) symptoms. Our findings suggest that the antidepressant effects of DMPFC-targeted rTMS may be mediated through enhanced inFC within the DMN, particularly between the DMPFC and precuneus.

Meanwhile, stimulating the left DLPFC resulted in an decrease in the negative inFC (towards positive connectivity after treatment) between the target site and the precuneus. Although no direct evidence supports our findings, earlier studies have shown that strengthened FC between the DLPFC and precuneus is associated with improved cognitive functioning in the verbal executive function domain(Panikratova et al., 2020). This is significant, as verbal executive function is well known to be impaired in patients with MDD (Marazziti et al., 2010). However, the correlation between altered DLPFC-precuneus inFC and clinical symptom improvement has not been observed in either previous studies or in our studies.

Although both DLPFC and DMPFC stimulation affect inFC with the precuneus, only DMPFC-precuneus inFC changes were associated with symptom improvement, suggesting the DMPFC-precuneus pathway may play a more specific role in the therapeutic effects observed. The DMPFC is involved in self-reflection, emotional regulation, and decision-making processes (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2010, Tomasi and Volkow, 2011), so enhancing DMPFC-precuneus connectivity may directly improve emotional symptoms. In contrast, the altered DLPFC-precuneus inFC may not be directly linked to symptom improvement, as the DLPFC is more involved in cognitive control and processing external information (Snyder, 2012). Although DLPFC stimulation also improves symptoms, its mechanism may not directly depend on FC changes with core DMN regions (i.e., precuneus), but more involved in cognitive regulation (Asl et al., 2024, Guse et al., 2010), which is critical for managing emotions symptoms including depression and anxiety.

inFC changes between the FPN and the DLPFC/DMPFC stimulation target and correlation with clinical symptoms

Our study found that stimulating the left DMPFC decreased inFC between the DMPFC and MCC and the decreases were associated with improvements in depressive symptoms, aligning with previous finding on the functional roles of these regions. This aligns with previous research highlighting the MCC/DMPFC as a key hub in emotion regulation and self-referential processing (Fan et al., 2011, Kühn et al., 2011). One study reported that mortality salience—a psychological stressor—reduced MCC/DMPFC activity during empathy for negative information (e.g. feeling pain), suggesting that modulation of this circuitry can influence affective responses (Luo et al., 2013). Similarly, our results suggest that disrupting excessive DMPFC-MCC connectivity via rTMS may normalize maladaptive self-referential and affective-processing networks in MDD. These findings support the MCC/DMPFC’s role in integrating emotional salience and executive function, and they reinforce the therapeutic potential of network-targeted neuromodulation in MDD.

Stimulation of the left DMPFC also resulted in increased inFC with IFG and SMA in our study. Previous studies have shown reduced FC between the left DMPFC and IFG in depressed adolescents (LeWinn et al., 2018) and cognitive training enhances neural activity in both regions (Mascaro et al., 2013). The DMPFC and IFG play distinct roles in cognitive control especially in visual perspective taking and social evaluations (Ferrari et al., 2016, Mazzarella et al., 2013), but exhibit less efficient engagement (Erk et al., 2010, Johnstone et al., 2007) in depression. Increased activation of the IFG is associated with effective cognitive strategies (Bluhm et al., 2009, Johnstone et al., 2007, McRae et al., 2012), potentially contributing to emotional regulation. Meanwhile, the enhanced inFC between the DMPFC and SMA may indicate a compensatory mechanism addressing deficits in emotional regulation and cognitive control in MDD, as the SMA is involved in both motor planning and cognitive functions like attention and working memory (Guida et al., 2023). This coupling may facilitate the integration of emotional and motor information, aiding individuals with MDD in regulating emotional responses and engaging in goal-directed behavior, though this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to limited prior evidence.

While stimulating the left DLPFC, we observed an increase in inFC between the DLPFC and mSFG, alongside a reduction in inFC between the DLPFC and IPL. In adolescents with depression, FC between left DLPFC and the SFG were found to be increased after taking antidepressant treatment (Lee et al., 2023). Moreover, negative inFC was prevalent in our participants and trended normalization after rTMS treatment, since FC between the left DLPFC and mSFG is typically positive in healthy participants (Gratton et al., 2013). Additionally, we observed inFC changes in the DLPFC-IPL, a finding rarely reported in previous fMRI studies. However, electrophysiological evidence, such as N45 wave alterations in the IPL (Dhami et al., 2021), supports that IPL activity may be responsive to DLPFC modulation. This sensitivity suggests that subtle changes in DLPFC-IPL connectivity might underlie rTMS’s therapeutic mechanisms. However, inFC alterations in the DLPFC-FPN were not associated with clinical symptoms improvement, despite observed alleviation of depressive and anxiety symptoms alleviation following DLPFC-targeted stimulation. This discrepancy may stem from limited statistical power due to sample size constraints, or inherent clinical heterogeneity in depression subtypes. Furthermore, undetected engagement of additional functional networks beyond our current analytical framework remains a plausible explanation.

Individualized FC vs. Group seed-based FC

Using the group seed-based FC method, no FC changes were observed in the DMPFC group. While the DLPFC group demonstrated FC changes between the DLPFC and FPN components (specifically left IPL, IFG and hippocampus), and DLPFC-FPN connectivity changes correlated with improvements in depressive symptoms. In contrast, individualized FC analysis revealed more robust and clinically relevant changes. Critically, this approach highlighted significant alterations involving the DMPFC and DMN/FPN components (e.g., precuneus and MCC). These changes were strongly associated with improvements in depressive symptoms, suggesting that precise modulation of self-referential and emotional regulation circuits plays a critical role in symptom reduction. This underscores the superior specificity of individualized targeting. While the group-based seed approach primarily identified effects within limited FPN regions, individualized analysis revealed widespread modulation within key networks implicated in MDD pathology (DMN/FPN) directly linked to symptom relief. These findings support the notion that individualized targets, by accounting for inter-subject variability in functional network architecture, may better capture the pathophysiologically relevant nodes for intervention. From both a mechanistic and clinical perspective, individualized targeting may thus enhance the precision and efficacy of rTMS intervention in MDD.

Comparison of symptom-specific circuit maps

We employed a comparative approach using symptom-specific maps to further explore the mechanisms underlying why different stimulation targets can produce similar antidepressant and anti-anxiety effects. Our findings revealed that depressive symptom-specific maps were similar for both the DLPFC and DMPFC targets, and the same was observed for anxiety symptom-specific maps. The strengths of this approach include an empirical data-driven method that is independent of a pre-existing models of symptom localization (Kozak and Cuthbert, 2016), replication across independent cohorts. More importantly, it demonstrated significantly better predictive performance for outcomes compared to the connectivity models based on prior assumptions about deep core regions (i.e., sgACC) (Siddiqi et al., 2021). This advantage was also evident in our study, where significant predictive performance was observed when correlating the normative connectivity maps with symptom improvements, while baseline inFC did not predict any anti-anxiety effect in the targeted DLPFC treatment. This may be attributed to the large variability in inFC among individuals, which was minimized when calculating the normative connectivity (Siddiqi et al., 2020; Siddiqi et al., 2021, Weigand et al., 2018).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data heterogeneity, small sample size (n = 64), and slight inter-group imbalance (DLPFC/DMPFC: 36/28) may collectively limit the generalizability of our findings, necessitating validation through larger, multicenter studies with more balanced group distributions. Second, insufficient demographic data (e.g., premorbid IQ, socioecnomic status, ethnicity, smoking/drinking history and menstrual cycle phases) may confound the observed results. Third, while resting-state fMRI data were collected with eyes closed, the study implemented no objective measures of arousal, such as no eye-tracking to detect microsleeps, no pulse oximetry to assess drowsiness-related autonomic shifts or no post-scan self-reports of alertness, as prior research suggests that drowsiness can affect DMN and PFN connectivity. Fourth, the potential influence of stable medication regimens during the intervention cannot be excluded. Fifth, the absence of a sham control group precludes complete dissociation between target-specific effects and potential placebo or non-specific treatment effects. Future studies should incorporate a sham-controlled condition to more rigorously assess rTMS effects and to distinguish genuine neural target engagement from placebo responses. Sixth, this study focused only on connectivity alterations between the stimulation targets and the DMN and FPN; future comprehensive research could delve into connectivity changes in other brain networks. Lastly, our assessment of neuroimaging changes was conducted over a 3-week period. Further investigations are needed to explore the enduring effects of the intervention and the underlying neural mechanisms.

Conclusions

Overall, our study demonstrated that targeting either the left DLPFC or the left DMPFC alleviated both depressive and anxiety symptoms and altered inFC with the DMN and FPN, aligning with the similar patterns observed in the symptom-specific circuit maps. However, the specific inFC changes and their associations with symptom improvements were target-dependent, providing new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying rTMS efficacy. Additionally, utilizing individualized targets to calculate inFC underscores the advantages of incorporating inFC in future investigations into the mechanisms of rTMS treatment.
