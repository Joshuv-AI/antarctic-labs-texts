Effectiveness of web-based simulation training on health information technology students’ skills in using hospital information systems
Authors: Elahe Hajiabadi, Faezeh Ghaffari, Malihe Ram, Reza Abbasi, Razieh Farrahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Simulation-based training helps healthcare professionals develop skills in a safe environment. It improves students’ knowledge, skills, attitudes, and confidence. This brief report examines the effect of web-based simulation on hospital information system (HIS)-related skills in health information technology (HIT) students before their internships.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This brief report presents a before–after quasi-experimental study conducted with seventh-semester Health Information Technology students. The web-based simulator included seven processes, each consisting of training, practice, and examining sections. Students initially completed pretests for each process, followed by 1 month of training and practice scenarios. Afterward, they took post-tests. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 23, applying descriptive statistics and a paired t-test to compare pretest and post-test scores.RESULT:The findings indicate that the educational intervention resulted in significant improvements in both performance scores and efficiency for certain processes. The electronic health record (SEPAS) process showed a significant increase in scores (P = 0.003) and a reduction in time (P = 0.032). In addition, significant improvements were observed in the Patient discharge process (P = 0.021), the Documentation of operating room procedures (P = 0.005), Diagnosis reporting (P = 0.009), and the Admission process for patients without a previous referral code (P = 0.03). However, some processes, such as visit, inpatient, and nutrition, did not show statistically significant changes.CONCLUSION:The results of this brief report confirm that simulation-based training positively affects the skills of HIT students in using of HIS. The significant improvement in post-test scores and the reduction in task completion times demonstrate that this training method enhances both students’ competency and efficiency in performing HIS-related tasks.

Introduction

Health information systems have become essential in healthcare, but their implementation faces challenges, particularly for novice healthcare staff who often lack critical informatics knowledge and skills.[1] Studies have shown that many users need more than two months to become comfortable with the use of health information systems.[12]

HIS is one of the most important subsets of health information systems that is widely used in Iran, and training is crucial for healthcare professionals to use this system effectively and improve patient care. The success of health information systems largely depends on proper user training; inadequate training in the use of these systems can lead to implementation failures and unintended consequences.[3] Research emphasizes the importance of effective HIS training for healthcare providers to optimize system use and ensure its successful implementation.[45] Inadequate training may lead to resistance and hinder HIS adoption.[67] To overcome these challenges, institutions must invest in comprehensive training programs that facilitate the optimal use of health information systems.[8]

The crowded hospital environment, characterized by limited access to computers, privacy concerns, and outdated infrastructure, hinders hospital staff from various departments—including HIT students, who are among the primary users of these systems—from effectively working with and gaining practical experience in information systems.[9]

Simulation-based training, as a new and promising approach, allows healthcare professionals to acquire technical and nontechnical skills in a safe environment without compromising patient safety.[10] Studies have shown that simulation-based training methods significantly enhance students’ knowledge, skills, attitudes, and confidence.[111213] A review study revealed that more than half of the studies related to information systems training via simulations reported significant improvements in student skill.[14] Simulation provides a safe and controlled environment for students to practice and develop their professional skills and prepare for real-life situations.[15] These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of simulation-based training in enhancing healthcare students’ competencies and in supporting their integration into educational curricula.

Given the potential of modern educational methods such as simulation to fill gaps in traditional educational approaches and competency-based skills, in this brief report, we developed a web-based simulator to train HIT students and evaluated its effect on enhancing students’ skills related to the hospital information system.

Materials and Method

Hospital information system simulator

The web-based simulator system for training includes key functional capabilities, such as interactive training courses, multimedia content, practical exercises, exam, and providing help and feedback to users to address potential errors. In addition, system’s administration, communication, and content management, information dashboards, reporting, question bank design, and messaging, have also been implemented to create an integrated educational system.

On the basis of the opinions of HIT three experts (HIS administrator, health information management (HIM) specialist, and one medical informatics (MI) specialist), seven important processes in the HIS were identified [Table 1]. Three scenarios were then defined for each process (training, practice, and examination scenarios). Finally, the scenarios were simulated.

Students have the opportunity to perform the steps gradually with simulator guidance anytime, anywhere, and on any device (mobile phones, tablets, laptops, or personal computers). The examination section was developed based on the educational content and practical scenario-based exercises. The validity and reliability of the exams, designed using real patient data and actual processes, were reviewed and approved by domain experts, including specialists in HIM and MI, who are familiar with the system's design and functionality. Each scenario step was scored according to a weighted importance analysis, determined through expert judgment. The simulator then calculates a process-specific score, with a defined maximum for each process.

Study setting and population

This brief report presents the findings of a before–after quasi-experimental study. The participants in this brief report were seventh-semester HIT students at the Birjand University of Medical Sciences, Iran, in 2023. Due to the small research population, sampling was not performed, and all the students (15 people) were included in the study. After obtaining ethical approval (IR.BUMS.REC.1402.445) code from the Birjand University of Medical Sciences and receiving implicit consent from the students in the classroom to participate in this research, a briefing session was held for all participants.

Intervention description

During this session, the researchers introduced the simulator and provided a detailed explanation of the training, practice, and examination section, objectives, and the significance of the study. Sufficient time was allotted to address any questions or concerns from the students regarding the process.

Students were granted permission to log into the system. At the beginning of the assessment, demographic information, including gender, age, and residential location, was recorded on a checklist. Students then logged into the system using their username and password and completed the exam scenario in examination section. This scenario served as the pretest for each of the seven processes in the simulator and was used to determine their pretest scores. Students gained access to training and practice for each process. They were required to first complete the training section, followed by the practical exercises for each process.

One month later, to assess the students’ skills, they were asked to complete the posttest with same scenario in examination section of the simulator. The pretest and posttest scores were compared to evaluate the effect of simulator training on the students’ ability to use the HIS. In addition, during these steps, the system recorded the start and end times for each student, which were used to calculate the total time spent.

Data analysis

After receiving demographic information and students’ test scores through the simulator system, these data were entered into SPSS software ver. 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation (SD)) used to summarize the data. A paired t test was used to compare the pretest and posttest scores to assess student skills.

Specifically, in the electronic health record (SEPAS) process, the mean score increased from 16.33 ± 1.88 in the pretest to 18.8 ± 1.7 in the post-test, which was statistically significant (P = 0.003). In addition, significant improvements were observed in the Patient discharge process (P = 0.021), the Documentation of operating room procedures (P = 0.005), Diagnosis reporting (P = 0.009), and the Admission process for patients without a previous referral code (P = 0.03).

On the other hand, some processes did not show a significant difference. For example, the scores for visit (P = 0.099), inpatient (P = 0.063), and nutrition (P = 0.062) process did not show statistically significant differences between pre-test and post-test. In addition, within the medical records process, subcategories such as wards’ performance report (P = 0.154) and admission of patients with a previous referral code (P = 0.131) showed score improvements, but these changes were not statistically significant.

These results indicate that in certain evaluated areas, the educational intervention or training had a positive effect on participants’ performance, while in others, the effect was not substantial.

Discussion

The findings of this brief report indicate a statistically significant improvement in hospital information system-related skills in health information technology students following simulation-based training. The posttest scores were significantly higher than the pretest scores in all processes, demonstrating that this educational approach effectively enhanced students’ competency in utilizing the HIS. These findings align with the primary objective of this study, which was to evaluate the effect of simulation-based training on hospital information system-related skills.

The results confirmed that simulation-based education can bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical skills, particularly in complex healthcare environments where hands-on experience is limited. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies. For example, Ghaffari et al.[16] demonstrated that simulation training significantly improved students’ knowledge and skills in health information systems. In addition, numerous studies have confirmed that simulation-based training enhances technical knowledge, increases skill acquisition, reduces performance anxiety, and increases student confidence.[171819] VanAtta et al.[20] reported that health information systems simulation-based training significantly enhances students’ perceived preparedness and confidence.

However, research on simulation-based training in medical education shows mixed results regarding its effectiveness in real-world clinical settings, some studies have shown that while simulation-based training improves knowledge and skills, it does not always translate into better performance in real-world settings because of the complexities of the clinical environment.[212223]

In addition, this study showed that simulation-based training significantly reduces the time required to complete HIS processes. This result aligns with those of previous studies. For example, Nabovati et al.[14] confirmed that simulation-based training optimizes workflows in healthcare settings by decreasing task completion time. Furthermore, evidence suggests that simulation-based training facilitates faster adaptation to new systems and processes, which is crucial in fast-paced healthcare environments.[24] However, some studies have noted that while time efficiency improves, the quality of training and its applicability in real-world settings should be continuously evaluated to ensure that increased speed does not negatively impact the learning experience.[2526] Studies have shown that simulation-based training for HIS can significantly increase the efficiency and number of task completions among healthcare professionals.[1213] If this reduction in task completion time translates into real-world settings, it could substantially impact HIS productivity, increase the available time for patient care, and reduce staff workload.

The results of this study emphasize the effectiveness of simulation-based training in improving students’ skills and addressing a significant challenge in HIT education: limited hands-on experience with HIS due to crowded hospital environments and information security concerns. The findings suggest that, given the increasing reliance on HIS in-healthcare systems, integrating simulation-based training into the HIT curriculum can better prepare students for real-world challenges, ultimately enhancing HIS implementation, optimizing patient care, and improving workflow efficiency. However, this brief report examined only short-term changes in post intervention test scores and did not assess the long-term effect of this training on students’ professional or occupational performance. In addition, factors such as students’ prior experience and the quality of instructional guidance, which could have influenced the results, were not examined. Future research should investigate the long-term effects of this training and educational intervention while considering students’ baseline computer skills to gain a deeper understanding of the effectiveness of simulation-based HIS training.

Conclusion

The results of this brief report confirm that simulation-based training positively affects the skills of HIT students in using of HIS. The significant improvement in posttest scores and the reduction in task completion times demonstrate that this training method enhances both students’ competency and efficiency in performing HIS-related tasks. Although this brief report provides strong evidence of short-term improvements, it also highlights the need for further investigation of the long-term effects of simulation-based training on professional performance and real-world applications. Exploring faculty perspectives on the effect of simulation training on student learning and its influence on training methods could be valuable topics for future research.

Key messages

Health information systems training using new educational technologies, such as simulation, can enhance users’ skills and improve the quality of healthcare services.
