Microenvironment-responsive hydrogels for spatiotemporal delivery of epigallocatechin gallate and BMP-2 to promote osteoporotic bone defect repair
Authors: Peilun Xiao, Guang Shi, Zeyao Lu, Shijia Liu, Chongjun Huang, Yuhai Zhao, Lei Wang, Zhihang Wang, Huiyi Zhang, Yuanfan Liu, Jingfeng Li, Ying Xu, Ye Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegenerating osteoporotic bone defects is still a major challenge. Conventional bone tissue engineering materials often fail to achieve on-demand drug delivery. At the same time, the therapeutic logic of osteoporotic bone defects requires spatiotemporal drug delivery to eliminate excess inflammation and high reactive oxygen species (ROS) at an early stage. When inflammation subsides, appropriate drugs are needed to match osteoblast differentiation and bone regeneration. Therefore, we designed a ROS-responsive Polyvinyl alcohol–4-(4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)-4-methylphenylboronic acid (PVA-TSPBA) hydrogel loaded with epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2). On the one hand, the ROS-responsive properties of composite hydrogels enable controlled delivery of EGCG in the high ROS microenvironment at an early stage of osteoporotic bone defects. On the other hand, BMP-2 is anchored within tannic acid-loaded zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (TA-ZIF-8), which allows a sustained release and matches late osteoblast differentiation and bone repair. The metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)-enhanced composite hydrogel has good mechanical properties and can provide good support for bone defects. Furthermore, the EGCG + BMP-2@TA-ZIF-8+PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (abbreviated as E + B@TZ + Gel) exhibited excellent biocompatibility. Bioinformatics analysis showed that the composite hydrogel enhanced bone repair by promoting osteogenesis via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Thus, the hydrogel system with therapeutic logic and microenvironment regulation offers a promising strategy for the regeneration of osteoporotic bone defects.Keywords: Osteoporotic bone defects, PVA hydrogel, Responsive release, Treatment logic, EGCG, BMP-2

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a widespread metabolic bone disease characterized by decreased bone mass and deteriorated bone microarchitecture, which significantly increases the risk of fragility fractures—even after minor trauma or routine activities [1]. These fractures can seriously affect the patients’ quality of life and lead to high rates of disability and mortality, particularly among middle-aged and elderly populations [2]. Surgical reduction and fixation remain the mainstay of treatment strategies for osteoporotic fractures. However, reduction and screw fixation of large fracture fragments may fail due to poor bone quality and slow healing in osteoporotic patients [3]. Furthermore, managing bone defects from trauma or tumor resection is more challenging in osteoporotic patients than in those with healthy bone tissue due to impaired bone regeneration, abnormal inflammatory responses, and harsh bone microenvironment [4]. Although autografts have shown favorable outcomes in treating osteoporotic bone defects, their clinical application is limited by additional surgical trauma and availability of the donor site [5]. Bone allografts and xenografts also carry risks of immunogenic reactions and disease transmission. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop advanced biomaterials that can effectively promote bone regeneration under osteoporotic conditions (see Scheme 1).

Osteoporosis is closely associated with a chronic inflammatory state, which is further exacerbated upon bone injury [6]. The pathological microenvironment, characterized by elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and dysregulated inflammatory responses, disrupts the balance of bone formation and resorption, thereby hindering bone healing [7,8]. Macrophages exhibit two phenotypes, including pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. The balance between M1 and M2 macrophages plays a key role in bone regeneration in osteoporotic defects [9,10]. Osteoporotic bone defects are often associated with an imbalance favoring M1 macrophages, alongside decreased M2 activity, which further impairs bone regeneration [11]. Moreover, increased ROS levels not only exacerbate inflammation but also impair osteoblast function [12]. Consequently, effective bone repair strategies must be tailored to the harsh osteoporotic environment to reduce the inflammatory response and promote osteogenesis by modulating immunocytes at the defect site [13].

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), a major polyphenolic compound derived from green tea, exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects [14]. The presence of a galloyl group at carbon 3 and multiple hydroxyl groups on the B ring enhances EGCG's antioxidant capacity compared with other catechins [15,16]. EGCG has been shown to modulate macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype and ameliorate inflammatory conditions in bone defects, thereby supporting bone regeneration [17,18]. Bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), a key osteoinductive growth factor [19], plays an essential role in bone remodeling by modulating the osteogenic differentiation of human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) [20]. While recombinant BMP-2 has been FDA-approved for clinical applications such as spinal fusion and tibial fracture repair, its rapid degradation and potential for adverse effects (e.g., ectopic bone formation) highlight the necessity of controlled release systems [21,22]. Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), a type of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) with osteoinductive properties, offers promising drug delivery capabilities due to its tunable structure, biocompatibility, and high loading efficiency [23,24]. ZIF-8 has been used to anchor BMP-2 for long-term sustained release [25]. Furthermore, modifying ZIF-8 with natural polyphenols such as tannic acid (TA) can improve its stability and hydrophilicity, thereby enhancing its drug delivery performance [26,27].

Spatiotemporal delivery hydrogels have emerged as a key research focus. However, most spatiotemporal release hydrogels fail to achieve precise, stimulus-responsive drug delivery [28,29]. The biocompatible Polyvinyl alcohol–4-(4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)-4-methylphenylboronic acid (PVA-TSPBA) hydrogel has recently gained attention for the delivery of drugs, cytokines [[30], [31], [32]], and stem cells [33]. Notably, the PVA-TSPBA hydrogel is ROS-responsive, undergoing degradation via chain scission and hydrolysis under oxidative stress to release p-hydroxybenzyl alcohol, thereby offering on-demand drug release capabilities.

In this study, we developed a dual-logic-based hydrogel system that integrates EGCG and TA-modified ZIF-8 loaded with BMP-2 within a ROS-sensitive PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (abbreviated as E + B@TZ + Gel). This hydrogel exhibits a responsive “diagnostic” logic by releasing EGCG in response to ROS, thereby mitigating inflammation. Simultaneously, it employs a “therapeutic” logic, enabling controlled, spatiotemporal release of both EGCG and BMP-2 to match the early inflammatory phase and subsequent osteogenic phase. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the hydrogel modulated immune responses, suppressed inflammation, and enhanced angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Furthermore, we validated its osteogenic potential in an ovariectomized rat model and conducted RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analyses to explore the underlying regenerative mechanisms. Overall, this dual-logic-based hydrogel represents a promising strategy for improving the local microenvironment and promoting bone regeneration in osteoporotic bone defects.

Materials and methods

Preparation of BMP-2@TA-ZIF-8 (B@TZ)

B@TZ was synthesized following modified protocols [34,35]. Briefly, 2.3 g of 2-methylimidazole and 20 μg of BMP-2 were dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water. Then 88.4 mg of zinc nitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in 2 mL of deionized water and slowly added to the above solution under continuous stirring. White B@TZ was collected after stirring for 2 h at 37 °C and centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. After washing B@TZ with deionized water two times, 20 mL of deionized water and 100 mg of TA were added and stirred at 37 °C for 12 h [36]. After centrifugation and washing, the final product was dehydrated to obtain B@TZ nanocrystals. ZIF-8 and TZ were synthesized using the same procedure, except without the addition of BMP-2. Detailed methods for BMP-2@ZIF-8(B@Z), B@TZ characterization are provided in the Supporting Information.

Synthesis of E + B@TZ + Gel

To prepare TSPBA, 0.4 g of N,N,N′,N'-tetramethyl-1,3-propanediamine and 2 g of 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid were added to 40 mL N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and stirred for 24 h at 50 °C. The supernatant was collected, and 100 mL tetrahydrofuran (THF) was added to precipitate a white solid. The white solid was washed with THF, centrifuged and lyophilized to yield 1.2 g of TSPBA. Then, PVA was proportionally dissolved in deionized water and stirred at 80 °C for 24 h to obtain a (5 %) PVA solution. Equal volumes of PVA (5 wt %) and TSPBA (5 wt %) solutions were mixed to form homogeneous PVA-TSPBA hydrogel. For the preparation of E + B@TZ + Gel, 20 mg of EGCG (1 wt %) and different concentrations of B@TZ (0.6 wt %, 0.8 wt %, 1.0 wt %, 1.2 wt %, 1.4 wt %) were added to 1 ml of TSPBA (5 wt %) and mixed well. The mixed solution was then blended with an equal volume of PVA to obtain a composite hydrogel.

Compatibility of the composite hydrogels

Composite hydrogels were prepared in 24-well plates and then seeded with MC3T3-E1 cells (5 × 104 per well). After 24 and 48 h, cells were washed with PBS and stained using a live/dead kit. Fluorescent images were captured with an inverted fluorescence microscope (IFM) (Olympus). Cell proliferation was also assessed using the CCK-8 assay and the EdU proliferation kit according to standard methods. Additionally, live/dead kit was employed to test the proliferation of Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) co-cultured with hydrogels.

In vitro hemolysis assays

Fresh rat blood was collected, and erythrocytes were isolated by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min) and washed with PBS. Erythrocytes (200 μL) were exposed to 1 mL of H2O, PBS, or different hydrogels and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The mixture was then centrifuged (3000 rpm, 5 min), and the supernatant's absorbance was measured at 577 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis rate was calculated using the formula: Hemolysisratio=(ODt−ODnODp−ODn) × 100 %, where ODp, ODn, and ODt are the OD values of the positive, negative, and test samples, respectively.

In vitro ROS scavenging capacity of hydrogels

The antioxidant activity of hydrogels was evaluated by the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay. Briefly, 1 mg of DPPH was dissolved in 25 mL of ethanol to prepare the DPPH solution. The hydrogel solution was then mixed with the DPPH solution and incubated in the dark for 4 h. The scavenging ability was assessed by measuring absorbance at 517 nm. The following formula measured the hydrogels’ scavenging efficiency: Scavengeefficiency%=(1−Asample−AblankAcontrol) × 100 %, where A control is the absorbance of the control group (DPPH solution), A sample is the absorbance of the hydrogel group, and A blank is the absorbance of the blank group.

Intracellular ROS scavenging capacity of hydrogels

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed by the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescence probe and flow cytometry. Hydrogels were first filtered and decontaminated and laid flat on 24-well plates. MC3T3-E1 cells (5 × 104 cells/mL) were seeded in hydrogels for 6 h. After waiting for the cells fully attached, all groups, except the negative control, were treated with complete medium containing H2O2 (200 μM) for 12 h. After treatment, cells were incubated with the ROS Detection Kit for fluorescence analysis.

JC-1 assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential was measured by 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazol-ocarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) dye, which accumulates in mitochondria based on the membrane potential. Red fluorescence indicates higher membrane potential. Conversely, green fluorescence indicates an abnormal decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential. Cells were analyzed using a JC-1 assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions, and mitochondrial potential was also quantified by flow cytometry.

MC3T3-E1 migration assays

MC3T3-E1 migration was evaluated using a Transwell assay. MC3T3-E1 cells (5 × 104 cells) were seeded in the upper chamber of a 24-well plate, with hydrogels placed in the lower chamber. Then, 24 h later, non-migratory cells were eliminated. After fixing the cells with 4 % paraformaldehyde, the cells were stained with crystal violet and imaged under an IFM. Finally, the positive cells were quantified using Image J.

In vitro osteogenesis study

The effects of hydrogels on osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells were investigated. Osteogenic activity and calcium accumulation were evaluated by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining, respectively. Briefly, MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (5 × 104 per well) encapsulated in hydrogels. The MC3T3-E1 cells were then cultured in an osteogenic induction medium containing α-MEM, 5 % FBS, 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid, 10−2 M sodium β-glycerophosphate, and 10−7 M dexamethasone. After 7 days, cells were fixed and stained with ALP for 30 min. After 21 days, cells were stained with ARS for 5 min, washed with PBS, and visualized using an IFM.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to examine the expression levels of osteogenesis-related proteins in MC3T3-E1 cells, including osteopontin (OPN) and collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1). Briefly, 7 days after osteogenesis was induced in MC3T3-E1 cells, the cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed at room temperature for 15 min. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. After blocking, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody (Abcam, UK, 1:300). After incubation, the cells were washed three times with TBST (Baiyutian, China). Then, the cells were treated with goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, UK, 1:500) and Actin-Tracker Red (Baiyutian, China) for 1 h. The nuclei were then stained for 10 min with DAPI containing anti-fluorescence quencher (Beyotime, China). Fluorescence images were then captured by IFM.

RT-qPCR

To evaluate the potential of hydrogels in osteogenesis at the gene expression level, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed to measure the expression of key osteogenic markers, including COL1A1, runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), OSTERIX, and OPN. MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured on hydrogels for 7 days and total RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction kit as directed (Beyotime, China). The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a microspectrophotometer. Next, cDNA was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit (Novoprotein, China) and analyzed by RT-qPCR using a Real-Time PCR system (Novoprotein, China). The related expression of mRNAs was measured by ΔCT(2−ΔΔCT) method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Transcriptome analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which E + B@TZ + Gel promotes osteogenesis, transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed. MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in complete medium containing 200 μM H2O2, and RNA was extracted after 7 days of treatment with or without the composite hydrogel. Total RNA was extracted from each group using Trizol reagent following the manufacturer's protocol and stored at −80 °C prior to sequencing. RNA-seq was performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using volcano plots and heat maps. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed based on the DEGs obtained from RNA-seq.

Assessment of macrophage polarization

The effect of hydrogels on the polarization of RAW264.7 cells was evaluated using immunofluorescence and flow cytometry. In brief, hydrogels were placed into 24-well plates, and RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured with hydrogels and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. The cells were then fixed and treated with a blocking buffer before incubating overnight with CD206 or CD86 antibodies. M1 macrophages, characterized by CD86 expression, and M2 macrophages, marked by CD206, were observed and imaged using an IFM. The average fluorescence intensity was quantified by Image J. Additionally, flow cytometry was used to assess the number and proportion of M1 (CD86) and M2 (CD206) macrophages.

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of the hydrogels, the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines in the culture supernatants—including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and IL-4—were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. RT-qPCR was conducted to assess the expression levels of genes associated with macrophage polarization. Expression of M1 markers (CD86, iNOS, and TNF-α) and M2 markers (Arg-1, IL-10, and TGF-β1) was analyzed. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

In vitro evaluation of angiogenic properties

We investigated the impact of hydrogels on HUVECs migration. The detailed method was described above. To evaluate their angiogenic potential, a tube formation assay was also performed. HUVECs were first seeded on hydrogels and incubated for 72 h. Subsequently, these pre-treated HUVECs were digested, counted, and seeded onto 48-well plates (3 × 104 cells per well) pre-coated with Matrix-Gel (Beyotime, China). Then, 48-well plates were incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Tubular structures were then captured using an IFM, and the number of tubes and junctions was quantified with Image J.

In the scratch wound assay, sterile hydrogels were placed in each well of a 24-well plate. HUVECs were seeded (5 × 104 cells per well) on the hydrogels and co-cultured for 3 days. Once the cells reached confluence, a scratch wound was introduced. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h using an IFM to assess cell migration.

For RT-qPCR, angiogenesis-related genes, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) were quantified and analyzed. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining was used to assess the expression of angiogenesis-related proteins, including VEGF and CD31, following procedures similar to those described above. Quantitative analyses were performed based on relative fluorescence intensity using Image J, and comparative analyses were conducted.

In vivo bone regeneration assessment

Establishment and evaluation of the osteoporosis rat model

Animal procedures in the present study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Medical University (No. CMU20240697) and conducted in compliance with the Guidelines for Animal Treatment issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China. To assess the bone regeneration potential of hydrogels in an osteoporotic environment, an osteoporotic Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat (12 weeks old, female) model was first established. Bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) or sham surgery was conducted based on the standard protocols. After anesthetization with sodium pentobarbital, the rats were placed in the prone position, and the dorsal area was sterilized with povidone-iodine and alcohol. Two 1-cm longitudinal incisions were made on each side of the back to expose and remove the ovaries. The oviducts were ligated, and the wounds were sutured in layers. In the Sham group, an equivalent amount of perivarian adipose tissue was excised without ovary removal. Postoperative care included intramuscular injection of penicillin at 800,000 units per day for 3 consecutive days. Three months post-surgery, the establishment of osteoporosis was confirmed by micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and histological analysis of the femur.

Cranial defect establishment

We established a cranial defect model in osteoporotic rats. The preoperative preparation was conducted as described previously. A longitudinal 1.5 cm incision was made along the midline of the skull of SD rats. The soft tissue and periosteum were carefully retracted, and two symmetrical round defects (5 mm in diameter) were drilled on both sides of the sagittal suture using a dental drill (FASTRONG, China). The animals were randomly divided into six groups Sham group, control group, TZ + Gel group, B@TZ + Gel group, E + TZ + Gel group, and E + B@TZ + Gel group. A sterile hydrogel (5 mm diameter) was implanted into each defect, and the incisions were sutured in layers. Postoperatively, penicillin was administered for 3 days at 800,000 units per day. At 6 and 12 weeks post-surgery, cranial tissue samples were harvested and fixed for further analysis.

Micro-CT analysis

At designated time points post-surgery (6 and 12 weeks), the rats were euthanized using CO2, and the skulls were harvested. The skulls were subsequently fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. A SKYSCAN 1276 micro-CT scanner (Bruker BioSpin) was then used to assess bone regeneration in the cranial defects of the rats. The Micro-CT scanning parameters were set as follows: X-ray current = 250 μA, X-ray voltage = 50 kV, object-to-source distance = 91 mm, scan duration = 4 min, and pixel size = 20 μm. The resulting scans were reconstructed using database software to produce three-dimensional images. CTvox and CTAn software were used to quantify the healing of the defect, measuring parameters such as the defect area, trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp), trabecular volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) of the new bone.

Histological examination

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson's staining were performed on cranial specimens at 6 and 12 weeks to evaluate new bone formation in vivo. The collected skulls were fixed and followed by decalcification in 12.5 % EDTA for 14 days. After decalcification, H&E and Masson's staining were performed based on standard protocols. At 12 weeks, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining were used to assess osteogenic, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenic markers. CD31 and CD34 were used as the angiogenic markers, while RUNX2 and OPN were selected as the osteogenic markers for immunohistochemistry. CD86 (M1 macrophage marker) and CD206 (M2 macrophage marker) were chosen for immunofluorescence staining. All procedures followed standard methodologies. Quantification of positive cells was performed using Image J.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with details of the statistical methods provided in the figure legends. All experiments were performed independently at least thrice (n ≥ 3). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test to evaluate differences among groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism9 (USA).

Results and discussion

Characterizations of B@Z and B@TZ

The synthesis of B@Z and B@TZ is shown in Fig. 1A. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals that B@Z exhibits a regular polygonal morphology (Fig. 1B). The morphological structure of B@TZ and B@Z was further examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 1C and S1). To verify the chemical composition of B@TZ, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to analyze the elements C, N, O, and Zn in the nanoparticles (Fig. 1D and S2). The mean particle size of B@TZ (202.8 ± 2.0 nm) was slightly smaller than that of B@Z (218.5 ± 3.8 nm) (Fig. 1E). The reaction between TA with Zn2+ results in the formation of a metal-phenol coordination film on the surface of B@Z crystals, leading to the creation of a core-shell structure and a slight reduction in particle size [37,38].

Scale bar: 200 nm. (E) Size distribution of B@Z and B@TZ (n = 3). (F) Zeta potential of ZIF-8, B@Z, and B@TZ (n = 3). (G) The Tyndall effect in DI water of B@Z and B@TZ at 0 and 10 days. Zeta potential analysis showed that the ZIF-8 surface had a positive charge (8.01 ± 0.93 mV), and B@Z surface had a higher positive charge (11.23 ± 1.46 mV). In contrast, the B@TZ surface exhibited a much larger negative charge (−32.02 ± 4.52 mV) (Fig. 1F).

This is attributed to the negative charge of TA, and thus, B@TZ is more stable dispersed in aqueous solution than B@Z. In addition, due to the phenolic hydroxyl groups in TA, B@TZ exhibited improved dispersibility in deionized water, as evidenced by the Tyndall effect (Fig. 1G). In addition, B@TZ significantly prolonged the release of BMP-2. Specifically, B@Z had 89.9 ± 2.4 % BMP-2 released on day 10, whereas B@TZ had only 63.8 ± 2.8 % during the 25 days of measurement time (Fig. 1H).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra showed that the B@Z nanoparticles displayed a typical structure highly consistent with that of the ZIF-8 crystals (Fig. 1I), suggesting that BMP-2 was encapsulated within the ZIF-8 framework. The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of B@Z and B@TZ are shown in Fig. 1J. The B@Z spectrum displayed two prominent absorption peaks at 3135 and 2927 cm−1, corresponding to C–H stretching vibrations in the methyl and imidazole rings, respectively [39]. Peaks at 1146, 994, and 420 cm−1 were assigned to Zn–N stretching, C–H bending, and C–C–N twisting, respectively [40]. A sharp peak at 1576 cm−1 was identified as the C=N stretching mode, while the peaks at 1307 and 1459 cm−1 corresponded to ring stretching [41]. For B@TZ, additional absorption peaks at 870 and 675 cm−1 were observed, which corresponded to the C–H bending of the phenyl ring and the out-of-plane bending vibration of the phenolic hydroxyl group (O-H), respectively. Furthermore, peaks at 2966, 1700, and 1052 cm−1 were assigned to O-H stretching from tannic acid, C=O absorption from carboxylic acid, and C-O stretching vibrations, respectively. These new peaks confirmed the successful grafting of tannins into the B@Z structure [36]. Moreover, B@TZ was further analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The full-spectrum investigation and Zn 2p high-resolution XPS spectroscopy demonstrated that BMP-2 is not involved in surface coordination, but rather encapsulated within TZ (Fig. 1K–L).

Characterization of E + B@TZ + Gel

To determine the effect of composite hydrogels with different concentrations of B@TZ (0.6 wt %, 0.8 wt %, 1.0 wt %, 1.2 wt %, 1.4 wt %) on cell viability, CCK8 and live-dead cell staining were performed. It was found that the composite hydrogel containing 1.0 wt% B@TZ significantly enhanced MC3T3-E1 cell viability (Fig. S3–S4). Therefore, 1.0 wt% B@TZ was chosen for experiments. The appearance of PVA, PVA-TSPBA, and E + B@TZ + Gel is shown in Fig. 2A. The morphology of E + B@TZ + Gel was examined by SEM. The hydrogel exhibited a porous structure with pore size exceeding 100 μm, which is favorable for supporting cell growth. High-magnification images revealed that numerous B@TZ nanoparticles were embedded within the composite hydrogel (Fig. 2B). The existence of these nanoparticles was further confirmed by EDS, which demonstrated the uniform distribution of the Zn2+ elemental throughout the porous structure (Fig. 2C). The ROS-responsive TSPBA linker was confirmed using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) (Fig. S5). We tested their viscoelasticity using the frequency sweep analysis (0.1–100 rad/s). The storage modulus (G′) values of both PVA-TSPBA hydrogels and TZ + PVA-TSPBA were consistently higher than the corresponding loss modulus (G″) values across the entire frequency range, showing typical elastic solid behavior. Notably, the G′ values of TZ + PVA-TSPBA were significantly higher than PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (Fig. 2D). The TZ + PVA-TSPBA showed enhanced elasticity and toughness due to the nano-reinforcement effect of TZ MOFs. Tensile and compression tests further confirmed the superior mechanical strength of TZ + PVA-TSPBA compared to PVA-TSPBA hydrogel. Tensile tests showed that the tensile strength of the TZ + PVA-TSPBA (156.8 ± 9.7 kPa) was significantly higher than that of the PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (84.2 kPa ± 3.5 kPa) (Fig. 2E and F). The tensile modulus of the TZ + PVA-TSPBA (17.1 ± 1.5 kPa) was also significantly higher than that of the PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (11.1 ± 2.1 kPa) (Fig. 2F). In addition, the TZ + PVA-TSPBA hydrogel demonstrated a significantly higher compressive strength (725.3 ± 21.3 kPa) at 60 % strain compared to the PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (515.6 ± 19.89 kPa) (Fig. 2G–H), confirming that the addition of ZIF-8 MOFs enhanced the mechanical strength of the hydrogel network. The compressive modulus of TZ + PVA-TSPBA was 38.6 ± 2.8 kPa, which provided considerable mechanical support to the bone defect (Fig. 2H). Cyclic compression tests revealed that TZ + PVA-TSPBA exhibited a loading-unloading hysteresis loop over five cycles (Fig. 2I), indicating no adhesive failure or permanent damage after repeated loading. Furthermore, the tensile and compression loading–unloading curves demonstrated that TZ + PVA-TSPBA had good resilience (Fig. S6A–B). An ideal hydrogel should also be capable of adapting to the localized complexity of the defect site. Therefore, the self-healing ability of hydrogels is crucial for providing adequate support to bone defects under complex situations. Tensile tests were used to assess the self-healing properties of the composite hydrogel. The tensile modulus of the self-healed hydrogel samples (23.6 ± 6.0 kPa, with no healing delay) was comparable to that of the undamaged samples (38.4 ± 2.7 kPa), although the fracture strain was notably lower (Fig. S7). The self-healing properties of the composite hydrogels primarily arise from multilevel reversible bonds, including reversible covalent interactions between PVA and TSPBA, intramolecular hydrogen bonding within PVA, and hydrophobic and electrostatic attractions involving the embedded nanoparticles. Furthermore, the composite hydrogels exhibited favorable adhesion to the bone tissue (Fig. S8), which contributes to stable localization within the defect site and supports effective bone regeneration. In vitro hydrolysis of the composite hydrogels was evaluated by measuring the percentage of dry weight loss over time after incubation in PBS and H2O2 at 37 °C. The presence of H2O2 accelerated the degradation of the composite hydrogels (Fig. 2J). On day 7, the weight loss of the composite hydrogel in PBS, 1 mmol/L H2O2, and 2 mmol/L H2O2 was 9.4 %, 22.9 %, and 36.9 %. On day 14, the weight loss of the composite hydrogel in PBS, 1 mmol/L H2O2, and 2 mmol/L H2O2 was 12.5 %, 43.4 %, and 58.2 %, respectively. When ROS levels are elevated, the degradation of PVA-TSPBA is enhanced due to polymer chain breakage and hydrolysis, resulting in the formation of p-hydroxybenzyl alcohol molecules [42]. The in vivo degradation behavior was further investigated by subcutaneous implantation of the hydrogels in the back of SD rats. The nano-enhanced TZ + PVA-TSPBA hydrogel showed slower degradation, with a weight loss of 46.4 % after 14 days of implantation, compared to the PVA-TSPBA hydrogel (Fig. S9). This biodegradable profile allowed the composite hydrogel to support cell migration during the initial implantation period, and subsequently provided space for new bone formation. We also examined the swelling properties of the hydrogels. Both PVA-TSPBA and TZ + PVA-TSPBA reached equilibrium swelling after soaking in PBS for about 18 h. The swelling ratio of PVA-TSPBA and TZ + PVA-TSPBA hydrogels were 16.1 ± 1.2 % and 12.2 ± 1.2 % (Fig. S10).

In vitro drug release from the composite hydrogel

We tested the encapsulation efficiency of BMP-2 within ZIF-8, and the result indicated that encapsulation efficiency was 89.4 % ± 3.3 % (Fig. S11). Next, we evaluated the release profile of EGCG from the composite hydrogels in PBS and 1 mmol/L H2O2, respectively (Fig. 2K). EGCG was released faster in H2O2 than in PBS. On days 1, 10, and 25, the cumulative release of EGCG in PBS was 17.5 % ± 2.0 %, 63.0 % ± 2.0 %, and 71.6 % ± 2.6 %, respectively. In contrast, in H2O2, the release rates were 47.3 % ± 1.9 %, 78.8 % ± 2.1 %, and 84.5 % ± 3.2 % at the same time points. This accelerated release is attributed to the ROS-responsive nature of the TSPBA-PVA network, which undergoes chain cleavage and hydrolysis into p-hydroxybenzyl alcohol molecules in the presence of elevated ROS levels. The release profile of BMP-2 in H2O2 was also faster than in PBS (Fig. 2L). In PBS, the cumulative release of BMP-2 was 10.7 % ± 1.4 %, 46.5 % ± 2.1 %, and 52.9 % ± 1.6 % on days 1, 10, and 25, respectively. In H2O2, the corresponding values were 16.3 % ± 1.7 %, 59.7 % ± 3.0 %, and 65.8 % ± 2.1 %.

We verified the in vitro drug release kinetics of EGCG and BMP-2 over a period of 14 days (Fig. S12). In PBS, EGCG exhibited a faster release than BMP-2 during the first 5 days, whereas BMP-2 maintained a sustained and stable release profile after day 5. In H2O2, EGCG was also released faster than BMP-2 during the first 4 days, and BMP-2 achieved long-term sustained release after day 4. The delayed and sustained release of BMP-2 effectively aligns with the activation phase of osteoblasts, thus effectively promoting osteogenesis. The in vivo imaging system (IVIS) results further supported this conclusion (Fig. 2M − N). E + B@TZ + Gel exhibits enhanced BMP-2 release efficiency in H2O2 compared to PBS. The release of BMP-2 from hydrogels in H2O2 is significantly accelerated, and the radiation signal starts to enhance at 1 day and peaks at 3–5 days. We also assessed the Zn2+ release from TZ + Gel. The composite hydrogel exhibits sustained Zn2+ release over a 25-day observation period (Fig. S13).

In vitro biocompatibility Assessment of the hydrogels

The biocompatibility of materials is crucial for their application in bone tissue engineering. The cytotoxicity of TZ was first assessed using the CCK-8 assay. At low concentrations, the viability of MC3T3-E1 cells increased in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating that low doses of TZ may promote cell proliferation. However, when the concentration reached 75 μg/mL, cell viability began to decline, and at 150 μg/mL, it dropped below 80 %, suggesting that higher concentrations of TZ exert cytotoxic effects on MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. S14). Subsequently, we assessed the effect of composite hydrogels on the viability and proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells using CCK-8, live-dead cell staining assays and the EdU method. The results indicated that all hydrogels slightly enhanced MC3T3-E1 cell viability and promoted cell proliferation. Among them, B@TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel exhibited significantly stronger effects in stimulating cell growth, which could be attributed to the activity of BMP-2 (Fig. 3A–D). Subsequently, the viability of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) cultured with the composite hydrogels was evaluated using live-dead cell staining assays. The results indicated that the composite hydrogel effectively supported the proliferation of HUVECs (Fig. S15). Collectively, these findings confirm that E + B@TZ + Gel is non-cytotoxic, promotes cell proliferation, and is a promising candidate for bone tissue engineering applications.

Total Antioxidant Assessment and Intracellular ROS scavenging

The harsh microenvironment associated with osteoporosis significantly impairs bone tissue regeneration. Elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and the continuous accumulation of ROS at osteoporotic bone defect sites are key contributors to impaired healing. Evidence suggests that sustained ROS overproduction intensifies the inflammatory response, thereby hindering the repair process of bone defects [43]. Consequently, bone repair materials must exhibit potent antioxidant properties to effectively counteract oxidative stress and promote regeneration in osteoporotic environments [44,45]. The DPPH free radical scavenging assay demonstrated that the hydrogels exhibited varying degrees of efficacy in scavenging ROS (Fig. 4A). We found that TZ + Gel and B@TZ + Gel groups partially scavenged DPPH free radicals caused by H2O2, which may be attribute to the antioxidant effect of Zn2+ released from TZ [46]. TZ + Gel and B@TZ + Gel cleared approximately 33 % of DPPH. In contrast, over 90 % of DPPH free radicals were scavenged in the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel hydrogel groups compared with the control group, indicating that EGCG released from the composite hydrogel exhibits potent antioxidant and ROS-scavenging properties. We employed DCFH-DA as a ROS indicator, which fluoresces green when oxidized by cell membrane esterases, to assess the scavenging of intracellular ROS by the composite hydrogel. The results showed that the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel hydrogels cleared the majority of H2O2-induced intracellular ROS (Fig. 4B and C). We further verified the ROS-scavenging ability of the hydrogels using flow cytometry, and the results were consistent with the fluorescence assay described above (Fig. 4D and E). We assessed the mRNA expression levels of the antioxidant-related genes catalase (Cat), superoxide dismutase 1 (Sod1), and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPx1) in MC3T3-E1 cells by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4F–H). H2O2 exposure significantly downregulated the expression of Sod1, Cat, and GPx1, whereas treatment with composite hydrogels markedly upregulated these genes. Notably, the E + B@TZ + Gel group showed the highest gene expression levels among all hydrogel-treated group. These results indicate that E + B@TZ + Gel enhances cellular antioxidant defenses, thereby reducing ROS accumulation, oxidative stress, and subsequent cellular damage.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a primary source of ROS production in osteoporotic bone defects [47]. To further explore the composite hydrogel's ability to scavenge mitochondrial ROS, we employed MitoSox Red, a highly selective fluorescent dye closely linked to mitochondrial ROS levels. The results showed that the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel groups cleared most of the increased mitochondrial ROS induced by H2O2 (Fig. S16). The mitochondrial membrane potential was used to evaluate the impact of hydrogels on the mitochondrial function of MC3T3-E1 cells. Damaged mitochondria with compromised membrane integrity exhibit decreased membrane potential, leading to the formation of JC-1 monomers that fluoresce green. In contrast, normal mitochondria have a high membrane potential, and JC-1 forms aggregates that emit red fluorescence. The results showed that E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel help protect mitochondrial function, maintain normal mitochondrial membrane potential, and restore the endogenous antioxidant system. Notably, E + B@TZ + Gel nearly restored the membrane potential to normal levels (Fig. 4J–K). Assessment of macrophage polarization by composite hydrogels

Osteoporotic bone defect repair is a multifaceted process in which the local immune-inflammatory response plays a crucial role in bone regeneration. Notably, prolonged infiltration of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages has been shown to impede the healing process significantly [48]. In contrast, successful bone healing is typically associated with the resolution of acute inflammation and the polarization of M1 macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [49,50]. To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of the composite hydrogels, we evaluated their influence on macrophage polarization. RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured with hydrogels in the presence of LPS to mimic the inflammatory microenvironment characteristic of osteoporotic bone defects. Immunofluorescence analysis of macrophage phenotypic markers revealed a predominance of CD86-positive (M1-labeled) macrophages in the control group, with relatively few CD206-positive (M2-labeled) macrophages (Fig. 5B–C and S17). In contrast, all hydrogel-treated groups showed varying degrees of CD86 reduction and CD206 upregulation. Specifically, the TZ + Gel and B@TZ + Gel groups exhibited a modest decrease in CD86-positive macrophages, accompanied by a corresponding increase in CD206-positive macrophages compared to the control group. This effect is likely attributable to the anti-inflammation role of Zn2+ from the TZ component, which may facilitate the polarization shift from M1 to M2 macrophages [51]. A more pronounced polarization from M1 to M2 macrophages was observed in the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel groups compared to the other groups. The results suggest that the composite hydrogels effectively attenuate inflammation and promote the conversion of M1 to M2. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed these findings (Fig. 5D). Specifically, the proportions of CD86-positive (M1 phenotype) and CD206-positive macrophages (M2 phenotype) were significantly altered in the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel groups relative to the control group. The percentage of CD 86-positive macrophages was 85.5 %, 65.9 %, and 64.6 % in control, E + TZ + Gel, and E + B@TZ + Gel, respectively. The percentage of CD206-positive macrophages was 6.00 %, 38.2 %, and 43.5 % in control, E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel, respectively. This reduction in pro-inflammatory macrophages and an increase in anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages plays a critical role in regulating the inflammatory process and facilitating the repair of bone defects [52].

The RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that the composite hydrogels modulate macrophage polarization at the genetic level. Compared with macrophages treated with LPS alone, RAW264.7 cells cultured with E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel exhibited significant downregulation of M1 phenotype and pro-inflammatory markers (CD86, iNOS, TGF-α), along with marked upregulation of M2 phenotype and anti-inflammatory markers (Arg-1, IL-10, TGFβ-1) (Fig. 5E). A similar trend was observed in cytokine secretion as measured by ELISA (Fig. 5F–I), with significant reductions in IL-1β and TNF-α levels and elevated concentrations of IL-4 and IL-10 in the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel groups. In brief, an insufficient number of M2 macrophages fail to provide adequate anti-inflammatory cytokines or promote further regeneration. The release of trace amounts of Zn2+ from TZ may contribute to modulating macrophage polarization [53]. More importantly, EGCG, a natural anti-inflammatory agent, may inhibit chronic inflammation by reducing inflammatory cytokines and increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines [54]. In summary, E + B@TZ + Gel effectively attenuates inflammation and mediates macrophage polarization. After 24 h, the E + B@TZ + Gel group had nearly closed the scratch area, indicating enhanced cell motility and migration. The hydrogel's strong affinity for cells facilitated the fast aggregation of HUVECs and fused endothelium formation. Additionally, BMP-2 released from the composite hydrogel is known to exert potent pro-angiogenic effects [56], which likely contributed to the observed results.

In vitro osteogenic capacity of composite hydrogels

Cranial bone defects in osteoporotic rats

Osteoporotic bone defects, which are challenging to heal, require specialized treatment due to their harsh microenvironment. The composite hydrogel was designed to accommodate the conditions of osteoporotic bone defects. To evaluate its therapeutic potential, we established cranial bone defect models in osteoporotic rats. The operating time points are outlined in Fig. 8A. Twelve weeks after the osteoporosis model was established, two round defects (5 mm in diameter) were created symmetrically adjacent to the sagittal suture in the calvaria of OVX rats. Based on the predetermined experimental groups, these defects were then filled with hydrogels or left untreated. The rats were divided into six groups: Sham group, OP group, TZ + Gel group, B@TZ + Gel group, E + TZ + Gel group, and E + B@TZ + Gel group. The results of micro-CT 3D reconstruction and quantitative analysis of cranial defect repair are listed in Fig. 8G–H. Significantly less new bone formation was observed in the control (OP) group compared to the sham-operated group, indicating impaired bone regeneration in osteoporotic rats relative to healthy controls. In all treatment groups, new bone formation was observed extending from the margins toward the center of the cranial defect. At 6 weeks, minimal bone regeneration was detected in the OP group, while the other groups exhibited varying degrees of new bone formation. Notably, the E + B@TZ + Gel group had the highest level of new bone formation, with a new bone volume fraction of 55.1 % ± 3.1 %, exceeding that of the sham group (38.0 % ± 2.5 %). By 12 weeks, the OP group still exhibited only limited bone repair, whereas the E + B@TZ + Gel group demonstrated substantial bone regeneration, with newly formed bone covering most of the defect area. Furthermore, the E + TZ + Gel group was higher than the OP and TZ + Gel groups at both weeks 6 and 12, suggesting that EGCG loading is also able to promote bone repair to a certain level.

In vitro experiments demonstrated that the hydrogels could reduce the inflammatory response. These findings suggest that hydrogels co-loaded with both therapeutic agents achieved superior repair outcomes compared to those containing a single agent, underscoring the potential of multifunctional hydrogels to maximize osteoporotic bone regeneration. Quantitative analysis of new bone parameters based on micro-CT data is shown in Fig. 8I–L. Significant differences in bone morphology were observed among the groups at both time points. The E + B@TZ + Gel group exhibited notably higher BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th values than other groups (Fig. 8I–K). Meanwhile, Tb.Sp values progressively decreased from the OP group to the E + B@TZ + Gel group (Fig. 8L). These quantitative findings aligned with the results of micro-CT three-dimensional reconstruction. Among all groups, E + B@TZ + Gel showed the most effective repair, indicating that PVA-TSPBA loaded with EGCG and B@TZ maximally promoted new bone formation. The enhanced osteogenesis is attributed to the synergistic effects of bio support from hydrogel and the active ingredients release, working through multiple mechanisms to achieve optimal bone regeneration.

The histomorphology of the bone defects was further examined, and bone regeneration was assessed using H&E and Masson staining. Six weeks postoperatively, the E + B@TZ + gel group had the greatest new bone formation and the most favorable regenerative outcomes than the other groups (Fig. 8M). At 12 weeks post-surgery, cortical bone gradually healed in the TZ + Gel, B@TZ + Gel, E + TZ + Gel, and E + B@TZ + Gel groups, with the E + B@TZ + Gel group showing nearly complete healing. The control group displayed primarily fibrous tissue covering the defects [61], with no evidence of cortical bone formation, indicating that bone defects remained unhealed over time. Masson staining revealed a large amount of mature mineralized collagen (red) in the E + B@TZ + Gel group, while the defect edges were primarily composed of immature, non-mineralized collagen (blue). This staining pattern reflects that the composite hydrogel facilitated centripetal bone regeneration from the periphery toward the center of the defect. Additionally, neovascularization was observed at the repair site in the treatment group, indicating that E + B@TZ + Gel may enhance bone repair by promoting vascularized bone regeneration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that E + B@TZ + Gel significantly enhances the repair of osteoporotic bone defects through synergistic effects, exhibiting excellent biodegradability, biocompatibility, and the ability to promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis.

At 12 weeks post-surgery, osteogenesis-related proteins OPN and RUNX2 at the bone defect site were assessed using immunohistochemical staining. The staining revealed minimal positive staining in the OP and TZ + Gel groups. In contrast, E + TZ + Gel, B@TZ + Gel, E + B@TZ + Gel groups showed a marked increase in positive staining, indicating a higher level of OPN and RUNX2 expression (Fig. 9A–C). The strongest expression of both markers was observed in the E + B@TZ + Gel group, suggesting that this composite hydrogel efficiently promotes osteogenesis. To evaluate neovascularization, CD31 and CD34, markers for endothelial cells, were assessed. In the control group, limited neovascularization was observed at the defect site, contributing to poor bone repair. In contrast, the B@TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel groups exhibited a progressive increase in capillary formation, with the E + B@TZ + Gel group displaying numerous large-caliber blood vessels (Fig. 9D–F). The results suggest that the composite hydrogel significantly promotes angiogenesis, thereby facilitating vascularized bone regeneration. To assess the anti-inflammatory effect of the hydrogels, immunofluorescence staining was performed at the cranial defect site after 12 weeks of treatment, focusing on macrophage phenotypes in response to different hydrogels (Fig. 9G–J). The OP group showed the highest expression of M1 macrophage marker CD86 and the lowest M2 macrophage markers CD206, indicating a pro-inflammatory state. The TZ + Gel and B@TZ + Gel groups exhibited a moderately increased CD206-positive and decreased CD86-positive staining, consistent with in vitro findings. In contrast, the E + TZ + Gel and E + B@TZ + Gel groups demonstrated the highest levels of CD206-positive macrophages and lowest levels of CD86-positive macrophages, indicating a clear shift toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-dominant phenotype. The phenotypic shift is likely attributed to potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of Zn2+ and EGCG. Collectively, the composite hydrogel system alleviates inflammatory cell infiltration and reduces ROS accumulation within the osteoporotic microenvironment, thereby enhancing osteogenesis. This study presents a promising therapeutic strategy for the effective treatment of osteoporotic bone defects.

Biosafety evaluation

The biosafety of the composite hydrogel was assessed in rats by evaluating its biocompatibility and potential toxicity. Twelve weeks after implantation, blood samples and vital organs were collected for analysis. No significant abnormalities were observed in hematological parameters compared to the control group (Fig. S23). Furthermore, no signs of damage or inflammation were found in the hydrogel-treated rats' heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys (Fig. S24). These findings indicate that E + B@TZ + Gel exhibits excellent biocompatibility and negligible systemic toxicity, indicating its safety and suitability as a candidate material for osteoporotic bone defect repair.

Osteogenic mechanisms of E + B@TZ + Gel

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the osteogenic effect of the composite hydrogel, MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in complete medium containing 200 μM H2O2, and RNA sequencing was performed after 7 days of treatment with or without E + B@TZ + Gel (Fig. 10A). The volcano plot analysis revealed 2068 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the E + B@TZ + Gel and control groups, including 664 upregulated and 1404 downregulated genes (Fig. 10B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes such as ossification, biological adhesion, vasculature development, and response to stress (Fig. 10C). These included the MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, calcium signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, and TGF-beta signaling pathway (Fig. 10D). Among these, the PI3K/AKT and calcium signaling pathways-which are integral to osteoblast function and bone development-were prominently represented. Additionally, cellular communication pathways, such as the MAPK signaling pathway, play an important regulatory role in various cellular processes, such as proliferation and differentiation, exerting their critical importance in bone tissue formation [62]. Enriching these pathways suggests that the composite hydrogel exerts a multifaceted regulatory effect on the osteoblast, promoting osteogenesis through a complex interplay of molecular networks and signaling cascades. Importantly, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway has been widely recognized as a key regulator of osteogenesis and is critical for bone tissue regeneration [63]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed the upregulation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in the E + B@TZ + Gel group compared to the control group (Fig. 10E). We analyzed DEGs involved in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. The gene expression heat maps indicated an upregulation of genes such as Col1a1, Akt1, Pik3ap1, Itga2b, and Itga6 within the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 10F).

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway regulates the cell cycle and is involved in angiogenesis [64] and osteogenesis [65,66]. PI3K is an intracellular kinase involved in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and glucose transport and is an upstream regulator of AKT. PI3K activation results in the conversion of PIP2 to PIP3, which is essential for the activation of AKT. Activated AKT regulates mTOR phosphorylation. To validate the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, we performed Western blotting to assess both total and phosphorylated forms of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR. The results indicated that the ratios of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT, and p-mTOR/mTOR were significantly elevated in MC3T3-E1 cells treated with E + B@TZ + Gel group than control group under H2O2 exposure (Fig. 10J–K). Immunofluorescence and quantitative analyses were performed on the cranial bone defect cranial. The results showed that the expression levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT and p-mTOR were significantly elevated in the E + B@TZ + Gel group compared to the control group (Fig. 10G–I and L), which was consistent with the in vitro results. These findings suggest that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway was activated in E + B@TZ + Gel-treated rats with osteoporotic cranial defects, in agreement with RNA sequence results. Thus, we hypothesize that E + B@TZ + Gel enhances osteogenesis in osteoporotic environment through activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

Conclusion

We successfully developed a reversibly crosslinked, ROS-responsive hydrogel incorporating EGCG and B@TZ for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects. The ROS-responsive properties of composite hydrogels enable controlled delivery of EGCG in the high ROS microenvironment during the early stages of osteoporotic bone defects. Additionally, BMP-2 is anchored within the TZ, allowing for sustained release that matches late osteoblast differentiation and bone regeneration. This hydrogel system exhibits excellent biocompatibility, anti-inflammatory properties, and the ability to modulate macrophage polarization, while also promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis. These multifunctional characteristics render the composite hydrogel a promising therapeutic strategy for the effective treatment of osteoporotic bone defects, potentially offering novel clinical solutions for challenging bone repair scenarios.
