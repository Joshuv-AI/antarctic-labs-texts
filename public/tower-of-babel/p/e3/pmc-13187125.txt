Extensive screening of ten bacteriophage cocktails revealed an optimal combination with potent therapeutic activity against Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Thanchanok Sawaengwong, Sujintana Janesomboon, Varintip Lerdsittikul, Veerachat Muangsombut, Siwaporn Prachoochote, Narisara Chantratita, Sunee Korbsrisate, Patoo Withatanung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter baumannii poses a major clinical challenge due to its association with high morbidity and mortality, necessitating alternative treatment strategies. Bacteriophages offer a promising solution; however, their narrow host range limits efficacy against diverse A. baumannii strains. To address this limitation, we isolated five distinct phages (vB_AbaSI_1–5) of the class Caudoviricetes, each exhibiting a narrow host range (2.2%-29.6%) against 135 clinical A. baumannii isolates. Ten phage cocktails (A-J) were formulated and tested for host range expansion and bactericidal activity. Cocktails A, D, and E demonstrated enhanced efficacy, infecting 68 of 135 isolates (50.4%) and statistically significantly outperforming individual phages (p < 0.0001). Among these, cocktail A (comprising phages vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, and vB_AbaSI_3) exhibited the highest killing efficiency against extensively drug-resistant A. baumannii strain DMST43250, along with superior biofilm inhibition and degradation capabilities. Application of cocktail A completely inhibited biofilm formation within 24 h. In vivo efficacy was evaluated using a Galleria mellonella infection model, in which cocktail A improved larval survival to 85.0% on day 1 and 60.0% by day 7, exceeding other cocktails (p < 0.0001). These results highlight cocktail A as a promising candidate for phage therapy targeting A. baumannii infections and associated biofilms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46878-z.Subject terms: Diseases, Microbiology

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii is a Gram-negative coccobacillus and a major cause of hospital-acquired infections, particularly in critically ill patients and those requiring medical devices such as ventilators and catheters1. A major concern with A. baumannii is its multidrug resistance (MDR), which limits treatment options and contributes to high morbidity and mortality rates. The ability of A. baumannii to form biofilms establishes a protective matrix that impedes antibiotic penetration2, protects cells from desiccation and disinfectants3, and shields bacteria from mechanical removal and host immune responses4. A meta-analysis reported the global prevalence of MDR A. baumannii in hospital-acquired pneumonia to be 79.9%, with mortality rates ranging from 37.2% to 48.1%5. Remarkably, carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii strains, resistant to antibiotics considered “last-line” treatments6, have risen sharply worldwide, reaching approximately ~ 78% in the United States (2019–2020)7. Hence, developing alternative antimicrobial strategies to effectively target this high-priority pathogen is essential.

Phage therapy utilizes bacteriophages to specifically target and lyse bacterial pathogens, representing a promising strategy against MDR A. baumannii infections. Phages offer notable advantages, including ease of isolation, robust environmental stability, and high specificity. Moreover, their rapid biodistribution in vivo8 and capacity for self-replication confer an auto-dosing effect9. Furthermore, phages can inhibit biofilm formation and degrade preformed biofilms10. A recent study demonstrated that phage vB_AbaM-SHI effectively penetrates biofilm matrices, enhancing antibiotic efficacy and markedly reducing A. baumannii biofilm biomass11.

The narrow host range inherent to many phages is advantageous, ensuring a high degree of specificity; however, this characteristic can also be a limitation, as certain bacterial strains may inherently resist phage treatment. Therefore, broadening the host range of phages is essential to enhance their therapeutic efficacy. Genetic modifications, such as altering phage tail tubular proteins, have been demonstrated to expand the infectivity of phage vB_Ab4_Hep4 through receptor binding modification12. The development of phage cocktails represents a promising strategy, because combining multiple phages can broaden the host range and mitigate the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial strains. For example, the combined lysis spectrum of 4 phages achieved 87.5% (42 of 48) efficacy against carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii clinical isolates, substantially greater than that of individual phages13. Additionally, Schooley et al. reported successful treatment of an MDR A. baumannii infection in which the use of a phage cocktail resulted in complete bacterial elimination and patient recovery14. Despite the reported effectiveness of existing phage cocktails against A. baumannii, ongoing exploration for additional phages remains crucial to ensure efficacy against genetic and phenotypic variation among A. baumannii strains across different geographic locations15,16.

In this study, five different phages targeting A. baumannii were isolated from Thailand. Phage cocktails were generated from three-phage combinations of the five individual phages, resulting in ten different cocktails. The host range, biofilm inhibition, and biofilm degradation of each individual phage were compared with those of the different phage cocktail combinations. In addition, phage treatment efficacy was investigated using Galleria mellonella larvae as an in vivo infection model. The results identified a cocktail that provided statistically significantly greater antibacterial activity against A. baumannii than the other cocktails, with a broader host range and highly effective biofilm inhibition and degradation.

Results

Plaque morphologies and electron microscope analysis of five phages targeting A. baumannii

From 100 sewage samples collected across Thailand, newly isolated phages designated vB_AbaSI_2 through vB_AbaSI_5 were selected for further characterization, along with a previously isolated phage, vB_AbaSI_117. Plaque morphology was determined using each phage’s host strain (Table 1). As shown in Fig. 1A and D, phages vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_4 produced plaques with distinct morphologies. Phage vB_AbaSI_1 formed large, clear plaques with a widely spread halo, whereas vB_AbaSI_4 produced similar plaques but with a turbid outer ring, a clearer and more distinct inner zone, and a more defined halo. The halo plaque morphologies of phages vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_4 suggested the presence of a depolymerase, an enzyme that enhances phage lytic efficiency by degrading extracellular polysaccharides. Such halos are typically associated with phage-encoded depolymerases diffusing into the surrounding medium and degrading capsular or exopolysaccharides beyond the initial site of infection18. Phage vB_AbaSI_2 produced smaller, slightly turbid plaques compared with those of the other phages (Fig. 1B). In contrast, phages vB_AbaSI_3 and vB_AbaSI_5 formed very small and indistinct plaques compared with the other phages (Fig. 1C and E). Based on the different plaque morphologies and the ability of each phage to form plaques on different A. baumannii isolates, these five phages appear to be distinct, making them suitable candidates for inclusion in a phage cocktail to broaden host range.

Phages vB_AbaSI_1–5 exhibited a narrow host range against A. baumannii

Infectivity of each phage was tested against 135 A. baumannii isolates, with Table 2 indicating varying levels of effectiveness. Phage vB_AbaSI_1 exhibited the highest efficacy, lysing 29.6% of tested isolates (40 of 135), followed by phage vB_AbaSI_3, which affected 25.2% of isolates, and phage vB_AbaSI_5, which demonstrated lytic activity against 22.2% of isolates. In contrast, phages vB_AbaSI_2 and vB_AbaSI_4 showed lower efficacy, infecting 17.8% and 2.2% of isolates, respectively, indicating the necessity of broadening the host range.

Phages vB_AbaSI_1–5 show a high degree of genomic divergence

The genome sequences of phages vB_AbaSI_1–5 were identified and analyzed for gene synteny and sequence similarity using Clinker v0.0.25. As shown in Fig. 2A, all isolated phages showed a high degree of divergence, with only a few regions exhibiting similarity in synteny and coding sequences. When comparing the genome sequences of each phage to that of phage vB_AbaSI_1 using average nucleotide identity (ANI), phages vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_3, and vB_AbaSI_4 showed no detectable nucleotide similarity under the ANI thresholds applied, whereas vB_AbaSI_5 exhibited an ANI value of 77.7%. These results indicated that phages vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_3, and vB_AbaSI_4 are highly divergent from vB_AbaSI_1, whereas vB_AbaSI_5 shares a moderate level of nucleotide similarity, suggesting that vB_AbaSI_5 and vB_AbaSI_1 possess areas of conserved nucleotide similarity despite broader genomic divergence.

To further investigate genetic relationships among these phages, phylogenetic analysis was performed using VipTree based on whole-genome sequences, comparing the five isolated phages (vB_AbaSI_1–5) with 63 A. baumannii phages deposited in the GenBank database. As shown in Fig. 2B, the five isolated phages formed distinct clustering patterns, indicating that they are genetically different. This diversity highlights their uniqueness and potential synergy, aiding the selection of genetically distinct phages for cocktails to improve host range and effectiveness against A. baumannii.

Functional diversity of phages vB_AbaSI_1–5

Circular genome maps of each phage highlight key genes involved in the development of various phage functional proteins, including those for DNA replication, structural assembly, baseplate structural proteins, tail connector proteins, receptor-binding proteins, and lysis mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, phage vB_AbaSI_3 contains a gene encoding tail lysozyme (Fig. 3B), and phage vB_AbaSI_4 (Fig. 3C) contains a gene encoding a tail spike protein. These features suggest that combining these phages in a cocktail could enhance bacterial lysis by targeting multiple infection mechanisms. Phage vB_AbaSI_5 (Fig. 3D) possesses unique genes, including GDSL-type esterase/lipase family proteins, which are commonly associated with hydrolysis of ester bonds and may contribute to the degradation or modification of host surface polysaccharides [19], and an N-acetylmuramidase, a peptidoglycan-hydrolyzing enzyme typically involved in bacterial cell wall lysis [20]. These results highlighted the distinct functional capabilities of the phages, particularly the diversity of proteins involved in bacterial cell lysis. Such variation can be strategically exploited to design an effective phage cocktail targeting bacterial cells through multiple infection and lysis mechanisms. Notably, whole-genome sequencing revealed that phages vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_5 are temperate phages due to the presence of integrase genes, whereas vB_AbaSI_2–4 lack lysogeny-related genes, suggesting a strictly lytic nature.

Genome-based prediction of receptor-binding proteins

Comparative bioinformatic analysis of the five A. baumannii phages (vB_AbaSI_1–5) revealed several putative receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) with differing predicted structural features (Table 3). Phages vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, and vB_AbaSI_5 encode TipJ-like proteins containing fibronectin type III and immunoglobulin-like domains, motifs that have been associated with non-enzymatic surface interactions in other phages [21]. These features are consistent with possible engagement of outer-membrane components during adsorption [22]. Phage vB_AbaSI_3 encodes a short tail fiber protein homologous to the T4 gp12 adhesin [23], with a conserved receptor-binding region but no identifiable catalytic motifs. Proteins of this type in other systems commonly interact with surface polysaccharides, suggesting a potential preference for lipopolysaccharide or related glycans [24]. Phage vB_AbaSI_4 harbors a tail spike protein with a predicted pectin lyase-like β-helix fold (InterPro IPR011050), a structural feature characteristic of polysaccharide-targeting enzymes described in A. baumannii phages such as φAB6 [25]. This architecture suggests a possible role in interacting with capsular polysaccharides. Together, these genome-based predictions indicate that the five phages may utilize various receptor-recognition strategies, potentially involving outer-membrane proteins (vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_5), surface polysaccharides (vB_AbaSI_3), or capsular polysaccharides (vB_AbaSI_4), supporting their functional complementarity within cocktail formulations. However, these predicted interactions remain hypothetical and require experimental validation to define actual receptor specificities.

Three-phage combinations for the generation of ten phage cocktails

Due to the narrow host range of each phage (vB_AbaSI_1–5), phage cocktails were generated from combinations of three of the five isolated phages, resulting in ten phage cocktails, designated cocktails A-J, which were tested against 135 A. baumannii isolates. Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1 show the bactericidal activity of each cocktail, indicating that cocktails A (comprising vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, and vB_AbaSI_3), D (comprising vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_3, and vB_AbaSI_4), and E (comprising vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_3, and vB_AbaSI_5) were the most potent, lysing 50.4% (68 of 135) of A. baumannii isolates. Additional cocktails, such as cocktail H (comprising vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_3, and vB_AbaSI_5), also showed notable lytic activity, affecting 45.9% of isolates, whereas cocktail J (comprising vB_AbaSI_3, vB_AbaSI_4, and vB_AbaSI_5) lysed 45.2% of tested isolates (Table 2). The three most effective cocktails (A, D, and E) shared phages vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3, suggesting that these two phages play a key role in the strong overall efficacy of the cocktails.

To demonstrate the overlapping infectivity scheme of phage cocktails A, D, and E, Venn diagrams were generated. As shown in Fig. 4, phages vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 exhibited the broadest lytic activity, each making substantial contributions to the overall efficacy of these combinations. In contrast, although phages vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_4, and vB_AbaSI_5 target overlapping bacterial subgroups, they did not broaden the spectrum of lysed bacterial strains because A. baumannii isolates susceptible to these phages were already susceptible to the two principal phages within each cocktail. However, these additional phages may still serve as complementary agents, potentially enhancing overall efficacy and providing redundancy in the phage cocktail.

Cocktail A exhibited the highest bacterial killing efficiency against A. baumannii

The bacterial killing efficiency of five individual phages was compared with that of three phage cocktails against planktonic A. baumannii ATCC19606 and DMST43250 isolates, using an MOI of 10, for 24 h. The A. baumannii ATCC19606 strain, isolated from the urine of a patient in the United States, was chosen because it is widely utilized as a standard model strain for studying A. baumannii infection, particularly in the context of nosocomial infections [26]. In contrast, the A. baumannii DMST43250 isolate was cultured from a patient in Thailand, providing a clinically relevant perspective with a local bacterial strain. A. baumannii ATCC19606 is an antibiotic-susceptible strain, whereas A. baumannii DMST43250 is classified as an extensively drug-resistant strain (Supplementary Table S2). Therefore, using both strains allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of phage antimicrobial and antibiofilm efficacy across different resistance profiles and geographic origins of A. baumannii [15,16].

Regarding A. baumannii isolate ATCC19606 (Fig. 5A), cocktails A and D displayed the highest killing efficiency, as indicated by the lack of increased turbidity due to effective bacterial lysis (p < 0.0001), whereas cocktail E exhibited less activity, although it still significantly reduced bacterial growth compared with the untreated control (p < 0.0001). For A. baumannii isolate DMST43250 (Fig. 5B), cocktails A, D, and E statistically significantly inhibited bacterial growth (p < 0.0001).

Compared with individual phages, cocktails A and D statistically significantly outperformed phage vB_AbaSI_2 (p < 0.05) against A. baumannii ATCC19606. However, for isolate DMST43250, no statistically significant differences were observed between the cocktails and individual phage treatments, indicating that the relative benefit of cocktail therapy is strain-dependent. Overall, these results suggested that phage cocktails could enhance antibacterial activity, although their added benefit varies between isolates.

To determine whether the enhanced antimicrobial activity observed in cocktails A, D, and E was primarily due to the combined effect of vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 or to the contribution of additional phages, a two-phage cocktail containing only vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 was also evaluated. This two-phage combination statistically significantly reduced growth of both A. baumannii ATCC19606 and DMST43250 (p < 0.0001) compared with the untreated control and all individual phage treatments (Fig. 5). The bactericidal activity of vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 against ATCC19606 was strong but remained statistically significantly lower than that of cocktails A, D, and E (p = 0.0003, 0.0002, and 0.0386, respectively). When testing against A. baumannii DMST43250, the two-phage cocktail exhibited comparable killing activity to cocktails A, D, and E, with no statistically significant differences observed among these treatments. These findings indicated that vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 form the core contributors to antimicrobial activity, whereas the inclusion of vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_4, or vB_AbaSI_5 provides additional benefit primarily in the ATCC19606 strain but not in DMST43250, likely owing to differences in resistance backgrounds of A. baumannii strains.

One-step growth characteristics underlying cocktail A’s performance

To further investigate factors contributing to the enhanced activity of cocktail A, one-step growth curves were analyzed. Phage vB_AbaSI_1 exhibited a latent period of approximately 30 min and a burst size of 136 PFU/cell [17], whereas vB_AbaSI_3 showed a shorter latent period of 25 min with a smaller burst size of 65 PFU/cell (Supplementary Fig. S1). The combination of these two phages (vB_AbaSI_1 + 3) therefore provided both timely initiation of lysis and sustained amplification. Phage vB_AbaSI_2 displayed a similar latent period (30 min) but a markedly higher burst size of 182 PFU/cell (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating a substantially greater phage yield per replication cycle. The incorporation of vB_AbaSI_2 into cocktail A likely increased phage amplification during infection, supporting higher phage densities and contributing to more persistent bacterial suppression. Together, these observations indicated that the replication kinetics of the component phages contributed to the enhanced performance of cocktail A, with the combined properties of vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, and vB_AbaSI_3 providing a replication advantage during infection.

Phage cocktail A effectively inhibits biofilm formation of A. baumannii

To evaluate the effectiveness of the three-phage cocktails on biofilm inhibition, their activity against A. baumannii ATCC19606 and DMST43250 isolates was assessed. Regarding A. baumannii isolate ATCC19606, cocktails A, D, and E inhibited biofilm formation to below 10.0%, with cocktail A demonstrating the highest efficiency (1.2% biofilm formation detected). In comparison, treatment with individual phages resulted in higher biofilm formation than that observed with the cocktails; phages vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, and vB_AbaSI_5 resulted in 51.5–61.6% biofilm formation, whereas vB_AbaSI_3 and vB_AbaSI_4 reduced biofilms to 15.7% and 24.9%, respectively (Fig. 6A).

For A. baumannii isolate DMST43250 (Fig. 6B), cocktail A reduced biofilm formation to 1.0%. In comparison, individual phages (vB_AbaSI_1, vB_AbaSI_2, vB_AbaSI_4, and vB_AbaSI_5) also inhibited biofilm formation, but biofilm levels ranged from 18.4% to 43.5%, which was higher than the effect of cocktail A. Notably, phage vB_AbaSI_3 showed the highest inhibition rate, with 3.6% biofilm remaining, however, although cocktail A showed a lower biofilm level, the difference between the two treatments was not statistically significant (p = 0.9959). Phage vB_AbaSI_3 exhibited better biofilm control in DMST43250 than in ATCC19606, highlighting the variability in phage efficacy between strains.

To clarify whether the enhanced antibiofilm activity of cocktails A, D, and E resulted primarily from the combined action of vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 or from contributions of additional phages, a two-phage cocktail composed of vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 was evaluated (Fig. 6). In A. baumannii ATCC19606, the two-phage combination markedly inhibited biofilm formation (p < 0.0001), although its inhibitory effect was statistically significantly lower than those of cocktails A, D, and E (p < 0.0001, 0.0005, and 0.0019, respectively). In contrast, for the extensively drug-resistant strain DMST43250, biofilm inhibition did not differ statistically significantly between the two-phage and three-phage cocktails. Taken together, these results indicated that although the additional phages enhance biofilm inhibition in ATCC19606, they did not confer further benefit beyond vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 in DMST43250.

Phage cocktail A enhances biofilm degradation in A. baumannii

In addition to biofilm inhibition, the effectiveness of the three-phage cocktails in biofilm degradation was tested. Compared with the untreated control, which formed biofilm without degradation, cocktail A demonstrated the strongest biofilm degradation capacity, achieving high degradation rates with 14.9% and 4.6% biofilm remaining for A. baumannii isolates ATCC19606 (Fig. 6C) and DMST43250 (Fig. 6D), respectively. Cocktails D and E showed similar biofilm degradation in ATCC19606 (28.4–28.7%) and DMST43250 (25.6–43.7%). Overall, these findings highlighted the superior biofilm-degrading potential of phage cocktail A, which was most effective in disrupting established biofilms in both antibiotic-susceptible A. baumannii ATCC19606 and antibiotic-resistant A. baumannii DMST43250 strains.

In the biofilm degradation assay, the two-phage cocktail reduced preformed biofilms in both A. baumannii strains; however, its degradative activity was statistically significantly lower than that of cocktail A in ATCC19606 (p = 0.0070) and DMST43250 (p = 0.0076), demonstrating that cocktail A was more effective at disrupting established biofilms. Together, these findings suggested that vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_3 constitute the core drivers of antibiofilm activity, whereas additional phages further reinforce overall inhibition and degradation, an effect that appears to vary across strain backgrounds.

Phage cocktail A enhances A. baumannii-infected G. mellonella survival

The G. mellonella survival assay was conducted following infection with either A. baumannii isolates ATCC19606 or DMST43250. For ATCC19606-infected larvae, survival in the untreated control group was markedly reduced over the seven-day observation period (Fig. 7A). Individual phage treatments of infected larvae with any of the five individual phages (Fig. 7A, left panel) resulted in survival rates below 15% at day 7, except for phage vB_AbaSI_2, which maintained a survival rate of 60%. These results suggested that phage vB_AbaSI_2 was particularly effective in protecting larvae from A. baumannii infection. When A. baumannii ATCC19606-infected larvae were treated with cocktail A, a survival rate of 85% on the first day was observed, which decreased to 75% by day 3 and further declined to 60% by day 7 (Fig. 7A, right panel). This survival rate was statistically significantly higher than that of the untreated control group (p = 0.0027), highlighting the strong protective effect of cocktail A in vivo. In contrast, cocktails D and E mediated initial survival rates of 50–55% on the first day, which dropped to 20% and 35% by day 7, respectively.

Similar results were observed for A. baumannii isolate DMST43250, the untreated control groups injected with only A. baumannii without phage treatment (Fig. 7, black lines), experienced rapid and substantial mortality, with no larvae surviving beyond day 3 in most cases. Individual phage treatments (Fig. 7B, left panel) resulted in survival rates below 10% at day 7, except for phage vB_AbaSI_2, which maintained a 45% survival rate but was still generally less effective than phage cocktail A. larvae treated with cocktail A maintained 80% survival up to day 3, with survival decreasing to 65% by day 7 (Fig. 7B, right panel). The survival rate was statistically significantly higher than that of the untreated control group (p < 0.0001). In comparison, cocktails D and E provided 20% survival by day 7. These findings underscored the enhanced efficacy of phage cocktail A in conferring protection against lethal A. baumannii infections in the G. mellonella model.

Discussion

The rise of MDR bacteria, particularly A. baumannii, a pathogen common in ICUs, is a critical health threat. Its ability to form biofilms on medical devices such as endotracheal tubes and intravascular catheters can serve as reservoirs for A. baumannii, increasing the risk of patient colonization and leading to respiratory or bloodstream infections [27]. Therefore, targeting A. baumannii biofilms is a crucial strategy for reducing nosocomial infections and limiting bacterial spread. Phage therapy has gained increasing recognition in research and experimental therapeutic settings as a promising approach for managing multidrug-resistant bacterial infections [28,29]. This study provides supporting evidence that phage cocktail therapy can effectively control A. baumannii both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating its potential to eliminate biofilms.

Five distinct phages (vB_AbaSI_1–5) exhibited a narrow host range, with infection rates ranging from 2.2% to 29.6% against 135 A. baumannii isolates. Previous studies had reported varying host range outcomes for phages targeting A. baumannii, highlighting considerable variability across different isolates. Margulieux et al. (2023) demonstrated that phage Eab13 exhibited a broad host range, lysing 86.7% (91 of 105) of MDR A. baumannii isolates [30], in contrast to the general trend of narrow host ranges observed in A. baumannii phages. For example, phage Abp95 achieved a host range of 29.0% (58 of 200) [31], and phage vB_AbaS_SA1 lysed 33.0% (10 of 30) of MDR isolates [32]. Earlier studies also reflected this limitation, with phage ZZ1 infecting 13.0% (3 of 23) of strains and phage AB1 infecting 19.7% (25 of 127) strains [33–35]. These findings underscore the challenge of narrow host ranges in A. baumannii phages and highlight the need for approaches that enhance phage applicability across diverse bacterial populations.

This limitation highlights the need for strategies to expand phage host range, including genetic engineering approaches [36] such as genome editing to modify receptor-binding proteins (e.g., tail tubular proteins) [12] and non-genetic engineering approaches, such as natural selection through serial host adaptation using the Appelmans protocol [37]. Although engineered phages allow tailored interventions, they face challenges such as time-consuming processes, potential off-target effects, and regulatory hurdles [36]. In contrast, phage cocktails provide a robust and impactful strategy, simultaneously expanding the range of bacterial targets while reducing the risk of resistance development [38]. Several studies have investigated the efficacy of phage cocktails against MDR A. baumannii infections, both in vitro and in vivo, utilizing different numbers of phages in their formulations. Regeimbal et al. (2016) found that a five-phage cocktail (Aba-Army1, Aba-Navy1, Aba-Navy2, Aba-Navy3, and Aba-Navy4) reduced MDR A. baumannii AB5075 burden and improved survival in a murine pneumonia model [39]. Recently, Rastegar et al. (2024) demonstrated that combining four temperate phage cocktails with antibiotics (ampicillin/sulbactam, meropenem, and colistin) effectively targeted extensively drug-resistant A. baumannii; at higher concentrations, phage cocktails markedly enhanced biofilm inhibition and degradation [40].

This study formulated three-phage cocktails (A, D, and E) that expanded the narrow host range of individual phages against 135 A. baumannii isolates, achieving 50.4% infection efficacy. The broadened host range observed in cocktails A, D, and E can be attributed to the diversity of receptor-binding proteins among their constituent phages. Each cocktail combines phages that recognize distinct bacterial surface receptors, including outer-membrane proteins, lipopolysaccharides, and capsular polysaccharides, as indicated by bioinformatic analyses (Table 3). This complementary receptor recognition likely underlies the enhanced host range and bactericidal efficacy of the cocktails.

In addition, comparative genomic and receptor-binding protein analyses (Table 3) showed that vB_AbaSI_2 encodes a TipJ-like tail protein containing fibronectin type III and immunoglobulin-like domains, motifs associated with non-enzymatic interactions with outer-membrane proteins in other phages [21]. Such a binding mode may facilitate efficient adsorption and help explain its strong bactericidal activity in planktonic cultures and improved larval survival, despite the absence of depolymerase activity or halo formation. Together, these observations suggested that vB_AbaSI_2 functions as a highly lytic, non-depolymerase phage whose contribution to cocktail efficacy arises primarily from rapid infection dynamics rather than biofilm matrix degradation, thereby complementing the depolymerase-encoding phage vB_AbaSI_1, which provides enzymatic biofilm-disrupting activity. In addition, the strong antibiofilm activity of the tail-lysozyme-encoding phage vB_AbaSI_3 across most isolates further supports the effectiveness of cocktail A.

A previously reported 14-phage cocktail exhibited 54.0% lytic activity against A. baumannii (298 of 552 isolates tested) [41]. In this study, the three-phage cocktails (A, D, and E) reached a comparable 50.4% efficacy against 135 isolates, demonstrating that a smaller but strategically selected combination of diverse phages can provide broad host coverage similar to much larger formulations. Four- and five-phage cocktails were also generated in the current study (Supplementary Table S1); however, their performance did not surpass that of the three-phage cocktails. The key advantage of a smaller cocktail may be reducing competition for bacterial receptors, potentially avoiding antagonistic interactions observed in larger cocktails. Given this, cocktails with only three phages were chosen for development because this approach maximized efficiency while minimizing unnecessary redundancy.

Cocktail A’s ability to disrupt A. baumannii biofilms enhances its therapeutic potential. Biofilms formed by A. baumannii pose substantial therapeutic challenges due to the extracellular polymeric substance matrix [42], which acts as a physical and biochemical barrier, limiting the penetration of antimicrobial agents, including phages [43]. These factors collectively make A. baumannii biofilms a critical target for improved therapeutic strategies. This study demonstrated that cocktail A showed notable efficacy in inhibiting and degrading biofilm biomass. In comparison to previous studies, in which phages such as AB7-IBB1 [44] and vB_AbaM-IME-AB2 [45] were reported to inhibit A. baumannii biofilms on both abiotic and biotic surfaces with inhibition rates of 60–80%, cocktail A proved more effective under abiotic conditions, achieving inhibition rates of 98.8% for ATCC19606 and 99.0% for DMST43250.

The biofilm inhibition and degradation assays revealed that the three individual phages (vB_AbaSI_1–3) exhibited variable activities against the two A. baumannii strains. For A. baumannii ATCC19606, vB_AbaSI_3 showed the highest single-phage activity in biofilm inhibition, although its degradation efficiency was not statistically significantly greater than that of vB_AbaSI_2. In contrast, for clinical isolate DMST43250, vB_AbaSI_2 demonstrated the strongest biofilm-degrading effect among the single phages, whereas vB_AbaSI_3 exhibited the greatest individual phage activity in biofilm inhibition, suggesting strain-dependent differences in phage-host interactions. Notably, cocktail A achieved statistically significantly greater biofilm inhibition and degradation than the individual phages in ATCC19606. However, for DMST43250, no statistically significant difference in biofilm inhibition was observed between cocktail A and the individual phages, although cocktail A showed statistically significant differences in degradation comparisons, highlighting a synergistic effect when combining phages with complementary infection and enzymatic profiles. The enhanced activity of the cocktail can be attributed to the combined actions of vB_AbaSI_1, which carries a depolymerase that degrades extracellular polysaccharides, and vB_AbaSI_3, which encodes a tail lysozyme that facilitates cell wall lysis. Together, these mechanisms likely improve phage access and penetration into the biofilm matrix, overcoming defense barriers that may limit single-phage efficacy. Overall, these results demonstrate that phage cocktails can achieve broader and more potent antibiofilm activity than individual phages.

Building on its strong antibiofilm activity, cocktail A also demonstrated promising therapeutic potential in vivo. In the G. mellonella model, it achieved survival rates of 65–70% at 7 days. These results surpass those of previous studies, such as the survival rates observed with phage Bϕ-R2096 (MOI100), which were limited to approximately 50% within 24 h [46], and vB_AbaP_AGC01, which yielded approximately 40% survival at 72 h post-infection [47]. Within cocktail A, phage vB_AbaSI_2 exhibited the narrowest host range but demonstrated greater efficacy, showing high killing efficiency in vitro and improved survival in vivo. This enhanced activity may be attributed to its lack of lysogeny-associated genes, such as integrase, which could enhance its bacteriolytic potential and improve survival outcomes, because strictly lytic phages are preferred for therapeutic applications. Strictly lytic phages can be generated by deleting integrase or repressor determinants to prevent integration and promote virulent infection [48, 49]. Supporting this, Mageeney et al. (2020) demonstrated that deletion of the integrase gene (Δint) in prophages converted them into non-lysogenic, strictly lytic variants that exhibited enhanced killing efficiency both in liquid culture and in a G. mellonella infection model of Pseudomonas aeruginosa [49]. However, despite also lacking integrase, phage vB_AbaSI_3 monotherapy did not statistically significantly improve survival in vivo compared with phage vB_AbaSI_2. This suggests that, although not a temperate phage, additional factors likely influence phage efficacy in vivo. Future studies should focus not only on eliminating lysogeny-related genes but also on optimizing cocktail formulation to enhance therapeutic efficacy under more complex conditions.

Regarding the sources of A. baumannii isolates, there was no statistically significant difference in the ability of cocktail A to inhibit in vitro biofilm formation or its in vivo effectiveness in improving larval survival between A. baumannii ATCC19606 (isolated from a patient in the United States) and DMST43250 (isolated from a Thai patient). Because only two A. baumannii strains were examined in this study, these findings indicate only that cocktail A can be effective against isolates of different origins. Additional testing across a larger and more diverse panel of clinical isolates will be required to determine the broader therapeutic potential of this cocktail.

Overall, these findings highlight the effectiveness of phage cocktail A in combating A. baumannii, providing valuable insights into the selection and use of phages as an alternative therapeutic strategy to conventional antibiotic treatment. The cocktail demonstrated strong efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo models, showcasing its ability to effectively inhibit and degrade biofilms as well as reduce bacterial loads. Such comprehensive characterization of phage biological properties and therapeutic performance is important to ensure reproducibility and translational relevance in phage therapy research [50]. Future engineering may be necessary to modify the temperate phages in this study (vB_AbaSI_1 and vB_AbaSI_5) to enhance their safety for practical use in treatment.

Materials and methods

Biosafety and animal ethics approvals

This research project was approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, and Faculty of Veterinary Sciences, Mahidol University. The approval certificate numbers are SI 2021-006, SI 2023-029, and MUVS-B-007/2568. G. mellonella larvae experiments followed the regulations of the Faculty of Veterinary Science, Mahidol University Animal Care and Use Committee (FVS-MU-IACUC approval no. MUVS-2024-05-31).

Bacterial isolates and culture conditions

All bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Criterion™, Hardy Diagnostics, USA) at 37 °C with continuous agitation for 18 h. The mid-logarithmic growth phase of A. baumannii was obtained by subculturing an overnight culture into LB broth and incubating until an OD600 of 0.17 ± 0.01 was reached, corresponding to approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL.

Phage isolation and propagation

Phage isolation was performed as previously described [17]. Phages were first screened by spot assay. Briefly, 100 µL of mid-log phase A. baumannii culture was mixed with 5 mL of LB top agar (0.35% w/v agar) before pouring onto LB bottom agar (1% w/v agar) plates. After setting for approximately 5 min, 10 µL of filtered sample was spotted onto the surface and incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h. Clear plaques appearing on the plates were selected for further purification via the double agar overlay plaque assay [51], performed at least ten times to obtain a clonal phage.

For large-scale phage propagation, approximately 30 plates per sample were subjected to the double agar overlay plaque assay [51]. Following overnight incubation, SM buffer was added to each plate and left to incubate at room temperature for 4 h with gentle shaking at 80 rpm. The resulting lysates were harvested and centrifuged, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.45 μm membrane filter (Whatman™, UK). The filtrate then underwent an additional centrifugation step at 18,000 x g for 30 min at 4 °C. The purified phage pellet was resuspended in SM buffer and stored at 4 °C for downstream analyses.

Host ranges of phage infection

The host range of phages in lysing different bacterial isolates was evaluated using the spot assay according to Janesomboon et al. [17]. Tenfold serial dilutions of each phage suspension were spotted on bacterial lawns to minimize lysis from without and to allow individual plaque formation. Individual phages and phage cocktails were tested against 135 clinical A. baumannii isolates in biological triplicate (Supplementary Table S1). After overnight incubation at 37 °C, outcomes were evaluated based on the presence of clear lysis zones, turbid spots, or the absence of lysis.

One-step growth curve of phage infection

One-step growth kinetics for phages vB_AbaSI_2 and vB_AbaSI_3 were evaluated according to Janesomboon et al. [17], while the one-step growth curve of vB_AbaSI_1 was previously reported [17]. Phages vB_AbaSI_2 and vB_AbaSI_3 were mixed with their host A. baumannii isolates (as listed in Table 1) at an MOI of 0.01 and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min to allow phage attachment. After centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in LB broth containing 5 mM CaCl₂ and incubation was continued at 37 °C. Aliquots (1 mL) were collected every 5 min for 2 h, serially diluted, and quantified using the double-agar overlay plaque assay [51]. Phage titers obtained at each time point were plotted to generate the one-step growth curve and to determine the latent period and burst size.

Transmission electron microscopy

The morphology of isolated phages was analyzed using TEM following a negative staining technique as described by Withatanung et al. [51]. Phage samples (108 PFU/mL) were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.3) and stained with 1% uranyl acetate for 30 s on Formvar-carbon-coated copper grids (200 mesh; Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA). Phage images were acquired with a JEM 1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL Co., Japan) set to an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. The dimensions of phage capsids and tails were measured using ImageJ software (version 1.49) for at least 20 individual phage particles.

Phage genomic DNA extraction

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using a modified phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (PCI) extraction method [51]. Briefly, a purified phage suspension (108 PFU/mL) was treated with 1 M MgCl2, DNase I (2000 U/mL), and RNase A (100 mg/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min. After incubation, 10 mg/mL proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 0.5 M EDTA, and 10% SDS were added, and the mixture was incubated again at 55 °C for 1 h. The solution was then extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) and centrifuged at room temperature. The aqueous phase was carefully collected and subjected to DNA precipitation. After centrifugation, the DNA pellet was washed, resuspended in nuclease-free water, and stored at -20 °C or -80 °C for long-term storage.

Whole genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

A phage genomic DNA library was prepared using the Illumina DNA Library Prep kit (Illumina, Inc., USA) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Post-sequencing, genome assembly, and annotation were conducted according to Withatanung et al. (2024) [51]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were analyzed for homology with existing genome sequences in the NCBI database. Genome maps were generated using Artemis version 17.0.1 [53]. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis was conducted using the OrthoANI algorithm implemented in the EZBioCloud ANI calculator (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/tools/ani) [54], with default parameters. Pairwise ANI values were calculated to assess nucleotide-level similarity among the phage genomes. For phylogenetic analysis, phage genome sequences were uploaded to ViPTree [54] to investigate proteome relationships. Comparative genome contents were visualized and compared using Clinker v0.0.25 [56]. Virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes encoded by the phages were analyzed using PhageScope [56]. Putative functions of predicted proteins were identified by querying them against BLASTP, HHPred v2.08 [57], and InterPro v106.0 [58], allowing annotation based on sequence similarity to known proteins.

Bactericidal activity of individual phages and phage cocktails

The bactericidal activity of individual phages and phage cocktails was assessed against clinically isolated A. baumannii in a 96-well plate [59] with some modifications. Phage titers used for MOI calculation were determined on each phage’s respective propagation host strain (Table 1), whereas the antimicrobial activity assays were performed using ATCC19606 and DMST43250. Briefly, mid-log phase A. baumannii (approximately 108 CFU/mL) in 96-well plates was mixed with either individual phage or phage cocktail at an MOI of 10 before static incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacterial turbidity after phage treatment was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch-2, BioTek, USA) with the microplate gently shaken for 3 s prior to each measurement. Control wells contained only bacterial suspension without phages. OD600 values were blank-corrected against culture medium alone; thus, values approaching zero indicate turbidity comparable to the negative control.

Microtiter plate biofilm inhibition and degradation assays

An overnight culture of A. baumannii, grown in LB broth with 0.25% glucose to promote biofilm formation at 37 °C, was adjusted to approximately 108 CFU/mL, and 100 µL of bacterial suspension (approximately 106 CFU/mL) was inoculated into each well of a sterile 96-well plate [61]. Individual phages or phage cocktails (107 PFU/mL) were added to achieve an MOI of 10 at time 0 before incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. Each well was then quantified for biofilm formation by washing with 1x PBS to remove planktonic cells. Adhered biofilms were fixed with 99% methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and solubilized with 30% acetic acid before measuring absorbance at 595 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch-2, BioTek, USA). Control wells contained bacteria without phages (positive control) or media alone (negative control).

For biofilm degradation assessment, preformed A. baumannii biofilms were washed with 1x PBS to remove planktonic cells before adding individual phages or phage cocktails (10⁸ PFU/mL) at time 0, followed by 24-h incubation at 37 °C. Control wells received sterile SM buffer instead of phages. Biofilms were then stained and quantified using the crystal violet method as described above. The biofilm formation percentage was calculated using the formula: %Biofilm = (OD595 of treated biofilm / OD595 of untreated biofilm) ×100.

In vivo G. mellonella larvae infection assay

Healthy larvae (250–300 mg) were maintained at 25 °C in the dark before the experiment, and larvae showing melanization or damage were excluded. Larvae were randomly divided into 15 groups (n = 20 per group): A. baumannii-infected larvae treated with either individual phages (vB_AbaSI_1–5) (groups 1–5) or three-phage cocktails A, D, and E (groups 6–8); uninfected larvae treated with individual phages (groups 9–13); uninfected larvae treated with PBS (negative control; group 14); and untreated A. baumannii-infected larvae (positive control; group 15).

Each larva’s left proleg was injected with 10 µL of A. baumannii cultures (108 CFU/mL), a bacterial challenge dose selected to induce reproducible mortality in untreated larvae, as commonly applied in Galleria mellonella infection models [62]. After 1 h, the treatment groups received 10 µL of individual phage or phage cocktail (108 PFU; MOI 100) in the right proleg, whereas control groups received PBS. Larvae were incubated at 37 °C and monitored for survival over 7 days. Death was defined as a lack of movement in response to prodding. Survival was assessed daily, and Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to compare survival between phage-treated and control groups. Absolute survival data for all treatment and control groups are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) using GraphPad Prism software. Comparisons between two groups were made using the student’s t-test, whereas differences among three or more groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Survival data were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test to compare survival curves between treatment and control groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001, ns; not significant).
