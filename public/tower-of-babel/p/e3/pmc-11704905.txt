Basic Hip Arthroscopy Part 2: Central Compartment Arthroscopy (Interportal Capsulotomy, Acetabuloplasty, and Labral Repair)
Authors: Jorge Chahla, Juan Bernardo Villarreal-Espinosa, Salvador Gonzalez Ayala, Joshua Wright-Chisem, Ron Gilat, Shane J Nho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past decade, hip-preservation strategies have gained momentum, resulting in a notable increase in the use of hip arthroscopy for diagnostic and therapeutic interventions in hip-related pathology. In this 3-part series, the authors will aim to comprehensively review the fundamentals of hip arthroscopy in the setting of femoroacetabular impingement. This Technical Note will thoroughly review the senior authors’ approach to managing the central compartment of the hip in the context of femoroacetabular impingement.

Over the past decade, the incidence of sports-related hip injuries has increased, likely attributable to increasing sports participation at younger ages.1,2 Concurrently, the use of hip arthroscopy has surged as a minimally invasive method to address these injuries.3,4 Although hip arthroscopy has proven to achieve successful outcomes for various hip-related pathologies, particularly femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), it is associated with a steep learning curve.5,6

In response to the recent increase in hip arthroscopy and the associated steep learning curve, the authors aim to provide a comprehensive series of Technical Notes on the fundamentals of hip arthroscopy. The present Technical Note will thoroughly review the senior authors’ approach to managing the central compartment during hip arthroscopy in the context of FAI.

Anatomical and Surgical Overview

When referring to hip arthroscopy, the hip can be divided into a capsular layer and 2 associated compartments: central and peripheral (Fig 1). The hip capsule is reinforced by 3 primary static stabilizers—the iliofemoral, pubofemoral, and ischiofemoral ligaments.7,8 The iliofemoral ligament (Bigelow’s ligament) represents the largest and strongest of the 3 ligaments and is incised for creation of the interportal and T-capsulotomies required to gain access to the central and peripheral compartments, respectively8 (Fig 2).

Although not as common as cam lesions, bony overgrowth of the acetabulum, known as pincer lesions, contribute to the soft-tissue impingement present in patients with FAI. Furthermore, the subspinal space is a virtual space located inferior and deep in the anterior inferior iliac spine that is expanded by debriding the soft tissues overlying the acetabulum (Fig 3). Acetabular decompression during the acetabular rim-trimming phase is crucial, as neglecting it has been observed to induce recurrent pain and revision surgery.9

(A) Sagittal view of a left hip cadaveric dissection demonstrating the labrum surrounding the acetabular edge as well as the C-shaped articular cartilage (lunate surface) with the ligamentum teres (LT) fat in the center of the acetabular notch. Interruption of the labrum and articular cartilage can be observed inferiorly at the level of the transverse ligament (TL). (B) Appreciation of the subspinal space demonstrated in a cadaveric model of a left hip. (C) Cadaveric dissection of a right hip after performance of a labral repair.

The labrum is a C-shaped fibrocartilaginous structure that encircles the rim of the acetabulum, enhancing the depth of the hip joint socket. This increased depth helps maintain the native negative intra-articular pressure, providing additional stability7 (Fig 3). Failure to address restoration of the native negative intra-articular suction seal via labral repair could contribute to the development of microinstability and eventual progression toward early arthritis.8

Surgical Technique

Interportal Capsulotomy

While viewing from the modified mid-anterior portal (mMAP), an arthroscopic blade (CAP-FIX; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is inserted through the previously placed anterolateral (AL) portal 5.5-mm cannula, which is then partially slid back toward the superficial tunnel end of the cannula for assessment of adequate AL portal location. After confirmation of adequate location, the blade is used to create a small 5-mm periportal capsulotomy at the midpoint between the femoral head and the acetabulum, as shown in Video 1. Shifting the arthroscope to the AL portal, a 1.5- to 2-cm interportal capsulotomy, parallel to the acetabular rim, is performed from the mMAP in direction toward the viewing AL portal (Figs 2 and 4). Subsequently, 2 traction stitches are placed through capsular tissue, at the location of the AL and modified midanterior portals, using a suture passer (SPEEDSTITCH, Smith & Nephew) to enhance visualization and facilitate the manipulation of structures within the central hip compartment. These sutures are then tensioned and secured 1 to 2 mm proud to the patient’s skin with a Crile hemostat, ensuring adequate tension for enhanced capsular retraction without macerating the underlying skin, as shown in Figure 5.

(B) Tension is kept by locking the suture within the Crile hemostat just proud with the patient’s skin in aims of avoiding macerating the patient’s skin.

Acetabuloplasty and Acetabular Rim Trimming

Once the central compartment structures (femoral head, labrum, acetabulum) are visualized while achieving ease of instrument manipulation, an arthroscopic shaver is used to debride the overlying frayed labral soft tissue. After this, an ablation probe (FLOW 50 COBLATION; Smith & Nephew) is used to ablate the overlying acetabular periosteum and soft tissue, preparing the acetabular bone located just superior to the labrum for subsequent acetabuloplasty and anchor deployment. The labrum is then evaluated for tears using an arthroscopic probe (Fig 6).

Arthroscopic image of the right hip demonstrating a torn labrum extending from the 12-to 2-o’clock position as viewed from the modified midanterior portal. A probe is being used for assessment of labral stability given the associated tear.

Viewing from the AL portal, acetabular rim trimming from the 2- to 3-o’clock positions in a right hip (9- to 10-o’clock in a left hip) is performed using a 5.5-mm burr, specifically targeting areas of apparent labral pathology, in preparation for subsequent anchor deployment for labral repair, while carefully preserving the peripheral chondrolabral junction (Fig 7). The desired resection depth is patient specific as it relates to the bony overgrowth size (pincer lesion) observed intraoperatively via fluoroscopy. Nonetheless, a general rule of thumb is to stop acetabular rim trimming when the pincer lesion angle is less than 30°. The camera is then switched to the mMAP to prepare the acetabulum from the 12- to 2-o’clock positions in a right hip (10- to 12-o’clock in a left hip), thus allowing for a balanced acetabuloplasty.

Right hip arthroscopic image demonstrating a burr performing the acetabular rim trimming in preparation for anchor deployment as viewed from the modified midanterior portal.

Labral Repair

Labral repair begins with the strategic placement of anchors, over the previously acetabular trimmed areas, under arthroscopic and fluoroscopic guidance, starting at the most posterior aspect of the tear, typically within the 11- to 12-o’clock position in a right hip (1- to 2-o’clock in a left hip), while viewing from the mMAP (Fig 8). A drill guide is positioned 3 to 5 mm superior from the area of labral tearing and on top of the previously trimmed acetabulum, and its trajectory away from the articular cartilage confirmed with fluoroscopy before drilling to ensure proximity to the rim without cartilage infringement. In addition, arthroscopic visualization of the underlying articular cartilage must be sought while drilling to ensure lack of cartilage mobilization which would indicate cartilage penetration. The first anchor (mini Q-FIX; Smith & Nephew) is then deployed, and sutures are passed through the labrum using a suture passer (ACCU-PASS DIRECT; Smith & Nephew). Using a small suture passer (<2 mm) while piercing through the base/chondrolabral junction of the labrum allows for an atraumatic passage of the sutures. A knot pusher is used to secure the repair at the desired tension. Attention then shifts to the 1- to 3-o’clock position in a right hip (9- to 11-o’clock in a left hip) by switching the camera to the AL portal and creating the distal anterolateral accessory portal at the previously marked location. Using fluoroscopy to confirm its trajectory, an additional anchor is deployed at the 1-o’clock position in a right hip (11-o’clock in a left hip) via the distal anterolateral accessory portal. Care should be taken to space the anchors evenly to maintain an effective suction seal and avoid overcrowding. After tying and cutting the sutures, a probe is used to inspect the repair and ensure the restoration of the hip's native suction seal (Fig 9). Adequate suction seal must be achieved before progression to assessment of the peripheral compartment structures.

Discussion

The therapeutic benefits of hip arthroscopy for managing FAI are undeniable. However, shortcomings in capsular management, acetabular rim trimming, anchor placement, or the labral repair itself can significantly compromise outcomes.8,10 To mitigate these risks, this Technical Note provides a detailed description of the senior authors’ approach to addressing the central compartment in the context of FAI management. Table 1 provides associated tricks and tips to optimize central compartment hip arthroscopy approach.

The interportal capsulotomy should be limited to 1.5 to 2 cm to minimize the risk of excessive scarring and subsequent microinstability. In addition, performing the T portion of the capsulotomy can reduce the need for a larger interportal capsulotomy, as improved visualization is achieved.11 In the senior authors’ preferred approach, early management of the capsulotomy begins with the placement of traction stitches, facilitating the characterization of the proximal and distal capsular leaflets. Then, after the peripheral compartment stage of the procedure, the interportal capsulotomy is closed for allowance of restoration of the capsule’s native biomechanics.12

When performing acetabular rim trimming or acetabuloplasty, it is crucial for physicians to avoid damaging the chondrolabral junction, as this could compromise the integrity of the subsequent labral repair. In addition, excising bony overgrowth in the subspinal space (inferior and deep to the anterior inferior iliac spine) is essential, as under-resection in this area has been associated with symptom recurrence and the need for revision hip arthroscopy.9 The described technique includes careful debridement and excision of any bony overgrowth in the acetabular subspinal space to address this issue effectively.

Assessing labral status is paramount, as labral tears are found in a significant percentage of patients undergoing arthroscopic surgery for FAI.13 Furthermore, studies have shown that restoring the chondrolabral junction through labral repair or reconstruction restores the suction seal more effectively than either labral debridement or acetabuloplasty alone.10 With the availability of curved guides and flexible anchors, the authors strongly recommend addressing the labrum—whether through repair or reconstruction—to ensure the adequate restoration of the suction seal.

Proper management of the central compartment structures during hip arthroscopy is crucial for the success of subsequent peripheral compartment procedures and capsular closure. In the present section of the series, a detailed technical approach for routine assessment of the central compartment is described, with the goal of optimizing outcomes after arthroscopic surgery for the management of FAI.
