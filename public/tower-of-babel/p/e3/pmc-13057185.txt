A high-fidelity simulator for evaluation of hemodynamic response during cardiopulmonary resuscitation in hypogravity environments
Authors: Zoé Lord, Christian Andrade, Lawrence Leroux, Lyes Kadem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the emergence of long-duration space travel, space exploration missions pose a major concern due to the heightened risk of medical emergencies, such as sudden cardiac arrest. While several cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) methods have been proposed for human spaceflight, their reliability and effectiveness remain uncertain, as these methods lack systematic evaluation through physiological metrics. To address this gap, a high-fidelity CPR simulator was developed to simulate blood circulation and deliver real-time hemodynamic feedback. Herein, we show that in normogravity, the CPR simulator generates compression-decompression waveforms that align with published animal and test bench studies. As an exploratory comparison, we also report relative differences in hemodynamic pressure observed between normogravity and hypogravity conditions. The findings highlight that internal physiological responses are critical for evaluating CPR effectiveness in hypogravity, with the CPR simulator serving as a plausible tool. The current study represents an initial step toward the validation of a gold standard CPR protocol and may contribute to the complex health challenges surrounding long-duration spaceflight.

Introduction

In the advent of modern space travel, the increasing duration and complexity of missions necessitate robust medical support systems to safeguard astronaut safety and mission success. Long-duration spaceflight increases both the likelihood of medical emergencies and the difficulty of delivering care, particularly as space exploration missions (SEMs) to the Moon and Mars introduce unprecedented logistical and technical challenges1. Serious medical events are estimated to occur at an incidence rate of approximately 0.06 per astronaut-year, which would lead to at least one emergency per flight to Mars2. Should a medical emergency occur during an SEM, the best possible treatments and protocols must be available to the crew medical officers (CMO) to assist those in need. Moreover, the constraints of SEMs limit immediate access to definitive medical care facilities, making onboard medical preparedness a major concern3. In missions beyond low Earth orbit, communication delays will prevent real-time telemedicine and require astronauts to manage medical emergencies with total crew autonomy4.

Furthermore, SEMs will expose astronauts to unique environmental stressors, including hypogravity, radiation, isolation, and confinement, which can have profound effects on human physiology1. Prolonged exposure to such austere environments can lead to trauma, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle atrophy, and bone density loss2,4. These physiological alterations may increase cardiovascular risks, including sudden cardiac arrest (SCA)5,6. SCA, characterized by the abrupt loss of effective cardiac output, requires immediate resuscitation to restore cerebral and organ perfusion4. Rapidly achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) is critical to maximize survival and favourable neurological outcomes7. In terrestrial settings, international cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) guidelines emphasize immediate chest compressions and rapid defibrillation to increase the likelihood of ROSC2,8,9. However, adapting these medical procedures in a hypogravity environment poses significant challenges3. The absence of gravity affects the rescuer’s ability to maintain position and perform stabilized chest compressions10. While protocols for non-critical medical conditions in hypogravity are well-established in the literature, medical emergencies requiring resuscitation, intubation, and surgical interventions lack a universally accepted gold standard5,11,12.

Several CPR methods, such as the Handstand, Evetts-Russomano, Standard Size Straddle, Waist Straddling Maneuver, and Reverse Bear Hug, are currently proposed to perform chest compressions in spaceflight conditions13,14. These methods attempt to compensate for the absence of gravitational force, however, each introduces limitations that may compromise survival. For instance, the Handstand method requires advanced life support resources, including the crew medical restraint system (CMRS) and multiple rescuers, making it impractical in time-sensitive medical emergencies8,9. The Evetts-Russomano method follows basic life support protocols with only one rescuer and no CMRS usage, however, the physical constraints of the technique may impair compression depth and reduce CPR quality13,15. The Reverse Bear Hug method, meanwhile, often fails to achieve adequate compression depth or rate, limiting its efficacy13,14. Furthermore, their reliability and effectiveness remain uncertain, as these methods lack validation under naturalistic spaceflight conditions. These limitations are highlighted in a review by Hinkelbein et al.14, which evaluated available CPR methods for spaceflight conditions through compression analyses (i.e., chest compression depth and rate) and emphasized that there is insufficient evidence to support any single method as the gold standard. Adding to these concerns is the physical toll of manual chest compressions in hypogravity, where elevated heart rates, reduced oxygen uptake, fatigue from overuse of the elbow flexion, and rapid performance decay, as well as limitations linked to the rescuer’s weight and height are shown to further reduce CPR quality7,16,17.

Recent studies are shifting their focus toward the use of mechanical devices such as automated chest compression devices (ACCDs) to improve the outcome of SCAs18–21. Various investigations evaluated the performance of these devices on CPR manikins, which assessed parameters such as compression depth, rate, and release. For instance, research by Forti et al.21 demonstrated that ACCDs could maintain consistent compression parameters in simulated hypogravity, suggesting a promising solution for CPR delivery in austere environments. While these studies highlight the potential of ACCDs, they primarily focus on external metrics and provide only indirect assessments of CPR’s physiological outcomes13. Studies have shown that there is a critical need to assess whether chest compressions in hypogravity generate sufficient forward flow to sustain cerebral perfusion, as these parameters are key predictors of ROSC10,13,22. Addressing this gap will enable the ability to evaluate CPR protocols and better estimate the likelihood of achieving ROSC during medical emergencies in SEMs.

To address this gap, we developed the CRISiS (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation Identification Simulator in Space) research payload, a high-fidelity CPR simulator designed to replicate cardiac arrest physiology in hypogravity conditions. This study presents its first application during parabolic flight to assess whether mechanical CPR (mCPR) can generate physiologically meaningful forward flow and arterial pressure under hypogravity conditions.

Specifically, the current study aims to: (1) develop and implement a high-fidelity CPR simulator capable of reproducing physiological compression-decompression waveforms under both normogravity and hypogravity conditions, and (2) validate the simulator’s fidelity by assessing normogravity output with benchmark values reported in existing literature. As an exploratory comparison, we report relative differences in hemodynamic response observed during mCPR between normogravity and hypogravity conditions.

First, we hypothesize that our CPR simulator during mCPR will be able to generate compression-decompression waveforms that contain arterial pressure peaks associated with chest compression, dicrotic notches consistent with aortic valve closure at the end of the compression phase, and positive-pressure decompression troughs that are indicative of effective chest compression23,24. Secondly, we hypothesize that arterial pressures from our CPR simulator will align with physiological hemodynamics observed in chest compressions across animal and test bench studies23,25–27.

Methods

The CPR simulator was designed to model hemodynamic response during mCPR under normogravity and hypogravity conditions. The simulator utilized a standard training CPR manikin (PRESTAN Products, United States) fitted with standard compression springs, which was further modified to contain a 3D-printed heart model and sternum, mechanical heart valves, left common carotid artery model, and a mock circulatory loop (MCL). The MCL included connected tubing, peripheral chambers, and mechanical components to provide real-time hemodynamic feedback during mCPR.

3D-printed heart model and sternum

A CAD heart model of a patient-specific healthy male was modified in a CAD modelling software (Dassault Systèmes, France)27. A 3D-printing software (Ultimaker B.V., Netherlands) was employed to print the CAD heart model on a 3D-printer (Lulzbot Taz 6, United States) (Fig. 1). 3D-printing the fully functional heart was achieved using NinjaFlex filament and required 38 h to print. The NinjaFlex filament, a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) elastomer, was selected for its suitable elasticity and flexibility properties, which allowed the 3D-printed heart model to undergo the mechanical stresses of mCPR28. Post-processing involved removing support materials and sealing with marine-grade silicone sealant to ensure the structural integrity of the 3D-printed heart model in preparation for hemodynamic performance testing.Fig. 13D-printing of anatomical models.a Fully functional heart model using NinjaFlex filament; b Sternum model using PLA filament.

The CAD sternum model was obtained from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) 3D Exchange, which was rendered from anonymized medical imaging data to preserve anatomical accuracy29. The sternum model was 3D-printed at full scale using polylactic acid (PLA) filament due to its rigidity and ability to mimic the mechanical properties of human bone (Fig. 1)30. The 3D-printed sternum was designed to endure repeated compressions at a force consistent with international CPR guidelines8,9,14. This approach enabled both the 3D-printed heart model and sternum to maintain accuracy in simulating compression-decompression waveforms and to withstand continuous use throughout the parabolic flight campaign.

Brachiocephalic vessels

The left common carotid artery (CCA) and left brachiocephalic vein (BCV) were designed as CAD models and subsequently 3D-printed using PLA filament. The 3D-printed left CCA and BCV models served as moulds for building a silicone tubing structure, which contributed to the MCL (Fig. 2). The silicone material provided the flexibility needed to replicate the compliance of a native left CCA and BCV, while also supporting a smooth surface to facilitate the safe insertion of a pressure catheter for data collection without risk of damage. The left CCA tubing consisted of a spherical geometry to depict an accurate anatomical representation of the artery, with a diameter of 8 mm and a length of 130 mm. Moreover, the left BCV tubing consisted of an elliptical geometry, with a diameter of 13 mm and a length of 251 mm. Dimensions of the left CCA and BCV tubing were validated against the literature to maintain anatomical accuracy31–34.Fig. 23D-printed heart model.Fully functional 3D-printed heart model with an open circulatory system.

Mock circulatory loop

The MCL within the CPR simulator was designed to simulate hemodynamic pressure during mCPR in normogravity and hypogravity (Fig. 3)35,36. A 35% (v/v) glycerol-water solution was utilized to approximate the physiological blood flow and viscosity under experimental conditions. Silicone and latex tubing structures were connected to the 3D-printed heart and peripheral chambers within the MCL, which provided flexibility and elasticity akin to human vessels. The peripheral chambers that represented the cerebral, pulmonary, and peripheral circulations played a role in reproducing systemic and regional blood flow dynamics. Each of these chambers were equipped with unidirectional mechanical valves to prevent backflow and ensure that fluid either remained within the chamber or was propelled forward. To simulate organ-specific vascular behavior, each chamber included a compliance balloon housed within a rigid shell. During compression, simulated blood entering the rigid shell displaced the balloon, thereby reproducing compliance changes with increasing intravascular volume. During decompression, the balloon recoiled, generating pressure within the shell and driving blood flow toward the venous side.Fig. 3Configuration of the CPR simulator.a Top view of the CPR simulator to showcase the MCL; b top view of the CPR simulator to illustrate the 3D-printed heart model that is integrated into the chest wall of the modified CPR manikin.

Automated chest compression device

The electric linear actuator (MOONS’ Industries, China) was employed as the ACCD to perform mechanical chest compressions. It was selected due to its dimensional compatibility with the internal size constraints of the protective casing used for parabolic flight. Commercially available ACCDs such as the LUCAS (Stryker, Sweden) and AutoPulse (ZOLL, United States) were also considered20,21, however their specifications exceeded sizing constraints and therefore could not be integrated into the current study. The ACCD provided a 50 mm stroke, with a positioning repeatability of ±0.02 mm and a maximum travel speed of 250 mm/s. The ACCD was operated and monitored through a servo motor control software (Applied Motion Products, United States) to maintain precise control over compression depth, rate, and release. The system was programmed to perform chest compressions at a depth of 5 cm at a rate of 110 compressions per minute (cpm) and could generate a maximum force of 300 N. The compression and release settings were configured to produce symmetric, continuous strokes with no dwell time, allowing the piston to return fully to its baseline position on each compression-decompression cycle and maintain a 50% duty cycle. These parameters aligned with the technical specifications of commercially available ACCDs20,37 and adhered to international CPR guidelines38–40. The ACCD and its servo motor were mounted on a metal support frame, with the ACCD positioned above the CPR simulator (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Experimental setup for parabolic flight.Top view of the experimental setup for parabolic flight, showcasing the ACCD positioned above the CPR simulator.

Parabolic flight

The CPR simulator was tested during the 2023 Canadian Reduced Gravity Experiment Design Challenge (CAN-RGX) parabolic flight campaign aboard the National Research Council’s (NRC) Falcon 20 aircraft in Ottawa, Canada. The study was selected by SEDS-Canada for participation and developed in collaboration with both the NRC and the Canadian Space Agency. The research received full airworthiness approval from the NRC prior to flight operations.

The Falcon 20 aircraft, a commercial jet modified with specialized hydraulic and fuel systems for parabolic maneuvers, operated between altitudes of 4000 and 8000 meters in order to alternate between phases of hypogravity and hypergravity. During steady flight, normogravity remained at 1 G. Parabolas were initiated by gradually raising the aircraft’s pitch to a 45-degree angle, resulting in a hypergravity phase for approximately 20 s with an experienced acceleration of 1.8 G. This was followed by a free-fall trajectory for 15 to 20 s, during which hypogravity conditions (< 0.1 G) were achieved. The recovery phase involved a pull-out at a 1.8 G acceleration, returning the aircraft to a stabilized flight path.

A total of ten parabolas were allocated to the CRISiS research payload during the CAN-RGX parabolic flight campaign, with data collected exclusively during the hypogravity phases of flight. For each parabola, the ACCD was activated immediately prior to the onset of hypogravity and remained operational throughout the phase. Five parabolas involved mCPR at a compression depth of 5 cm, while the remaining five were performed at a depth of 4 cm. The current study focuses on the five parabolas conducted at 5 cm, with the 4 cm dataset reserved for comparative analysis in future work. Hypergravity phases were not recorded, as the CPR simulator was designed to assess relative hemodynamic response during the hypogravity phase of flight most relevant to in-flight resuscitation. Operationally, the ability to provide critical care during re-entry in a medical evacuation is likely to be limited due to the incompatibility of an unstable patient enduring high G-forces, restricted access to the patient’s airway and upper chest imposed by the extravehicular mobility unit (EMU) and gravitational restraints, potential neuro-vestibular impairment of the CMO, and the confined volume of the crew return vehicle which may not accommodate ACCD deployment or allow effective medical intervention2,41.

Data collection

The experimental setup was placed in a hard-shelled, 0350 Protector Cube Case (Pelican Products, United States) with inner dimensions of 50.8 × 50.8 × 46.6 cm. An internal mounting plate was fixed to the base of the Pelican case to anchor the CPR simulator, metal support, and data acquisition equipment (Fig. 4). The Pelican case and mounting plate, both provided by the NRC, served as a protective envelope for containment of potential hazards and as a secure mount to prevent movement during the hypergravity and hypogravity phases of flight.

Hemodynamic readings within the MCL were collected using a high-fidelity pressure transducer (FISO Technologies, Canada). Prior to data collection, the pressure transducer was zeroed (nulled) to atmospheric pressure for each condition. Zeroing was performed on the ground for the normogravity condition at an ambient pressure of 99.72 kPa. For the hypogravity condition zeroing was performed during steady flight prior to the parabolic maneuvers42,43, where the cabin pressure was stable at 99.69 kPa. These readings were transmitted via a catheter that was inserted into the left CCA through the cerebral chamber of the CPR simulator (Fig. 5). Data acquisition was continuous at a sampling rate of 125 Hz and was logged using the Evolution software (FISO Technologies, Canada). This allowed compression-decompression waveforms to be monitored and recorded throughout the study. Compression rate was computed from the reciprocal of the time interval between successive compression peaks identified in the recorded waveforms, yielding an approximate 8 ms temporal resolution per cycle.Fig. 5High-fidelity pressure transducer and catheter inserted into the MCL.This setup allowed for the measurement of arterial pressure waveforms localized in the left CCA near the entrance of the aortic chamber.

Statistical analysis

No statistical power calculation was conducted prior to the study, as the sample size was determined by the number of parabolas available and corresponding mCPR compressions performed. We planned to assess normality using Repeated Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. If the data were normally distributed, comparisons between normogravity (ground-based) and hypogravity (parabolic flight) conditions would be performed using Independent Samples t-tests. If the data were non-normal, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test would be applied. A criterion of p < 0.05 was prespecified for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software version 29.0.2.0 (IBM, United States).

Exploratory comparison of hemodynamic response across gravitational conditions

Repeated Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests indicated that the distribution deviated from normality, therefore Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare relative hemodynamic responses observed between normogravity (ground-based) and hypogravity (parabolic flight) conditions. Under hypogravity, hemodynamic responses were higher across all metrics while the compression rate remained largely unchanged (Table 1). Mann-Whitney U tests indicated statistically higher SP (60.9 ± 0.3 vs. 49.4 ± 0.3 mmHg; z = −15.62, p < 0.001, r = 0.84), DP (26.5 ± 0.1 vs. 19.0 ± 0.1 mmHg; z = −16.05, p < 0.001, r = 0.86), MAP (38.0 ± 0.1 vs. 29.2 ± 0.1 mmHg; z = −15.99, p < 0.001, r = 0.86), and PP (34.4 ± 0.2 vs. 30.4 ± 0.2 mmHg; z = −12.62, p < 0.001, r = 0.68). A Mann-Whitney U test also found a statistically lower compression rate under hypogravity, though the effect size was small (106.6 ± 0.1 cpm vs. 107.0 ± 0.1 cpm; z = −3.22, p < 0.001, r = 0.17).

Discussion

The current study aimed to develop a high-fidelity simulator designed to reproduce CPR hemodynamics following cardiac arrest under normogravity conditions and to allow systematic investigation of relative hemodynamic responses in hypogravity.

The primary objective was to show the CPR simulator’s capability to generate a compression–decompression waveform that is characteristic of effective CPR. Key waveform landmarks were identified in both the compression and decompression phases during mCPR. In the compression phase, a rapid upstroke was observed with a sudden rise in arterial pressure, reaching a compression peak of 49.4 ± 0.3 mmHg in normogravity and 60.9 ± 0.3 mmHg in hypogravity. This profile reflected the forward arterial surge produced by a chest compression24,27. The waveform displayed a shoulder plateau followed by a dicrotic notch, corresponding to aortic valve closure and marking the end of the compression phase. The absence of backflow in the waveform was further compatible with the unidirectional valves used in the MCL36. The onset of the decompression phase was evidenced by a smooth exponential-like decay in arterial pressure, reaching a distinct decompression trough of 19.0 ± 0.1 mmHg in normogravity and 26.5 ± 0.1 mmHg in hypogravity. These pronounced troughs corresponded to the lowest arterial pressure during the decompression phase which also featured positive-pressures as a characteristic of effective chest compression. The subsequent increase in pressure suggested completion of the cycle and the onset of the next compression. These findings support the hypothesis that the arterial pressure waveforms recorded from the CPR simulator, under both normogravity and hypogravity conditions, resemble those observed during chest compressions in humans23,24.

The second research question explored the CPR simulator’s fidelity by assessing whether the normogravity hemodynamic outputs replicated trends described in the literature. Previous animal studies highlight a wide variability in compression peaks and decompression troughs observed during mCPR at comparable settings (100 cpm, 5 cm depth). Reported compression pressures spanned 40–120 mmHg, while decompression troughs ranged between 7–64 mmHg23,25,26. Moreover, an in vitro test bench study by Khoury et al. observed a compression peak of 58 mmHg27. This variability across studies is expected given differences in simulator designs, forces applied by mCPR, vascular models, fluidic properties, and experimental configurations. These reference points provide important benchmarks for assessing the physiological realism of CPR simulation tools. In the current study, our CPR simulator produced a compression peak of 49.4 ± 0.3 mmHg and a decompression trough of 19.0 ± 0.1 mmHg under normogravity, both of which fall within these reported ranges. Although slightly lower than the compression peak values obtained by Khoury et al., our findings remain consistent with the lower bound of the physiological envelope documented in the literature27. Therefore, we believe our CPR simulator can be considered as a plausible tool for assessing hemodynamic response not only for ground-based applications but also as a useful platform to study hypogravity exposure, where such physiological benchmarks are largely absent2.

As an exploratory comparison, the current study observed statistically higher hemodynamic pressure during mCPR in hypogravity relative to normogravity while the compression rate remained stable across both conditions. Specifically, SP, DP, MAP, and PP increased in hypogravity by 23.2%, 39.3%, 30.3%, and 13.0% respectively. While the findings highlight the relative differences in arterial pressure between conditions, their absolute values should be interpreted with caution. Hypogravity maneuvers of less than 20 s with rapid gravitational force transitions simulate a highly dynamic setting, impeding the ability to reach a sustained steady-state environment. As cabin pressure was reported in prior work to transiently drop during parabolic maneuvers, this effect was further evaluated as a potential source of bias44. Accounting for the varying cabin pressure42,43 produced a small upward shift in SP, DP, and MAP of 3.4 ± 0.6 mmHg with no change in PP. In addition, the atmospheric pressures used as a reference when zeroing the pressure transducer differed slightly between conditions by 0.23 mmHg. Together, the dynamic cabin pressures and atmospheric reference pressures are unlikely to explain the relative between-condition differences in hemodynamic response. While the observations suggest potential hemodynamic pressure variations in hypogravity, further research is necessary to understand the full physiological implications and to inform the optimization of CPR protocols accordingly.

The findings of this study may have implications for efforts in establishing a gold standard CPR protocol for human spaceflight. Prior research indicates that CPR protocols currently proposed to address SCA may not be effective during a medical emergency in SEMs2,10,13. The current study highlights the importance of considering internal physiological responses, such as arterial pressure, in assessing CPR effectiveness in space. Current metrics to evaluate CPR performance focus on external parameters, such as compression kinematics, and may not fully capture the resuscitation efficacy in achieving ROSC13. Our CPR simulator provides a valuable tool for evaluating CPR methods under varying gravitational conditions, supporting the refinement of CPR techniques based on internal physiological metrics. Moreover, the use of ACCDs for spaceflight applications may offer advantages over manual CPR7,19,20. These devices can provide consistently reliable compressions and be programmed for reduced-gravity considerations21,41. However, further research is needed to determine the optimal settings and design features for such devices in hypogravity environments.

As a limitation of the current study, the mCPR applied was performed using an ACCD, which may not fully reproduce physiological outcomes similar to those of manual CPR performed by a human25. While the arterial pressure waveforms resembled physiological traces, the measurements for the compliance of vessels, chambers, and 3D-printed heart were approximated as a result of the mechanical limitations of the CPR simulator and may not precisely reproduce in vivo cardiovascular mechanics during cardiac arrest. Moreover, the chest-wall mechanics and thoracic compliance in the CPR simulator did not account for the anatomical changes to the thorax that may occur in weightlessness45–49, potentially affecting the compression kinematics and resulting compression-decompression waveforms in the current study. In the next phase of our work, we plan to integrate a high-fidelity thoracic cage into the CPR simulator and include volumetric flow rate of the left CCA as a direct measure of whether mCPR can generate meaningful forward flow under hypogravity conditions. Although NinjaFlex TPU elastomers have shown to withstand a significant number of compression cycles with good fatigue resistance both in the current study and in existing literature28, future studies are required to quantitatively evaluate the fatigue behaviour of 3D-printed models during mCPR. Factors such as rescuer fatigue, manual force application, and ergonomic challenges in hypogravity were not accounted for7,16,17. As the compression rate was derived from pressure readings with an 8 ms temporal resolution, discrete between-condition differences of 2 ms per cycle may reflect sampling quantization rather than true variability. Additionally, the brief duration of weightlessness and number of parabolas in the parabolic flight campaign limited the generalizability of the findings in SEMs. Future studies should consider extended hypogravity exposure, such as experiments conducted on the International Space Station or commercial spacecraft, to validate these results over longer periods. Conducting swine model studies under hypogravity could also offer additional validation of the CPR simulator and further insight into the biological factors affecting CPR in space50.

In summary, this work presented a novel CPR simulator capable of operating in both normogravity and hypogravity. Our CPR simulator reproduced compression–decompression waveforms and produced normogravity pressure profiles consistent with the literature. The hemodynamic pressure during mCPR in hypogravity relative to normogravity were also observed. These findings emphasized the need to use physiological metrics, in combination with compression kinematics, to assess CPR effectiveness when evaluating candidate CPR methods for SEMs. Our CPR simulator may serve as a useful tool for assessing and refining CPR methods under hypogravity conditions. Thus, the current study represents an initial step toward the validation of a gold standard CPR protocol and may contribute to enhanced medical preparedness for future SEMs to the Moon and Mars.
