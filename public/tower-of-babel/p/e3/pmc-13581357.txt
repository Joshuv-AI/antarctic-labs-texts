The impact of conversion arthroplasty for failed femoral neck or intertrochanteric fractures on complication and mortality rates: A “second-hit” effect?
Authors: Jui-Chien Wang, Kun-Han Lee, Yueh-Ting Hung, Wei-Lin Chang, Shang-Wen Tsai, Cheng-Fong Chen, Po-Kuei Wu, Wei-Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Data on the risk of morbidity and mortality following conversion arthroplasty for fixation failure of femoral neck fractures (FNFs) or intertrochanteric fractures (ITFs) remains limited. This study compares medical outcomes between: (1) initial fixation and conversion arthroplasty for failed FNFs and ITFs, and (2) conversion arthroplasty for failed FNFs vs failed ITFs.Methods:A retrospective review of an institutional database at Taipei Veterans General Hospital identified patients who underwent conversion arthroplasty for failed internal fixation of FNFs or ITFs from January 2010 to December 2022. Primary outcomes included frequencies of in-hospital complications, 90-day readmissions following initial fixation and conversion arthroplasty, and 90-day and 1-year mortality after conversion arthroplasty. To compare outcomes for conversion arthroplasty between failed FNFs and ITFs, cohorts were matched by age, body mass index, and age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index.Results:A total of 70 patients with failed FNFs and 66 patients with failed ITFs were included. The in-hospital complication frequency was higher following conversion arthroplasty (FNF: 44.3% vs 17.1%, p < 0.001; ITF: 54.5% vs 31.8%, p = 0.008) compared with initial fixation. Although conversion arthroplasty for failed ITFs involved a longer procedure time (125.5 ± 46.2 vs 86.8 ± 31.1 minutes, p < 0.001), greater estimated blood loss (1088.1 ± 603.8 vs 513.1 ± 287.7 mL, p < 0.001), and higher transfusion rate (81.0% vs 31.7%, p < 0.001) and volume (2.6 ± 2.0 vs 0.8 ± 1.2 units, p < 0.001) compared with failed FNFs, the in-hospital complication, readmission, and mortality rates were similar between the two groups.Conclusion:Conversion arthroplasty for failed FNFs and ITFs was associated with worse medical outcomes, including a higher rate of in-hospital complications, compared with initial fixation. Despite greater surgical complexity associated with conversion arthroplasty for failed ITFs compared with failed FNFs, the in-hospital complication, readmission, and mortality rates were similar.

1. INTRODUCTION

As the global population ages, the incidence of hip fractures continues to rise. The estimated total annual number of hip fractures is expected to nearly double from 2018 to 2050, reaching an estimated 4.5 million cases worldwide by the year 2050.1,2 Non-displaced femoral neck fractures (FNF) and intertrochanteric fractures (ITF) are most commonly treated with fracture reduction and internal fixation as the index procedure.3–5 The incidence of fixation failure of hip fractures varies among different fracture types, ranging from 5% to 30%, while the burden of conversion arthroplasty on patients, surgeons, and the healthcare system due to fixation failure of hip fractures continues to increase.3,5–9

The complexity of the conversion hip arthroplasty procedure for fixation failure of FNF and ITF varied, resulting in different outcomes, including operation time, blood loss, and surgical complications.10–14 Compared with the primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) procedure, conversion arthroplasty for fixation failure of ITF is associated with a longer operation time, increased blood loss, an extended length of stay, and higher complication and readmission rates, more closely resembling a revision THA procedure.10 Conversion arthroplasty for failed FNF exhibited similar rates of complications, revisions, and implant survival, compared with primary THA.12 While many studies have addressed the surgical complexity and outcomes of conversion arthroplasty for failed FNF and ITF,6,7,10–14 there is limited evidence regarding the impact of these conversion procedures as a “second hit” to patients compared with the initial fracture fixation procedure. Magnuson et al15 conducted the only study that compared patients who underwent failed hip fracture surgery to a cohort matched by age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and fracture type that received a non-THA procedure. The conversion group exhibited higher 90-day readmission and medical complication rates, while the mortality rate was similar at postoperative 6, 12, and 24 months.15 However, this study analyzed patients who underwent failed hip fracture surgery as an entire cohort, including a mixture of FNFs and ITFs treated with fracture fixation or hemiarthroplasty. Further subgroup analysis by each fracture type (FNF, ITF) should be performed to validate potential differences in the risk of morbidity and mortality between the initial fracture fixation and the subsequent conversion arthroplasty procedure.

To address these gaps, this study has two primary aims. First, to compare outcomes following initial fixation and subsequent conversion arthroplasty for FNF or ITF within the same patient. Second, to compare outcomes following conversion arthroplasty between FNF and ITF patients, after employing appropriate matching techniques for these two cohorts. We hypothesize that (1) patients undergoing conversion arthroplasty procedures for failed fixation of ITF will exhibit a higher readmission rate compared with those undergoing the initial fixation procedure, but the rate will remain similar in the FNF cohort, and (2) conversion arthroplasty for failed fixation of ITF will be associated with worse outcomes compared with those for FNF, regarding readmission and mortality rates.

2. METHODS

2.1. Data collection and patient demographics

We conducted this retrospective study at Taipei Veterans General Hospital, a tertiary referral hospital in Taipei, Taiwan. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan (2024-07-004BC), with informed consent waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Between January 2010 and December 2022, we identified patients who underwent both an initial fixation procedure for FNF or ITF, and subsequently required conversion hip arthroplasty due to failed fixation of FNF or ITF, at our hospital. We identified eligible patients for analysis from our database using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance procedure codes. First, we screened for patients who underwent fixation for FNF (PCS-64029B) or ITF (PCS-64029B). Second, among these patients, we identified those who underwent a second procedure involving hardware removal from the proximal femur (PCS-64245C), combined with either hemiarthroplasty (PCS-64169B) or total hip arthroplasty (PCS-64162B). For patients aged 60 or older with a displaced FNF, the standard treatment is hemiarthroplasty. ITFs are treated with fracture fixation using cephalomedullary nails or dynamic hip screws. None of the patients with ITFs undergo hip arthroplasty as the initial procedure. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) underwent only the hardware removal for failed fixation of femoral neck or ITF; (2) presence of infection; 3) lost to follow-up within 90 days postoperatively following either the initial or conversion procedure (Fig. 1).

During the study period, 100 patients underwent fracture fixation for FNF and 81 patients for ITF. After applying the exclusion criteria, 70 patients were included in the FNF group and 66 patients in the ITF group. The mean age, height, weight, and BMI were 81.5 ± 8.3 years, 155.4 ± 8.5 cm, 55.7 ± 10.3 kg, and 23.0 ± 3.5, respectively. A majority of the cohort were women (N = 91, 66.9%). Most patients had an age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score of 4 (N = 35, 25.7%), 5 (N = 40, 29.4%), and 6 or above (N = 47, 34.6%). Demographic data were similar between the initial fixation and conversion procedures for patients with femoral neck or ITF (Table 1).

2.2. Surgical procedure and postoperative follow-up

The surgical procedures were performed under either spinal or general anesthesia, using a transgluteal or posterolateral approach with the patient in a lateral decubitus position.16 After accessing the hip joint, the femoral head was typically dislocated first before removing fixation devices, except in cases of significant screw or lag screw cut-out. The decision to perform hemiarthroplasty or THA depended on whether the acetabulum was significantly damaged by the protruding hardware. Regarding the choice of femoral stem, a cementless primary stem was used in every hip arthroplasty procedure for failed fixation of FNFs. For failed fixation of ITFs, a cementless primary stem, cementless revision stem, or cemented stem was chosen based on the condition of metaphyseal bone stock.3,5 Greater trochanter fragments along with the attached abductor muscles were reduced and fixed using cerclage wires or nonabsorbable sutures. A hemovac drain was inserted before wound closure.

After the procedure, an extended prophylactic intravenous antibiotic therapy was administered until discharge. Outpatient follow-up visits were scheduled at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, and every 3 months thereafter.

2.3. Outcome domains

The primary outcome domains of this study were (1) the in-hospital complication rate, (2) the 90-day readmission rate and reasons for readmissions following each procedure, and (3) the 90-day and 1-year mortality rate following conversion arthroplasty. Common in-hospital complications included hypovolemic shock, sepsis, cerebrovascular disease, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, pneumonia, surgical site complication, urinary tract infection, delirium, and acute urinary retention. Reasons for 90-day readmission included both surgical and medication complications. Common surgical complications comprised surgical site infection, periprosthetic joint infection, hardware failure following the initial procedure, dislocation, and wound pain. Common medical complications included coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, acute kidney injury, pneumonia, obstructive jaundice, acute pyelonephritis, urinary tract infection, and delirium. Furthermore, to define fracture collapse, the sliding distance of fracture site through hip screw or blade was measured, and a collapse of more than 5 mm was defined as collapse of fracture site.17 Implant cut-out was characterized as the collapse of the neck-shaft angle into varus, leading to extrusion of the screw from the femoral head.18 Nonunion was established using the FDA standard definition as a fracture persisting for a minimum of nine months without signs of healing for three consecutive months, confirmed by the absence of trabecular bridging across the fracture site on serial radiographs.19

The secondary outcome domains included (1) procedure time, (2) estimated blood loss, (3) transfusions, and (4) length of stay.

2.4. Statistical analysis

To mitigate potential confounding bias in this observational study comparing patients undergoing conversion THA for failed fixation of FNF or ITF, propensity score matching was applied to rigorously adjust for differences in measured baseline characteristics between the two groups, simulating the attributes of a randomized controlled trial within an observational study design.20,21 Propensity scores were estimated using multiple logistic regression analysis with confounding covariables (age, BMI, and age-adjusted CCI). After estimating propensity scores, a 1:1 matched cohort was conducted with the restriction of a caliper width equal to 0.2 of the SD of the logistic of the propensity score. One-to-one matched pairs were formed using nearest neighbor matching,22 minimizing the difference between the propensity scores of the paired patients.

For comparison between index fixation and conversion arthroplasty, and statistical difference between match FNF and ITF cohorts after propensity score matching, continuous data are described as mean ± SD and were compared using the paired t test. Categorical variables were compared using McNemar’s test. All reported p values were two-sided, with a p value <0.05 used to determine statistical significance. All hips were assumed to be independent in the statistical analysis. SPSS version 15.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) was used for all statistical analyses.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Details regarding initial and conversion arthroplasty procedures

The median interval between initial fixation and conversion arthroplasty was 5.5 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 2-15.3) in the FNF group and 3 months (IQR: 2-10.5) in the ITF group. For FNF fixation, multiple cannulated screws were used in all cases (N = 70, 100%), whereas for ITF fixation, cephalomedullary nails (N = 37, 56.1%) or dynamic hip screws (N = 27, 40.9%) were frequently used. Common reasons for initial fixation failure included fracture collapse or nonunion, implant cut-out, osteonecrosis, and osteoarthritis. The proportion of THA or hemiarthroplasty performed during the conversion arthroplasty procedure did not differ between the FNF and ITF groups. However, cementless revision stems were more frequently used in the hip arthroplasty procedures for failed ITF fixation (Table 2).

3.2. Outcome comparison between initial and conversion arthroplasty procedures in FNF patients

The overall in-hospital complication rate was higher (44.3% vs 17.1%, p < 0.001) following conversion arthroplasty compared with initial fixation. Additionally, the 90-day readmission rate (35.7% vs 8.6%, p < 0.001) was higher following initial fixation compared with conversion arthroplasty. The higher 90-day readmission rate was primarily due to surgical complications (32.9% vs 4.3%, p < 0.001). However, the 90-day readmission rate for medical complications did not differ between initial fixation and conversion arthroplasty. The 90-day and 1-year mortality rates following conversion arthroplasty was 5.7% and 7.1%, respectively (Table 3).

3.3. Comparison between initial and conversion arthroplasty procedures in ITF patients

The overall in-hospital complication rate (54.5% vs 31.8%, p = 0.008) was higher following conversion arthroplasty compared with initial fixation. Additionally, the 90-day readmission rate (50.0% vs 12.1%, p < 0.001) was higher following initial fixation compared with conversion arthroplasty. The higher 90-day readmission rate was primarily due to surgical complications (45.5% vs 4.5%, p < 0.001). However, the 90-day readmission rate for medical complications did not differ between initial fixation and conversion arthroplasty. The 90-day and 1-year mortality rates following the conversion arthroplasty procedure were 3.0% and 9.1%, respectively (Table 3).

Conversion arthroplasty was associated with a longer procedure time (125.2 ± 45.4 vs 90.8 ± 36.4 minutes, p < 0.001), increased estimated blood loss (1088.1 ± 603.8 vs 736.9 ± 504.7 mL, p = 0.004), higher transfusion rate (81.8% vs 34.8%, p < 0.001), and longer length of stay (10.6 ± 9.5 vs 7.1 ± 3.2, days, p = 0.007) compared with initial fixation (Table 3).

3.4. Outcome comparison following conversion arthroplasty in FNF vs ITF patients

Propensity score matching was performed using the variables including age, BMI, and age-adjusted CCI. In the matched cohort, patients who underwent conversion arthroplasty for failed fixation of ITF had a longer procedure time (125.5 ± 46.2 vs 86.8 ± 31.1 minutes, p < 0.001), increased estimated blood loss (1088.1 ± 603.8 vs 513.1 ± 287.7 mL, p < 0.001), and higher transfusion rate (81.0% vs 31.7%, p < 0.001). However, there were no differences in rates of in-hospital complications, 90-day readmission, 90-day or 1-year mortality, and length of stay between the two matched cohorts (Table 4).

4. DISCUSSION

The main findings of this study are as follows: (1) Conversion arthroplasty was associated with a higher rate of in-hospital complications compared with initial fixation (FNF cohort: 44.3% vs 17.1%, p < 0.001; ITF cohort: 54.5% vs 31.8%, p = 0.008); (2) Conversion arthroplasty for failed ITF was more surgically complex than for failed FNF, as shown by a longer procedure time (125.5 ± 46.2 vs 86.8 ± 31.1 minutes, p < 0.001), greater estimated blood loss (1088.1 ± 603.8 vs 513.1 ± 287.7 mL, p < 0.001), higher transfusion rate (81.0% vs 31.7%, p < 0.001), and increased transfusion volume (2.6 ± 2.0 vs 0.8 ± 1.2 units, p < 0.001). Despite these differences in surgical complexity, medical outcomes were similar between the matched cohorts with failed FNF and ITF, including in-hospital complication rates (46.0% vs 52.4%, p = 0.476), 90-day readmission rates (6.3% vs 12.7%, p = 0.225), 90-day mortality (4.8% vs 3.2%, p = 0.648), and 1-year mortality (6.3% vs 6.3%, p = 1.000).

Surgical complications following conversion arthroplasty for fixation failure of hip fractures were more frequently observed than those of primary THA, with higher overall reoperation rates as well as higher rates of deep infection and dislocation.6,23–25 The increased surgical complication rates may be attributed to the greater surgical complexity of the conversion procedure, along with the frailty of the patient population—characterized by osteoporotic bone, delayed or nonunion at the fracture site, distorted proximal femoral anatomy, and poor quality of the surrounding soft tissue envelope.6,24–28 Moreover, prolonged immobilization following the failed initial fixation and subsequent conversion procedure may further worsen pre-existing osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and functional status in terms of activities of daily living.26,27 In this study, conversion arthroplasty for failed FNFs and ITFs was associated with a longer procedure time, greater estimated blood loss, and higher transfusion rates and volumes, indicating increased surgical complexity compared with the initial fixation procedures. However, the effects of conversion arthroplasty on medical outcomes, such as in-hospital complications, readmissions due to medical complications, and mortality, have been less thoroughly investigated. The only study to compare medical outcomes following reoperation or conversion arthroplasty for failed hip fracture was conducted by Magnuson et al,15 who observed a “second-hit” effect, characterized by higher rates of readmission and medical complications. However, the authors included a heterogeneous population of FNFs and ITFs, treated with either fracture fixation or hemiarthroplasty, along with various types of reoperation procedures. Additionally, they compared outcomes between patients who underwent reoperations and a matched group who received fracture fixation for hip fractures.15 In contrast, our study compared outcomes within the same patients who underwent both the initial fracture fixation and the subsequent conversion arthroplasty at the same institution, providing a more robust control for evaluating the “second-hit” effect. We observed higher rates of in-hospital complications (FNF: 44.3% vs 17.1%, p < 0.001; ITF: 54.5% vs 31.8%, p = 0.008) and longer lengths of stay (FNF: 7.6 ± 3.4 vs 5.2 ± 2.0 days, p < 0.001; ITF: 10.6 ± 9.5 vs 7.1 ± 3.2 days, p = 0.007) following conversion arthroplasty compared with the initial fixation procedure. This “second-hit” effect might be attributed to an early systemic inflammatory response following major surgery, leading to the release of proinflammatory cytokines, microcirculatory disturbances, and cell-mediated immune dysfunction. The subsequent compensatory anti-inflammatory response may increase a patient’s susceptibility to infections, organ dysfunction, or even mortality.29 This phenomenon was also observed in a meta-analysis investigating the optimal interval between staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty procedures, where an interval of more than 6 weeks was associated with a lower risk of mortality as well as fewer pulmonary and infectious complications.30

The surgical complexity of conversion arthroplasty may differ between failed FNFs and ITFs. Compared with failed FNFs, failed ITFs present additional challenges during conversion arthroplasty including distorted proximal femoral anatomy, an incompetent abductor mechanism, deficient metaphyseal bone stock, and stress risers from implant removal. Consequently, an extensively coated, cementless revision stem is more commonly used in conversion procedures for failed ITFs,3,13 as observed in the present study. In general, conversion for failed FNFs more closely resembles a primary THA, whereas conversion for failed ITFs is considered more comparable to a revision procedure.3–5,9,10,13,25,26 In this study, the difference in surgical complexity between the cohorts was reflected in the significantly higher proportion of patients receiving general anesthesia (68.3% vs 27.0%, p < 0.001), a longer procedure time (125.5 ± 46.2 vs 86.8 ± 31.1 minutes, p < 0.001), greater estimated blood loss (1088.1 ± 603.8 vs 513.1 ± 287.7 mL, p < 0.001), and higher transfusion rates (81.0% vs 31.7%, p < 0.001) and volumes (2.6 ± 2.0 vs 0.8 ± 1.2 units, p < 0.001) in the ITF cohort compared with the FNF cohort. Nevertheless, these two heterogeneous groups are often analyzed together under the general category of hip fractures, and the medical outcomes following conversion hip arthroplasty are rarely compared or specifically validated between them. Interestingly, outcome domains—including in-hospital complication rates (52.4% vs 46.0%), length of stay (9.8 ± 8.7 vs 7.8 ± 3.6 days), 90-day readmission rates (12.7% vs 6.3%), 90-day mortality (3.2% vs 4.8%), and 1-year mortality rates (6.3% vs 6.3%)—were not different between the matched ITF and FNF cohorts. These findings suggest that the physiological stress associated with undergoing repeated surgery may have a more substantial influence on medical outcomes in this elderly, frail population with osteoporotic fractures, while the initial fracture type and the complexity of the conversion procedure might play a less significant role.

We should acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, our inclusion criteria involved patients who underwent both the initial fixation and subsequent conversion procedure for hip fractures at our hospital. While this study design offers strength in validating outcomes following both procedures on the same patient, only descriptive statistics, and not inferential statistics, could be reported for mortality rates following the conversion procedures. Additionally, to validate the impacts of conversion arthroplasty, the ideal cohort for comparison would be patients who had failed fixation for a hip fracture but did not undergo a subsequent procedure. However, this is not feasible. Second, our sample size was small due to the low incidence of fixation failure in FNF or ITF. Nonetheless, this study is still one of the largest series to date to validate readmission rates, medical and surgical complications, and mortality among cohorts with failed ITF and FNF. Third, this was a retrospective study, inherently subject to heterogeneity and potential confounders that could influence outcomes. Moreover, the retrospective design precluded systematic collection of standardized functional assessments such as the Harris Hip Score. These confounders include having procedures performed by multiple surgeons with different surgical approaches and techniques, as well as variations in perioperative care protocols.

In conclusion, conversion arthroplasty for failed FNFs and ITFs was associated with worse medical outcomes, including a higher rate of in-hospital complications, compared with initial fixation. Despite the greater surgical complexity of conversion arthroplasty for failed ITFs compared with failed FNFs, the rates of in-hospital complications, readmission, and mortality were similar between the two groups.
