RPL29 as a radiotherapy-sensitive prognostic biomarker in multiple myeloma
Authors: Yihua Wang, Qi Jia, Hongxiu Liu, Zhihua Li, Yanping Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) is a major hematologic malignancy with limited therapeutic progress in radiotherapy. Although radiotherapy is widely used for palliation, its molecular effects in MM remain poorly defined. This study identifies RPL29 as a novel radiotherapy-responsive gene in MM and evaluates its prognostic and therapeutic relevance.MethodsWe employed a multidisciplinary approach, including murine models, radiotherapy schedules, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and bioinformatics. Clinical samples from MM patients (age, sex, and ethnicity recorded) were included for validation. Statistical analysis aimed to identify differentially expressed genes and assess their prognostic significance. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted on irradiated and control groups. Venn diagram analysis and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction were used to identify key regulatory genes.ResultsRNA-seq analysis revealed 424 upregulated and 33 downregulated genes in the 2 Gy local tumor radiotherapy group compared to control. The tumor suppressor gene ADIPOQ emerged as a hub gene in the PPI network. Among the validated downregulated genes was UPK3A, while RPL29, not RPL19, was found to be consistently downregulated in the 2 Gy group. This finding was replicated in MM cell lines and patient samples, supporting its role in disease progression. Distinct radiotherapy doses and delivery methods induced specific biological processes and KEGG pathways, underscoring dose-dependent transcriptional effects.ConclusionsRPL29 downregulation following 2 Gy radiotherapy correlates with improved prognosis in MM, highlighting its potential as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker. These findings provide new insights into MM biology and establish a foundation for targeted therapeutic strategies involving RPL29 and related pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04038-z.Keywords: Multiple myeloma, Radiotherapy, RPL29, Biomarkers, Gene expression, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a commonly occurring blood cancer caused by the unregulated expansion of plasma cells in the bone marrow. In recent years, the global incidence of MM has been steadily increasing, with the current estimate in the United States being around 6.1 cases per 100,000 every year. There are significant improvements in treatment options available for patients with MM, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy; however, the prognosis remains unsatisfactory, especially in untreated patients with relapsed or refractory MM exposed to radiotherapy. Although radiotherapy remains a key treatment modality, its long-term efficacy is hampered by resistance, and there is still limited radiotherapy-specific research in MM compared with other therapeutic strategies. One of the major clinical challenges with radiotherapy is the development of resistance, which makes the treatment ineffective in the long term [1–3]. Importantly, while several studies have assessed tumor responses to radiation in general, a major knowledge gap exists regarding how different doses and delivery methods specifically influence resistance and relapse in MM. [4–6] This lack of radiotherapy-focused understanding, especially at the molecular and genetic level, underscores the urgent need for studies identifying gene expression changes that drive treatment outcomes. Addressing these issues is crucial to understanding how to better tailor therapeutic interventions to improve patient outcomes in this aggressive form of cancer.

Radiotherapy approaches differ in their biological impacts, strengths, and weaknesses. High-dose local radiotherapy (HDRT) directly destroys DNA within cancer cells, helping maintain control of localized tumor lesions and lower recurrence rates; however, this treatment does not improve survival rates [7, 8]. Total body irradiation (TBI), on the other hand, suppresses immune responses while destroying the majority of tumor cells, reducing the likelihood of recurrence, but greatly increasing systemic toxicity [9]. Low-dose local radiotherapy (LDRT) shifts the balance of the tumor immune microenvironment in the favorable direction through the increased immune cell infiltrate, macrophage M1 polarization, and activated T-cells. LDRT’s ability to enhance immune cell destruction of tumor cells and improve tumor blood vessel formation illustrates why it can be beneficial in treatment combinations [10]. Given these distinct mechanisms, the selection of an optimal radiotherapy strategy must be tailored to individual patient conditions and tumor characteristics.

Radiotherapy not only exerts direct cytotoxic effects on MM cells but also significantly reshapes the bone marrow immune microenvironment. Previous studies suggest that its impacts key immune cell populations, including T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and dendritic cells, potentially leading to immune suppression by depleting stem cell memory T lymphocytes and promoting an immunosuppressive milieu that could fosters MM progression and drug resistance. Furthermore, radiotherapy has been shown to reduce granulocyte counts, alter immune cell marker expression, and shift the cytotoxic phenotypes of NK and CD8 + T cells, further compromising antitumor immunity [11, 12]. At the genetic level, radiation can induce novel clonal evolution in plasma cells, driving aberrant proliferation while impairing apoptotic pathways. Interestingly, despite MM progression, the proportion and gene expression profile of GZMB + CD8 + T cells remain relatively stable, suggesting that radiotherapy-induced immunomodulation may play a pivotal role in disease dynamics [13, 14]. Moreover, radiation exposure may influence MM-associated chromosomal translocations and oncogene activation, further impacting patient prognosis. These multifaceted effects of radiotherapy, ranging from immune microenvironment alterations to genetic reprogramming, are likely influenced by factors such as radiation dose, fractionation scheme, treatment timing, and interpatient variability. Thus, the lack of radiotherapy-specific mechanistic insights continues to limit progress in overcoming resistance and optimizing outcomes in MM.

Today’s research has pointed out the role of some genes and signaling pathways in modifying reactions of MM cells to radiation. Among these, RPL29 (ribosomal protein L29) has drawn attention because of its involvement in proliferation, cellular survival, and tumor progression, yet its role in MM, particularly in relation to radiotherapy, remains poorly defined. This rationale underpins our focus on RPL29 as a candidate gene for understanding radiation sensitivity in MM. Exploring how RPL29 modulates radiation sensitivity may enhance the understanding of MM and help discover new clinical prognostic biomarkers.

RPL29 is a ribosomal protein part of the nucleolar pre-ribosomal particles and is essential for ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis. RPL29 also actively participates in cell apoptosis. RPL29 being a ribosomal protein means RPL29 is one of the markers framing the pathological events of cancer. Overexpression of RPL29 is reported in several cancers and there is strong evidence that it enhances the proliferative and invasive potential of tumor cells. Notably, knockdown of RPL29 has been shown to induce tumor cell apoptosis and inhibit both their proliferation and invasiveness [15, 16]. Given the importance of abnormal ribosome biogenesis in cancer development, targeting this process has emerged as a promising strategy for novel therapeutic interventions. Recent studies have explored the potential of drugs targeting ribosome biogenesis in cancer therapy [17, 18].

Interestingly, the downregulation of RPL29 following low-dose localized radiotherapy suggests its involvement in the cellular response to radiation-induced stress. However, the precise mechanisms by which RPL29 influences multiple myeloma (MM) cell behavior in response to radiotherapy remain unclear. Current evidence suggests that alterations in RPL29 expression may affect the tumor microenvironment and immune cell interactions, ultimately influencing treatment outcomes. Despite these insights, a significant gap persists in understanding the comprehensive role of RPL29 in MM, particularly regarding its potential as a therapeutic target or prognostic biomarker. Further investigations are essential to elucidate the functional implications of RPL29 modulation in MM and its interactions with key signaling pathways involved in disease pathophysiology. Furthermore, acquired resistance to radiotherapy in MM often involves clonal evolution, dysregulated DNA damage response, and adaptive changes in the bone marrow niche. Understanding how key genes such as RPL29 participate in these processes could provide critical insights into overcoming therapeutic resistance.

The purpose of this work is to examine how radiation affects MM cells, with a particular focus on the expression changes of the RPL29 gene. Addressing the persistent challenges in improving treatment outcomes for MM, especially concerning the efficacy of radiation therapy, remains a critical priority. Despite existing studies on the impact of radiation on various tumors, the specific effects of varying radiation doses and modalities on MM are not yet fully understood. By employing a combination of mouse models, radiation treatment, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and bioinformatics analysis, this research seeks to unravel the underlying mechanisms of radiation therapy in MM.

Our findings are expected to provide valuable insights into the role of RPL29 in the prognosis of MM. Investigating the influence of RPL29 on tumor occurrence, progression, and metastasis may help identify it as a novel biomarker and therapeutic target. This study aspires to contribute to the advancement of treatment strategies for MM, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes through a deeper understanding of the molecular responses to radiation therapy. Moving forward, it is vital to delineate the specific functions and regulatory mechanisms of RPL29 within cells, particularly its roles in tumor biology. Future research should also explore the impact of RPL29 expression on patient prognosis and its potential as a robust biomarker. In summary, the role of RPL29 in both physiological and pathological states warrants in-depth investigation, especially in the context of cancer biology.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The MPC-11 cell line was purchased from Wuhan Warner Bio-Cell Bank, the U266 cell line from Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and the MM.1 S cell line from ATCC. The OPM-2 cell line was obtained from Green Flag (Shanghai) Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. All cell lines were cultured in either Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Mediatech) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained at less than 80% confluence for all passages, and only early passage cells (passages 3–8 after thawing) were used for experiments. Authentication of all cell lines was performed in accordance with ATCC guidelines, including morphology assessment, growth curve analysis, and mycoplasma contamination testing within six months of use. The MPC-11 murine myeloma cell line was selected because it reliably establishes transplantable tumors in BALB/c mice, making it a widely used model for preclinical radiotherapy studies.

Animal studies

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Basic Theory of Chinese Medicine, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male and female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (temperature: 25 ± 2 °C, humidity: 60 ± 10%, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to sterilized water and chow. BALB/c mice were chosen because of their immunocompetence, reproducibility in MM xenografts, and consistent response to MPC-11 cell transplantation.

Tumor model

A BALB/c mouse model was established to simulate multiple myeloma (MM). Following one week of acclimatization, mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 10⁶ MPC-11 cells in either the right flank (for single-tumor models) or both right and left flanks (for dual-tumor models). Tumor volume was measured biweekly using calipers and calculated using the formula: (width² × length)/2. Mice were randomized into experimental groups once tumors reached a mean volume of 80–175 mm³. Tumor growth was monitored for up to 10 days post-treatment. Humane endpoint criteria included a tumor diameter exceeding 15–20 mm, severe distress (e.g., hunched posture), or veterinary recommendation, in compliance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines.

The primary comparison was conducted between the local tumor radiotherapy (2 Gy) group and the other treatment groups. External beam radiotherapy (EBRT) was delivered at doses of either 2–12 Gy on day 1 using an SR200 irradiator (Cere Instrument Company). Tumor-targeted irradiation was performed with the tumor exposed while shielding the rest of the body using custom-designed lead blocks. The X-ray radiation dose rate was 0.86 Gy/min, requiring 140 s for 2 Gy and 837 s for 12 Gy. The 2 Gy dose was selected to represent clinically relevant fractionated radiotherapy exposure, while the 12 Gy dose was applied to mimic ablative high-dose regimens reported in previous MM preclinical studies (refs).

For whole-body irradiation, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and exposed to X-rays in both Anterior-to-Posterior (AP) and Posterior-to-Anterior (PA) orientations to achieve a uniform midline dose of 2 Gy, without shielding.

Immediately following radiotherapy, tumor tissues and peripheral blood samples were collected. Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues using the TRIzol method for subsequent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Sample collection and Preparation

Total RNA was extracted from collected samples, and its integrity was assessed through multiple quality control steps.

RNA quantification and qualification

RNA degradation and contamination were evaluated using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA purity was determined utilizing a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). The integrity of the RNA was evaluated utilizing the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

Library preparation for transcriptome sequencing

A total of 1 µg RNA per sample was used for RNA sequencing library construction. Libraries were prepared utilizing the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Unique index codes were incorporated to assign sequences to specific samples.

Utilizing magnetic beads coupled to poly-T oligos, mRNA was separated from total RNA. Divalent cations were utilized for fragmentation in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5X) at high temperatures. First-strand cDNA was synthesized utilizing random hexamer primers and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H-). Second-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out utilizing DNA Polymerase I and RNase H, followed by conversion of overhangs into blunt ends via exonuclease and polymerase activities. After adenylation of the 3’ ends, NEBNext Adaptor with a hairpin loop structure was ligated to facilitate hybridization.

To enrich cDNA fragments of 250 ~ 300 bp in length, purification was performed utilizing the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Subsequently, 3 µL of USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was added to the size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 5 min. PCR amplification was conducted using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers, and Index (X) primers. The final PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system, and the quality of the constructed libraries was assessed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system.

Clustering and sequencing

Cluster generation was performed using a cBot Cluster Generation System and a TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

Data analysis

Quality control

Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were processed using in-house Perl scripts. Clean reads were retrieved by eliminating adapter sequences, poly-N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. The quality of clean reads was evaluated by calculating Q20, Q30, as well as GC content metrics. High-quality clean reads served as the foundation for all downstream analyses. To minimize technical variation, batch effects were assessed and corrected using the ComBat function from the sva R package prior to downstream analyses.

Mapping reads to the reference genome

Reference genome and gene annotation files were retrieved from publicly available genome databases. HISAT2 (v2.0.5) was utilized to create the reference genome index and to match paired-end clean reads to the genome. This tool was selected due to its ability to create splice junction databases from gene model annotation files, which produces better mapping accuracy than alignment methods that are non-splice-aware.

Novel transcript prediction

Mapped reads from each sample were assembled using StringTie (v1.3.3b) in a reference-based approach. StringTie employs a network flow algorithm and an optional de novo assembly step to reconstruct full-length transcripts, including multiple splice variants for each gene locus.

Quantification of gene expression levels

Gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (v1.5.0-p3), which counts the number of reads mapped to each gene. Expression levels were subsequently normalized and expressed as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), a widely used metric that accounts for sequencing depth and gene length.

Differential expression analysis

Using DESeq2 (for biological replicates)

Differential expression analysis between two conditions/groups (each with biological replicates) was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (v1.16.1). To evaluate differential gene expression, DESeq2 utilizes a model based on a negative binomial distribution. The Benjamini-Hochberg approach was utilized to control the false discovery rate (FDR) by adjusting P-values.

Using edger (for datasets without biological replicates)

For datasets lacking biological replicates, read counts were normalized using a scaling factor within the edgeR R package (v3.18.1). A differential expression analysis was conducted, where P-Values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. An adjusted P-Value threshold of < 0.05 alongside an absolute fold change of 2 was set in order to identify significantly differentially expressed genes.

Functional enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using the clusterProfiler R package, with corrections for gene length bias. GO terms with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. KEGG is a comprehensive database that facilitates the interpretation of high-level biological functions and interactions based on genomic and molecular datasets (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Significantly enriched pathways were identified based on an adjusted P-value threshold of < 0.05.

SNP analysis

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling was performed using GATK2 (v3.7) software. Raw variant call format (VCF) files were filtered using GATK’s standard filtering criteria and additional parameters (cluster: 3; WindowSize: 35; QD < 2.0; FS > 30.0; DP < 10). Annotation of variant sites was conducted using SnpEff software.

Alternative splicing analysis

Alternative splicing (AS) is a crucial regulatory mechanism affecting gene expression and protein diversity. AS events were analyzed using rMATS (v3.2.5) software.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of differentially expressed genes

The PPI network of differentially expressed genes was constructed according to data from the STRING database, which provides known and predicted protein-protein interactions.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) detection

Total RNA was extracted from serum and tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The integrity and purity of total RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) with gDNA Eraser to remove genomic DNA.

For tissue RNA, reverse transcription conditions were: 42 °C for 2 min, followed by 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. For serum RNA, cDNA synthesis was conducted using the RevertAid H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) under the following conditions: 25 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 60 min, and 70 °C for 5 min. The synthesized cDNA was stored at − 80 °C until use.

qRT-PCR was performed using an ABI 7500 PCR system with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s (primer-specific annealing temperature). The relative RNA expression levels were evaluated utilizing the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal reference. Primer specificity and amplification efficiency were validated through melting curve analysis and standard curve efficiency tests to ensure accurate quantification.

Data acquisition and processing

Gene expression profiling datasets were retrieved from the NCBI GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE6477 dataset was downloaded from the GPL96 platform ([HG-U133A] Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array) and included purified plasma cell samples from 103 multiple myeloma (MM) patients and 15 normal controls. Additionally, bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data as well as clinical information from relapsed MM cases (n = 80) were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Since the data were derived from publicly available sources, no patient consent or ethical approval was required.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was carried out utilizing the “survival” as well as “survminer” R packages (versions 3.4-0.4 and 0.4.9, respectively). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Gene expression analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis identified a total of 424 upregulated and 33 downregulated genes in the 2 Gy Tumor EBRT (External Beam Radiation Therapy) (2 Gy) group in comparison to the control group. In the 12 Gy Tumor EBRT (12 Gy) group, 42 genes were significantly upregulated, whereas 473 genes were significantly downregulated compared to the control. Additionally, in the 2 Gy Whole Mouse (WM) group, 221 genes were significantly upregulated, whereas 1,118 genes were downregulated compared to the control group (CG) (Fig. 1A).

The evidence indicates that different radiation dose levels as well as different treatment approaches cause profound differences in gene expression profiles.

Functional enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression analysis between the 2 Gy Tumor EBRT intervention group and the control group demonstrated significant enrichment in neutrophil and leukocyte chemotaxis related biological processes. These genes were enriched primarily in the following pathways: cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and viral protein interaction with cytokines and cytokine receptors.

On the other hand, in the 12 Gy Tumor EBRT intervened group, the differentially expressed genes as compared to the control group were mainly affiliated with the extracellular structure organization and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization. The primary pathways that were changed were ECM-receptor interaction and protein digestion and absorption.

For the 2 Gy Whole Mouse (WM) group, the differentially expressed genes were mostly associated with positive regulation of cell migration and positive regulation of cellular component movement. The impacted pathways included those involved in the complement and coagulation cascades and ECM-receptor interaction.

This data indicates that employing varying doses and methods of treatment generates different biological responses that may affect the therapeutic outcomes through modulation of diverse KEGG pathways (Fig. 2A, B). The complete list of differential genes, GO and KEGG enrichment results are shown in Supplementary Tables S1-S3.

The 2 Gy Tumor EBRT group had 394 uniquely upregulated genes and 16 uniquely downregulated genes when compared to the control group (Fig. 3A).

Moreover, among the differentially expressed genes, some were selected as putative key regulatory genes based on their high number of interactions through a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. In an analysis of the network, one of its central hubs, an identified strongly connected gene, was Adipoq, a candidate tumor suppressor gene suggesting that such genes can have major impacts on tumorigenesis and may act as key regulatory genes in response to radiotherapy (Fig. 3B).

Verification of specific genes

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of the clinical samples was performed on newly diagnosed and relapsed multiple myeloma patients. The data showed that the expression levels of RPL29 and UPK3A in relapsed multiple myeloma patients were significantly higher than in newly diagnosed patients, whereas ADIPOQ expression was significantly lower (Fig. 4A). In addition, RPL29 was found to be highly expressed in the majority of multiple myeloma cell lines (Fig. 4B).

TCGA database analysis

Examining the expression levels of the RPL29 gene in the TCGA database, its heighted expression was noted in multiple myeloma samples (Fig. 5A). Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis showed high RPL29 expression levels correlated with worse prognosis in multiple myeloma patients (Fig. 5B, C, D). This evidence indicates that RPL29 is likely an important candidate marker for disease progression and prognosis in multiple myeloma. In the TCGA (MMRF CoMMpass) database analysis, high and low expression groups of RPL29 were defined based on the median expression value. These findings suggest that RPL29 may not only serve as a prognostic biomarker but also contribute to radiotherapy resistance through mechanisms involving DNA repair, clonal selection, and remodeling of the immune microenvironment, as supported by recent literature.

Discussion

Multiple Myeloma (MM) is a type of blood cancer characterized by the abnormal expansion of plasma cells, and its pathophysiology involves complex interactions with the immune system, bone marrow microenvironment, and molecular drivers. Despite therapeutic advances, recurrence and treatment resistance remain major challenges [19, 20]. Radiotherapy, though widely applied in MM primarily for palliation, has underexplored biological effects on MM tumor cells and their associated molecular pathways [21, 22].

Our study provides evidence that radiotherapy induces distinct transcriptional responses in MM, which vary depending on dose and delivery modality [23, 24].

By comparing gene expression profiles across 2 Gy Tumor EBRT, 12 Gy Tumor EBRT, and 2 Gy Whole Mouse (WM) cohorts, we identified dose- and modality-dependent transcriptional changes (Fig. 1A, B). Such analyses are fundamental to understanding tumor biology under therapeutic stress [25]. In the 2 Gy Tumor EBRT group, 424 genes were upregulated and 33 were downregulated, consistent with adaptive stress responses [26, 27]. These findings suggest that low-dose irradiation triggers survival and DNA repair pathways [28, 29]. In contrast, 12 Gy EBRT produced fewer upregulated and markedly more downregulated genes, consistent with cytostatic arrest and apoptosis induction [30–32]. Whole-body exposure at 2 Gy led to broad gene suppression with only limited upregulation, likely reflecting systemic inflammatory responses and radiation-mediated cytotoxicity, [33–35] with inhibition of multiple pathways [36]. These results reinforce the concept that radiation-induced transcriptional changes modulate cellular physiology and therapeutic sensitivity, [37] with DNA repair, cell cycle, and apoptosis pathways being particularly critical determinants [38–40].

A variety of treatment types and radiation doses usually have different effects on specific biological processes and KEGG pathways that lead to different therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 2A, B). Dose-specific immune modulation was also observed. Low-dose 2 Gy EBRT enriched pathways related to neutrophil chemotaxis and leukocyte migration, [41–43] processes linked to anti-tumor immunity [44]. The enrichment of terms related to neutrophil and leukocyte chemotaxis suggests a potential activation of immune responses in the tumor microenvironment, which may involve the recruitment of immune cells following low-dose radiation. Based on established literature, neutrophils are vital in local inflammation and could potentially contribute to anti-tumor immunity. However, the specific composition and functional state of recruited immune cells remain to be directly validated in our model. Cytokine–cytokine receptor signaling was also prominent, [45, 46] resembling immune activation patterns triggered by viral infection [47–49]. Together, these changes imply that low-dose radiotherapy may enhance immune cell recruitment and cytokine communication, promoting anti-tumor immunity [50, 51]. The enrichment of cytokine-related pathways implies a complex interplay of signaling molecules that could regulate immune cell activity. It is intriguing to speculate, by analogy to other studies, that low-dose radiation might trigger a sterile inflammatory response reminiscent of antiviral immunity, which could potentially enhance tumor cell recognition and destruction. This interpretation, however, is derived from pathway analysis and requires further experimental confirmation to establish causality and specific immune mechanisms in our setting. Taken together, our transcriptomic data are consistent with the established concept that radiation-induced inflammatory responses involve leukocyte chemotaxis and cytokine signaling, which may collectively contribute to immune modulation.

Conversely, 12 Gy EBRT enriched ECM organization pathways, [52, 53] consistent with tissue remodeling, while WM exposure enriched complement and coagulation cascades, [54, 55] raising concerns about pro-metastatic potential [54].

Within these transcriptional networks, several key genes emerged. ADIPOQ was upregulated and acted as a hub gene in PPI analysis, consistent with its tumor-suppressive, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory functions [55–57]. By contrast, RPL29 was significantly downregulated following 2 Gy irradiation. Given that RPL29 is typically upregulated in MM and promotes tumor cell survival via protein synthesis and PI3K/AKT signaling, its suppression by radiotherapy suggests a role in radiosensitivity. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that ribosomal proteins such as RPL29 may participate in DNA damage response and repair pathways, potentially influencing radiotherapy resistance. For instance, dysregulation of ribosomal biogenesis has been linked to altered DNA repair efficiency and genomic instability, which could promote clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure [58]. In the context of multiple myeloma, radiotherapy-induced stress may select for clones with aberrant RPL29 expression, thereby enhancing survival through mechanisms such as impaired apoptosis or enhanced DNA repair. Furthermore, RPL29 may contribute to the remodeling of the bone marrow niche by modulating cytokine secretion or immune cell interactions, further fostering a permissive environment for drug resistance and disease relapse. Moreover, radiotherapy-induced alterations in the bone marrow immune microenvironment, such as T-cell exhaustion, NK cell dysfunction, and macrophage polarization, may be exacerbated by RPL29 overexpression. Recent studies indicate that ribosomal proteins can modulate immune checkpoint expression and cytokine networks, potentially contributing to an immunosuppressive niche that facilitates escape from immune surveillance and confers resistance to both radiotherapy and immunotherapy [58]. Thus, RPL29 may serve as a nexus between radiation-induced genetic changes and microenvironmental adaptation, highlighting its role as a key mediator of acquired resistance [58–61]. UPK3A, another downregulated gene, has been linked to cell differentiation and adhesion,64,65 and its aberrant expression contributes to bladder and gastric cancers [62–64].

Validation studies using patient samples from newly diagnosed and relapsed multiple myeloma cases confirmed that RPL29 and UPK3A expression levels were significantly elevated, whereas ADIPOQ expression was markedly reduced (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, RPL29 was highly expressed across various multiple myeloma cell lines (Fig. 4B). To further investigate its clinical significance, TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) database analysis was performed, which revealed that RPL29 is consistently overexpressed in both newly diagnosed and relapsed multiple myeloma patients (Fig. 5A). Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis demonstrated that high RPL29 expression is significantly associated with poorer prognosis in multiple myeloma patients, reinforcing its potential as a prognostic biomarker (Fig. 5B, C, D).

Analyzing The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases, we evaluated RPL29 gene expression levels in cases of multiple myeloma. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that high RPL29 expression correlated with worse prognosis, consistent with enhanced tumor aggressiveness, resistance to treatment, and potential metastatic capacity. These results indicate that RPL29 is likely involved in MM pathogenesis and represents a valuable prognostic indicator whose role as a therapeutic target should be investigated further. Other significant genes, such as UPK3A and ADIPOQ, which may co-express or interact with RPL29, also warrant further analysis to clarify their contributions to tumor biology. Studying the combined expression of RPL29, UPK3A, and ADIPOQ will therefore improve our understanding of MM diagnosis, prognosis, and the need for targeted therapeutic interventions. Overall, RPL29 emerges as both a prognostic marker and a potential therapeutic target in MM.

The strength of this study lies in its comprehensive approach, integrating murine models with clinical sample validation to enhance reliability. By examining RPL29 within the radiotherapy context, this research expands current knowledge on MM biology and highlights opportunities for therapeutic development aimed at improving patient outcomes.

Our study addresses key knowledge gaps in the existing literature. While previous research has demonstrated the impact of radiotherapy on tumors, the specific effects of different doses and treatment modalities have been less explored. Here, we systematically analyzed dose-dependent transcriptional changes in MM tumor cells. We found that local radiotherapy (2 Gy) led to marked downregulation of RPL29, linked to prognosis, underscoring the complexity between molecular alterations and clinical features. The reduction of RPL29 expression may influence essential cellular functions, including proliferation, migration, and apoptosis, thereby altering tumor evolution and treatment response. Moreover, RNA-seq confirmed RPL29 as a highly interconnected gene within the regulatory network, supporting its putative role as an oncogenic driver in MM progression.

Our findings provide novel insights into MM molecular mechanisms and their implications for treatment effectiveness. They support the design of innovative radiotherapy strategies alongside precision treatment plans. Altering RPL29 expression could improve radiotherapy outcomes and guide refinements in treatment protocols and patient-specific planning. As research progresses, the therapeutic potential of targeting RPL29 becomes increasingly evident, particularly in relapsed or treatment-refractory MM. This work also highlights that combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy may be promising, while investigating genes sharing regulatory pathways with RPL29 could deepen understanding of tumor biology and radiosensitivity.

Aberrant ribosomal protein expression, including RPL29, may disrupt proliferation–apoptosis balance, promoting MM cell survival. Its modulation of immune responses within the tumor microenvironment further underscores its biological importance. Targeted therapies that account for these mechanisms are needed to improve patient outcomes.

While our study identifies RPL29 as a strongly prognostic biomarker associated with radiotherapy response and disease progression in MM, we acknowledge that its precise functional role and mechanistic underpinnings remain to be fully elucidated. The observed downregulation following local radiotherapy (2 Gy) suggests a potential involvement in the cellular stress response, but it is unclear whether this change is a mere consequence of radiation-induced damage or an active mediator of therapy resistance. The absence of functional validation experiments, such as genetic manipulation (knockdown or overexpression) of RPL29 in MM cell lines followed by assessments of radiation sensitivity, proliferation, and apoptosis, represents a limitation of the current study. Future work must determine if targeting RPL29 can directly sensitize MM cells to radiotherapy, which would elevate its status from a biomarker to an actionable therapeutic target. Furthermore, the interplay between RPL29 dysregulation and established pathways of radioresistance in MM (e.g., DNA damage repair activation, NF-κB signaling, and interactions with the bone marrow microenvironment) was not examined in depth. Integrating our transcriptomic findings with these resistance mechanisms could yield a more comprehensive model of how RPL29 shapes treatment outcomes. Techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing on patient samples pre- and post-radiotherapy could offer higher-resolution insights into the heterogeneity of RPL29 expression within tumor subclones and its correlation with immune microenvironment changes, thereby addressing the limitations of bulk sequencing and limited sample size.

Future research will focus on (i) identifying genes co-regulated with RPL29, (ii) conducting in vitro and in vivo validation to assess its role in progression, treatment response, and drug resistance, and (iii) investigating its involvement in key signaling pathways such as cell migration and cytokine interactions. We also plan to evaluate the clinical significance of RPL29 through diagnostic and prognostic validation in patient cohorts, while exploring targeted therapeutic strategies including small-molecule inhibitors, RNA-based approaches, and immunotherapeutics. Finally, analysis of dose-dependent effects on oncogenic pathways will guide optimized radiotherapy dosing methods and support personalized MM treatment development.

While our study identifies RPL29 as a key mediator and prognostic biomarker in MM radiotherapy response, the precise mechanisms through which it influences the immune microenvironment, particularly regarding specific immune cell subsets like T cells and NK cells, remain to be fully elucidated and are an important direction for future research.

Limitation

Several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small patient sample size reduces statistical power and limits generalizability. Second, reliance on bulk RNA-seq obscures intratumoral heterogeneity, a key driver of relapse and resistance in MM; single-cell or spatial transcriptomics would provide greater resolution. Third, the absence of proteomic validation is a concern, since ribosomal proteins are subject to post-transcriptional regulation. Fourth, the lack of functional assays (knockdown or overexpression) prevents confirmation of RPL29’s mechanistic role in radiosensitivity. Fifth, reliance on murine subcutaneous tumor models does not fully replicate the complexity of the human bone marrow microenvironment. Sixth, external datasets such as GEO and TCGA may contain batch effects and clinical heterogeneity. Finally, survival analyses may be underpowered, raising the risk of overinterpretation. Addressing these limitations in future studies—including larger cohorts, proteomic confirmation, and in vivo functional validation—will be essential to establish RPL29 as a clinically relevant biomarker.

The primary limitation of this study lies in its cross-sectional design. The sample was analyzed at a single time point after radiotherapy, failing to capture the temporal dynamic sequence of gene expression changes or track the progression of drug resistance during multiple treatment cycles. Consequently, this research cannot determine whether RPL29 expression fluctuations serve as a predictive marker for drug resistance during early treatment or emerge as an event in advanced relapse stages. Addressing these limitations in future research will yield stronger evidence for RPL29’s role in MM and establish it as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target. In the future, a longitudinal profiling program will be carried out, in which a series of samples will be collected at multiple treatment time points (e.g., baseline, treatment, remission, and relapse) to dynamically monitor changes in RPL29 and other key molecules to better understand their clinical significance.

Despite the significant insights provided by this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and the absence of independent clinical validation cohorts may affect the generalizability of our findings. Larger, multi-center studies are required to robustly validate the prognostic utility of RPL29 and its interaction with radiotherapy modalities. Furthermore, the study lacks functional validation of the RNA-seq results. The biological significance of RPL29 downregulation post-radiation, and its role in mediating radio-sensitivity or -resistance, remains hypothetical without in vitro or in vivo gain-of-function or loss-of-function experiments. Establishing such causal relationships is crucial for defining RPL29 not only as a biomarker but also as a potential therapeutic target. Additionally, our analysis provides a broad transcriptomic overview but does not fully integrate these changes with specific, well-characterized pathways of resistance in MM. The potential heterogeneity within tumor samples, which could be resolved with techniques like single-cell RNA sequencing, was not assessed and might underlie some of the variability in our data. Dataset heterogeneity and potential batch effects, although mitigated by standardized bioinformatics pipelines, could also influence the stability of the results. Addressing these limitations in future research through functional assays, validation in expanded cohorts, and higher-resolution analytical techniques will be essential to solidify the role of RPL29 in MM pathophysiology and its therapeutic relevance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, local radiotherapy has a significant downregulation of RPL29 expression in multiple myeloma cells, demonstrating its central function in disease development. Our data indicate that gene expression and related signaling pathways impact treatment response and prognostic evaluation. Additionally, further elucidation of the role of RPL29 in immune cell regulation offers valuable information on the tumor microenvironment, opening avenues for new therapeutic interventions. The clinical importance of this work emphasizes the need for future research on RPL29 as a biomarker and therapeutic target. By shedding light on molecular mechanisms of radiotherapy response, this work lays a solid foundation for optimizing treatment outcomes and improving patient outcomes in multiple myeloma. Our study underscores the potential of RPL29 as a central player in mediating radiotherapy response and resistance in MM, possibly through mechanisms involving DNA repair, clonal selection, and immune microenvironment modulation. Future studies should explore these pathways to develop combinatorial strategies targeting RPL29 to overcome radiotherapy resistance.
