A rare presentation of a rare problem: mitral annular calcification-related calcific amorphous tumour with cavitation and left ventricle to left atrium communication—a case report

Introduction

Mitral annular calcification (MAC) is defined as the accumulation of calcium along the annulus, predominantly along its posterior aspect, with extension into the posterior leaflet or involvement anteriorly with increasing severity.1 It is an independent risk factor for the development of myocardial infarction or stroke and is associated with increased morbidity.2 Calcified amorphous tumour (CAT) is a rare, non-neoplastic cardiac mass of the heart identified by nodular calcium on a background of amorphous degenerating fibrinous material.3 CAT is predominantly seen in patients with MAC. MAC-related CAT has been reported most commonly in renal failure patients.4 CAT may cause fatal thromboembolism from the amorphous material [ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or ischaemic cerebral stroke]. Other presentations of CAT are rare and include encroachment onto the mitral leaflets, and geometric alteration of the co-optation point leading to mitral regurgitation and perforation of anterior mitral leaflet with mitral regurgitation.5,6 Acute embolic STEMI or stroke and acute severe mitral regurgitation resulting from MAC-related CAT have been termed as ‘MAC Attack’. We present a unique and previously unreported complication of MAC-related CAT with cavitation and left ventricle to left atrium fistulous communication causing mitral regurgitation.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old gentleman with hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on regular maintenance haemodialysis thrice weekly for 2 years, presented with severe breathlessness and pedal oedema for 10 days. There was no history of fever or chest pain. The patient was seen elsewhere and diagnosed to be having acute pulmonary oedema with severe mitral regurgitation (MR). He was treated with intravenous diuretics and afterload reduction and transferred to our centre for further management. On examination, he was tachypneic, with a blood pressure of 140/80 mm Hg, pulse rate of 120 beats/min, and SaO2 96%. Cardiac auscultation revealed a soft S2 and pan systolic murmur of mitral regurgitation with pulmonary congestion and signs of systemic venous congestion. Baseline blood investigations showed haemoglobin of 12 g/dl (13.5–17.5 g/dl), white blood cell count of 6500 cells/mm3 (4000–11 000 cells/mm3), platelet count of 112 200 cells/mm3 (150 000–450 000 cells/mm3), serum creatinine of 5.2 mg/dl (0.7–1.3 mg/dl), blood urea of 139 mg/dl (14–23 mg/dl), and sodium and potassium levels were 139 meq/l (135–145 meq/l) and 3.7 meq/l (2.5–5.5 meq/l), respectively. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin were unremarkable. Evaluation for infective endocarditis with multiple sets of blood cultures (aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal) was negative. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus tachycardia with QS complexes in leads V1 to V3 and ventricular premature complexes. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed hypokinetic basal inferior, inferoseptal, and inferolateral wall of left ventricle with an ejection fraction of 50% with dense calcification in the posterior mitral annulus and severe MR. Left atrial volume was 25.8 ml/m2, LV end diastolic volume (EDV) was 44.5 ml/m2, and end systolic volume (ESV) was 21.4 ml/m2. Transoesophageal echocardiography revealed extensive and dense calcification of the mitral annulus with an echolucent space in the posterolateral region of the MAC suggestive of cavitation of CAT (CAT cavity; approximately measuring 15 × 10 mm) which was communicating into both the left ventricle (LV) and the left atrium (LA) (LV to LA fistulous communication through CAT cavity) (Figure 1).

The communication to LA was narrow and tortuous and out of phase with the central MR jet (Video 1). This jet was not picked up on TTE. There was also mild thickening of posterior mitral leaflet with poor coaptation at medial aspect of P2 segment. There was severe MR with two jets: a large central jet from the valve coaptation area and another smaller eccentric jet via the LV–CAT cavity–LA communication (Figures 2–4; Videos 2–4).

The MR volume was 117 ml. There were no vegetations around this fistulous communication or on any valve leaflets and no pulmonary hypertension. Video 5 shows three-dimensional TEE en face view of mitral valve with leaflet prolapse.

Computed tomography coronary angiography and nuclear myocardial perfusion scan did not reveal any ischaemia. A diagnosis of MAC-related CAT with severe MR resulting from mitral coaptation defect and LV–CAT cavity–LA communication was made. The patient was managed conservatively with negative fluid balance achieved by haemodialysis, antiplatelet and anticoagulant, statin, and afterload reduction. He improved symptomatically but severe MR from both the jets persisted. Surgical repair of CAT cavity with LV–LA communication along with mitral valve repair/replacement was considered by our cardiac surgical team to be a simple and definitive treatment strategy for this patient. Theoretically, in prohibitively high-risk surgical scenario, transcatheter management with transcatheter edge to edge repair (TEER) with Mitraclip and vascular plugging of the CAT cavity LV–LA communication with AVP II could be considered. However, the patient and his family declined any further intervention and opted for continued medical management. Table 1 represents timeline of events.

Discussion

MAC has been independently associated with stroke in multiple cohorts, possibly being a source of high-risk emboligenic substrate. MAC-related stroke can occur from varying mechanisms such as atrial fibrillation, MAC association with aortic atheroma,7 and MAC acting as a nidus for infective endocarditis with vegetation embolism causing stroke. Additionally, due to the possible expansion of calcification and fibrosis onto the conduction system, MAC is linked to an increased prevalence of conduction system problems, including atrioventricular and bundle branch blocks.8 MAC can cause mitral valve dysfunction, and it most frequently results in MR due to MAC-related weakening of the mitral annulus’s sphincter-like function during systole or from MAC-induced distortion of the coaptation of the leaflets.9 MAC-induced traction-related chordal rupture has also been documented.10 Another problem associated with MAC is liquefactive necrosis with caseous calcification, also known as CAT or ‘toothpaste tumour’. Histological examination of CAT typically reveals a central calcified area surrounded by fibrous tissue, eosinophilic amorphous material with associated inflammatory infiltrate, and neovascularization.11

MAC-related CAT is common in patients with ESRD like in our case. The growth rate of MAC-related cardiac CAT is considerably faster than that of CAT in patients without MAC. According to earlier reports, some patients with MAC showed rapidly expanding cardiac CATs between 6 weeks and a year.12 MAC is dynamic and can thus present dramatically with acute presentations such as heart failure due to mitral stenosis or regurgitation or embolic stroke or embolic STEMI due to embolization of the amorphous material; these acute presentations have been termed as ‘MAC Attack’.13

Our patient had MAC-related CAT with ‘MAC Attack’ presentation of acute heart failure with pulmonary oedema due to acute severe mitral regurgitation. Geometric alteration of mitral coaptation leading to severe MR and CAT impingement leading to perforation of mitral leaflet have been reported before. We, for the first time, report the development of a trans-mitral annular LV–CAT cavity–LA communication as a cause for MR. Our patient also had a central coaptation MR. The differential diagnoses of ischaemic MR, ruptured papillary muscle-chordal tear with leaflet prolapse, and infective endocarditis of mitral valve were ruled out.

Surgical resection of CAT and mitral valve repair or replacement for severe MR forms the mainstay of therapy. In our patient, sole mitral valve repair or replacement surgery would have left behind LV–CAT cavity–LA communication resulting in surgical failure. If the patient presents only with MR due to LV–CAT cavity–LA communication, transcatheter closure with vascular plug could be a therapeutic option. This case highlights the importance of assessing the mitral valve and annular structures meticulously by TEE to avoid erroneous management. Our patient was treated conservatively because he was not willing to undergo surgical procedure. Anticoagulation has been advocated to reduce the incidence of embolism in individuals who are at high risk for surgery or are not willing for surgery. The patient is on strict medications and doing well on limited activity. He is on regular medical follow up.

Conclusions

MAC is increasingly come across in ageing and renal failure population and is many a time considered a benign finding of little clinical impact. CAT is very rarely reported in the literature, predominantly in patients with MAC (MAC-related CAT). MAC-related CAT can have dramatic acute presentations (MAC Attack). It is important to recognize that acute severe MR in this scenario can result from more than one pathology. This unique previously unreported complication of MAC-related CAT adds to the existing literature. The potential for MAC-related CAT to cause catastrophic sequelae needs to be highlighted among the cardiology community.

Take-home message

This case highlights the potential for severe mitral regurgitation due to multiple pathologies emphasizing the need for careful monitoring and management in patients with MAC.
