Re-evaluating corticosterone as a welfare biomarker in broilers using UPLC/MS-MS: Limited responsiveness to heat stress, disease challenges, and environmental enrichment
Authors: Patricia Soster, Marc Cherlet, Camila Lopes Carvalho, Frank A.M. Tuyttens, Siegrid De Baere, Luc Duchateau, Maarten De Gussem, Gunther Antonissen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corticosterone (CORT) is widely used as a physiological indicator of stress in poultry welfare research; however, its responsiveness across biological matrices under endogenous challenge conditions remains debated. The present study investigated whether experimentally induced changes in welfare status are reflected in CORT concentrations measured in plasma, dorsal feathers, and droppings using a validated UPLC–MS/MS method. Two experimental trials were conducted to induce variation in broiler welfare status. In the first experiment, male Ross 308 broilers were exposed to thermoneutral or cyclic heat-stress conditions during the finisher phase, with or without access to multifunctional cooling platforms as environmental enrichment. In the second experiment, Ross 308 broilers were challenged with two infectious disease models: coccidiosis (Eimeria spp.) and respiratory disease (infectious bronchitis virus combined with avian pathogenic Escherichia coli). At 41 days of age, plasma, dorsal feathers, and droppings were collected and analyzed for CORT. Across both experiments, plasma CORT was consistently detectable but remained at low concentrations and did not differ between control and challenged groups. Feather CORT was below the limit of quantification in all samples, and around 30% of dropping samples were below the limit of detection. These results suggest that, under the conditions tested, endogenous CORT measured by UPLC–MS/MS in plasma, feathers, and droppings shows limited responsiveness to common welfare challenges and enrichment strategy, raising questions about its sensitivity as a welfare biomarker in broilers.

Introduction

Corticosterone (CORT) is the main glucocorticoid hormone in poultry and a key indicator of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity. Plasma CORT reflects acute stress responses, feather CORT integrates hormone deposition over weeks to months during feather growth, and dropping CORT reflects systemic glucocorticoid excretion via the cloaca (Palme et al., 2005; Jenni-Eiermann et al., 2015). Together, these matrices may offer complementary temporal windows for welfare assessment. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) provides superior analytical specificity over immunoassays by avoiding cross-reactivity with structurally related steroids and matrix effects (Caulfield and Padula, 2020), and has been validated for CORT quantification in broiler plasma, feathers, and droppings by our group (Soster et al., 2025; Soster et al., conditionally accepted).

Despite methodological advances, it remains unclear whether CORT measured across these matrices reliably reflects endogenous HPA axis responses to experimentally induced welfare challenges. Studies using exogenous CORT administration have shown clear dose-dependent responses (Soster et al., 2025) conditionally accepted), but such models may not fully replicate the neuroendocrine dynamics of naturally occurring stress. Whether UPLC–MS/MS-quantified CORT in plasma, feathers, and droppings can detect biologically meaningful differences under controlled welfare-relevant conditions, including both negative challenges and positive interventions, has not been evaluated in broiler chickens.

To address this gap, the present study quantified CORT across all three matrices in two independent experiments one including heat stress with or without multifunctional cooling platform enrichment and the other one challenging birds with different infectious diseases (Eimeria spp. and infectious bronchitis virus, IBV, combined with avian pathogenic Escherichia coli, APEC). The objective was to evaluate the sensitivity of each matrix to detect CORT changes under adverse and enriching welfare conditions.

Material and methods

The Heat stress and Platforms Experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Flemish Research Institute for Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (ILVO), Merelbeke-Melle, Belgium (authorization 2022/414). The Infectious Diseases Experiment was approved by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine and the Faculty of Bioscience Engineering of Ghent University (approval 2023-084).

Heat stress and platforms experiment

The experimental setup has been previously described in detail by Soster et al. (2025). Briefly, the experiment comprised three production rounds, each serving as one replication, totaling three replications per treatment. Each round contained 560 male Ross 308 broilers (n = 1,680 broilers in total). Two treatments were applied: heat stress and platforms. In every round, one stable was split into two independently climate-controlled compartments, each housing two 36 m² pens (concrete floor, wood shavings 2.5 kg/m²) containing 140 one-day-old, as-hatched birds (14 kg/m²; 3.8 birds/m²). Four cameras and two microphones were installed per pen to continuously monitor indicators of bird health and welfare.

Multifunctional raised platforms (Khalfi et al., 2024) that served as an enrichment strategy against heat stress, incorporating an integrated cooling system to reduce thermal load, a dark sheltered area to promote resting behavior, and manure collection trays to maintain litter quality. Each platform was mesh-covered and accessed via two 1.2 m ramps (18° incline) leading to a 274 × 246 mm platform, with a shaded, rubber-enclosed area underneath and fringed entrances. These platforms have been shown to improve broiler welfare (Khalfi et al., 2026; Carvalho et al., 2026). In each compartment, one pen received three platforms and the other served as a no-platform control.

All birds received starter (d0–9), grower (d10–22), and finisher (d23–41) diets ad libitum according to Ross 308 management guidelines. The lighting schedule followed 23 h light:1 h dark during the first week, followed by 18 h light:6 h dark from day 7 onwards. Room temperature was set at 32°C during week 1 and reduced by 4°C per week until reaching 22°C by week 3, after which it was maintained through week 4. During the finisher period, heat stress (HS) was applied in one compartment on days 29–33 and 36–40, during these days, ambient temperature was daily gradually increased from 22°C to 32°C at 09:00, maintained at 32°C until 15:00, and subsequently gradually reduced back to 22°C; the other compartment remained thermoneutral (TN). Humidity, temperature, and CO₂ were logged hourly, and the temperature–humidity index (THI) was calculated (Buffington et al., 1981). Between rounds, platform pens and the HS compartment were rotated to balance pen and room effects.

On day 41, birds were individually restrained and culled by intravenous administration of 20% sodium pentobarbital (Euthanimal 20%, Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands; 0.5 mL/kg body weight) into the brachial vein by an experienced veterinarian. The entire procedure, including blood collection, was completed within two minutes per bird to minimize stress. Blood (1 mL) was collected from the jugular vein into K3EDTA tubes (13 × 75 mm, non-ridge Vacuette, Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria). Plasma was separated by centrifugation (3,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), transferred to Eppendorf tubes, and stored at −20°C until analysis. Dorsal feathers were collected by pulling from the area between the neck and wings. Droppings were collected from the rectum, avoiding the cecal portion. Both feathers and droppings were stored at −20°C, then dried and ground prior to analysis. Samples were collected from five birds per pen (20 samples per treatment group) for CORT quantification.

Infectious diseases experiment

The experimental setup is described in detail in Soster et al., 2026a and Soster et al. (conditionally accepted). Briefly, three treatments (control, Eimeria spp., and IBV combined with APEC) were evaluated across four experimental rounds, using four replicates per treatment (11 Ross 308 broilers per replicate; total n = 132). Each group was housed in an isolated 3 m² pen equipped with one feeder and one tubular drinker. To limit compartment bias, treatments were rotated across rooms between rounds. During the first two weeks, all chicks within a round were housed together in a separate pen. On day 14, birds were weighed and allocated into treatment groups with similar median body weights. One camera and one microphone were installed per pen to continuously monitor behavior and vocalizations.

Birds were allocated into three treatment groups namely, T1: Control group (Diseases free); T2: Birds subjected to Eimeria spp. (On day 14, oral gavage of 1 mL containing 15,000 E. maxima, 20,000 E. acervulina, and 7,000 E. tenella oocysts); T3: Birds subjected to IBV combined with APEC (On day 21, IBV (strain M41) administered via eye drop (0.1 mL; 5 log₁₀ EID₅₀), followed on day 24 by APEC (strain T.01.54) via the oculo-nasal route (0.1 mL; 5 × 10⁸ CFU). Light cycle, feed and water provision, and temperature management followed the same protocols as described in the Heat Stress and Platforms Experiment. On day 41, birds were culled and blood, dorsal feathers, and droppings were collected from five birds per pen (20 samples per treatment group) for CORT analysis, using identical sample collection, processing, and storage procedures.

Corticosterone analysis

CORT concentrations in plasma, feathers, and droppings were quantified using UPLC–MS/MS methods developed previously by our research group (Soster et al., 2025 and Soster et al., conditionally accepted).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in RStudio. For Heat stress and Platforms Experiment, a linear mixed model was fitted with thermal condition (thermoneutral vs. heat stress) and platform use (yes vs. no) as fixed effects, their interaction term, and production round as a random effect to account for repeated measures across rounds. For Infectious Diseases Experiment, each disease challenge was compared against the control within matched pairs using paired t-tests when residuals met normality assumptions and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests otherwise. Normality was assessed using visual inspection of Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

All CORT concentrations in feathers were below the LOQ (5 ng/g dry matter) in both experiments, meaning no samples were quantifiable in this matrix. All 60 plasma samples per experiment were above the LOQ and therefore fully quantifiable. CORT concentrations measured in the present study were markedly lower than values reported using ELISA. For CORT in droppings, Kwon et al. (2023) reported values ranging from approximately 22 to 27 ng/g, whereas values in the present study ranged from 0.15 to 6.6 ng/g. Similarly, CORT in plasma measured by ELISA typically ranges between 1 and 5 ng/mL under basal conditions, whereas our values remained below 1 ng/mL. UPLC–MS/MS is highly specific for CORT detection and resistant to cross-reactivity with structurally related steroids (Caulfield et al., 2020; Sadok et al., 2023), making analytical false negatives unlikely. In contrast, ELISA-based methods cannot distinguish between free and conjugated CORT fractions, and antibody cross-reactivity with adrenal steroids such as progesterone and 11-deoxycorticosterone may artificially inflate measured concentrations (Beerda et al., 1996; Sheriff et al., 2011). Therefore, higher CORT concentrations reported with immunoassays may partly reflect cross-reactivity with related steroids or metabolites that, while responsive to stress, do not represent CORT itself, limiting their ability to replicate the neuroendocrine dynamics of naturally occurring stress responses.

In Heat stress and Platforms Experiment, our previous research showed that while sound analysis did not reveal differences in vocalizations among treatments (heat stress and platform use as enrichment), image analysis showed changes in broilers behavior under heat stress, seen by reduced time at the feeder and increased time at the drinker during heat-stress bouts (Soster et al., 2025), suggesting that in fact birds were stressed. Also, platforms supported welfare by facilitating thermoregulation, improving litter conditions, and contributing to better leg health (Khalfi et al., 2026; Carvalho et al., 2026), suggesting relief of the stress.

In Infectious Diseases Experiment, vocalization patterns did not differ among disease groups (Soster et al., 2026b), whereas video analysis revealed distinct disease-specific behavioral patterns: Eimeria-infected birds showed sustained increased locomotion and exploratory behavior alongside variable drinking, representing a non-classical response to intestinal challenge, while IBV+APEC-challenged birds exhibited subtler changes, including decreased inactivity and increased panting (Soster et al., 2026c). These behavioral differences suggests that the experimental challenges were effective in inducing welfare-relevant responses; however, neither sound analysis nor CORT measurements were sensitive enough to detect treatment differences under the present conditions. Furthermore, even where CORT was detectable, coefficients of variation were consistently high across matrices and treatment groups (e.g., plasma CV ranging from 76% to 156% in Heat stress and Platforms Experiment), suggesting that substantial inter-individual variability may limit the practical utility of single time-point CORT measurements as a reliable welfare indicator at the group level, and repeated sampling throughout the experimental period may better capture the dynamic stress response than a single endpoint measurement, regardless of the analytical method employed.

The analytical framework used here successfully quantified elevated CORT concentrations following oral exogenous dosing in a prior experiment, where birds received daily administration of 0 to 5 mg CORT/kg (Soster et al., 2025; Soster et al., conditionally accepted), confirming that the method can detect CORT when present at sufficient concentrations. The low values observed in the present study therefore most likely reflect genuinely low endogenous CORT production under the welfare challenges applied when a precise method is used, rather than analytical failure.

However, some limitations should be considered. First, the UPLC–MS/MS method was validated only from male broilers under controlled conditions, without evaluation of matrix effects, sample stability, or comparison with ELISA, limiting broader applicability and cross-study comparability. Handling such as blood sampling, constrains to take feces and feathers constitute additional stressors may have elevated baseline CORT measurements. Furthermore, heat stress-induced increases in water consumption and litter moisture may have introduced additional welfare challenges beyond the intended experimental conditions. Future studies should include parallel quantification of CORT by both UPLC–MS/MS and ELISA within the same experimental setup, to enable direct methodological comparison and improve cross-study validation. In conclusion, CORT concentrations measured by UPLC–MS/MS were low and did not differ among treatments in either experiment, most likely reflecting genuinely low endogenous CORT production rather than analytical failure, while high inter-individual CORT variability further limits its utility as a group-level welfare indicator at a single time point.
