Flufenamic acid-based sulfonohydrazide and acetamide derivatives NSAI as inhibitors of multi-targets COX-1/COX-2/5-LOX: design, synthesis, in silico ADMET and binding mode studies
Authors: Shaimaa Hussein, Eman A. Fayed, Ahmed Ragab, Moustafa S. Abusaif, Yousry A. Ammar, Reda El-Sayed Mansou, Arafa Musa, Triveena M. Ramsis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although inflammation triggers immune-mediated healing and repair, chronic inflammation can result in several diseases. Cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes are inhibited by NSAIDs, which are used to relieve the symptoms of inflammation. Meclofenamic and Zileuton are examples of dual COX/LOX inhibitors that provide improved stomach protection and safer cardiovascular characteristics. This study was aimed to develop anti-inflammatory medications with improved safety profiles by presenting novel flufenamate conjugates that combine 5-LOX inhibitor efficacy with COX inhibition. The COX-1 inhibition of compounds 14 and 15 was higher than that of Celecoxib (IC50 = 77.4 µM), with an IC50 range of 15–26 µM. Compounds 14 and 16 showed the best selectivity indices (ratio between IC50 of COX-1 and COX-2), which were 5.01 and 5.86 µM, respectively. Conjugates 14 and 16 displayed excellent COX-2 inhibiting activity, with IC50 values of 5.0 -17.6 µM. Outstanding 5-LOX inhibition was demonstrated by all conjugates, with IC50 values varying between 0.6 and 8.5 µM. In RAW 264.7 cells, compounds 14 and 15 significantly decreased PGE2 levels to a range of 61–89 pg/mL in contrast to Celecoxib (119.9 pg/mL). Compounds 14 and 17 showed exceptional NO scavenging action, with IC50 values of 0.238 × 106 and 0.289 × 106 µM, respectively. mTOR levels dramatically diminished for all conjugates. Conjugates 14 and 17 markedly raised levels nrf2. Molecular docking studies were used to validate the findings of this investigation. Introduction

Inflammation remains the body’s reaction to an external stimulus or tissue damage, which triggers immune-mediated systems of reparation and healing [1–3]. However, chronic inflammation loses its positive effects and becomes a contributing factor to the emergence of many diseases [4–6]. Inflammation occurs in individuals with bacterial, fungal, viral or parasite infections, gout, rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune disorders, and gastrointestinal problems [7–10]. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs) are commonly used to treat inflammation symptoms and signs [8, 11]. They function via inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX), which serve as an enzyme that is essential in the production of prostaglandins, which are biosynthesized from arachidonic acid. Cyclooxygenases are categorized into two isozymes: COX-1 and COX-2. In contrast to COX-2, which is generated during inflammation, fever, and pain, COX-1 is inherently present and has a number of roles, including cytoprotective activity [12–14].

Through the COX pathway, NSAIDs are the first line of therapeutic therapy for inflammatory conditions. The toxicity and gastrointestinal hemorrhage linked to prolonged NSAID use make the quest for new anti-inflammatory medications more challenging [15, 16].

COX-2 inhibitors are promoted as a novel class of NSAIDs with a mechanism of action that is less hazardous to the gastrointestinal tract than older NSAIDs, despite studies showing that long-term use of these medications can result in cardiovascular consequences and asthma attacks [17, 18].

Leukotrienes, which are produced by the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway, are connected to several processes, including the pathophysiology of asthmatic bronchitis, the formation of edema, leucocyte activation and adherence to vascular endothelium, and injury to the stomach mucosa [19–21]. Medicinal chemists seeking to develop new medications with great anti-inflammatory qualities and little side effects found this intriguing. Dual COX/LOX inhibitors may therefore have several therapeutic advantages over conventional NSAIDs, such as improved stomach protection, a better cardiovascular profile, and anti-inflammatory effect [22–25].

Through the use of hybridization methods, several ligand treatments can work in complementary ways on one or more targets, minimizing toxicity or adverse effects [26–32]. The first dual COX-2/5-LOX inhibitor to be sold commercially was benoxaprofen. Unfortunately, this medicine showed to be possibly dangerous in elderly adults who obtained an acute overdose due to cholestatic jaundice [33, 34]. Inconsistent clinical outcomes prevented tepoxalin and lidofelone, which were designed as dual COX/LOX inhibitors, from reaching the market [35, 36]. Moreover, research is being conducted on darbufelone, a dual inhibitor that targets COX-2 and 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX), as a possible anticonvulsant (Fig. 1) [37, 38]. As a result, dual COX/LOX inhibitor design and research are still critically needed in the market.Fig. 1Structure of withdrawn or unmarketed dual COX-2/5-LOX inhibitors

Meclofenamic and Zileuton are two examples of LOX inhibitors that are used in clinical practice to treat inflammatory illnesses, including asthma [39]. Meclofenamic inhibits the COX2 and 5-LOX enzymatic pathways [40, 41]. Conjugation approaches enable multiple-ligand treatments to operate on one or more targets in a complementary manner, minimizing side effects or toxicity. [27, 28, 42].

The Carbon- Fluorine bond has been thought as an excellent equivalent of the C-H bond with little steric impact, since fluorine, particularly, is the smallest substitution that can displace hydrogen [43–46]. In medicinal chemistry, the substitution of fluorine atoms for hydrogen atoms is frequently employed [47, 48]. The main reasons for this are undoubtedly fluorine's characteristics, which include strong electron withdrawal, small atomic size, and high lipophilicity [49–51]. Nevertheless, fluorine is important for altering binding affinities with target proteins, which may give target proteins a tremendous amount of binding energy, in addition to influencing metabolic stability [52–54]. Due to the popularity of fluoroquinolones and fluorocorticosteroids, the total number of fluoro-pharmaceuticals authorized by the FDA has steadily expanded over the past 20 years. Globally, about 300 fluoro-pharmaceuticals are registered [55–57].

Introducing the trifluoromethyl (-CF3) group to ligands has grown in popularity and is a viable lead optimization technique to improve protein-drug interaction for biological purposes [58, 59]. Prior research has shown that adding trifluoromethyl groups to particular ligand sites has led to the creation of inhibitors in drug discovery endeavors [60, 61]. Trifluoromethyl groups are found in many FDA-approved medications used to treat various illnesses (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Structures and Biological Activities of FDA-approved Fluorinated Drugs

Several FDA-approved drugs are based on the chemistry of sulfonamides functional group [62, 63]. Thyroiditis, glaucoma, inflammation, diuresis, and hypoglycemia are among the conditions that can be treated with sulfonamides in vivo due to their diverse pharmacological characteristics, which include carbonic anhydrase and dihydropteroate synthetase inhibition. Sulfadiazine is another frequently used sulphonamide drug that is used in conjunction with the anti-malarial drug pyrimethamine to manage toxoplasmosis in warm-blooded animals [64].

In addition, among anti-inflammatory drugs, sulfonamide can be found in certain COX-2 inhibitors on the market, namely, parecoxib and valdecoxib (Fig. 3), [65–68] as well as Sulfasalazine, an acknowledged DMARD (disease-modifying antirheumatic drug) used in the management of rheumatoid arthritis (Fig. 3) [69, 70]. Oxicams are a class of NSAIDs that block both COX isoforms, in order to treat acute and chronic inflammation [71, 72]. Their structures also involve a cyclic sulfonamide group. One of the most important oxicam derivatives is Meloxicam, which is a selective COX-2 inhibitor (Fig. 3) [73].Fig. 3Structures of some anti-inflammatory drugs containing sulfonamide group

Experimental

Chemistry

Synthesis of compounds 6–9

Procedure

In a beaker, the respective hydrazide or aniline (1 mmol) was dissolved in dichloromethane. Chloroacetylchloride (1 mmol, 0.11 g) was added to the solution dropwise. The reaction mixture was left to stir for 30 to 60 min. The formed precipitate was filtered, washed with water and left to dry to be used for the next step without further purification.

Synthesis of compound 13

2-Chlorobenzoic acid (1 mmol, 0.15 g) and 3-trifluoromethylaniline (1 mmol, 0.16 g) were dissolved and mixed in DMF. K2CO3 (2 mmol, 0.26 g), a catalytic amount of KI, and a catalytic amount of Cu metal were poured into the reaction mixture. The reaction mixture was refluxed for 24 h. Following finalization of the reaction, the reaction mixture was poured in ice water. Insoluble substance was formed and filtered. The filterate was acidified with HCl and the generated precipitate was filtered. Further purification was not necessary.

Synthesis of compounds 14–17

Procedure

Flufenamate Sodium 13 (1 mmol, 0.3 gm) was dissolved in dioxane. The respective chloroacetohydrazide or chloroacetamide (1 mmol) was added, and the reaction mixture was refluxed for 24 h. Following the reaction completion, the reaction mixture was poured into an ice beaker. A precipitate was formed, filtered then recrystallized from ethanol.

Materials and methods

Enzyme (COXs) Inhibition Assay/In vitro COX-1 & COX-2 inhibition assay

The Biovision COX-1/COX-2 Inhibitor Screening Kit (Catalog #: K548) and (Catalog #: K547) were used to evaluate the inhibitory effectiveness of novel compounds against COX-1 and COX-2, respectively. The assay process was carried out in accordance with the supplier's guidelines. Celecoxib and indomethacin served as reference standards. After interacting with its substrate, arachidonic acid, cyclooxygenase produces prostaglandin G2, which then interacts with a particular probe included in the kit to produce fluorescence that can be measured at λEx = 535 nm/λEm = 587 nm for five minutes at room temperature [74].

In vitro 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) inhibitory assay

Using an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) kit (catalogue no. 760700, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA), the study determined the IC50 values and assessed the inhibitory potency of tested substances against 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions and protocols [75].

Prostaglandin E2 assay

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Invitrogen-Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to maintain the RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cell line. 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL Penicillin, and 100 μg/mL Streptomycin were added to this culture medium to enrich it. The RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cell line (obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China)) was maintained in a CO2 incubator with a 5% CO2 atmosphere and at a controlled temperature of 37 °C. For 24 h, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS. To get rid of particle debris, the cell culture supernatants were quickly collected after the treatment and centrifuged at 1000×g for 15 min. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) were used to measure prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) levels in compliance with the manufacturer's guidelines [76, 77].

Nitric oxide (NO) scavenging assay

With the use of spectrophotometric techniques, the NO scavenging activity was measured. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used to generate different dilutions of the compounds under investigation, which were then added to different test tubes at varying concentrations (25, 50, 100, 250, and 500 μg/mL). In order to reach a total volume of 1.5 mL, a sodium nitroprusside solution (5 mM) in phosphate buffer was poured into each test tube. The solutions remained incubated for thirty minutes at a regulated temperature of 25 °C. Griess reagent, which contains 0.1% naphthylethylenediamine dichloride, 1% sulfanilamide, and 3% phosphoric acid, was then poured into each test tube at a volume of 1.5 mL. At a wavelength of 546 nm, the absorbance was immediately measured, and the scavenging activity was determined in relation to ascorbic acid, employed as the standard reference in terms of IC50 [78].

Western blot

After collecting RAW264.7 cells, ice-cold lysis solution was used to lyse them. Samples were separated using 12% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and aliquots were then placed onto PVDF membranes (BioRad) and left for 30 min at 2.5 A and 25 V employing a semi-dry electroblotter (Biorad, USA). 5% nonfat dried milk in TBS-T was used to block the membrane for two hours. Following blocking, primary antibodies were added to the membrane and incubated for the whole night at 4 °C. Anti-Nrf2 and anti-mTOR antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) and β-actin antibodies (Sigma) were the main antibodies utilized. TBS-T was then used to wash the blots three times for ten minutes each. After a further hour at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with the complementary secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA). Three TBS-T washes (10 min each) were then performed. Per the manufacturer's directions, the blot was coated with Western ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). ECL solution A and B were mixed in equal quantities and applied to membranes for one minute. A CCD camera-based imager (Chemi Doc Imager, Biorad, USA) was used to record the chemiluminescence signals, and ImageLab (Biorad, USA) was used to measure the band intensities. In order to identify the gel transfer zones for particular proteins and assess transfer effectiveness, protein-sized markers were utilized in every gel [79].

Statistical analysis

For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by posthoc analysis using the Tukey–Kramer test. Results are reported as mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance. The statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism software (version 8, ISI®, San Diego, CA, USA), and the graphics were created using the same program.

In silico ADMET prediction

All of the synthesized conjugates in this investigation were screened using the Swiss ADME (http://www.sib.swiss) interface, along with the standard reference medications, Indomethacin, Celecoxib and Zileuton. Chem Draw 19.0 was used to switch the molecular structures into the SMILES database. The Swiss ADME website was then employed to estimate the physicochemical and pharmacokinetics parameters [80, 81].

The anticipated toxicity of three reference anti-inflammatory medicines Zileutone, Celecoxib, and Indomethacin as well as the synthesized flufenamate conjugates, through pkCSM (http://biosig.unimelb.edu.au/pkcsm/prediction) and ProTox-II (https://tox-new.charite.de/protox) [82, 83].

Molecular docking

The 3D crystal structure of COX-1 (PDB ID: 2OYE, resolution: 2.85 Å) in complex with Indomethacin-(R)-alpha-ethyl-ethanolamide, COX-2 (PDB ID: 3LN1, resolution: 2.40 Å) in complex with Celecoxib, as well as 5-LOX (PDB ID: 3V99, resolution: 2.25 Å) in complex with arachidonic acid were obtained from the RCSB PDB (https://www.rscb.org) in.pdb format to be employed in this docking investigation [84, 85]. The protein structure was imported in Molecular Operating Environment (Montreal, QC, Canada) (MOE 2014.09) [86]. MMFF94x was the forcefield setting [87–91]. A database containing the produced chemicals'structures was loaded. Throughout the docking process, London dG was employed as a scoring function and Triangle Matcher was used for placement. As a refinement, the rigid receptor technique was employed [92–96].

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The conjugation of flufenamate Na+ salt with capping groups of chloroacetamide or chloroacetohydrazide served as the basis for the creation of our targeted compounds. The capping groups were synthesized according to Scheme 1. Chloroacetyl chloride was reacted with either benzenesulfonohydrazide derivatives, aniline derivatives, resulting into capping groups 6–9. Flufenamate Na+ salt 13 was prepared by refluxing 2-chlorobenzoic acid and 3-trifluoromethylaniline in presence of K2CO3, KI and copper metal, followed by addition of aqueous NaOH in ethanol (Scheme 2). The mechanism of synthesis of flufenamic acid is based on modified Ullmann reaction involving oxidative addition of aryl halide to copper(I) followed by reduction elimination of copper(III) organometallic intermediates to yield flufenamic acid, through two proposed pathways (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Mechanism of modified Ullmann reaction pathways through oxidative addition/reductive elimination

Mechanism of modified Ullmann reaction pathways through oxidative addition/reductive elimination

The targeted compounds 14–17 were synthesized by reacting to each of the capping groups with Flufenamate Na+ salt according to Scheme 3.

The structures of the target hybrids were verified using IR, 1H-NMR and 13C-NMR. 1H-NMR of all synthesized targets confirmed the presence of the methylene protons bridging the carbonyl and ester groups appearing downfield as a singlet at approximately δ 4.9 ppm, integrating for 2 protons. Signals of aromatic protons appeared between δ 7 and δ 8 ppm.

The 13C-NMR of all compounds confirmed the presence of 2 carbonyl groups with signals showing at approximately δ 168 and δ 167 ppm. The aromatic carbons signal appeared between δ 115 and δ 160 ppm. The most downfield aliphatic carbon signal appeared at approximately δ 64 ppm, representing the methylene carbon.

Additionally, the 13C-NMR of all compounds clearly displayed the 19F-13C splitting as quartet signals, where long range, 2 J, 3 J and 4 J were observed. The trifluoromethyl carbon showed at approximately δ 122 ppm with a J value of approximately 271 Hz. Long range, 2 J, 3 J and 4 J were observed in aromatic carbons with J values of approximately 32, 4 and 3 Hz, respectively.

The 1H-NMR spectra of compounds 14 and 15 confirmed the existence of two doublet NH signals at approximately δ 8.6 and δ 7.1 ppm, as well as one singlet NH signal at approximately δ 7.2 ppm, each integrating for 1 proton. The presence of aromatic protons was confirmed by signals integrating for 13 and 12 protons, respectively. The benzylic protons of compound 15 were shown as a singlet at δ 2.4 ppm, integrating for 3 protons.

The 13C-NMR of conjugate 15 confirmed the presence of the benzylic CH3 at approximately δ 20 ppm.

The 1H-NMR spectra of compounds 16 and 17 confirmed the existence of two singlet NH signals at approximately δ 9.7 and 7.2 ppm, each integrating for 1 proton. Signals of aromatic protons integrated for 12 protons, each.

In case of compound 16, the 13C-NMR clearly displayed additional 19F-13C splitting as quartet signals, where 1 J, as well as long range, 2 J, 3 J and 4 J were observed in trifluoromethyl carbon signal, as well as aromatic carbons with J values of approximately 271, 32, 4 and 3 Hz, respectively.

In case of compound 17, the 13C-NMR clearly displayed additional 19F-13C splitting as doublet signals, where 1 J, as well as long range, 2 J, 3 J and 4 J were observed in aromatic carbon signals with J values of approximately 246, 22, 9 and 3 Hz, respectively.

Biology

In vitro COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitory assay

The inhibitory effectiveness of tested conjugates for COX-1 and COX-2 isozymes was investigated and the results were reported as IC50 values. Moreover, the COX-2 selectivity index (SI value), defined as COX-1 IC50/COX-2 IC50 was determined for the designed hybrids. SI value greater than 1 indicates better selectivity towards COX-2 isozyme. Additionally, the calculated selectivity index values were compared to those of Celecoxib and indomethacin, (Table 1). The findings demonstrated that conjugates 14, and 15, displayed excellent inhibitory effect on the COX-1 isozyme in comparison to Celecoxib, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) range of 25.3 and 15.3 µM, respectively (Fig. 6). All conjugates except 17 were found to display excellent inhibitory activity against COX-2 enzyme, with IC50 values ranging from 5.0 to 17.6 µM. In the context of selectivity indices (SI), all conjugates, displayed SI ratios greater than 1, indicating promising selectivity towards COX-2. Importantly, numerous investigations have clarified the influence of Nrf2 on the activity of NF-kB. Notably, the lack of Nrf2 has been found to intensify NF-kB activity, leading to an increased secretion of inflammatory cytokines [101, 102]. In contrast, the enhancement of Nrf2 expression has demonstrated the capacity to alleviate inflammatory reactions in animal models of hepatic and renal injury [103, 104]. These observations underscore the essential function of Nrf2 as a vital modulator of the inflammatory environment, providing protection against excessive inflammation and its adverse effects. The inhibitory effects of the novel hybrids on COX-1, COX-2 and 5-LOX, were observed to conduct SAR study. Conclusion

Dual COX-2/5-LOX inhibition is an emerging approach in the development of anti-inflammatory medications. Inhibiting prostaglandins and leukotrienes, two significant mediators of inflammation, is the primary rationale underlying the creation of COX-2/5-LOX dual inhibitors. In order to create anti-inflammatory medications with improved safety profiles, novel hybrids with desirable dual COX-2 and 5-LOX inhibitory actions were created. The COX-1 inhibition of two conjugates was higher than that of Celecoxib, with an IC50 range of 15–26 µM. Conjugates 14, 15, and 16, showed excellent COX-2 enzyme inhibition, with IC50 values of 5–18 µM, where compounds 14 as well as 16 exhibited the best SI values of 5.01 and 5.68, respectively. All flufenamate conjugates showed effective 5-LOX inhibition with IC50 values extending from 0.6 to 8.5 µM. Conjugates 14–15 resulted in significant reduction in PGE2 levels down to a range of 61–89 pg/mL. Compounds 14 and 17 demonstrated outstanding NO scavenging activity, with IC50 values of 0.238 × 106 and 0.289 × 106 µM. The majority of compounds significantly decreased in mTOR levels and significantly increased nrf2 levels. The results of this investigation were confirmed through molecular docking studies. Such findings are promising for further in vivo studies in our future work.
