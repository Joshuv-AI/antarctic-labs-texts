Community care for individuals with borderline personality disorder in Japan: a narrative review
Authors: Miwa Sasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: To synthesize Japanese literature on community care for individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD) and identify key providers, care settings, support components, and implementation challenges to inform primary care involvement in community BPD care.Methods: We searched the Ichushi-Web and CiNii Research databases for Japanese-language publications on community-based care for individuals with BPD in Japan indexed through December 2025. We extracted data and synthesized findings regarding care settings and providers, support content, collaboration, and implementation challenges.Results: The analysis included 24 publications (17 commentaries or feature articles and seven research articles or reports). Reported support included community living and crisis support, discharge and transition support, perinatal and family support, and interagency coordination. Collaboration often relied on personal relationships, and maintaining continuity of care was challenging.Conclusion: The Japanese literature describes practical approaches to community care for BPD. Strengthening team-based management and primary care-linked care pathways may improve continuity and safety.Keywords: borderline personality disorder, community care, multidisciplinary collaboration, primary care, Japan

Introduction

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is characterized by emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and unstable interpersonal relationships and self-image1). Its chronic course is often complicated by self-harm and suicidal behaviors. Because symptoms fluctuate, individuals with BPD are prone to recurrent crises, interrupted treatment, and inconsistent help-seeking, which can place substantial burdens on patients, families, and health care providers1, 2).

Internationally, care for BPD has evolved from provider-dependent practice to more comprehensive systems centered on evidence-based, structured psychotherapies, such as dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), mentalization-based treatment (MBT), and transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), alongside crisis management1). For example, the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline for BPD emphasizes systematic care, including pathways to specialist services, ongoing support, care coordination, and crisis response3). The American Psychiatric Association (APA) has also updated its guidance on assessment, treatment planning, and psychosocial interventions, reflecting progress in developing health-system frameworks for BPD4).

However, in routine practice, patient symptom fluctuations and interpersonal instability can affect the therapeutic relationship itself, sometimes eliciting strong emotional reactions (countertransference) among staff and leading to disagreements within teams regarding assessment and care policies5). These dynamics can contribute to provider fatigue and burnout6) and may disrupt continuity of care, thereby exacerbating crises. Accordingly, community care for BPD requires a shared approach across professionals and organizations, with clearly defined roles and information-sharing processes, so that support is delivered as a continuous pathway rather than as fragmented, episodic contact3, 7).

Although international guidelines for BPD are increasingly available8) and related guidance has been developed in Japan9), challenges remain in disseminating standard structured psychotherapies overseas and in implementing multidisciplinary, team-based community care, as recommended by NICE3). Moreover, domestic knowledge of BPD-related support is often fragmented across domains, including direct support for individuals, family, and providers, as well as across the various settings in which care is delivered (medical care, public health, welfare, and others). For example, in the domain of family support, the significance of family support groups involving the parents of young people with mental disorders has been reported10). However, such findings tend to remain confined to specific domains. Consequently, systematically integrating cross-sector knowledge is difficult, and implementable collaboration models and barriers are not well organized.

Specialized treatment resources are unevenly distributed across regions. In regions with limited access to specialized institutions and professionals, the design and operation of collaborative systems for crisis response and ongoing support can strongly influence care outcomes. BPD is relatively prevalent in primary care settings11), and while community-based practitioners are often the first point of contact for patients, these practitioners are also frequently required to coordinate referrals to specialized psychiatric services and liaise with administrative and welfare systems. Therefore, clarifying the practical issues and key operational points for implementing community care through a review of the Japanese literature is of practical importance.

This review aimed to examine the Japanese literature on community care for BPD and to clarify (1) the professionals and settings involved in community support, (2) the types of support provided and forms of collaboration, and (3) barriers to sustaining support and implementation challenges. We synthesized these findings and provided practical suggestions for the implementation of community care, including primary care.

Methods

Literature search

In this narrative review, we conducted a literature search of the Ichushi-Web and CiNii Research databases to map existing knowledge on community care for people with BPD in Japan. The search covered publications indexed up to December 2025 (search date: December 31, 2025). Search terms related to BPD (e.g., borderline personality disorder), community care settings (e.g., community, home-based, outpatient, home-visit nursing, public health nurse, outreach, community mental health, welfare, emergency, multidisciplinary collaboration), and support/collaboration (e.g., support, care, intervention, program, coordination, and information sharing) were combined. To minimize omissions, we also searched the J-STAGE database and screened the reference lists of the included articles.

Selection criteria and data integration methods

From the eligible literature, we extracted information on (1) care settings and providers, (2) support content and collaboration methods (e.g., information sharing, referral and back-referral, crisis communication, role allocation, and coordination for community transition), and (3) barriers to sustaining support and implementation challenges. We organized the findings thematically, compared descriptions of collaborations and challenges across publications, and integrated similar content.

The author selected the articles and extracted the data. The reasons for exclusion were documented to enable verification and enhance transparency and procedural consistency. As this was a literature review and did not involve human participants, it was exempt from ethical approval.

In Japan, knowledge of community care for people with BPD is presented not only in research articles, but also in commentaries, case reports, guidelines, and other formats. Because implementation issues are difficult to capture solely through effectiveness evaluations, this review prioritized practical knowledge such as provider roles, collaboration procedures, and organizational frameworks. Accordingly, we included commentaries and special features, emphasizing descriptions of community care components and practice-relevant discussion points, rather than methodological rigor.

The inclusion criteria were a clear association between BPD and community care, a description of care settings or providers, support content, collaboration, and challenges in sustaining support. We excluded studies that focused only on inpatient treatment, lacked descriptions of community-based support, or did not primarily address individuals with BPD.

Results

Overview of the literature selection

The literature search yielded 45 records from Ichushi-Web and 18 from CiNii Research (a total of 63). After removing six duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 57 records were screened, and five proceedings (including conference abstracts) were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 52 records were assessed, and 22 studies that met the inclusion criteria were included. Manual searches of the reference lists identified two additional publications, resulting in a final set of 24 publications (17 reviews/special features and seven research articles/reports). Characteristics of the included publications

Among the 24 included publications, 17 were reviews or special features (general overviews), and seven were research articles or reports. The reviews and special features mainly addressed conceptual understanding and clinical responses to BPD, perinatal mental health care, parenting challenges among mothers with BPD, and activity reports on community support for individuals and families in Japan. Settings and support providers

Most of the seven research articles were case studies or practical reports covering settings such as community living support, psychiatric emergency care, child welfare (including infant care facilities), adolescent psychiatric outpatient clinics, community transition support, and perinatal (obstetric and neonatal) care. In addition to psychiatric professionals, supporters included emergency and somatic care providers, midwives and maternal health nurses, psychologists and childcare staff in child welfare facilities, public health nurses, and community social workers.

Support content and collaboration methods

The support content of the research articles and reports was organized into the following categories: (1) community life support (ongoing support by public health nurses and others), (2) crisis-avoidance and crisis-prevention interventions, (3) psychological support and psychoeducation in child welfare settings, (4) outpatient and adolescent support, (5) pre- and post-discharge coordination for community transition and self-care support, and (6) safety assurance and life support, including perinatal and mother-child support.

Community life support (ongoing support by public health nurses, etc.)12)

In community living support, public health nurses led care by building trust and helping individuals with BPD to rebuild their daily lives. Activities also included providing advice and mediation within families and raising community awareness to reduce stigma. Collaboration often involved sharing care policies and plans through meetings and other coordination among healthcare staff.

Interventions for crisis avoidance and prevention13)

In psychiatric emergencies and other acute-care settings, in addition to ensuring safety and providing medical responses to repeated self-harm or suicide-related behaviors, the reports described short-term crisis-avoidance interventions and support aimed at preventing recurrence. The interventions included treatment agreements to maintain motivation, as well as elements of cognitive-behavioral therapy. Collaboration among emergency medicine (including psychiatric consultations), psychiatry, and general medicine was also noted.

Psychological support in the field of child welfare14)

In child welfare settings (such as infant homes), psychological support for children abused by mothers with BPD included play therapy and other methods to verbalize confusion and restore a sense of security. Coordination between facility staff and support frameworks, including psychoeducation for mothers and guardians, was also implemented. Collaborative efforts included cooperation between psychologists and childcare staff, sharing information with mothers and guardians, and coordination among relevant organizations.

Outpatient and adolescent support15)

For adolescent females with BPD, including those with comorbid developmental disorders, the included reports described support to prevent repeated suicide attempts, including psychoeducation for patients and guardians, introduction of treatment, and support for family functioning.

Coordination for community transition16)

Nursing interventions for individuals with BPD who exhibit acting-out behaviors leading to early rehospitalization after discharge include the rebuilding of daily life through self-care support. Beyond individual interventions, the included reports also described the importance of formulating pre- and post-discharge support plans and sharing and coordinating information with relevant organizations in preparation for community transition.

Perinatal and mother-child support17,18)

In the perinatal field, discharge support ensures newborn safety while addressing symptom exacerbation and life instability in mothers with BPD, from pregnancy through the postpartum period. The reports described efforts to balance mother-child safety with support continuity through the use of social resources (e.g., adjusted living arrangements and short-term admissions) and sharing information among supporters. Collaborative approaches to perinatal care included multidisciplinary partnerships and conferences involving obstetrics, psychiatry, maternal and child health, and community support.

Factors impeding continued support and implementation challenges

The challenges to sustained support in community living included the development of community support systems and the expansion of social support networks12). Maintaining treatment agreements, sustaining motivation, and responding to recurrent crises were identified as issues13). In the field of child welfare, the challenges included providing support while understanding mothers without blaming them and coordinating multidisciplinary collaboration14). Other challenges included diagnosis and interventions considering comorbidities and continued responses to suicide risk15), difficulties in handling early rehospitalization or acting-out behavior after discharge16), ensuring safety and establishing systems to prevent child abuse17), creating seamless support systems, and promoting an interdisciplinary understanding of pregnancy through the postpartum period18).

Overall, the Japanese literature suggests that community care for people with BPD often starts with a crisis response and is then linked to welfare, administrative, and family support. Across settings, collaborative operations such as information sharing, role allocation, and discharge/transition coordination were repeatedly described as key to sustaining support, indicating a stronger emphasis on “support systems” rather than on specific intervention techniques.

Discussion

In this narrative review, we summarized current Japanese literature on community care for people with BPD. Most of the included sources were commentaries or special features, and primary research was limited. However, research articles and reports described practices in which support begins not only in psychiatric outpatient settings but also in sectors where crises and life difficulties often emerge, such as emergency care, perinatal care, child welfare, and community transition support. These findings suggest that support for people with BPD in Japan cannot be delivered solely through disorder-specific specialist services and instead depends on the involvement of multiple community sectors and providers. Recent domestic reports have highlighted the limited diagnostic and management capacity and treatment resources for BPD, underscoring the need for feasible models of community-based operations that can sustain local support19).

Issues in implementing community care: From crisis response to continued support, systematizing collaborative operations.

The included research articles and reports described crisis interventions for self-harm, suicidal behaviors, and safety management in emergency settings13). In the perinatal field, reports noted difficulties in providing support when symptoms worsen during pregnancy and the postpartum period17, 18). Because such practices often focus on crisis response, they risk reinforcing short-term care rather than sustained support16). Therefore, crisis responses should include operational elements that promote continuity, such as early follow-up after emergency visits or discharge and shared regularly updated care plans. In settings with limited access to specialist resources, community-based crisis response systems must be designed; for example, by clarifying criteria for consultation and organizing emergency contact pathways20, 21).

Reports on community life support highlighted barriers such as underdeveloped support frameworks and limited social support12). Other challenges included early post-discharge acting out, leading to rehospitalization, stagnated interagency support, and delayed responses to perinatal support16,17,18). Individualized crisis responses and the burden of cross-sector coordination were also identified. Accordingly, reviewing linkages among community crisis resources (psychiatric emergency services, general emergency services, and administrative and welfare agencies) and identifying and strengthening points that are prone to dysfunction is important.

When access to specialist treatment resources is limited, the availability of crisis support and coordinated operations can more greatly impact care outcomes7, 11). Primary care can simultaneously address comorbid physical conditions and life problems and may contribute to the early identification of crisis signs, coordinate access to psychiatric care, and provide information to families22). Effective primary care function requires role-sharing with psychiatric specialists and psychiatric emergency services, clarification of administrative/welfare resources, and sharing of emergency contact routes and response scope20, 23). One pragmatic approach is to define and operate a minimum collaborative package with limited resources. Such packages may include shared and updated care and crisis response plans, unified contact points and communication methods, early follow-up after emergency visits or discharge, clear criteria for referral and back referral, and regular interdisciplinary review meetings24, 25).

This review did not stratify the literature based on the availability of specialist resources (e.g., rural or remote areas) and, therefore, did not draw community-type-specific conclusions. However, issues such as unclear access points, inconsistent care policies, inadequate discharge, and transition coordination are likely to be more pronounced in resource-limited settings. Depending on local characteristics, clarifying emergency contacts and re-consultation criteria, agreeing on role-sharing and referral and back-referral criteria, creating opportunities for case reflection and supervision, and incorporating family support may represent a minimum set of elements for implementing community care, including primary care. Building routine collaborations across mental health, general medical, and welfare services may reduce confusion during crises and help sustain support.

Implementing community care for BPD, including primary care, requires not only improved diagnostic certainty and access to structured psychotherapies, but also the establishment of operational systems that can respond to crises and relationship fluctuations7, 11, 20,21,22,23,24,25). Key elements include ensuring safety during crises (e.g., risk assessment for self-harm and encouraging timely care-seeking); consistent responses even during brief contacts (e.g., shared boundaries and explanations); consultation and referral pathways to specialist psychiatric services; and information sharing with administrative, welfare, and family stakeholders. As these processes can easily become dependent on individual providers, the first step is to inventory community resources and clarify and share procedures for consultation, emergency contact, and community transition20, 22).

Foundations of support: support for supporters and families

Care providers for people with BPD may experience intense emotional reactions, sometimes described as countertransference5). Rather than a personal issue, this should instead be recognized as a relational clinical phenomenon, and opportunities should be provided for reflection, verbalization, and adjustment. Provider exhaustion and burnout can undermine the quality and continuity of community services6). Moreover, disagreements in assessment and policy can also lead to “team splitting”, which fragments care teams26). Sustained community-based support requires mechanisms such as case conferences, supervision, and consultation to manage emotional reactions and care policies at the team rather than the individual level. In resource-limited areas with few alternative services, provider support should be treated not as optional but as a prerequisite for sustaining care27).

Families of people with BPD often play key roles in crisis safety management and continuity of care, but also carry substantial burdens, underscoring the importance of family support28, 29). However, primary research on family support, specifically for BPD, is limited in Japan, making it difficult to accumulate implementable models. The Japanese version of Family Connections (FCJ), a psychoeducation program for families coping with emotional dysregulation, reportedly reduces family burden30) and may be a promising community-based option. Additionally, reports from family associations on youth mental health suggest that peer forums for parents can reduce reluctance to seek help and feelings of isolation10). For community care, the essential elements include providing families with information, sharing crisis contact methods, and ensuring that they have their own channels for consultation and support to promote the sustainability of care.

Study limitations and future challenges

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, we did not apply systematic methods for comprehensive literature coverage, formal quality appraisal, or standardized synthesis, which are typical of systematic reviews. Second, because the search was largely limited to Japanese-language publications in the Ichushi-Web and CiNii Research databases, unpublished materials and municipal-level practical knowledge might not have been captured. Third, most of the included sources were overviews, case reports, or practice reports; we did not identify any studies that evaluated intervention effects or outcomes. Therefore, the implications summarized here should be interpreted as provisional insights into the components and operational issues of community care from the perspective of feasibility rather than as evidence of efficacy.

Future studies should examine how differences in community coordination and operational models influence outcomes, such as follow-up after crisis intervention, rehospitalization or repeat emergency visits, self-harm-related behaviors, and family and provider burdens. In addition, informed by existing policy research on crisis intervention22) and current diagnostic and management practices in Japan19), further implementation studies are needed to identify the minimum feasible elements for resource-limited areas and compare replicable components with those that can be adapted to local contexts.

Conclusion

The results of this narrative review of the Japanese-language literature suggest that community care for people with BPD in Japan often starts with a crisis response and that support has developed across sectors, such as psychiatric emergency care, perinatal care, and child welfare. However, the reliance on personal connections for collaboration, inconsistencies in support policies, difficulties in discharge and community transition, and insufficient attention to provider and family burden were identified as barriers to sustaining support. To ensure continuity and safety, community care for BPD should include not only support but also coordination and operational mechanisms across the entire care system, including primary care.
