The ubiquitin E3 ligase TRIM59 in myeloid cell regulates complement C3 through degrading CCAAT/enhancer binding protein β to promote neuron survival after ischemic stroke
Authors: Xiang Li, Xinjuan Tian, Jingjing Zhang, Mengtian Pan, Baohui Xu, Jie Wei, Dongmei Yan, Weirong Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic stroke is a cerebrovascular disease that activates the complement system, leading to inflammation and neuronal injury. TRIM59, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, has been extensively studied in cancer, sepsis, and myocardial infarction, and is upregulated in macrophages after ischemic stroke. This study used myeloid cell Trim59 conditional knockout and macrophage-specific Trim59 overexpression mice to investigate the role of macrophage TRIM59 in regulating complement C3 release and neuronal phagocytosis following cerebral ischemia.MethodsMyeloid cell Trim59 conditional knockout (Trim59-cKO) mice were generated and bred using the Cre-loxP system, and macrophage-specific Trim59 overexpressing (AAV-Trim59) mice were constructed via intra-bone marrow injection. Middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) and photothrombotic (PT) surgery were used to mimic the cerebral ischemia in mice. RT-qPCR, ELISA and immunofluorescence staining were performed to detect the expression of complement molecules and synaptic engulfment. Neurobehavioral tests were performed during recovery phase. Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay were utilized to analyze the effect of TRIM59 on C3 expression and its transcription factor CCAAT/enhancer binding protein β (c/EBPβ).ResultsCompared to Trim59flox/flox mice, Trim59-cKO promoted excessive expression and release of complement components C3 and C9 in macrophages after cerebral ischemia, resulting in aggravated pathological injury and reduced neuronal survival. In contrast, macrophage-specific TRIM59 overexpression reversed the above results and alleviated acute-phase injury. Furthermore, deficiency of Trim59 led to excessive macrophage-derived C3 deposition around neurons, enhanced phagocytic activity, and aggravated synaptic loss during the acute phase after MCAO/R injury, ultimately causing delayed recovery of cerebral blood flow, impaired learning and memory function, and lower survival rates during the chronic phase after PT induced ischemic stroke. The transcription factor c/EBPβ was identified as a key mediator of macrophage TRIM59-dependent regulation of C3 expression, verified by silencing of c/EBPβ in Trim59 knockout macrophages could attenuate the neuronal C3 deposition and synaptic damage. Further research shows that TRIM59 promotes K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of c/EBPβ through its RING domain.ConclusionMacrophage TRIM59 is critical for controlling excessive complement C3 activation, release, and phagocytosis after ischemic stroke by promoting ubiquitin-mediated degradation of its transcription factor c/EBPβ, providing a potential therapeutic target for cerebral ischemia.

Introduction

Stroke is a cerebrovascular disease that causes acute neurological damage due to blockage or bleeding of cerebral blood vessels, which is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. Ischemic stroke is the most common type, and incidence rate is higher than hemorrhagic stroke and subarachnoid hemorrhage [2]. Due to the limited treatment options for ischemic stroke and the narrow therapeutic time window of recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (rt-PA) [3], it is still urgent to find new potential targets and interventions for ischemic stroke.

Peripheral immune cells migrate and infiltrate at the site of the ischemic lesions following ischemic stroke, further producing complement components and exacerbating the complement cascade [4, 5]. The complement system can be activated through three pathways: the classical pathway, the alternative pathway, and the lectin pathway, with complement C3 being the convergence point of these pathways and playing a pivotal role in innate immunity [6]. It is the central hub of the complement cascade, leading to the generation of effector molecules that mediate opsonization, inflammation, and cell lysis. Dysregulation of C3 activation has been implicated in various inflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases, including ischemic stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, and multiple sclerosis, where excessive complement activation contributes to neuronal and synaptic injury [7]. It has been shown that microglia/macrophages play a key role in the production of C3 and the activation of the complement system [8].

The tripartite motif (TRIM) family proteins, composed of more than 80 members, are highly conserved in structure, featuring RING, B-box, and Coiled-coil domains from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. TRIM59 is a member of the TRIM family with E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, and has been associated with the progression of a variety of diseases, including tumors, sepsis, and myocardial infarction [9–11]. TRIM59 was upregulated in the ischemic brain, and was predominantly expressed in microglia/macrophages in our previous study. Myeloid cell Trim59 conditional knockout (Trim59-cKO) exhibited higher cerebral infarct size and neurological deficits on day 3 after MCAO/R as compared to littermate Trim59flox/flox mice. Proteomic sequencing results further revealed that complement cascade signaling pathway was differentially expressed between the two genotypes [12].

In this research, we found that myeloid cell-specific Trim59 deficiency exacerbated complement activation, increased C3 expression and deposition, and aggravated neuronal and synaptic loss, whereas macrophage Trim59 overexpression reversed the above results. Mechanistically, TRIM59 acts as the E3 ubiquitin ligase for the transcription factor c/EBPβ, promoting its K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby downregulating complement C3 expression. TRIM59 holds the potential to become a novel therapeutic target for stroke, and our findings provide a direction for future research and clinical applications.

Material and methods

Animals

Male mice aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 20–25 g, were fed ad libitum at 20–24 °C in alternation between day and night. All mouse strains were from a C57BL/6N genetic background. Wild type (WT) C57BL/6N mice were purchased from Qinglongshan Animal Breeding Centre (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Mice expressing Loxp-flanked Trim59 (Trim59flox/flox mice) were obtained from the Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) through CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. LysM-Cre mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were crossed with Trim59flox/flox mice to generate myeloid cell-specific Trim59 conditioned knockout (Trim59-cKO) mice and their littermate controls (Trim59flox/flox mice). Genotyping results can be found in a prior study [12]. The experimental procedures and use of animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of China Pharmaceutical University (approval number: 2021-03-020) and followed the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animal (https://oacu.oir.nih.gov/regulations-standards). All experiments were designed and performed according to the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/).

Cell culture

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from 8-week-old C57BL/6N mice as previously described [10]. Briefly, bone marrow cells were flushed from the femurs and tibias of mice using ice-cold PBS. Cells were collected and cultured for 7 days in DMEM supplemented with 10% fatal bovine serum and 30% conditioned medium from L929 cells. To establish the inflammatory model, BMDMs were incubated with 200 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for different times. RAW264.7 cells (ATCC, TIB-71), a murine macrophage-like cell line, and HEK293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum. All the cell types were cultured in a cell incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air.

Primary mice cortical neurons were cultured as described previously [13], with minor modification. Primary mice cortical neurons were prepared from embryonic-day-18 brains. The cerebral cortices were digested with papain (2 mg/ mL, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and DNase solution (0.125 mg/mL, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C. The dissociated neurons were cultured in Neurobasal medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 2% B27 Supplement (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The medium was renewed every 2 days until the cells appeared in neuronal morphology. The primary neurons were treated with oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) to mimic cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury in vitro [14]. Briefly, Neurobasal medium was replaced with DMEM and cells were transferred to a 5% CO2 and 95% N2 atmospheric incubator for 2 h at 37 °C. After that, neurons were cultured in Neurobasal medium again and maintained in a 5% CO2 atmospheric incubator for the indicated time periods. After treating BMDMs under different conditions, the culture medium was collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the supernatant, which was used as the conditioned medium [15]. Primary cortical neurons were treated under different conditions, and then the conditioned medium from BMDMs was added to the neurons at a ratio of 1:1. The neurons were co-cultured with the conditioned medium for 24 h before subsequent experiments were performed.

Plasmid and siRNA transfection

Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime, C0533, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plasmid used in this study was pLV3-CMV-TRIM59(mouse)-3 × FLAG-CopGFP-Puro (Miaoling Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China).

For siRNA transfection, AccuFect RNAi Transfection Reagent (Accurate Biology, AG51017, Changsha, Hunan, China) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. The siRNAs (GenePharma Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) were as follows:

si c/EBPβ-1 sense: 5′-ACUUCUACUACGAGCCCGATT-3′

si c/EBPβ-1 antisense: 5′-UCGGGCUCGUAGUAGAAGUTT-3′

si c/EBPβ-2 sense: 5′-GACUACGGUUACGUGAGCCTT-3′

si c/EBPβ-2 antisense: 5′-GGCUCACGUAACCGUAGUCTT-3′

si c/EBPβ-3 sense: 5′-CCCGCGGACUGCAAGCGCGTT-3′

si c/EBPβ-3 antisense: 5′-CGCGCUUGCAGUCCGCGGGTT-3′

si NC sense: 5′-UUCUUCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′

si NC antisense: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′

Middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) surgery

The mouse MCAO/R model was performed according to a previous study [12]. In brief, mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and maintained at 1% to 2% isoflurane using a face mask. The right common carotid artery, external carotid artery, and internal carotid artery were carefully separated. A silicon-coated nylon filament (Cat No. 0624, Yushun Biotech, Pingdingshan, Henan, China) was slowly inserted into the internal carotid artery from the common carotid artery and gently advanced about 10 mm to block the blood supply to the middle cerebral artery. After 1 h occlusion, the filament was withdrawn for reperfusion. Sham mice underwent the same operation, except for the occlusion of the middle cerebral artery. The body temperature of mice was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with a surface heating pad during the surgery and recovery period.

Trim59 overexpression with adeno-associated virus (AAV)

Three weeks prior to MCAO/R surgery, Trim59flox/flox mice were subjected to intra-bone marrow injection as previously described [16]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, the hair around the knee joint was shaved, and the skin was disinfected with iodophor. Subsequently, a Hamilton syringe was inserted into the joint surface of the tibia through the patellar tendon and then inserted into the bone marrow cavity. Then, 20 μL of AAV2/9-F4/80-Trim59-ZsGreen or AAV2/9-F4/80-ZsGreen (1.5 × 1012 vg/ mL, Hanbio Tech, Shanghai, China) was injected at a rate of 2 μL/min. The needle was maintained in situ for 10 min and then slowly withdrawn to avoid virus efflux. After injection, the skin incision was sutured and mice were placed in a warm cage to recover from anesthesia prior to being returned to the home cage.

Photothrombotic (PT) stroke surgery

Cortical photothrombosis was induced using the Rose Bengal method [17]. Anesthetized mice were placed in a stereotaxic frame, the skull was exposed through a midline scalp incision and Rose Bengal (80 mg/kg, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was injected intraperitoneally. After 5 min, the skull and underlying brain tissue were illuminated for 10 min by a cold light source (Z-LITE, World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA) positioned at anterior–posterior + 0.5 mm and medial–lateral—2.7 mm relative to Bregma, targeting the border between left primary somatosensory and motor cortex [18]. Sham mice received the same dose of Rose Bengal without illumination.

5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining

Mice were sacrificed under deep anesthesia 72 h after MCAO/R surgery and brains were quickly removed. The brains were cut into five 2-mm-thick coronal sections and immersed in 2% TTC (Solarbio, Beijing, China) solution at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. The serial sections were photographed by camera and analyzed by Image J software (version 1.52v, http://imagej.net). To avoid the influence of cerebral edema, the infarct size was calculated as follows: Infarct size (%) = (contralateral area − ipsilateral non infarct area)/contralateral area × 100 [19].

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

After 72 h of reperfusion, mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused sequentially with cold PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde. The brain tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h, then paraffin-embedded and sectioned into 5 µm thick sections. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, and then with eosin for 3 min. After dehydration and clearing, sections were mounted with neutral resin. The brain tissue injury was observed and photographed under an optical microscope (MSHOT MF52-N, Guangzhou Micro-shot Technology Co., Ltd, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The histopathological scoring criteria for H&E staining results are as follows: 0 = normal, 1 = minor cell damage, no vacuoles around the cells, 2 = obvious cell damage, with a few vacuoles around the cells, 3 = obvious cell damage, with obvious vacuoles around the cells, 4 = a large number of cells are damaged, with a large number of vacuoles around the cells [20].

Nissl staining

The brain sections were prepared as previously described. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were stained in Nissl staining solution (Powerful Biology, Wuhan, Hubei, China) for 5 min. Excess dye was rinsed off under running water, the sections were dried at 65 °C, and then mounted with neutral resin. The images of the infarct and peri-infarct areas of damaged cortex were obtained using an optical microscope (MSHOT MF52-N, Guangzhou Micro-shot Technology Co., Ltd, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). Neurons with round and pale-stained nuclei were considered surviving, while shrunken cells with pyknotic nuclei were considered damaged. The number of surviving neurons was quantified using Image J software. Surviving neurons (%) = number of surviving neurons/number of total neurons × 100 [21]. Brain atrophy at 21 days after PT stroke was also measured by Nissl staining. Tissue loss was expressed as a percentage of the contralateral hemisphere with the following formula: Tissue loss (%) = (contralateral area − ipsilateral non-infarct area)/contralateral area × 100 [22].

Cerebral blood flow (CBF) measurement using laser speckle flow imaging

The Moor FLPI-2 laser speckle blood flow imaging system (Moor Instruments, Axminster, Devon, UK) was used for dynamic monitoring of CBF in mice. Measurements were performed before PT modeling, on day 0 after modeling, and on day 21 after modeling. After anesthetizing the mice with isoflurane, they were fixed in a prone position, and the skull was fully exposed. During the detection process, sterile normal saline was dripped to keep the surface of the skull moist. The laser speckle imaging covered the middle cerebral artery area, with the image acquisition frame rate set to 25 Hz, and 300 consecutive frames of images were collected for each mouse. The region of interest (ROI) was delineated using analysis software to determine the local CBF perfusion signal. The relative CBF (rCBF) at each time point after surgery was calculated with the baseline CBF of the same mouse before modeling as the control, and statistical analysis was performed based on the calculated rCBF values.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mice were sacrificed on day 3 following the MCAO/R surgery. Brain homogenates were prepared by mixing ischemic brain tissue with PBS (10 mg tissue/100 µL) and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min to collect the supernatants. The culture supernatants of cells treated under different conditions were collected by centrifuging at 2000 rpm for 20 min. The levels for complement C3 and C9 were determined using corresponding ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serial dilutions of the provided standards were used to generate standard curves, with standard concentration plotted on the x-axis and absorbance values (OD at 450 nm) plotted on the y-axis. Only standard curves with a correlation coefficient (R2) ≥ 0.99 were accepted for quantitative analysis. The concentrations of C3 and C9 in each sample were calculated by interpolation from the corresponding standard curve equation according to the measured OD values. Each sample was assayed in quadruplicate wells, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The total RNA from ischemic brain tissue and cultured cells was extracted using the RNAex Pro Reagent and SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, Hunan, China). Afterwards, cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, Hunan, China). The qPCR analysis of cDNA was performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, Hunan, China) on the LineGene 9600 Plus Fluorescent Quantitative Detection System (Bioer Technology, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). β-actin was used as the internal reference gene. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, where ΔCt was defined as Ct_target gene—Ct_β-actin, and ΔΔCt was normalized to the corresponding control group. Data were presented as fold changes relative to control. A single peak in the melting curve was considered indicative of specific amplification. Each sample was analyzed in quadruplicate technical replicates, and the mean Ct value was used for subsequent statistical analysis. The primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were as follows:

C3 forward: 5′-AGAAGCGTCTCCATCAAGATTCC-3′

C3 reverse: 5′-ACCACTGTCACGTACTTGTGC-3′

C9 forward: 5′-GGGTGTCAATGCACAGATGC-3′

C9 reverse: 5′-GAGGCAAGGATCACACTCTGA-3′

c/EBPβ forward:5′-ACTTCTACTACGAGCCCGACT-3′

c/EBPβ reverse:5′-CTCAGCTTGTCCACCGTCTT-3′

β-actin forward: 5′-AGAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3′

β-actin reverse: 5′-CAATAGTGATGACCTGGCCGT-3′

Immunofluorescence staining

At 72 h after reperfusion, mice were anesthetized and perfused intracardially with cold PBS followed by 4% polyformaldehyde. Brains were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde for 72 h and then embedded in paraffin to prepare 5-μm sections. Immunofluorescence staining of brain sections was performed using a Fluorescent Double-label Kit (AiFang biological, Changsha, Hunan, China) based on the tyramide signal amplifcation technique [12]. Briefly, brain sections were treated with EDTA antigen retrieval solution at 100 °C and endogenous peroxidase was inactivated using 3% H2O2. After blocking non-specific antigenic sites with 8% goat serum, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used in immunofluorescence were as follows: Trim59 (1:1000, Abcam, ab69639, Cambridge, MA, USA), C3 (1:1000, Proteintech Group, 21337-1-AP, Wuhan, Hubei, China), F4/80 (1:1000, Proteintech Group, 18705-1-AP, Wuhan, Hubei, China), NeuN (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, D4G40, Danvers, MA, USA), CD68 (1:200, Huan, HA722737, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), PSD95 (1:500, Huan, ET1602-20, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) and SYP (1:500, Huan, ET1606-56, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and the corresponding tyramide working solution. One representative brain section was collected from each animal, and standardized ROI was delineated in the peri-infarct area. Stained images were captured by a fluorescent microscope (MSHOT MF52-N, Guangzhou Micro-shot Technology Co., Ltd, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China), and all images were acquired under identical microscopic settings (exposure time, gain and resolution). Image J software (version 1.52v, http://imagej.net) was used to analyze the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), positive area ratio, and the number of double-positive cells. Notably, cellular MFI = integrated fluorescence intensity/number of cells within the ROI; tissue MFI = integrated fluorescence intensity/area of the selected ROI. All image acquisition and quantitative analyses were performed in a blinded manner by an investigator who was unaware of the experimental group allocation. Three mice were selected in each group. Each mouse was regarded as one independent biological replicate.

Western blot

For Western blot analysis, total proteins were extracted from the ischemic brains or cultured cells using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and protein concentrations were determined by a BCA protein assay kit (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies against C3 (1:1000, Proteintech Group, 21337-1-AP, Wuhan, Hubei, China), Trim59 (1:1000, Abcam, ab69639, Cambridge, MA, USA), c/EBPβ (1:1000, Abcam, ab32358, Cambridge, MA, USA) and β-tubulin (1:2000, Affinity Biosciences, AF7011, Changzhou, Jiangsu, China) at 4 °C overnight followed by reaction with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Proteintech Group, SA00001-2, Wuhan, Hubei, China). The proteins were visualized by an Enhanced Chemiluminescence Kit (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) using Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Protein band intensities were quantified using Image J software after background subtraction. β-tubulin served as the internal loading control, and the relative expression of each target protein was calculated as the ratio of the grayscale intensity of the target band to that of the corresponding β-tubulin band. Densitometric analysis was performed from three independent experiments, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed using IP Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Cell lysates were incubated with anti-c/EBPβ antibody (1:50, Abcam, ab32358, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-Myc antibody (1:1000, Proteintech Group, 16286-1-AP, Wuhan, Hubei, China) or normal IgG antibody (1:50, Abcam, ab172730, Cambridge, MA, USA) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with precleared protein A/G beads (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) at 4 °C for 4 h. After washing, samples were boiled for 5 min in sample-loading buffer, then examined by immunoblotting with anti-c/EBPβ (1:1000, Abcam, ab32358, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-Trim59 (1:1000, Abcam, ab69639, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-Myc antibody (1:1000, Proteintech Group, 16286-1-AP, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-HA antibody (1:5000, Proteintech Group, 51064-2-AP, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-Flag antibody (1:5000, Proteintech Group, 8H6A10, Wuhan, Hubei, China), β-actin (1:80000, Abclonal, AC026, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-ubiquitin (1:500, Bioworld Technology, BS62242, Bloomington, MN, USA) antibodies, anti-K48-linkage Specific Ubiquitin antibody (1:500, Abclonal, A3606, Wuhan, Hubei, China), and anti-K63-linkage Specific Ubiquitin antibody (1:500, Abclonal, A18164, Wuhan, Hubei, China).

Neurobehavioral tests

Mice were trained before PT stroke surgery and neurobehavioral tests were performed on days 3, 7, 14, and 21 after surgery. Adhesive removal test The adhesive removal test was performed to assess tactile response and sensorimotor asymmetry. A piece of adhesive tape dots (3 mm × 3 mm) was used to cover the palmar surface of the left forepaw (the injured forepaw). Tactile response was measured by recording the time taken to completely remove the adhesive tape, with a maximum observation time of 120 s [23]. Cylinder test The cylinder test was used to assess forelimb use and asymmetry. Mice were placed in a plastic cylinder (15 cm tall with a diameter of 5.5 cm) and videotaped for 5 min. Two mirrors were arranged at 90 angle and placed behind the cylinder to assist with detailed analysis of all movements. The times of the left paw or right paw placement on the wall of the cylinder were recorded. The forelimb asymmetry index is calculated as left paw contacts/(left paw contacts + right paw contacts) × 100 [18]. Accelerated rotarod test To assess motor function after stroke, mice were tested on a Rotarod (R03-1 Rota Rod, Yuyan Instruments, Shanghai, China.) with speeds accelerating from 4 to 40 rpm in 300 s. The time that the mice remained on the rotating rod (time on rotarod) was recorded, with a maximum value of 300 s [24]. Grid walking test The grid walking task was used to evaluate mice sensorimotor function impairment after stroke. Mice were placed on a 35 cm × 25 cm × 50 cm (length × width × height) grid area with 11-mm square mesh and allowed to walk freely for 5 min. If the left forelimb (the injured forelimb) fell into a grid hole or the mouse rested on the grid at the wrist level, it was recorded as a foot fault. The foot fault rate was calculated using the following formula: foot faults/total steps × 100 [25].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 19.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and a value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was first used to assess the normality of data distribution, and the Levene test was used to test the homogeneity of variances. For data that were normally distributed and had homogeneous variances, the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two independent groups; one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three or more independent groups. LSD test was employed for post-hoc multiple comparisons when variances were homogeneous, while Games–Howell test was used when variances were heterogeneous. Non-parametric tests were used for data that did not conform to normal distribution: the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for three or more groups. Longitudinal repeated-measurement data from the same mice at multiple time points were analyzed using mixed-design repeated-measures two-way ANOVA, and Bonferroni’s method was used for pairwise comparisons between groups at each time point. The Log-rank test was used to compare the survival curves of mice among different groups.

Myeloid cell Trim59 deficiency induces complement over activation and exacerbates brain injury in MCAO/R-induced stroke mice

TRIM59 expression in ischemic brains peaked on the 3rd day, and proteins differentially expressed following ischemic stroke in Trim59-cKO brains were mainly enriched in the complement cascade signaling pathway as shown in our published article [12]. Immunofluorescence double staining showed that complement C3 was co-localized with macrophages (F4/80+) and deficiency of Trim59 in macrophages promoted complement C3 expression (P < 0.01, Fig. 1C, D). Macrophage Trim59 overexpression suppresses complement activation and attenuates brain injury in MCAO/R-induced stroke mice

To specifically overexpress Trim59 in macrophages, we constructed an adeno-associated virus (AAV2/9-F4/80-Trim59-ZsGreen) to overexpress Trim59, where Trim59 expression is driven by the F4/80 promoter. We also built an AAV2/9-F4/80-ZsGreen without Trim59 as a negative control. AAV (AAV-Trim59 or AAV-NC) were injected into the tibial bone marrow cavity of mice 21 days before the MCAO/R surgery. Compared to mice injected with AAV-NC, the expression of TRIM59 in macrophages in the ischemic brain tissue of mice injected with AAV-Trim59 was significantly upregulated (P < 0.01, Fig. 2A, B). Macrophage Trim59 overexpression markedly inhibited mRNA and protein expression of complement C3 and C9 in the ischemic brain (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, Fig. 2C, D), and suppressed complement C3 expression in macrophages (F4/80+) (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E, F). Consistently, histological analyses showed markedly attenuated tissue injury in AAV-Trim59 mice, with the TTC staining indicating a significantly reduction of infarct size (P < 0.01, Fig. 2G, H). Discussion

The present study demonstrates that macrophage TRIM59 is essential for suppressing the overactivation of the complement C3 and neuronal phagocytosis by promoting the K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of the transcription factor c/EBPβ, during the acute phase of ischemic stroke. Furthermore, macrophage TRIM59 contributed to neurological recovery during the post-ischemic chronic phase.

TRIM family proteins are involved in a variety of physiological and pathological processes, playing roles in innate immune responses, tumor formation, and central nervous system diseases [26-28]. TRIM29 [29] and TRIM31 [30], two members of theTRIM family, are involved in cerebral ischemic injury through regulating protein ubiquitination. Recent studies have shown that TRIM59directly interacts with NLRP3 and promotes its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby suppressing pyroptosis and alleviating ischemicbrain injury [31]. These studies suggest that the TRIM family, including TRIM59, plays a crucial role in the onset and progression ofcerebral ischemia.

We previously observed that TRIM59 expression is markedly increased in both microglia and macrophages following cerebral ischemia [32]. Whether and how macrophage TRIM59 involved in ischemic stroke remain largely unexplored, and we employed a LysM-Cre-mediated conditional knockout (cKO) mouse model to further research [25]. LysM-Cre targets multiple myeloid populations, including monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and, to a lesser extent, brain-resident microglia [33]. Our previous experiments showed that LysM-Cre-mediated Trim59 deletion is highly efficient in macrophages, with only a mild, statistically non-significant reduction in neutrophils [34]. Collectively, these data suggest that the phenotypic effects observed in the present study are predominantly attributable to TRIM59 deficiency in peripheral myeloid-derived macrophages, rather than neutrophils or resident microglia.

The complement system is a key component of innate immunity, responsible for host defense and clearance of damaged cells. However, excessive complement activation contributes to secondary neuronal injury after ischemic stroke. Among its pathways, complement component C3 serves as a convergence point and a central effector molecule mediating inflammation and cell lysis [35-39]. In the present study, myeloid cell-specific Trim59 deletion markedly increased the expression and release of complement components C3 and C9 in the ischemic brain, which was accompanied by aggravated pathological damage, characterized by larger infarct size, higher histopathological scores, and reduced neuronal survival. Conversely, macrophage-specific overexpression of Trim59 effectively suppressed complement activation, mitigated tissue damage, and reduced neuronal loss.

Physiological synaptic pruning is mediated by complement signaling, in which C1q and C3 label vulnerable synapses for phagocytic clearance [40]. However, excessive activation of this pathway in disease settings can result in pathological synapse loss [41, 42]. In ischemic stroke, excessive complement activation contributes to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal injury during the recovery phase [43]. However, most previous studies have primarily focused on microglia, while the role of infiltrating macrophages remains less well understood. Following ischemic injury, peripheral myeloid cells infiltrate the peri-infarct region and exhibit strong phagocytic activity, suggesting a potential role in complement-dependent neuronal and synaptic clearance [44]. In the present study, we observed a significant reduction of surviving neurons in the ischemic penumbra of Trim59-cKO mice at 72 h after cerebral ischemia. To explore the underlying mechanism, we examined whether complement-mediated synaptic remodeling contributes to this phenotype. We found that TRIM59 deficiency promotes excessive complement C3 deposition on neurons, enhances macrophage-associated phagocytic activity, and accelerates synaptic loss, which may contribute to increased neuronal vulnerability in the ischemic penumbra after stroke.

Complement C3 is not only functional in the acute phase of stroke [45], but complement-mediated microglia/macrophage phagocytosis of synapses can still be observed at least 30 days after ischemic brain injury in mice. Therefore, C3-mediated synaptic loss not only induces neuronal death in the ischemic penumbra during the acute stage of stroke but also impairs neurological functional recovery in the chronic recovery phase. We adopted the PT stroke model to explore long-term functional outcomes after cerebral ischemia, as it presents stable lesion distribution, low mortality, and is suitable for long-term continuous monitoring of CBF and behavioral recovery. Our results showed that myeloid-specific Trim59 deficiency delayed CBF restoration, reduced neuronal survival, and impeded sensorimotor functional recovery in stroke mice. It's worth noting that no significant difference in baseline CBF was observed between groups, indicating that Trim59 deficiency does not affect basal cerebral perfusion but mainly interferes with CBF reconstruction during post-ischemic tissue repair.

Meanwhile, Trim59-cKO mice exhibited more pronounced sensorimotor deficits, in the adhesion removal test, cylinder test, and grid walking test, with decreased perception of tactile stimuli, marked limb asymmetry, and worse motor coordination. No significant difference was observed in the rotarod test, suggesting that TRIM59 deficiency primarily affects fine motor and sensorimotor rather than balance functions. Given that PT-induced injury is primarily confined to the sensorimotor cortex, this pattern of behavioral impairment is highly consistent with focal cortical neuronal and synaptic network damage, further indicating that the impact of TRIM59 deficiency on post-stroke recovery mainly involves sensory integration and fine motor control rather than general balance ability. In light of the acute complement-mediated neuronal and synaptic injury observed in our study, we speculate that persistent immune activation induced by myeloid cell TRIM59 deficiency not only aggravates early neuronal structural damage, but may also further impair the reconstruction of the local neurorepair microenvironment after ischemia, thereby jointly contributing to restricted sensorimotor functional restoration.

The transcription factor c/EBPβ is an important regulator of pro-inflammatory gene expression [46]. Within 6 to 72 h of cerebral ischemia and reperfusion, the expression of c/EBPβ in the mouse brain was increased and knockout of c/EBPβ resulted in a decrease in the infarct size, apoptotic cells, and expression of pro-inflammatory transcriptional products induced by ischemia, indicating that c/EBPβ plays a role in exacerbating ischemic brain injury [47]. c/EBPβ can bind to the distal sequence (− 616/ − 599) of the complement C3 promoter, directly regulate C3 gene expression [48]. The E3 ubiquitin ligase COP1 could degrade c/EBPβ in microglia/macrophages and inhibit the expression of pro-inflammatory genes. Knockout of COP1 in microglia increased intracellular c/EBPβ, which in turn drove C3 gene expression and exacerbated complement-mediated neuronal toxicity [49].

The results confirmed that TRIM59 interacts with c/EBPβ and restricts excessive C3 transcription and complement activation in macrophages after ischemic injury. Upon LPS stimulation of BMDMs, the expression of TRIM59 in macrophages was significantly downregulated, while the expression of C3 and its transcription factor c/EBPβ was significantly upregulated, further supporting their inverse relationship. Functional experiments further demonstrated that siRNA-mediated knockdown of c/EBPβ significantly reversed the increased neuronal C3 deposition and synaptic damage induced by conditioned medium from TRIM59-deficient macrophages. These findings indicate the causal relationship within the TRIM59-c/EBPβ-C3 axis at the functional level.

Mechanistically, we found that TRIM59 regulates c/EBPβ protein stability through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Specifically, TRIM59 promoted K48-linked polyubiquitination of c/EBPβ, which is a classical ubiquitin chain, mainly mediating the proteasomal degradation process of substrate proteins. We further identified the RING domain of TRIM59 as the critical region responsible for its interaction with c/EBPβ. It should be noted that the precise ubiquitination site of c/EBPβ was not clarified in this study, and subsequent research should be conducted to confirm this through site-directed mutagenesis and rescue experiments.

There are still some limitations in current research. First, the precise mechanisms by which TRIM59 regulates CBF after ischemic stroke remain unclear and require further investigation. Second, although LPS was used to establish a stable inflammatory activation model in BMDMs, it does not fully recapitulate the complex sterile inflammatory environment of ischemic stroke. Future studies should consider damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP)-based stimulation or other physiologically relevant models to better mimic in vivo conditions. Furthermore, although our findings indicate a TRIM59-c/EBPβ-C3 axis in regulating macrophage-mediated neuronal injury, direct causal evidence (e.g., via C3 inhibition) still needs to be verified experimentally. These experiments will further strengthen the mechanistic link between TRIM59 deficiency, complement dysregulation, and neuronal damage after stroke.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study indicates that macrophage TRIM59 is an important protein involved in the pathological process of ischemic stroke. Macrophage TRIM59 inhibits the activation of the complement system in the brains of stroke mice by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of c/EBPβ (a complement C3 transcription factor), thus limits macrophage-associated over phagocytic and synaptic loss, which may contribute to neuronal survival and neurological functional recovery.
