Introduction

Primary mediastinal seminoma is a rare extragonadal germ cell neoplasm that predominantly affects young males and often presents diagnostic challenges due to its nonspecific initial symptoms and atypical anatomical location [1]. Although its histopathological features resemble those of testicular seminoma, this tumor is not associated with a primary gonadal malignancy, necessitating a meticulous diagnostic approach involving imaging and immunohistochemistry [2,3]. In low-resource settings such as Indonesia, limited access to advanced diagnostic modalities—including PET-CT, comprehensive immunohistochemical panels, and molecular pathology services—frequently leads to delayed diagnosis and suboptimal management. This is further compounded by low clinical awareness of primary mediastinal tumors and restricted specialist referral pathways in remote areas [4,5]. Therefore, reporting this case is essential to raise clinical vigilance, reinforce multidisciplinary approaches, and promote early diagnosis in resource-constrained environments. The report was prepared in accordance with the 2025 SCARE guidelines [6].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old Indonesian male presented to a secondary referral hospital with progressive dyspnea and a persistent non-productive cough of three months' duration. Initially, the dyspnea was exertional, occurring only during strenuous physical activity; however, it gradually progressed to manifest during minimal exertion. The cough remained refractory to prior empirical treatments. The patient denied any history of tuberculosis, asthma, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, or COVID-19 infection, and no relevant family history was reported.

Physical examination revealed asymmetrical chest wall movement, with delayed expansion of the left hemithorax. Tactile fremitus was reduced, percussion was dull over the upper third of the left lung field, and vesicular breath sounds were diminished in the left hemithorax. Laboratory investigations demonstrated a mildly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 1.2 mg/dL, which was interpreted as a nonspecific inflammatory response supporting the suspicion of an underlying mediastinal mass rather than an infectious process. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed mild hypoxemia, with a pO₂ of 70 mmHg and oxygen saturation of 94 %, while pH and pCO₂ remained within normal limits.

Chest radiography showed a homogenous opacity with obtuse margins in the para-cardiac region (Fig. 1). A thoracic computed tomography (CT) scan identified a large, solid mass measuring 15.3 × 11 × 14.5 cm in the anterior mediastinum, with irregular borders and evidence of local invasion (Fig. 2). Tumor marker analysis revealed alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) of 1 ng/mL, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) of 0.96 ng/mL, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 487 U/L, and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-HCG) of 2.3 mIU/mL. Ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy yielded adequate tissue for rapid onsite cytological evaluation (ROSE), which revealed malignant round cells (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated CD117 positivity and CD45 negativity, consistent with a diagnosis of mediastinal seminoma. Microscopically, the tumor cells exhibited abundant clear cytoplasm with distinct cell borders, centrally located round nuclei, and prominent nucleoli. The neoplastic cells were arranged in nests and sheets separated by delicate fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes, which are characteristic features of seminoma.

Due to limitations in surgical and oncologic infrastructure, the patient underwent a sternotomy in a resource-constrained setting. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found to have invaded major vascular structures and the myocardium. Due to extensive invasion and intraoperative hemodynamic instability, only partial resection (approximately 30 %) was feasible, prioritizing patient safety over radical excision. This partial resection was performed as a pragmatic measure in the context of limited resources and intraoperative instability and should not be regarded as a standard therapeutic recommendation. The patient subsequently received 3 cycles of bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin (BEP) chemotherapy, which represents the established first-line treatment for mediastinal seminoma according to international guidelines.

The patient has been followed regularly through outpatient visits and imaging surveillance. As for the third-year post-treatment, he remains clinically stable with no signs of recurrence or distant metastasis. He continues to survive without limitations in daily activities, and serial imaging—including chest CT scans—has consistently shown no evidence of residual or progressive disease. Discussion

Primary mediastinal seminoma is a rare extragonadal germ cell tumor, comprising approximately 1–3 % of all mediastinal masses and predominantly affecting young adult males. Unlike its gonadal counterpart, mediastinal seminoma frequently presents with non-specific respiratory symptoms—such as dyspnea and cough—resulting from mass effect on adjacent thoracic structures. These vague clinical manifestations often contribute to delayed diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to advanced imaging modalities and subspecialty expertise may be constrained [7,8].

This case highlights the diagnostic acumen that can be achieved within a secondary referral hospital, despite its infrastructural limitations. The use of ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy, complemented by rapid onsite cytological evaluation (ROSE), facilitated timely histopathological confirmation. Immunohistochemical staining revealed CD117 positivity and CD45 negativity, consistent with seminoma and effectively excluding differential diagnoses such as lymphoma, thymic carcinoma, or other germ cell tumors [4,9]. A brief consideration of alternative diagnoses further enriches the educational value of this case, as thymoma may present as an anterior mediastinal mass; its hallmark is epithelial cell proliferation with admixed immature T-lymphocytes [10]. Lymphoma often shows diffuse sheets of atypical lymphoid cells with CD45 positivity, while non-seminomatous germ cell tumors display more heterogeneous morphology and may require additional markers such as AFP or β-hCG for confirmation [11]. In our case, the combination of clear cytoplasm, fibrous septa with lymphocytic infiltration, and an immunoprofile of CD117 positivity with CD45 negativity effectively excluded these possibilities [12]. Nevertheless, we explicitly acknowledge that the diagnostic panel was limited, as advanced germ cell markers such as PLAP, OCT3/4, and D2–40 were not available in this setting. The diagnosis was therefore established based on the characteristic morphology and the available IHC profile (CD117+/CD45–), with careful interpretation to minimize misclassification. This limitation underscores the importance of cautious diagnostic reasoning in resource-constrained environments, where access to comprehensive immunohistochemical panels remains restricted [13]. This approach exemplifies how targeted diagnostic strategies can be successfully adapted for use in low-resource settings without compromising accuracy. This structured exclusion of differential diagnoses illustrates how even limited IHC panels, when interpreted carefully, can yield reliable conclusions in resource-constrained settings [4,9,13].

In contrast, in high-resource settings, the diagnostic work-up for primary mediastinal seminoma would typically include comprehensive immunohistochemical panels (e.g., OCT3/4, PLAP, SALL4, D2–40) and advanced imaging modalities such as PET-CT to refine staging and guide management [13]. Surgical intervention would generally be avoided at presentation, with cisplatin-based chemotherapy initiated as the standard first-line therapy, and surgery reserved only for selected post-chemotherapy residual masses within specialized cardiothoracic centers. The divergence between this ideal pathway and the pragmatic approach adopted in our case underscores the adaptability required in resource-limited environments, while also highlighting that favorable outcomes can still be achieved through judicious use of available diagnostic and therapeutic tools [12,14].

In our case, however, the intraoperative findings necessitated a pragmatic surgical approach. A particularly compelling aspect of this case is the surgical management under constrained conditions. Intraoperative findings revealed extensive invasion of major vascular structures and the myocardium, precluding complete resection. Nonetheless, partial debulking (approximately 30 %) was performed to alleviate compressive symptoms and enable subsequent chemotherapy. This pragmatic decision reflects the delicate balance between oncologic intent and patient safety in settings lacking advanced cardiothoracic support or intensive care infrastructure [5,15]. Nevertheless, partial resection carries inherent risks, including the potential for local recurrence and the need for prolonged surveillance, which must be carefully considered when adopting such an approach [12,16,17].

The patient's favorable response to 3 cycles of BEP chemotherapy is consistent with established treatment protocols for seminomatous germ cell tumors. Notably, longitudinal follow-up over three years demonstrated sustained clinical stability, with no evidence of recurrence or distant metastasis. The patient returned to full functional capacity and remains asymptomatic, underscoring the chemosensitivity of seminoma and the efficacy of surveillance-based management following incomplete resection [18,19]. This outcome is also in accordance with the ESMO and NCCN guidelines, which recommend cisplatin-based chemotherapy as the standard first-line treatment for primary mediastinal seminoma, with surgery reserved only for selected post-chemotherapy residual masses [12,14]. Furthermore, multiple published series and retrospective cohorts have consistently demonstrated excellent long-term outcomes with chemotherapy alone, achieving cure rates exceeding 80–90 % in seminomatous mediastinal germ cell tumors. By situating our findings within this global evidence base, our case reinforces the universal applicability of guideline-based chemotherapy and highlights that favorable oncologic outcomes can still be achieved even when surgical options are constrained [16,20].

Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of chemotherapy alone. While seminoma is highly chemosensitive, exclusive reliance on systemic therapy may not address bulky residual disease, which can occasionally harbor viable tumor or teratomatous elements. In addition, chemotherapy is associated with cumulative toxicities—including pulmonary, renal, and gonadal impairment—that require careful monitoring and long-term survivorship care. In high-resource settings, surgical resection of residual masses after chemotherapy is considered in selected cases to ensure complete disease control. These considerations highlight that, although chemotherapy alone can achieve excellent outcomes, its limitations must be recognized, particularly in resource-limited environments where options for salvage surgery or advanced supportive care may be restricted [12,14,16,17].

From a global health perspective, this case demonstrates that oncologic success can be achieved in resource-limited settings through adaptive diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. It also provides transferable lessons for similar contexts, including the pragmatic use of limited immunohistochemical panels, selective surgical debulking when complete resection is not feasible, and reliance on cisplatin-based chemotherapy as the cornerstone of treatment. Sustained follow-up and multidisciplinary collaboration remain essential to mitigate risks and ensure long-term outcomes [21].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that primary mediastinal seminoma, though rare and diagnostically challenging, can be effectively managed in resource-limited settings through adaptive clinical strategies. Despite incomplete surgical resection due to extensive tumor invasion and infrastructural constraints, the patient achieved long-term remission following BEP chemotherapy, with no evidence of recurrence or metastasis at three-year follow-up. Nevertheless, the risks associated with partial resection and the limitations of chemotherapy alone must be acknowledged, underscoring the importance of cautious long-term surveillance. The successful outcome underscores the importance of pragmatic decision-making, multidisciplinary coordination, and sustained surveillance, offering valuable insights for clinicians managing complex thoracic malignancies in underserved settings.
