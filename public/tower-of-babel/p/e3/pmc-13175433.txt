Introduction

Hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies are rare but clinically significant hematologic disorders that require lifelong follow‐up and tailored management. Among bleeding disorders, haemophilia A, haemophilia B and von Willebrand disease (vWD) account for approximately 95%–97% of cases, while rare deficiencies such as fibrinogen, Factors II, V, VII, X, XI and XIII represent the remaining 3%–5% [1, 2]. The prevalence of vWD is estimated to be 0.6%–1.3% in the general population, making it the most common inherited bleeding disorder, whereas haemophilia A and B are less frequent, occurring in approximately 1 in 5000 and 1 in 30,000 live male births, respectively [2, 3].

The clinical presentation of hereditary factor deficiencies varies widely, depending not only on the specific factor involved but also on its activity level and individual patient characteristics. Symptoms can range from mucocutaneous bleeding, such as epistaxis, gingival bleeding and easy bruising, to deep‐seated haemorrhages, including hemarthroses and intracranial bleeding [4, 5]. Pregnancy introduces additional complexity due to the risk of early pregnancy loss, antepartum haemorrhage, postpartum haemorrhage and other obstetric complications. While physiological changes during pregnancy—including increased levels of fibrinogen, Factors VII, VIII, X and vWF—partially enhance haemostatic capacity, these changes may not be sufficient to prevent bleeding in affected women [6].

Furthermore, certain rare deficiencies, such as Factor V, VII and XI, demonstrate poor correlation between measured activity levels and clinical bleeding risk. Severe deficiencies can remain asymptomatic, while even mild reductions in other factors may necessitate therapeutic intervention [2]. These intricacies underscore the importance of individualized peripartum planning and the involvement of a multidisciplinary team.

The present study aims to share our 10‐year single‐centre experience in managing pregnant women with hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies, highlighting clinical features, obstetric management strategies and peripartum complications.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study included all pregnant women diagnosed with hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies who delivered at the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Clinic of a tertiary referral centre between January 2015 and April 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (Approval No: 2025/0056).

Study Population and Inclusion Criteria

Eligible patients were those with a confirmed diagnosis of hereditary coagulation factor deficiency based on laboratory testing and whose pregnancies resulted in delivery at our institution. Seventeen women with inherited coagulation factor deficiencies were included in the study. Fifteen women contributed one pregnancy each, while two women had two pregnancies during the study period; thus, obstetric outcomes were analysed per pregnancy.

Patients with incomplete laboratory data were included; missing factor levels were noted separately. Two cases without available factor activity measurements were retained because their coagulation profiles were consistent with the diagnosis and no alternative causes were identified in hospital records.

Data Collection

Demographic information, deficient factor types and levels, complete blood counts, coagulation parameters (PT, aPTT), gestational age at delivery, delivery mode, antepartum and postpartum complications and treatments administered (e.g., fresh frozen plasma [FFP], recombinant factors, tranexamic acid,Haemate‐P) were extracted from patient files and hospital records.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (range), and categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages.

Results

A total of 19 pregnancies in 17 pregnant women diagnosed with hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies were included in the study. The mean age of the cohort was 30.2 years (range: 17–46 years). The distribution of coagulation disorders was as follows: von Willebrand disease (vWD) in seven pregnancies (37%), factor XI deficiency in four (21%), factor VII deficiency in three (16%), factor X deficiency in two (11%) and factor XIII deficiency in one pregnancy (5%). One patient had combined deficiencies of von Willebrand factor, factor V and factor VIII.

Obstetric Outcomes

Fifteen pregnancies (78%) resulted in term delivery, two (11%) were late preterm, and two (11%) ended in early pregnancy loss: one due to missed abortion at 18 weeks and another through medical termination because of multiple foetal anomalies. Six patients (32%) delivered vaginally. Caesarean delivery was performed in 11 of 19 pregnancies (58%). All caesarean deliveries were undertaken for obstetric indications including repeat caesarean section, cephalopelvic disproportion, non‐cephalic presentation, multiple pregnancy with a leading breech presentation and elective indication. No caesarean delivery was performed solely because of the underlying coagulation disorder. General anaesthesia was used for all caesarean deliveries.

Haematological Findings and Peripartum Laboratory Results

Coagulation factor levels showed both inter‐individual and inter‐trimester variability across the cohort, reflecting physiological changes during pregnancy as well as differences in the timing of laboratory assessment. In Patient 6, factor VIII and von Willebrand factor antigen levels varied between trimesters, consistent with expected gestational fluctuations. Among patients with von Willebrand disease, ABO blood group information was available for all cases, with three of seven patients having blood group O. In Patient 10, factor VII activity levels decreased during pregnancy without associated bleeding complications. Detailed laboratory data including vWF antigen levels and activity‐to‐antigen ratios are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Pre‐delivery haemoglobin levels were available for all pregnancies, with a median value of 12.1 g/dL (IQR: 11.35–13.30). Early postpartum haemoglobin measurements showed a median level of 10.6 g/dL (IQR: 9.4–11.35). The median haemoglobin decrease associated with delivery was 1.7 g/dL (IQR: 1.2–3.1), with a maximum observed decrease of 5.1 g/dL.

Antepartum haemorrhage was not observed in any of the cases. Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) requiring red blood cell transfusion occurred in four pregnancies (21%).

Treatment Strategies

Haemate‐P was administered to four (24%) patients as part of delivery planning, all of whom had vWD. None of these patients developed haemorrhagic complications. FFP was used in cases of Factor VII, X and XI deficiencies due to limited availability of specific factor concentrates. Recombinant factor VIIa was administered in one case following severe bleeding during curettage after incomplete abortion. Tranexamic acid was used as an adjunctive treatment in selected cases.

Discussion

Hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies are rare but clinically significant bleeding disorders that can complicate pregnancy management. The absence of a consistent correlation between factor activity levels and clinical bleeding severity poses an additional challenge in developing optimal management strategies. In this study, we evaluated the obstetric outcomes, management approaches and peripartum complications of 19 pregnancies affected by various factor deficiencies over a 10‐year period. Our findings demonstrate both similarities and important distinctions compared to previously published literature.

Most patients included in this study were referred to our tertiary centre and had not been previously followed at our institution. Consequently, coagulation factor levels were measured at different stages of pregnancy. It is well established that during gestation, fibrinogen, factor VII, factor VIII, factor X and von Willebrand factor levels physiologically increase, whereas factor XI levels show variable patterns and factors II and V remain relatively stable [6]. In this context, inter‐trimester variability in measured factor levels may reflect physiological fluctuations, differences in sampling timing and assay‐related variability rather than a pathological process. These dynamic changes limit the ability to establish direct relationships between laboratory values and clinical outcomes.

In our cohort, vWD was the most frequently observed inherited bleeding disorder and constituted the largest diagnostic group among the included patients. Although vWF and Factor VIII levels usually rise from the 10th week of gestation and peak in the third trimester, they decline rapidly within the first postpartum week [7, 8], predisposing patients to delayed postpartum haemorrhage. In Patient 6, variability in factor VIII and von Willebrand factor antigen levels observed across trimesters was consistent with these known physiological changes and likely influenced by differences in timing of laboratory assessment.

In our study, only one patient with vWD experienced a postpartum haemoglobin decrease, despite having third‐trimester levels above the haemostatic threshold. Her bleeding was attributed to surgical loss rather than factor deficiency. Importantly, she did not develop complications during her subsequent caesarean delivery, where prophylactic haemate‐P was used. Overall, four patients received haemate‐P, and none developed postpartum haemorrhage. Haemate‐P is a von Willebrand factor/factor VIII concentrate containing high–molecular‐weight multimers of von Willebrand factor [9], and its prophylactic use in selected cases appears to play a critical role in delivery planning. Nevertheless, close postpartum surveillance remains essential given the rapid decline in von Willebrand factor and factor VIII levels after delivery [10].

Blood group O is associated with lower circulating von Willebrand factor levels due to increased clearance [8]. In our cohort, nearly half of the patients with von Willebrand disease had blood group O, which may partly explain lower baseline von Willebrand factor levels and underscores the importance of considering ABO blood group when interpreting laboratory findings during pregnancy.

One patient had combined deficiencies of vWF, Factor V and Factor VIII, along with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and a history of deep vein thrombosis, managed with low‐molecular‐weight heparin throughout pregnancy. Despite a first‐trimester Factor V level of 10%, no significant bleeding occurred, likely due to the hypercoagulable state associated with SLE and elevated Factor VIII activity. This case highlights the importance of individualized risk assessment in pregnant women with coexisting hematologic and autoimmune conditions [11, 12].

Factor VII deficiency was the most common rare factor deficiency observed in our cohort. Factor VII activity levels below 10% are generally considered severe, 10%–20% moderate and 20%–50% mild [13]. Although physiological increases in factor VII activity may occur during pregnancy [14], factor VII levels are known to exhibit considerable intra‐individual and assay‐related variability and are not consistently elevated during gestation. In Patient 10, the observed decline in factor VII levels during pregnancy likely reflects this biological and analytical variability rather than disease progression.

In our cohort, one patient with severe factor VII deficiency experienced incomplete abortion at 18 weeks’ gestation, requiring treatment with fresh frozen plasma, tranexamic acid and recombinant factor VIIa. In contrast, patients with mild to moderate deficiency delivered uneventfully without prophylactic treatment, reinforcing that bleeding risk cannot be predicted by factor activity levels alone [15, 16].

Although bleeding severity does not consistently correlate with measured factor activity levels, standardized bleeding assessment tools such as the ISTH Bleeding Assessment Tool (ISTH‐BAT) were not systematically available in our cohort. Consequently, management decisions were primarily based on individual bleeding history and multidisciplinary clinical assessment. In addition, peripartum and early postpartum haemoglobin measurements were used as objective markers of blood loss and were integrated with clinical findings to guide patient management, reflecting real‐world practice in the care of pregnant women with rare coagulation disorders.

Factor X deficiency, though rare, can lead to severe complications in its homozygous form. In our two cases, PT and aPTT were significantly prolonged (> 120 s), suggesting severe deficiency, yet successful deliveries were achieved with prophylactic FFP. These findings support early haematology involvement and careful planning. On the other hand, homozygous infants face a reported intracranial haemorrhage risk of up to 15%, which may justify consideration of elective caesarean delivery when both parents are known carriers [17, 18]. When both parents are carriers, prenatal diagnosis and caesarean delivery may be considered to reduce neonatal haemorrhagic risks [19].

Factor XI deficiency is known for its unpredictable correlation between factor levels and bleeding risk. Severe deficiency is typically defined as activity < 15%, and partial deficiency as 15%–45% [20]. In our study, all four patients had activity levels < 15%, yet none experienced major bleeding. These results reinforce the widely documented importance of individual bleeding history over factor levels for predicting peripartum outcomes [21]. With appropriate planning, vaginal delivery is a safe option for many women with severe Factor XI deficiency.

One patient with Factor XIII deficiency developed significant intra‐abdominal bleeding requiring laparotomy despite having third‐trimester activity above the commonly accepted surgical threshold [1]. Additional obstetric risk factors, including twin gestation, hypertension and obesity, likely contributed to the adverse outcome. This emphasizes the necessity of individualized risk assessment that incorporates both obstetric and hematologic factors.

FFP was frequently used in our cohort due to limited availability of specific factor concentrates. However, excessive FFP administration carries a risk of transfusion‐associated circulatory overload, as observed in one patient with Factor XI deficiency who required intensive care unit monitoring [22, 23]. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing access to targeted factor replacement therapies and implementing protocols to minimize transfusion‐related complications.

In summary, the variability in clinical presentation and outcomes among patients with hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies underscores the importance of individualized delivery planning. A multidisciplinary team approach—including obstetricians, haematologists, anaesthesiologists and neonatologists—is essential for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes in this high‐risk population.

Conclusion

Hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies represent a rare but clinically significant challenge in obstetric care, requiring meticulous planning and multidisciplinary collaboration to ensure optimal maternal and neonatal outcomes. In our 10‐year single‐centre experience, we observed considerable variability in clinical presentations and bleeding risks, even among patients with similar factor deficiencies and activity levels. Notably, some women with severely reduced factor activity experienced uneventful deliveries, whereas others with near‐normal levels developed significant haemorrhagic complications.

Our findings underscore several key principles in the management of these high‐risk pregnancies. Bleeding risk cannot be reliably predicted by coagulation factor levels alone, and individual bleeding history remains the most important determinant of peripartum outcomes. Prophylactic approaches should therefore be tailored to the underlying disorder; in our cohort, haemate‐P was effective and well tolerated in women with von Willebrand disease, while factor replacement using fresh frozen plasma or recombinant products was critical in selected deficiencies. Close monitoring during the postpartum period, particularly within the first week, is essential because of rapid declines in certain coagulation factors, especially von Willebrand factor and factor VIII. Finally, careful and judicious use of blood products, including slow infusion protocols for fresh frozen plasma, is necessary to minimize transfusion‐related complications such as transfusion‐associated circulatory overload.

Given these complexities, delivery planning for women with hereditary coagulation factor deficiencies should involve early haematology consultation, availability of appropriate replacement therapies and coordination among obstetric, anaesthetic and neonatal care teams. Establishing standardized care pathways and improving access to specific factor concentrates would likely enhance safety and reduce morbidity.
