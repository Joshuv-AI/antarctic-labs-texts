Forecasting influenza activity based on internet retrieval behavior and meteorological data in four megacities of China
Authors: Chun-Xi Shan, Yan-He Wang, Qiang Xu, Guo-Lin Wang, Chen-Long Lv, Li-Qun Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate and timely early warning of seasonal influenza epidemics continues to pose a critical public health challenge. Innovative methodologies leveraging network data sources, such as internet search queries, serve as a valuable complement to traditional laboratory surveillance in terms of timeliness. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep-learning model for influenza prediction based on multi-source data.MethodsUsing etiological surveillance data of influenza viruses among influenza-like illness (ILI) cases in four megacities (Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, and Shenzhen) during 2013 and 2018, we developed prediction models based on weekly Baidu index data and meteorological indicators. A long short-term memory (LSTM) model was compared against three machine learning algorithms. Although the significance of predictors varied geographically, the Baidu index for “Tamiflu” was consistently a dominant predictor in three megacities.ConclusionsThis study validates the significant potential of integrating network big data and deep-learning algorithm for influenza surveillance. Background

The influenza caused by the influenza virus (IFV) is characterized by symptoms such as fever, headache, myalgia and fatigue [1]. Approximately one billion individuals worldwide are annually infected with IFVs [2], and when influenza epidemic season arrives, the sudden influx of influenza patients overwhelms emergency department rapidly, posing a great challenge to the response capacity of healthcare infrastructure [3–5]. Therefore, early detection and warning of influenza outbreaks are crucial for public self-protection, medical resources allocation, and strategies of control and prevention [6].

The current influenza surveillance system is based on case reports from medical institutions, which may be delayed by laboratory testing and reporting procedures, affecting timely epidemiological guidance and healthcare response [7–9]. The surveillance using Internet search data provides a more timely approach to estimate the epidemic trend of influenza, e.g., Google search data has been used in forecasting influenza trend in the United States, providing practical experience for Internet-based influenza surveillance [9]. Furthermore, meteorological factors (e.g., temperature, humidity) have been well-established drivers of influenza transmission and seasonality [10, 11], which implies their significance as predictors.

As one of the widely used search engines in China, Baidu’s search volume for specific keywords can mirror the concerns of Chinese population [12]. Some studies have made predictions regarding influenza trends in China at large spatial scales [12, 13], while others focus on individual megacities [7, 14]. However, limited research has considered the heterogeneity of influenza epidemics across different cities and incorporates city-specific characteristics. To address this limitation, we aim to develop and validate predictive models that account for such heterogeneity by integrating multi-source heterogeneous data. In this study, we have characterized the epidemiological features of influenza in four megacities spanning three climate regions of China: Beijing and Tianjin (both located in the high-latitude and temperate region but representing distinct political-economic centers), Shanghai (middle-latitude and subtropical region), and Shenzhen (low-latitude and tropical region). We have developed four modelling algorithms: the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) algorithm (a deep-learning model) to capture complex temporal dependencies, and three widely-used and robust machine-learning models, including support vector machine (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. These three machine learning models were chosen due to their strong performance in regression tasks, interpretability, and representativeness of different algorithmic families. By integrating Baidu search data, meteorological data, and etiological surveillance data on influenza-like illness (ILI) cases, we have predicted the epidemic trend of influenza for each megacity separately. We aim to enhance the accuracy of capturing dynamic changes in influenza outbreaks by using open-source Baidu index data, thereby providing epidemiological intelligence to local health institutions for early prevention of influenza outbreaks.

Methods

Data collection and management

The individual-level data of ILI cases from four megacities with more than 10 million urban residents across three climate regions, including Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai and Shenzhen, between the 22nd week of 2013 (started from 3 June, 2013) and the 23rd week of 2018 (ended at 10 June, 2018), were extracted from the National Surveillance Network of ILI (detailed in Supplementary Methods) [15, 16]. This study period was determined to develop a predictive model for seasonal influenza under typical epidemiological conditions because the global circulation patterns of influenza viruses were substantially disrupted by strict non-pharmaceutical interventions during the COVID-19 pandemic [17, 18]. These data were aggregated and processed to calculate the weekly positive detection rate of IFV at city level, which served as the primary outcome for all modeling process in our analysis. For the purpose of demographic description, the cases were categorized into three broad age groups: 0‒17 years (children), 18‒59 years (younger adults), and ≥60 years (older adults). These groups correspond to distinct phases in terms of social interaction patterns, healthcare-seeking behavior, and potential risk of severe outcomes, which are relevant for contextualizing population-level transmission dynamics. The data on Baidu index (https://index.baidu.com), which captures search behavior and frequency of online users for specific keywords, was extracted from Baidu search platform. Following previous studies [7, 13, 14], we collected daily-level Baidu index data for 47 influenza-related keywords for each of the four megacities. The keywords included disease name, therapeutic and preventive measures, symptoms, as well as phrases closely related to influenza (Table S1). The weekly cumulative Baidu index values for each keyword in four megacities were used as input features for modelling analysis. Weekly-level data on five meteorological factors, including average temperature, average relative humidity, average wind speed, accumulated sunshine duration, and accumulated precipitation were collected from 613 meteorological stations in China (available at Chinese National Meteorological Information Center, http://data.cma.cn/). These data were projected onto a 10 × 10 km2 grid using kriging methods, and zonal statistical methods were used to determine city-level values of these factors [19].

Variable screening for the modeling analysis

The data from the 22nd week of 2013 to the 21st week of 2017 was used as the training set, while the data from the 22nd week of 2017 to the 23rd week of 2018 served as the testing set. Subsequently, after standardizing 47 Baidu index and five meteorological predictors (processed by StandardScaler function from the sklearn version 1.0.2 in Python version 3.7.4, Python software foundation 2021, https://www.python.org/) [20], we combined all the data from the four cities to establish a random forest (RF) model, which determined the feature importance of each predictor. In this combined RF model, six features with a relative contribution exceeding 3% of the total contribution were selected. These features include “Tamiflu” with a relative contribution of 23.47%, “influenza A” with 23.01%, “weekly average temperature” with 7.84%, “sore throat” with 6.75%, “what medicine to take for the flu” with 3.78%, and “fever” with 3.07%. Furthermore, we incorporated the previous week’s positive detection rate of IFVs for each city as one of the features into the subsequent model to capture the autocorrelation between influenza epidemics in consecutive weeks [21]. The Spearman correlation coefficients between the features and outcome were presented in Fig. S1, which were calculated using pandas (version 1.3.5) in Python (version 3.7.4). The trending patterns of the features and outcome included in the model were illustrated in Fig. S2.

Model construction

In order to achieve optimal predictive performance for influenza epidemics, four models were constructed separately for each megacity, including a deep-learning model known as long short-term memory (LSTM) and three conventional machine-learning models: support vector machine (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. The model structure of LSTM was illustrated in Fig. 1. The input section described the construction process of the sample set in LSTM. Timesteps indicated the time length of historical data for prediction, ranging from one to 52 weeks. Advanced weeks represented the number of weeks that can be advanced for predicting the outcome, ranging from one to four weeks. Each sample consists of an outcome and a feature matrix. The feature matrix comprised the seven features mentioned in variable screening, with each feature representing a time series over timesteps, collectively forming a 7 × timestep feature matrix. The weekly positive detection rate for nth week (n equals to advanced week) after the final week in the feature matrix of each sample served as the outcome for that specific sample. The samples were constructed continuously on the time axis in sliding windows with a time step of 1 week until the outcome reached the last week of the entire data set. The sample set was composed of numerous sequentially arranged samples along the time axis, and training set and testing set were divided based on the week corresponding to the outcomes in each sample according to the time division specified in the variable screening. The batch size for each training sample set was 32, meaning that 32 consecutive samples are input for training at a time, until the entire training set is fully trained. In the LSTM layer, each neuron was attached to a neuron that outputted the cell state ct-1 and the hidden state ht-1 along the time axis. After processing by forget gate, input gate and output gate, it obtained a new cell state ct and a new hidden state ht, and then passed them to the next neuron. The dropout layer adjusted the parameters of each neuron by randomly removing some neurons to prevent overfitting. The linear layer converted the output to the required dimension and generated the final prediction. The loss function of the model was set to mean squared error (MSE). The above process was defined as single-step prediction and 208 models (52 × 4) were constructed for each city. Permutation feature importance, defined as the increase in the loss when value of a feature is temporarily shuffled while values of other features remain unchanged [22, 23], was calculated in LSTM to assess the contribution of the seven selected features for each city.

The dropout layer randomly turns off some neurons in the network to prevent overfitting. The linear layer transforms the output to the required dimension and generates the final prediction. The hyperparameters for the SVM, XGBoost and LASSO regression models were selected using random grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation. Subsequently, separate models were constructed for each city using different weeks in advance (ranging from one to four weeks) to determine the optimal advanced weeks.

All models were trained using data from the 22nd week of 2013 to the 21st week of 2017 as training set, and evaluated using data from the 22nd week of 2017 to the 23rd week of 2018 as testing set. LSTM model was performed using torch (version 1.13.1), while the RF, SVM, XGBoost and LASSO regression models were performed using sklearn (version 1.0.2) in Python (version 3.7.4). The specific hyperparameters of the models were given in Table S2 and Table S3.

Model comparison

For each city, we separately used the four modelling algorithms (LSTM, SVM, XGBoost and LASSO regression) to identify the corresponding optimal model based on coefficient of determination (R2; advantages: standardized and intuitive; disadvantage: can be inflated by irrelevant variables), with a higher R2 value indicating a better fitting effect. For LSTM, we determined the optimal model by adjusting the combination of timesteps and advanced weeks; whereas for the other three conventional machine learning models, modifying only the length of advanced weeks was sufficient to identify the optimal model. Subsequently, we compared the performance of the best models given by the four modelling algorithms for each city, through three evaluating indicators: R2, root mean square error (RMSE; advantages: sensitive to large errors, scale-dependent; disadvantage: sensitive to outliers), and mean absolute error (MAE; advantages: robust to outliers, easily interpretable; disadvantage: does not indicate error direction) [24–26].

Rolling forecasting performance

The robustness of 208 trained LSTM models for each city was verified by conducting the rolling forecasting method. Specifically, for each LSTM model of each city, we used the predicted value (the predicted weekly positive detection rate) of sliding window t as the autocorrelation term of sliding window t+1, with the batch size for each sample entering model as one. The above process was repeated to achieve a rolling forecast of the next outcome with adjustment for autocorrelation of the predicted previous one, and thereby a series of new predicted values were obtained [27, 28]. Considering the potential accumulation of errors resulting from using predicted values as autocorrelation terms instead of observed values, there is a possibility of decreased prediction performance with an extended forecasting period. To address this issue, we adopt the model with the maximum area under the curve (AUC) on the R2 curve as the optimal model, indicating superior fitting and more stable prediction capabilities. Specifically, the rolling forecasting period spanned from the first week to the last week of the test set, i.e., ranging from one week to 54 weeks, and starting from the second week of rolling prediction, we can obtain the R2 of the model for the corresponding time period with the extension of the forecasting time period. These R2 constituted a curve varying with the prediction period, and the AUC with values greater than 0 were calculated. Finally, we selected the optimal model with the largest AUC of R2 for the rolling forecasting in each city. To further validate the identified optimal advance week for Shenzhen, an additional multi-start-week analysis was conducted within the rolling forecasting framework. Thirteen distinct starting weeks were systematically selected at 4-week intervals across the 2017‒2018 influenza seasons. For each week, the prediction errors for 1-, 2-, and 3-week advances were compared to determine which advance time most frequently yielded the minimum error.

Results

Demographic and epidemiological characteristics of ILI cases

From the 22nd week of 2013 to the 23rd week of 2018, a total of 201,241 ILI cases of were reported in 46 sentinel hospitals belonging to four megacities, of which 43,841 (21.79%) were confirmed as IFV infection through laboratory testing. H3N2 subtype exhibited the highest positive detection rate (21,103 cases, 10.49%), followed by Yamagata (9,193 cases, 4.57%), H1N1 (pdm09) (9,157 cases, 4.55%) and Victoria (3,177 cases, 1.58%) (Table 1). 71 cases (0.04%) were co-infected with multiple strains of IFVs. The overall positive rate of IFVs and each major identified subtype were higher among females compared to males. Younger adults (18‒59 years old) showed the highest positive rate of H1N1(pdm09) (5.27%), while the positive rate of H3N2 (15.60%) and Yamagata (6.56%) was the highest in elderly people (≥60 years old). In addition, Victoria displayed a higher positive rate observed in children (0‒17 years old) compared to the other two age groups.

Among the four megacities, Shanghai recorded the highest number of confirmed cases of influenza (24,814 cases, 25.19%), followed by Beijing (10,657 cases, 18.13%), Tianjin (6,500 cases, 19.79%), and Shenzhen (1,870 cases, 16.84%) (Table 1). The influenza epidemics in Beijing and Tianjin mostly presented a unimodal pattern, typically occurring during the winter season, with sporadic secondary peak in summer and autumn (Fig. S3). In contrast, the influenza outbreaks in Shanghai demonstrated a bimodal pattern, characterized by alternating waves of high activity during summer-autumn and winter seasons. These distinct seasonal patterns were primarily associated with the alternating circulation of influenza A(H3N2) or A(H1N1pdm09) and influenza B/Victoria or B/Yamagata, as detailed in Fig. S4. In addition, the influenza transmission in Shenzhen was nearly continuous throughout the year, occasionally interrupted briefly between September and December. The average weekly detection number was the highest in Shanghai (376 cases), followed by Beijing (224 cases), Tianjin (125 cases), and Shenzhen (42 cases).

Model comparison

The optimal LSTM model showed higher R2 values compared to the corresponding SVM, XGBoost, and LASSO regression models in each city. Tianjin and Shenzhen achieving the highest (0.94) and the lowest (0.80) R2 values respectively (Table 2). The median R2 values of SVM (ranging from 0.02 to 0.82), XGBoost (ranging from 0.25 to 0.85) and LASSO (ranging from 0.09 to 0.73) were found to be 0.75, 0.52 and 0.36 respectively. The LSTM model presented superior performance compared to the other three models for R2, RMSE, and MAE in each megacity. The LSTM models indicated that two (Tianjin and Shanghai) out of four megacities displayed an optimal advanced period of one week, while the predictive performance for Beijing and Shenzhen was found to be best at two and three weeks in advance, respectively.

Single-step prediction based on LSTM

The optimal timestep of the LSTM model varied across different cities: 52 weeks for Beijing, 39 weeks for Shenzhen, and relatively shorter timesteps for Tianjin (15 weeks), and Shanghai (1 week) (Fig. 2A‒D). The optimal advanced prediction period also differed according to cities: 1 week for Tianjin and Shanghai, and 2 and 3 weeks for Beijing and Shenzhen, respectively. Overall, the predictive positive detection rates of IFV in each city were generally consistent with the observed situation from the 22nd week of 2017 to the 23rd week of 2018. The predicted curves captured the major epidemic peaks, with the dual peaks in Tianjin and Shanghai being especially well-captured (Fig. 2E‒2 H).

Rolling forecasting based on LSTM

In the context of the rolling forecasting analysis, the optimal model parameters exhibited variation across different cities. Specifically, the optimal timesteps for Beijing and Shenzhen were 52 and 39 weeks, respectively, which is consistent with the single-step prediction results. In contrast, for Tianjin and Shanghai, the optimal timesteps were 13 and 22 weeks, differing from the single-step prediction results (Fig. 3A–D). Regarding the optimal advanced weeks, they were consistent with the single-step prediction across all the cities (2, 1, 1, and 3 weeks for Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, and Shenzhen, respectively). The performance of the rolling forecasting, as measured by R2, reached its peak at different time points: week 14 for Beijing (R2 = 0.92), week 35 for Tianjin (R2 = 0.92), week 22 for Shanghai (R2 = 0.87), and week 34 for Shenzhen (R2 = 0.81). During the extended forecasting period, the R2 values for Beijing, Tianjin, and Shenzhen showed stability, whereas the R2 value for Shanghai initially declined before attaining stability (Fig. 3E–H). The multi-start-week validation confirmed that the 3-week advance prediction yielded the lowest error in 7 out of 13 independent cases (53.8% win rate), outperforming the 1- and 2-week advance predictions (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Permutation importance of predictors

The ranking of permutation importance varied among different cities and between distinct prediction modes. The weekly average temperature was the sole meteorological predictor retained in the final models, and its relative importance differed according to the city. In the single-step prediction, the keyword “Tamiflu” was the most significant predictor for Beijing, Tianjin, and Shenzhen, while the autocorrelation term was the most important for Shanghai (Fig. 4). Regarding the rolling forecasting, the importance ranking remained unchanged compared to the single-step prediction for Beijing and Tianjin (Fig. 4A and D). For Tianjin, the ranking was largely comparable, with the exception that the keyword “what medicine to take for the flu” dropped to the lowest position (Fig. 4B). For Shanghai, the importance ranking changed: the autocorrelation term shifted to the fourth position, and the importance values of various predictors became more similar in numerical range (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Accurately predicting influenza activity at the city level continues to pose a challenge, particularly when aiming to balance timeliness with adaptability to heterogeneous epidemic patterns across different urban settings. While internet search data offer a real-time proxy for disease activity, their effective integration with other predictors like meteorological factors, and their utility in advanced forecasting frameworks such as rolling prediction, require further validation in multi-city contexts. This study aimed to address these gaps by developing and comparing LSTM models with conventional machine learning algorithms for influenza forecasting in four Chinese megacities. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate whether integrating Baidu search indices and meteorological data could yield accurate city-specific predictions. Furthermore, we assessed the robustness of a rolling forecasting method designed to minimize immediate dependence on surveillance data, and identified the key predictive features and their variation across cities. Compared to previous studies that utilized internet search data for influenza forecasting, such as those employing traditional models or focusing on single-city or national-level analyses [29, 30], our work provides a direct multi-city comparison and explicitly evaluates the added value of LSTM within an integrative data framework. The LSTM model was ultimately demonstrated to outperform the other three models in predicting the dynamics of influenza epidemics. Meanwhile, the rolling forecasting method effectively reduced the model’s reliance on etiological surveillance data. Given its robust performance across various conditions, this prediction method holds promising potential for extensive applications.

The utilization of observed data for single-step prediction can enhance the model’s performance; however, the timeliness of the prediction may be compromised by this approach, given the inherent nature of etiological surveillance network data. Through the implementation of the rolling forecasting methodology, we have demonstrated that LSTM, trained on robust long-term data, processes a certain degree of predictive capability without heavy reliance on observed data. Moreover, satisfactory long-term prediction results can be achieved in three of the four megacities. This extends the findings of studies that rely on strict real-time data input, by demonstrating a practical method to maintain prediction stability amidst potential surveillance delays, a crucial step towards operational early-warning systems. In the case of Shanghai, although there is a slight decrease in the effectiveness of the long-term rolling forecasting, accurate predictions for medium-term periods are notably observed and align well with the observed values. The two LSTM-based prediction methods we developed address distinct and practical public health needs. The single-step prediction method, which achieves high accuracy with a lead time of 1‒3 weeks when surveillance data are concurrently available, is tailored for short-term operational response. It can directly inform urgent measures such as anticipatory allocation of hospital beds, antiviral drugs, and diagnostic resources in sentinel hospitals, particularly in cities like Beijing and Shenzhen where the lead time allows for proactive preparation. In contrast, the rolling forecasting method, designed to operate with minimal immediate input from traditional surveillance, serves as a strategic planning and resilience tool. It enables health authorities to maintain situational awareness and assess potential outbreak trajectories even during periods of surveillance data delay or disruption. This capability is invaluable for longer-term preparedness, including guiding the timing of public health advisories, optimizing seasonal vaccination campaign strategies, and managing stockpiles over an epidemic season. By providing this dual-model framework, our study offers flexible tools that can adapt to varying data availability scenarios, thereby enhancing the responsiveness and robustness of influenza early-warning systems in practice.

The importance of predictors varied across different megacities. However, it is worth noting that in Beijing, Tianjin and Shenzhen, regardless of single-step prediction or rolling forecasting, the keyword “Tamiflu” ranked first in terms of feature importance. In Shanghai, “Tamiflu” also exhibited relatively high importance among the features excluding autocorrelation terms. This serves as a reminder to pay closer attention to netizens’ search habits when conduct forecasts. With the advancement of online healthcare and widespread dissemination of medical knowledge, netizens may increasingly seek more precise medical solutions such as specific medicines for particular disease, especially those with brand names rather than generic ones. This aspect should be given greater consideration when extrapolating the model to other regions or diseases. The keywords and categories we provided can serve as valuable references for conducting research on individual’s online retrieval behaviors related to health information. Notably, the final model considered meteorological factors across all of the megacities studied, though with notable selectivity. The weekly average temperature emerged as the sole meteorological predictor retained across all cities, while others like humidity or precipitation were filtered out during feature selection. This aligns with the established primary role of temperature in driving influenza seasonality [10, 31]. Interestingly, its permutation importance varied geographically, and this heterogeneity suggests that the influence of temperature on transmission dynamics and its value as a predictive signal may be modulated by broader climatic context or urban-specific factors. This nuance warrants further investigation in multi-city forecasting frameworks. This underscores the impact of meteorological factors on influenza epidemics, regardless of prevailing conditions. Our result, which indicates that only temperature was consistently selected as a predictive meteorological feature across diverse cities, complements and refines the broader findings of studies that incorporate multiple climatic variables, pointing to temperature as a robust core indicator in data-driven feature selection for urban influenza forecasting. In addition, our study has confirmed the exceptional importance of autocorrelation, aligning with a study conducted in Latin America that highlighted the presence of strong autocorrelation in influenza epidemic [21]. This finding further emphasizes the significance of influenza surveillance networks, as historical observed data serves as a foundational component for model training and prediction. Therefore, it is crucial to foster advancements in influenza etiological surveillance networks and broader respiratory disease surveillance systems [32].

Our model leverages search data as a predictive, rather than causal, signal. Increased search volumes are primarily an early consequence of influenza activity (public concern or symptoms), which precedes laboratory confirmation. We use this temporal lead to forecast trends 1‒3 weeks ahead. Any visual impression that predictions lag behind observations is likely an artifact of connecting discrete weekly forecasts. The model makes independent weekly predictions; minor point-to-point errors, when connected, can create a perception of phase shift without implying a failure to anticipate the epidemic trend. The high overall concordance (R2) confirms the model successfully captures future trajectories.

There are several limitations in our research. Firstly, the applicability of the Baidu index may limit the generalizability of our framework to regions where other search engines dominate. Further inclusion of additional search engine data such as Google search index and meteorological information from diverse countries and regions is helpful for providing valuable insights for global influenza prediction efforts. Secondly, due to challenges in quantification and estimation, we did not incorporate news data into our analysis. However, news reports may influence people’s search behaviors [33], and with the rapid evolution of new media platforms, the increasing impact on the individuals is becoming more apparent. Thirdly, our model was developed and validated using data from the pre-pandemic period. While this ensures a clear assessment of its performance for typical seasonal influenza, its predictive accuracy for future seasons, especially in the post-pandemic era where transmission dynamics may have shifted, requires continuous evaluation and potential model recalibration with new data. Specifically, periodic and comprehensive retraining is essential when a complete new dataset from an epidemic season becomes available, particularly for the rolling forecasting model to reset error accumulation. For routine annual updates, a sliding-window retraining approach offers a robust balance between adapting to new trends and preserving historical stability. Furthermore, the exploration of online learning techniques represents a promising avenue for achieving more efficient incremental updates, subject to the implementation of careful performance monitoring protocols. Finally, although our model demonstrated strong predictive power using 2013‒2018 data, we acknowledge that the evolution of online behavior, including the rise of AI-powered chatbots, may influence the future applicability of search-based predictors. Future applications should periodically re-evaluate and potentially update the keyword library to reflect contemporary search habits.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated that the LSTM model outperformed conventional machine learning models in accurately predicting influenza epidemic dynamics. The integration of network big data with meteorological data holds significant potential for enhancing influenza surveillance and early-warming; Nonetheless, the traditional high-quality etiological surveillance networks remain indispensable. Our efforts provide novel insights into streamlining and facilitating influenza surveillance, thereby enabling local health departments to promptly detect and respond to influenza epidemics.
