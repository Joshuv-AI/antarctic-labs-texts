Association between a plant-based diet and the risk of sarcopenia among older adults: a case-control study
Authors: Marzieh Mahmoodi, Mitra Soltani, Mehran Nouri, Zainab Shateri, Marzieh Shoja, Milad Rajabzadeh-dehkordi, Nasrin Nasimi, Mohammad Hossein Dabbaghmanesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited studies have shown a negative correlation between healthy plant-based diets and sarcopenia compared to unhealthy and more limited plant-based diets. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the association between the plant-based diet index (PDI) and the odds of sarcopenia among the elderly.MethodsIn this case-control study, 80 people over 65 years old with sarcopenia and 80 people without sarcopenia participated. Based on the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia guidelines, sarcopenia was diagnosed. Additionally, a 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire was applied to evaluate the participants’ dietary intake. According to the PDI method, food items were classified into 18 food groups to calculate PDI, healthy PDI (hPDI), and unhealthy PDI (uPDI). Moreover, multivariable logistic regression models were considered to evaluate the association between PDIs and the odds of sarcopenia.ResultsAfter adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, education level, and income in the second model, a significant positive association was observed between higher adherence to the uPDI and sarcopenia; participants in the highest tertile of uPDI had increased odds of sarcopenia compared to those in the lowest tertile (odds ratio (OR) = 3.689, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.284–10.593, P = 0.015). In contrast, in the fully adjusted model (adjusted for age, BMI, physical activity, energy intake, protein intake, education level, and income), higher adherence to the overall PDI was associated with reduced odds of sarcopenia; participants in the second (OR = 0.128, 95% CI: 0.030–0.556, P = 0.006) and highest tertile (OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.024–0.718, P = 0.019) of PDI showed a lower risk compared with those in the lowest tertile.ConclusionsThe findings of the present study showed a negative association between PDI and the odds of sarcopenia. Further studies are recommended to confirm the current results.Keywords: Plant-based diet, Plant-based diet index, Sarcopenia, Older adults

Introduction

Improvements in the healthcare system have led to higher life expectancy in the last few decades [1]. Aging is a natural loss of physiological function that can be attributed to an increased risk of morbidity and mortality [1]. Aging is associated with the development of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, neurological and musculoskeletal disorders, and cancers [2]. Various underlying mechanisms are involved in the progression of age-related diseases, including genetic alterations, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation and oxidative stress, cellular senescence, and telomere shortening [3, 4].

Sarcopenia is one of the most common consequences associated with aging. Based on global consensus, the definition includes the deterioration of muscle quantity and quality, strength, and physical performance [5]. From the 4th to the 8th decade of life, approximately 50% of muscle mass and strength decrease following a linear pattern [6]. Chronic inflammation and cytokine disturbances, immunological dysregulation, neuromuscular dysfunction, especially neuron loss, altered hormonal levels and sensitivity, particularly sex and anabolic hormones, malnutrition, and physical inactivity are proven to be involved in the pathogenesis and progression of sarcopenia [7, 8]. Sarcopenic status is associated with an increased risk of frailty, dependent living, and healthcare burden [9]. Additionally, muscle loss leads to fatigue, physical inactivity, and reduced energy expenditure [10].

Non-pharmacological interventions for sarcopenia emphasize physical activity, mostly resistance exercise, and nutritional strategies [11]. The effect of supplementation with protein and amino acids, vitamin D, antioxidants, and omega-3 fatty acids on sarcopenia has been largely studied [12, 13]. Despite the strong correlation between specific micronutrients and macronutrients and disease risk, dietary elements’ synergistic and cumulative effects have many benefits in terms of nutritional indices. The plant-based diet index (PDI) was introduced to evaluate adherence to a healthier plant-based diet (consuming more whole grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, seeds, and nuts) and a lower intake of unhealthy plant groups (including refined grains and sugar-containing foods and beverages) [14–16]. Following a healthy PDI was associated with a lower risk of non-communicable diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, obesity, cancer, and all-cause mortality [14–16]. A well-planned, healthy, plant-based diet that provides adequate high-quality protein offers adequate amounts of vitamins, minerals, and antioxidant nutrients to develop and maintain skeletal muscle structure. Limited studies have shown a negative correlation between healthy plant-based diets and sarcopenia compared to unhealthy and more limited plant-based diets [17, 18].

Therefore, given the growing interest in research on plant-based diets and their promising effects in prevention and treatment, we aimed to evaluate the association between PDI and the odds of sarcopenia among older adults through a case-control design.

Methods

Study population

In the present case-control study, the cases were 80 participants aged 65 years and older with sarcopenia, and the controls were 80 non-sarcopenic participants aged 65 years and older who were matched with the cases by sex. Cases and controls were selected from community-dwelling older adults referring to health centers in Shiraz, Iran, from August 2017 to February 2018 in a previous cross-sectional study [19] who were eligible to be included in the present study.

In the present study, individuals over 65 who were independently living and physically active were considered inclusion criteria. However, participants with a history of chronic diseases, including severe cardiac, pulmonary, or musculoskeletal diseases, severe neurological disorders, stroke, cancers, or any acute organ failure and cognitive problems were excluded from the study. Therefore, all participants were assessed for these comorbidities.

The height and weight of the participants were determined based on standard methods, and then body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight (kg) by the square of the height (m2). In addition, a general checklist including age, sex, education, and smoking status was completed and collected for all participants. For the physical activity assessment, a validated international physical activity questionnaire (IPAQ) was used [20].

An informed consent form was also collected for all participants. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.SCHEANUT.REC.1402.121). Some details of the present study have been previously published [1, 21].

Sarcopenia diagnosis

According to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), the diagnosis of confirmed sarcopenia was based on low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and/or low muscle function [22]. Therefore, body composition was evaluated to determine the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) [23] (appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) (kg)/squared height (m2)) using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (InBody S10 analyzer (BioSpace Co., Ltd., South Korea)). An SMI of less than 7 kg/m2 for men and less than 5.7 kg/m2 for women was considered the diagnostic cut-off point for low muscle mass. Handgrip strength (HGS) was also determined using a hydraulic hand dynamometer (model MSD, Sihan, Korea), and was considered low muscle mass strength if it was less than 26 kg for men and less than 18 kg for women. Muscle function was assessed using walking speed for 4 m (gait speed), and values less than 0.8 m/s were used as the diagnostic cut-off point for sarcopenia [19, 22].

Dietary assessment and food grouping

In the current study, to evaluate the participants’ dietary intake, a 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was completed for all participants, and the obtained records were then converted to grams [24]. Finally, the received energy and nutrients were determined and used for the final analysis using the adapted version of Nutritionist IV for Iranian software (version 7.0 N-Squared Computing) [25].

According to the PDI method, food items were classified into 18 food groups to calculate PDI, healthy PDI (hPDI), and unhealthy PDI (uPDI). Finally, they were classified into three main categories according to the type of food groups, including healthy plant foods (whole grains, nuts, vegetables, vegetable oils, fruits, legumes, and tea/coffee), less healthy plant foods (refined grains, sugar-sweetened drinks, fruit juices, potatoes, and sweets/desserts), and animal foods (eggs, dairy, fish/kinds of seafood, animal fats, meat, and miscellaneous animal-based foods). Then, all 18 food groups were converted into deciles, and each group was given a score from 1 to 10 or a reverse score based on the index. For the PDI, positive scores were considered for healthy and less healthy plant food items and reverse scores for animal-based food groups. For the hPDI, reverse scores were given to less healthy plant and animal groups and positive scores for healthy plant group items. Also, for the uPDI, positive scores were considered for less healthy plant-based food items and reverse scores for healthy plant and animal-based food groups. The final score for each PDI, uPDI, and hPDI was calculated between 18 and 180; thus, a higher final score was related to greater adherence to the dietary pattern [26–28].

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 24). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered a statistically significant level. The normal distribution of the data was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Independent sample t-tests and chi-squared tests were used to compare continuous and categorical variables between participants, respectively. Moreover, multivariable logistic regression models were considered to evaluate the association between PDIs and sarcopenia odds. The confounding variables used in the regression model included age, BMI, physical activity, energy intake, protein intake, education level, and income.

Discussion

These findings indicate that adherence to a plant-based diet is associated with lower odds of sarcopenia. However, given the study design and relatively small sample size, these results should be interpreted with caution and cannot establish a causal relationship. Also, the odds of abnormality in muscle strength were significantly decreased across tertiles of the PDI scores. Moreover, uPDI was associated with higher odds of abnormality in gait speed.

The results of the present study showed a significant inverse relationship between the second and highest tertiles of PDI and the risk of sarcopenia. These findings suggest that individuals who follow plant-based diets may have a lower risk of sarcopenia. There are limited results in the correlation between PDI and sarcopenia. In a population-based study, a higher dietary diversity score (DDS) [29] of the total diet, animal-based diet, and plant-based diet was associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia; however, the indirect correlation between the odds of sarcopenia and DDS of a plant-based diet without legumes and nuts wasn’t identified. Indeed, DDS, which is interpreted as the number of foods or food groups included in the diet at a particular time, was inversely associated with the possibility of sarcopenia. In addition, in subgroup analysis by sex, the negative association between DDS of the total, animal-based, and plant-based diet and the risk of sarcopenia was observed in only the male group [17]. One of the most prevalent consequences of sarcopenia is frailty in older adults. A cohort study showed that an increased hPDI score was associated with a lower incidence of frailty. At the same time, a higher uPDI was positively correlated with the odds of frailty in the community-dwelling geriatric population [18]. Another consistent study revealed that every 10-unit increase in hPDI was associated with a 15% reduced risk of frailty; conversely, a positive correlation was observed between the risk of frailty and uPDI [30].

Nutrition and physical activity are two key components of improving body composition. As explained earlier, the PDI represents the consumption of plant-based foods along with the lower consumption of animal-based foods. The hPDI includes whole grains, vegetables and vegetable oils, fruits, nuts, legumes, tea, and coffee. In contrast, the uPDI includes fruit juices, potatoes, refined grains, sweets and desserts, and sugar-sweetened beverages [31].

The most relevant dietary factors for muscle health are vitamin D, calcium, n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, B9, and B12 [32–34]. Plant-based food groups can provide these nutrients to some extent, except for B12. In addition, plant foods can sufficiently provide protein requirements. Proteins strongly enhance muscle synthesis, strength, and performance, thus protecting against sarcopenia [35]. In addition, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant substances found in abundance in vegetarian diets can block inflammatory and oxidative damage to muscle fibers [32]. On the other hand, high glycemic index foods such as refined grains, sugars, sweetened beverages, and fruit juices induce spikes in blood glucose. Improved glycemic control and insulin sensitivity have been reported to correlate favorably with sarcopenia and its components, including muscle mass, HGS, and gait speed. Moreover, glycemic level was also suggested as a risk factor for sarcopenia [36]. Hyperglycemia deteriorates skeletal muscles through various mechanisms [37]. Furthermore, in a hyperglycemic state, alterations of amino acids in the structure of proteins or lipids by monosaccharides accelerate the production of advanced glycation end products (AGEs). This phenomenon leads to non-enzymatic cross-links in the muscle collagen structure and thus increases the risk of fragility, strength reduction, and sarcopenia [37]. A recent systematic review of observational studies concluded that while AGEs are indirectly associated with muscle mass, strength, and physical performance, they are predictive of sarcopenia [29].

Another explanation for the protective effect of hPDI against sarcopenia may be due to dietary acid-base load. Alkalinogenic dietary parameters such as calcium, magnesium, and especially potassium are abundant in fruits and vegetables, and it has been shown that a greater alkaline dietary load has a favorable relationship with SMI in women [38]. Alkalinogenic diets can mildly attenuate the acidogenic properties of food, mainly by plentiful consumption of fruits and vegetables [39]. Accordingly, previous evidence can explain why hPDI positively affects muscle health compared to the negative impact of uPDI. However, healthy plant foods can provide the dietary recommendation for protein; still, in older adults with suppressed appetite, the intake may not reach the recommended level. In addition, limiting amino acids and protein-binding compounds such as phytic acid may interfere with the efficacy of plant proteins. Furthermore, there are controversial results regarding the effectiveness of animal and plant proteins on muscle mass [40, 41]. Therefore, it seems that less restrictive, healthful vegetarian diets may be a better recommendation for older adults to protect against sarcopenia.

Limitations and strengths

This present study had limitations. First, due to the nature of case-control studies, the exact effect of plant-based diets on muscle status cannot be clearly investigated. Therefore, it is recommended to conduct clinical trials. Second, on the one hand, case-control studies consider a specific period, and on the other hand, muscle mass is affected by lifelong nutritional patterns and lifestyle, particularly physical activity. Therefore, conducting long-term cohort studies is recommended. Third, the assessment of markers of nitrogen balance and muscle wasting can directly clarify the relationship between dietary elements and sarcopenic status. Fourth, the small sample size was another limitation of the present study. On the other hand, since the current study included elderly subjects with sarcopenia and a healthy control group, the female participants were in a postmenopausal state. Hence, the deleterious effect of gender and menopausal conditions was eliminated. In the present study, the analyses were adjusted for confounders to remove the plausible effects. However, there may be other confounding factors, such as alcohol consumption or a measure of adiposity (waist or hip circumference or waist-to-hip ratio), that were not included in the present study. The evaluation of the PDI score from a valid and reliable 168-item FFQ was another strength of the present study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of the present study showed a negative association between PDI and the odds of sarcopenia. It is recommended to evaluate the effect of a healthy plant-based diet on the incidence, progression, and severity of sarcopenia.
