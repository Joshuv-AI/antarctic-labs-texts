A Prenatally Diagnosed Rare Vascular Ring Formed by Left Aortic Arch and Right Ductus Arteriosus
Authors: Nana Matsuzawa, Yuka Yamamoto, Eri Kitamura, Yuri Ishida, Jun Takeda, Noboru Tanaka, Lisa K Hornberger, Atsuo Itakura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA vascular ring arises during the development of the fetal arches and is often associated with a double aortic arch or right-sided aorta, an aberrant left subclavian artery with a posterior esophageal component, and a left-sided ductus arteriosus.Case SummaryThis is a rare vascular ring formed by a left aortic arch, aberrant right subclavian artery, and right ductus arteriosus that was diagnosed prenatally by fetal echocardiography. The 3-vessels and trachea (3VT) view with 2-dimensional and color Doppler sweeps were helpful in defining the vascular pathology. Tracheomalacia was also noted, and genetic tests confirmed the presence of 22q11.2 deletion.DiscussionThe distal portions of the right sixth and right eighth dorsal arches were persistent, whereas the right fourth and distal part of the left sixth arches disappeared in the process of fetal development.Take-Home MessageUse of the 3VT echocardiography sweep facilitates prenatal diagnosis of rare vascular rings.Key words: vascular ring, left aortic arch and right ductus arteriosus, prenatal diagnosis

History of Presentation

A previously healthy 39-year-old gravida 2 para 1 woman with a spontaneous otherwise uncomplicated singleton pregnancy was referred to our hospital for a suspected fetal vascular ring. Her family history was unremarkable.

The differential diagnosis was vascular ring with double aortic arch or right-sided aorta.

Investigations

At 29 weeks, obstetrical ultrasound revealed an estimated fetal weight of 1433 g (appropriate for gestational age), moderate polyhydramnios, normal left-sided stomach bubble, and no extracardiac abnormalities. Fetal echocardiography revealed situs solitus, normal segmental anatomy with symmetrical 4 chambers, and normally related and symmetric great arteries. In the 3-vessel and trachea (3VT) sweep, the aortic arch coursed in front and then to the left of the trachea, continuing to the descending thoracic aorta (Figure 1A, Video 1). The ductus arteriosus arose from the main pulmonary artery, coursed along the right side of the trachea, joining an aberrant right subclavian artery, which coursed behind the trachea to join the descending aorta (Figure 1B, Video 2). The left aortic arch and right ductus arteriosus formed an unusual type of vascular ring around the trachea (Figure 1). A hypoplastic thymus was also suspected from this 3VT view. In the presence of this unusual vascular ring, there were concerns that the polyhydramnios could be secondary to airway and/or esophageal obstruction. Otherwise, this pregnancy course was not eventful.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

After consulting with our pediatric team and with concerns that respiratory management of the newborn could be challenging, the baby was delivered by elective cesarean section at 38 weeks 5 days gestation. A female newborn, weighing 2340 g, cried immediately at delivery, and initially had no respiratory distress. Apgar scores were 4 and 8 points at 1 minute and 5 minutes, respectively. The baby did not require oxygen for the first few hours but thereafter developed progressive inspiratory stridor. On bronchoscopy, although there was no tracheal stenosis, there was softening of the hypopharynx, decreased mobility of the epiglottis, and salivary inflow into the airway, consistent with dysphagia. With increasing respiratory distress and hypoxia, the baby was intubated.

Postnatal contrast-enhanced computer tomography confirmed the unusual nature of the vascular ring (Figure 2). With spontaneous ductus arteriosus closure on day 3, a second bronchoscopy showed no compression of the trachea. A barium swallow performed on day 8 of life showed normal passage of contrast medium, without evidence of stenosis (Figure 3), and the infant was extubated on day 9. However, on day 36 of life, increased respiratory effort and wheezing developed, accompanied by decreased oxygen saturations, and a third bronchoscopy was performed. This bronchoscopy showed a posteriorly positioned tongue base obstructing the airway and a large amount of airway secretions. The baby was intubated again, and tracheostomy was performed at 2 months. Surgical division of the vascular ring was not performed owing to the absence of airway or esophageal compression as well as concerns about potential complications such as recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy. The computed tomography angiography multiplanar reconstruction images at 3 months of age demonstrated the relationship of the vascular ring to the airway and esophagus. The distance between the trachea, the esophagus, and the vascular ring is relatively well preserved, and there is no obvious evidence of compression or stenosis (Figure 2D). The infant was discharged at 4 months of age. After discharge, home oxygen therapy was initiated only during nighttime, and her course has been uneventful. A genetic diagnosis of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome was confirmed. The underlying cause of both the dysphagia and laryngomalacia was considered to be 22q11.2 deletion syndrome.

Discussion

We present the prenatal diagnosis of a very rare form of a vascular ring: that of a left aortic arch, aberrant right subclavian artery, and right-sided ductus arteriosus. To date, only 20 cases have been reported in the literature,1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with all but 1 case5 diagnosed postnatally, at 3 months to 24 years. As has been reported for other types of vascular rings and slings,5,6 the 3VT sweep facilitated its diagnosis. Normally the left aortic and ductal arches course toward the left of the trachea, converging at the descending aorta in a V-shaped pattern recognized in the 3VT view (Figures 1A and 1B). The most common type of vascular ring is associated with a U-shaped appearance as the right-sided aortic arch gives rise to an aberrant left subclavian artery that runs posterior to the esophagus and trachea, with the ductus arteriosus joining the subclavian to the left side of the trachea. In contrast, double aortic arches form a more O-shaped vascular structure surrounding the trachea and esophagus.

Reviewing embryologic development of the aortic and ductal arches provides insight into how this rare vascular ring may occur. As shown in Figure 4A, the normal left-sided aortic arch evolves through regression of the distal part of the right sixth arch (R6 = right ductus arteriosus) and disappearance of the right dorsal aortic eighth segment (R8).7 In our case (Figure 4B), the distal portion of the right sixth arch (R6) and the right dorsal aortic eighth segment (R8) were persistent, whereas the right fourth arch (R4) and the distal part of the left sixth arch (L6 = left ductus arteriosus) disappeared, leaving a left-sided aortic arch and right-sided ductal arch (Figure 4). Remnants of R8 became the Kommerell diverticulum, which arose from the aberrant right subclavian artery.

(A) In normal development of the aortic arch, the distal portion of the right sixth arch and the right dorsal aortic eighth segment disappears. (B) In contrast, in the present case, the right fourth arch (R4) and the distal part of the left sixth arch (L6) disappeared, resulting in the formation of a vascular ring with the left aortic arch and the right ductus arteriosus. aAo = ascending aorta; dAo = descending aorta; E = esophagus; LCCA = left common carotid artery; LPA = left pulmonary artery; LSCA = left subclavian artery; PA = pulmonary artery; RCCA = right common carotid artery; RPA = right pulmonary artery; RSCA = right subclavian artery; T = trachea.

Polyhydramnios can be found in the context of important airway and esophageal obstruction, but it may also occur in other conditions, including genetic syndromes with reduced fetal swallowing. We suspect that the polyhydramnios was secondary to the genetic diagnosis of 22q11.2 deletion, with lack of everted diaphragms, echogenic lungs, and even the presence of ascites—features of severe congenital high airway obstruction syndrome—reported for one case of vascular ring.8

This rare type of vascular ring was first reported in 1948.1 Although its reported incidence is very low, this may be due to the difficulty of achieving an accurate diagnosis after postnatal ductus arteriosus closure. Having said this, and although other vascular ring diagnoses have been facilitated by application of 3VT screening,9 only a single previous group has identified this rare form of vascular ring prenatally, in a fetus at 19 weeks of gestation in 2024.5 Similar to the present case, the patient was also diagnosed with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome.5 Among fetuses with a right aortic arch and left ductus, genetic syndromes have been reported in 9%, and 22q11.2 deletion was present in 6%.10 Double aortic arches are associated with a genetic syndrome in 11.5%, and 22q11.2 deletion syndrome in 3.8%,6 After a prenatal diagnosis of a vascular ring, genetic counseling and availability of advanced genetic testing are critical in the care of affected pregnancies.

The key imaging modality in this case is the 3VT sweep. Abnormalities were initially detected through routine 3VT-based screening, and detailed assessment of vascular branching patterns, interpreted in the context of embryologic development, was particularly useful for making the diagnosis. The postnatal course and prognosis are significantly influenced by the underlying genetic background; in this case, the presence of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome had a major impact on the prognosis. The 3VT view can also be evaluated as early as the first and second trimesters, and early recognition of abnormal findings on 3VT view may prompt timely genetic testing and enable more comprehensive prenatal management.

Conclusions

We present a prenatal diagnosis of a very rare type of vascular ring formed by a left aortic arch, an aberrant right subclavian artery, and right ductus arteriosus associated with 22q11.2 deletion. Use of the 3VT sweep facilitated its diagnosis.
