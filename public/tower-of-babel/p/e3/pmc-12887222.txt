Low‐Dose Rivaroxaban and Deferred Stenting for STEMI With Large Coronary Thrombus Burden: A New Hope?
Authors: Keerthi T Gondi, Kayla Secrest, Amit Goyal
Published: 2025

ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is an emergent diagnosis necessitating rapid and timely revascularization to restore perfusion and limit the extent of myocardial injury. The presence of a large coronary thrombus burden presents unique procedural and clinical challenges, increasing the risk of embolization and microvascular obstruction during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The coronary slow flow or no‐reflow phenomenon resulting from distal embolization translates to impaired stent performance, increased infarct size, and ultimately poorer outcomes. Over the last several decades, multiple approaches have been investigated, including intracoronary fibrinolytics, thrombus aspiration, 1 glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (GPI), 2 and intracoronary vasodilators, 3 but none have demonstrated a clear benefit. The optimal revascularization strategy for STEMI with large coronary thrombus burden remains unclear.

One alternative strategy in highly thrombotic lesions is the delay or deferral of stent implantation after initial coronary reperfusion has been achieved. Deferred stenting may allow time for thrombus reduction, thereby reducing the risk of procedural embolization and potentially improving myocardial salvage and clinical outcomes. Despite the potential benefit of reducing no‐reflow and infarct size, 4 the strategy of deferred stenting in clinical trials has not demonstrated a survival benefit when combined with contemporary antithrombotic therapy, including aspirin, clopidogrel, heparin, and GPI. 5 , 6 Therefore, deferred PCI is not recommended as a routine strategy; however, a recent SCAI expert consensus statement acknowledges that deferral of stenting while treating with prolonged antiplatelet or antithrombotic therapy may be warranted in select patients. 7 One challenge with deferred PCI is uncertainty regarding the ideal intervening antithrombotic therapy. Low‐dose rivaroxaban is a novel oral anticoagulant with established benefit in the secondary prevention of cardiovascular events following acute coronary syndrome. 8 The demonstrated antithrombotic efficacy and comparable bleeding risk of low‐dose rivaroxaban in combination with standard DAPT make it a promising option for patients with large coronary thrombus burden. The use of oral anticoagulants to reduce thrombus burden as part of a deferred stenting strategy in the acute setting has not previously been studied. Given the significant morbidity associated with thrombotic lesions and no‐reflow, there remains ongoing interest in identifying an optimal “goldilocks” approach of deferred stenting with the use of short‐term neoadjuvant antithrombotic therapy to reduce thrombus burden while minimizing bleeding risk.

In this context, Mennuni et al report the primary results of the “Reduction of thrombus burden with short‐term, low‐dose rivaroxaban use inacute myocardial infarction: the ARISE‐ARMYDA 7 randomized trial” in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), 9 a mechanistic pilot study investigating the use of low‐dose rivaroxaban to reduce thrombus burden before deferred PCI for ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) with large thrombus burden. The study randomized 40 patients with STEMI and large coronary thrombus burden to receive either low‐dose rivaroxaban in addition to standard DAPT or DAPT alone for 5 to 7 days before deferred PCI. All patients underwent emergent reperfusion on presentation, with either balloon angioplasty or aspiration thrombectomy, and 75% received peri‐procedural GPI therapy. Importantly, patients were excluded if they had a high bleeding risk. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) was used to define thrombus burden during the index and deferred stenting procedures. In this setting, the addition of low‐dose rivaroxaban significantly reduced thrombus burden at re‐OCT, with a lower Thrombus Score (61% relative reduction with low‐dose rivaroxaban compared with 36% reduction in the control group) and significant quantitative reductions in thrombus area, length, and volume compared with baseline OCT. The use of low‐dose rivaroxaban was well‐tolerated, with no significant differences observed in the incidence of stroke or major bleeding between groups. Deferred stenting was also associated with favorable outcomes in this small cohort, as there were no cases of distal embolization, no‐reflow, or vessel occlusion.

The authors are to be commended for the design and execution of this innovative pilot trial in a STEMI population. The principle finding of this study is that the use of low‐dose rivaroxaban in combination with DAPT is technically feasible and effective in reducing thrombus burden before deferred PCI in STEMI patients with large coronary thrombus burden who achieved initial reperfusion with balloon angioplasty and/or thrombus aspiration. Importantly, this study establishes that the addition of low‐dose rivaroxaban is safe when used alongside standard contemporary therapies, including procedural heparin with or without GPI, along with high‐potency DAPT, without excess bleeding. This contrasts with other antithrombotic therapies such as GPI and fibrinolysis, which also reduce thrombus burden at the cost of increased rates of bleeding. The study is strengthened by its use of OCT to objectively assess coronary thrombus, showing a significant reduction in multiple parameters of thrombus burden in the low‐dose rivaroxaban arm. The use of OCT establishes a causal link between low‐dose rivaroxaban and potential improvement in infarct size and clinical outcomes.

Importantly, this is a mechanistic pilot study with a small sample size, intended to establish feasibility and safety in a select patient cohort with low bleeding risk. Although large thrombus burden is associated with increased infarct size and a worse prognosis, the present study findings should not be interpreted to imply an improvement in clinical outcomes in low‐dose rivaroxaban until further data are available in a more generalized population.

In our experience, there is significant variability in real‐world practice patterns for thrombotic lesions in STEMI. Anecdotally, the most common approach is the use of thrombus aspiration, intracoronary GPI, and vasodilators during the index procedure, followed by maintenance therapy with DAPT, intravenous anticoagulation, and/or GPI until a “re‐look” angiogram is performed, at which point deferred PCI is considered. It remains unclear how low‐dose rivaroxaban would compare with a more typical approach using GPI and anticoagulation during the interval between the index and repeat angiograms. Patients with STEMI exist on a spectrum of thrombotic and bleeding risk, and it is reasonable that a more “aggressive” approach employing GPI and anticoagulation may be useful in patients with high thrombotic and low bleeding risk (such as those in the present study). Meanwhile, patients with high bleeding risk may theoretically benefit from low‐dose rivaroxaban for thrombus reduction, in order to spare the use of more potent therapies like GPI. The comparative thrombotic efficacy and bleeding risk of low‐dose rivaroxaban versus other contemporary therapies require further investigation to aid in risk stratification and patient selection.

Patients in this study received low‐dose rivaroxaban for 5 to 7 days before deferred PCI. This duration of delay may be challenging in routine clinical practice, particularly with respect to prolonged hospital stays and resource utilization. In contrast, deferred PCI was performed at 9 hours in the Deferred Stenting Versus Immediate Stenting to Prevent No‐ or Slow‐Reflow in Acute ST‐Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (DEFER‐STEMI) trial 4 and 3 days in the Deferred versus conventional stent implantation in patients with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (DANAMI‐3‐DEFER) trial. 5 Whether a shorter duration of low‐dose rivaroxaban would be sufficient to effectively reduce thrombus burden remains unanswered.

The key putative mechanism by which thrombus reduction and deferred PCI might improve clinical outcomes is the reduction in distal embolization and infarct size. To clarify this point, it would be helpful to know the comparative rate of peri‐procedural MI in patients receiving low‐dose rivaroxaban versus standard therapy. If thrombus reduction with low‐dose rivaroxaban confers benefit by limiting infarction size, this should be reflected by a reduction of biomarker‐detected myocardial injury. Such findings would support the hypothesis that a deferred PCI strategy incorporating low‐dose rivaroxaban may improve myocardial salvage and reduce infarct size.

STEMI complicated by large coronary thrombus burden is a challenging clinical scenario, as thrombus embolization and no‐reflow can exacerbate infarct size and worsen clinical outcomes. There is significant variation in clinical decision‐making with respect to primary PCI and selection of thrombus reduction therapy (Figure). Deferred PCI is an appealing approach to allow time for thrombus reduction with antithrombotic therapy, with the cost of elevated risk of bleeding. The presented data offer optimism that the use of oral anticoagulation with low‐dose rivaroxaban may potentially benefit patients with highly thrombotic lesions undergoing deferred PCI. Larger randomized clinical trials of low‐dose rivaroxaban and deferred PCI, with a focus on imaging‐derived infarct size and survival, are needed to further elucidate the clinical benefits. In the meantime, the ARISE‐ARMYDA 7 trial offers new hope for the use of deferred PCI in this high‐risk patient population.
