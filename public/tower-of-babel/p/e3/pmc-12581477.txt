Spatiotemporal trend of sensory impairments in China and its provinces from 2011 to 2018: insights from CHARLS
Authors: Zhijian Zhang, Zhennan Cai, Cong Li, Shunming Liu, Sheng Li, Lei Liu, Lijun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSensory impairments impact public health both the health and quality of life among middle-aged and older adults. However, in China there is little awareness of the impact of sensory disabilities. In this study, we rates were assessed for isolated visual impairments, isolated hearing impairments, and dual sensory impairments among elderly Chinese from 2011 to 2018 using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the risk factors for sensory impairments. Spatial statistical analyses were conducted to assess the differences in sensory impairments across Chinese provinces. Our study highlights significant variations in the prevalence of sensory impairments across different provinces in China, with notable improvements in visual impairments over time but persistent or worsening trends in hearing impairments and dual sensory impairments. The spatial clustering of these impairments, particularly in western regions such as Xinjiang, underscores the need for targeted public health interventions. We also found that Gender consistently associated with sensory impairments, while diabetes and digestive system diseases significantly affecting visual impairments. Government should consider enhancing public health initiatives focused on the prevention and treatment of sensory impairments, especially in regions with higher prevalence rates, to improve the overall health and quality of life of the aging population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25086-w.Keywords: Sensory impairments CHARLS public health

Introduction

Sensory impairments (SI), particularly those relating to vision and hearing loss, represent not only substantial public health challenges but also profoundly affect individuals’ overall health and quality of life [1]. These two impairments are often studied separately in research and considered individually in the planning and delivery of services, which are termed single visual impairments (SVI) and single hearing impairments (SHI). However, they can occur concurrently, and this condition is more severe, commonly referred to as dual sensory impairment (DSI) [2]. These sensory impairments are especially common among older adults, as vision and hearing deteriorate with age [3]. It is estimated that by 2050, more than 900 million people worldwide will experience clinically significant hearing impairment (HI), and approximately 2.2 billion people will have some form of visual impairment (VI) [4]. A study on hearing loss (HL) in the UK found that from 2002 to 2017, HL prevalence increased significantly by 10.2%, and there was a substantial difference in prevalence between the southern and northern regions. This finding suggests that geographical patterns are an important factor influencing SI [5]. Many developing industrialized countries may face a higher prevalence of VI. However, these countries lack comprehensive public health statistics, making it impossible to conduct a thorough and reliable analysis of SI prevalence [6].

China, home to one-fifth of the world’s population (1.41 billion) and 190 million people aged ≥ 65 years (13.5%), bears a heavy burden of deafness and blindness [7]. Yet, few studies have reported the national epidemiological characteristics of DSI, partly because conducting joint surveys on vision and hearing poses considerable logistical and societal challenges. In visual health, the Chinese government has strengthened research funding, expanded population-based cataract studies [8], and advanced blindness prevention through improved management, technical guidance, and service systems [9]. In hearing health, it has surveyed elderly populations across regions, promoted hearing loss prevention, and enhanced treatment strategies to reduce its social impact [10]. However, existing studies on VI or HI are separate [10, 11]. Xian et al. reported substantial geographic disparities in HI prevalence (up to 77% in North China) without examining temporal trends [10], while Jin et al. linked SI prevalence to socioeconomic status but did not consider age or gender [12]. To date, no comprehensive spatiotemporal study has assessed VI and HI at both national and provincial levels, limiting evaluation of China’s public health interventions and SI prevalence trends.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative biennial survey of adults aged 45 and older across 28 provinces. Covering four waves from 2011 to 2018 [13]. CHARLS provides a valuable foundation for long-term health trend tracking and supports public health research, particularly in investigating the evolution and burden of sensory impairments among China’s aging population.

This study aims to utilize the CHARLS database to calculate the prevalence of DSI, SVI, and SHI in China from 2011 to 2018, analyze the associated influencing factors, and reveal the spatiotemporal variations in prevalence across different provinces. The goal is to enhance public awareness of sensory impairment prevention, increase attention to provinces with a high prevalence of SI in China, and provide references for public health policy formulation.

Method

Database

All data were enrolled from CHARLS dataset (https://charls.pku.edu.cn/). In essence, CHARLS collects high-quality data through face-to-face interviews and structured questionnaires from a nationally representative sample of Chinese residents aged 45 and older, selected using a multistage, stratified, and proportional-to-size sampling method, with stratification based on region, urban-rural status, and GDP. The baseline survey, conducted in 2011, included 17,708 respondents from 450 villages or urban communities in 28 of the 31 provincial-level administrative divisions in mainland China. Follow-up surveys were conducted every two years, specifically in 2013, 2015, and 2018. The study followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Definition of sensory impairment

VI was identified in the CHARLS data through self-reported visual function assessments. Participants were asked two questions related to visual impairment: (1) How is your distance vision, for example, recognizing a friend across the street? (2) How is your near vision, for example, reading a regular newspaper? Responses to these questions were “poor,” “fair,” “good,” “very good,” and “excellent.” In the current study, participants who reported “poor” vision for either distance or near tasks were classified as having VI. HI was identified in the CHARLS data through self-reported hearing function assessments. How is your hearing? Responses to these questions were “poor,” “fair,” “good,” “very good,” and “excellent.” In the current study, participants who reported “poor” hearing were classified as having HI. When both VI and HI are present, it is classified as DSI. If only VI is present, it is classified as SVI. If only HI is present, it is classified as SHI.

Variates

Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, educational level, residence, and marital status, lifestyle such as smoking and drinking status, presence of chronic diseases, activities of daily living (ADL) difficulty, and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) difficulty were also collected. In current study, we excluded participants who were younger than 45 years of age and those with missing data regarding SI or covariates to avoid bias and estimation errors.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a comprehensive and detailed analysis of statistical data using Stata 18 and R 4.4. Univariate ANOVA (Continuous variables with normal distribution and homogeneity of variance), Kruskal-Wallis test (Variables with non-normal distribution or heterogeneous variance), and chi-square test (Categorical variables) were employed to contrast the baseline characteristics of participants rooted in the gathered data. Age-standardized prevalence rates of DSI along with their associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) was analysed using the age composition data from 2018 for reference. Correlation analysis was conducted using weighted logistic regression to enhance the comparability and accuracy of the results, with weights provided by the CHARLS database. To measure the strength and significance of associations between these varying variables and sensory impairments, weighted multivariable logistic regression was used. This enabled us to calculate Odds Ratios (OR) with their accompanying 95% confidence intervals, while simultaneously adjusting for potential confounders. To further analyze the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics of DSI prevalence, we utilized ArcGIS 12.0 to assess spatial autocorrelation with Moran’s I index and identify specific clustering patterns using the local Moran’s I index. This analysis includes four clustering types: High-High Cluster, Low-Low Cluster, High-Low Cluster, and Low-High Cluster. A High-High Cluster signifies that both a province and its neighboring areas have high prevalence values. Additionally, we also employed the Getis-Ord G statistic to complement Moran’s I in detecting local hotspots and cold spots, thereby determining whether statistically significant high-value or low-value clusters exist in each province and its neighboring regions. In defining spatial relationships, we used the Queen contiguity method, which considers regions that share either a boundary or a vertex as neighbors, allowing for a more inclusive assessment of spatial interactions. We use SaTScan software for spatio-temporal scan analysis and visualize the results with ArcGIS 12.0, clarifying the spatial extent and temporal phases of SI disease clustering. P value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Temporal changes in SI prevalence across different provinces

The prevalence and temporal trends of DSI exhibit significant geographical variations, as evidenced by our comprehensive analysis. From 2011 to 2018, there has been a notable upward trend in the prevalence of DSI across most provinces (Fig. 2), although the magnitude of change was relatively small. Specifically, the prevalence of DSI in Chongqing remained the highest (15.27% in 2011 and 19.41% in 2018), followed by Qinghai, Anhui, and Yunnan. In contrast, Shanghai consistently had the lowest prevalence, showing a decreasing trend over the study period. Additionally, the prevalence of DSI in Chongqing, Yunnan, and Guangxi significantly increased over time, while Shandong saw a significant decrease. The prevalence of SVI showed an overall decreasing trend (Fig. 3), with significant changes in several provinces. However, SVI prevalence increased in Beijing, Fujian, Gansu, Xinjiang, and Yunnan, with Xinjiang showing the largest increase. Qinghai consistently had the highest prevalence of SVI in China (34.43% in 2011 and 32.05% in 2018). Notably, significant decreases in SVI prevalence were observed in Chongqing, Anhui, Shandong, Jiangxi, Guangdong, Tianjin, and Shanghai. The prevalence of SHI demonstrated a general uptrend (Fig. 4), with notable provincial disparities. Significant increases in SHI prevalence were observed in Qinghai and Xinjiang, while significant declines were noted in provinces such as Shanxi and Guizhou.

Factors associated with SI

Regarding DSI, several factors were found to be significantly associated with its prevalence. Age (OR: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.04–1.05, P < 0.01), urban-rural residence (OR: 1.74; 95% CI: 1.51–2.01, P < 0.01), and education level (OR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.54–0.91, P = 0.01) were significant determinants. Additionally, having arthritis (OR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.34–1.75, P < 0.01), lung disease (OR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.07–1.49, P = 0.01), kidney disease (OR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.16–1.66, P < 0.01), and digestive disease (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.09–1.42, P < 0.01) were correlated with higher DSI prevalence (Fig. 5). For SVI, female gender (OR: 1.45; 95% CI: 1.25–1.67, P < 0.01), smoking (OR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.09–1.46, P < 0.01), urban-rural residence (OR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.13–1.38, P < 0.01), having diabetes (OR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.13–1.38, P < 0.01), digestive system diseases (OR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.14–1.39, P < 0.01), and arthritis (OR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.87–1.26, P < 0.01) were significantly associated with SVI prevalence (Figure S1). In the regression analysis for SHI, age (OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.04–1.06, P < 0.01) and female gender (OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.53–0.90, P = 0.01) were significantly associated with SHI prevalence (Figure S2), The results of the sensitivity analysis are consistent with it (Table S12).

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Local Moran’s I analysis revealed significant spatial clustering of SI prevalence in China from 2011 to 2018.

For DSI (Figure S3), high-high clusters in 2011 included Henan, Anhui, Hubei, Shaanxi, Sichuan, and Gansu, with Xinjiang as a low-high cluster. In 2013, high-high clusters were Inner Mongolia, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Anhui; Xinjiang remained low-high. In 2015, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, Hubei, and Anhui were high-high clusters; Xinjiang was still low-high. By 2018, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, Hubei, and Xinjiang became high-high clusters, while Anhui showed a high-low pattern. Notably, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Hubei were repeatedly high-high across years, and Xinjiang shifted from low-high (2011–2015) to high-high (2018), consistent with its rising DSI prevalence.

For SVI (Figure S4), high-high clusters in 2011 were Shandong, Henan, Anhui, Zhejiang, Hubei, and Shaanxi, with Tianjin high-low and Xinjiang low-high. In 2013, Anhui, Hubei, Shaanxi, Gansu, Sichuan, Qinghai, and Xinjiang were high-high; Tianjin again high-low. In 2015, Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei, Shaanxi, Gansu, Sichuan, Qinghai, and Xinjiang were high-high. In 2018, Anhui, Shaanxi, Gansu, Sichuan, Qinghai, and Xinjiang remained high-high; Hebei was high-low; Tianjin and Beijing were low-low. Xinjiang’s transition from low-high (2011) to persistent high-high (2013–2018) matches its increasing SVI prevalence.

For SHI (Figure S5), in 2011 high-high clusters included Inner Mongolia, Shaanxi, Shanxi, Henan, Anhui, and Sichuan; Shanghai was low-low. In 2013, Sichuan and Anhui were high-high. In 2015, Gansu and Anhui were high-high; Jiangsu was high-low. By 2018, Xinjiang, Gansu, and Anhui were high-high, Jiangxi was high-low, and Shanghai remained low-low, indicating sustained SHI concentration in Anhui and new clustering in Xinjiang.

Spatio-Temporal scan analysis

A spatio-temporal scan analysis of SI prevalence from 2011 to 2018 identified three significant clustering areas (all P < 0.05). Also in 2018, a DSI cluster centered in Qinghai with a radius of 1337.10 km, involving 13 provinces, primarily located in the central region (Figure S8). In 2011, an SVI cluster centered in Hubei with a radius of 717.81 km, involving 11 provinces, primarily located in the southwestern region (Figure S9). In 2018, an SHI cluster centered in Chongqing with a radius of 911.92 km, involving 12 provinces, primarily located in the western and central regions (Figure S10). Discussion

Our study systematically analyzed the spatial and temporal distribution characteristics of sensory impairments (SI) among individuals aged 45 years and older in China, based on CHARLS data from 2011 to 2018. Spatial analysis revealed significant provincial disparities in SI prevalence rates. Temporally, the overall national SI prevalence showed a declining trend; however, the extent of decline varied considerably across provinces.

Further analyses of isolated sensory impairments unveiled regional variations. While most provinces showed a decreasing trend in single visual impairments (SVI) prevalence, Beijing, Fujian, Gansu, Xinjiang, and Yunnan experienced an upward trend, with Xinjiang showing the most notable increase. single hearing impairments (SHI) significantly increased in Qinghai and Xinjiang. Chongqing consistently had the highest prevalence of dual sensory impairments (DSI) in both 2011 and 2018, with a significant upward trend.

In addition to spatial-temporal variations, logistic regression analysis was employed to identify consistent influencing factors of SI prevalence across the four survey waves from 2011 to 2018. Age, urban-rural residency, and education level emerged as stable predictors of DSI. Gender, diabetes, digestive system diseases, and arthritis consistently influenced the prevalence of SVI, while SHI prevalence was primarily affected by age and gender.

DSI prevalence: stable influencing factors

In current study, we found that age, educational attainment, urban-rural residence, and arthritis were stable risk factors for DSI prevalence across four surveys [14]. Older age, lower education, rural residence, and having arthritis increased the prevalence of DSI. People with lower education and those living in rural areas have relatively poorer healthcare conditions and lack health awareness, which increases the risk of DSI. Arthritis often affects vision [15]. Regarding SHI, aside from steroids, salicylates used to treat arthritis have strong ototoxicity, which significantly increases the prevalence of HI. Consequently, patients with arthritis often experience DSI. Notably, kidney disease was also a risk factor for DSI prevalence in three surveys, particularly from 2013 to 2018. This is consistent with the findings of study by Liu et al., which indicated that kidney disease can cause hyponatremia, and sodium levels are an important indicator of hearing [16]. Studies have shown that improving sodium levels can significantly restore hearing. Additionally, kidney disease, as a microvascular complication, is also associated with retinal diseases, further contributing to VI [17]. There is also evidence that complications of chronic kidney disease (anemia and uremia) and treatment methods significantly increase the risk of VI [18]. Therefore, in the course of public health interventions for DSI, special attention should be paid to these stable influencing factors within the population.

DSI prevalence: regional distribution and spatial analysis

Our study found persistently high DSI prevalence in Chongqing, Qinghai, and Yunnan, with rising trends in Xinjiang and Guangxi. These areas, mostly in western China, tend to have lower socioeconomic development, and higher prevalence is also seen among people with lower education levels and rural residents.

In the east, Anhui showed high prevalence despite being in the Yangtze River Delta, possibly due to lower population attractiveness compared with developed neighbors [19]. Beijing remained relatively low but showed an upward trend, potentially linked to higher chronic disease rates, while Shanghai’s low and declining prevalence likely reflects better healthcare access and public awareness [20].

Spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed sustained high-high clustering in Shaanxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Hubei, regions characterized by large populations, higher altitudes, and limited healthcare resources, contributing to their long-term high prevalence of DSI. Notably, Xinjiang transitioned from low-high clustering (low local prevalence but surrounded by high-prevalence provinces such as Gansu, Qinghai, and Sichuan) during 2011–2015 to high-high clustering by 2018, indicating a marked rise in local prevalence and regional clustering. This transformation is likely driven by multi-dimensional factors: socioeconomically, Xinjiang experiences uneven economic development and disproportionate healthcare investment, with rural and remote communities facing persistent barriers to specialized ophthalmic and otologic care; environmentally, the vast territory and dispersed population increase travel distances and costs, limiting timely diagnosis and treatment; and policy-wise, despite the expansion of national vision and hearing screening programs, implementation in remote border regions may lag due to logistical challenges and healthcare workforce shortages, resulting in inadequate early detection and intervention. Together with accelerated population aging and sustained high prevalence in neighboring provinces, these factors have transformed Xinjiang from a peripheral low-prevalence area into a regional high-prevalence cluster, thereby exacerbating spatial health inequalities.

Local hotspot analysis identified Hubei, Chongqing, and Guizhou as consistent hotspots, aligning with Moran’s I analysis. Space-time scan analysis additionally highlighted Qinghai as a significant cluster in 2018, consistent with its steadily rising DSI prevalence.

These findings underscore the urgency for targeted interventions in southern and western China. For western provinces such as Qinghai and Xinjiang, challenges like high altitude, sparse populations, and transportation barriers necessitate prioritizing telemedicine systems, exemplified by the establishment of a remote consultation sub-center for complex eye diseases in Qinghai by Aier Eye Hospital, and the opening of new ophthalmic hospitals in Qinghai and Xinjiang to improve patient access. In hearing care, Beijing medical teams have completed online hearing screening for over 1,000 older adults in Xinjiang and established practical bases in five hospitals, demonstrating the potential of mobile diagnostic vehicles equipped with vision and hearing screening devices and AI-based platforms that integrate screening, remote hearing aid fitting, and follow-up. Public health education could include community workshops and the distribution of illustrated materials in minority languages to improve accessibility. In southern provinces including Anhui and Chongqing, addressing uneven healthcare distribution and medical talent outflow requires strengthening primary healthcare investments, expanding satellite clinics linked to tertiary hospitals, and implementing school and workplace based sensory health screening programs. Moreover, the government should ensure adequate funding for assistive devices, such as subsidized hearing aids and magnifying devices, in provinces with high DSI prevalence to enhance residents’ quality of life.

SVI prevalence: stable influencing factors

With global population aging, the risk of VI will further increase [21]. A study by Hanyuan Ye et al. found that the prevalence of VI among middle-aged and elderly people in China increases with age, and chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, lung disease, stroke, and heart disease are also influencing factors of VI, with the prevalence of VI in diabetics doubling [22]. Our findings are consistent with Hanyuan Ye’s report that diabetes can significantly promote the prevalence of SVI. Diabetes commonly occurs in middle-aged and elderly people and can lead to various eye diseases, including diabetic retinopathy [23], cataracts [24], glaucoma [25], macular edema [26], and retinal vein occlusion [27], making it logical that diabetic patients are more prone to VI. Our investigation also found that digestive system diseases and arthritis are stable influencing factors for SVI prevalence. Digestive system diseases often lead to malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies (such as vitamins A, C, E, zinc, and protein) [28], further causing night blindness, dry eyes [29], increasing the risk of VI. Arthritis is the most common immune disorder, often associated with dry eye syndrome [15], and can cause eye inflammations such as iritis and uveitis [30]. Moreover, arthritis treatment often requires glucocorticoids [31], and long-term use may affect vision [32], increasing the risk of VI. This study also found that gender affects the prevalence of VI, with higher prevalence among women than men, which is consistent with global survey findings. Therefore, women’s visual health is a key area of focus in global public health [33].

SVI prevalence: regional distribution and spatial analysis

Our study identified a significant decrease in SVI prevalence in Shanghai and Tianjin, likely attributable to robust healthcare infrastructure and higher education levels in these developed cities, contributing to reduced SVI risk [22]. Conversely, Qinghai consistently had the highest national SVI prevalence, with Xinjiang also experiencing yearly increases. Both provinces, located in remote western China, face challenges such as lower socioeconomic development, intense ultraviolet exposure, limited healthcare accessibility, and inadequate public awareness about eye health, factors contributing to persistently high SVI prevalence rates [22]. Additionally, higher SVI prevalence in rural areas and among populations with lower education levels further intensified regional disparities [22].

Local Moran’s I analysis combined with local hotspot analysis revealed consistently high SVI prevalence in Qinghai and Xinjiang throughout the survey years. Local Moran’s I analysis also demonstrated persistent high-high clustering in Anhui, Shaanxi, Gansu, Sichuan, and Hubei. Space-time scan analysis identified a significant cluster in Hubei in 2011. These regions, primarily in southern and western China, experience higher solar radiation exposure, exacerbating eye diseases such as pterygium, cataracts, and macular degeneration, consequently increasing SVI prevalence [34].

These geographic patterns align closely with public health policy efforts. In high-prevalence provinces such as Qinghai and Xinjiang, targeted strategies from the 12th Five-Year Plan (2012–2015) and 13th Five-Year Plan for Eye Health (2016–2020) including strengthening eye disease prevention, expanding health education, and improving ophthalmology service coverage are critical for reducing prevalence. The national program increased the number of ophthalmologists to 47,000 and expanded cataract surgery to county-level hospitals, reaching one million surgeries annually, contributing to the nationwide decline in SVI prevalence from 2013 to 2018. As a result, China’s blindness prevalence is now below the global average, and in 2018 its provincial SVI rates were significantly lower than Japan’s 11.4% [35], underscoring the pivotal role of government-led public health interventions.

SHI prevalence: stable influencing factors

SHI is a critical degenerative condition affecting the elderly. According to global statistics, approximately 5% of the population experiences hearing impairment (HI), with a higher prevalence in males than females [36], consistent with our study’s findings. Regression analysis results indicate that gender and age are stable risk factors for SHI prevalence across the four surveys. Therefore, special attention should be paid to SHI among older men, and targeted prevention and intervention measures should be strengthened.

SHI prevalence: regional distribution and spatial analysis

Notably, this study found significant increases in SHI prevalence in Qinghai and Xinjiang, with poor sanitary conditions and underdeveloped economies being potential contributing factors [22]. In low- and middle-income areas, limited access to hearing loss prevention programs, outdated technology, and fewer medical and rehabilitation interventions significantly increase the likelihood of HI, with less than 3% of patients having access to hearing aids [37]. Hotspot and Coldspot Analysis consistently identifies Xinjiang as a hotspot area. In the Local Moran’s I analysis, we found that Anhui has consistently exhibited high-high clustering, with significantly higher DSI prevalence rates compared to other provinces. This highlights the urgent need for focused attention on HI issues in the Anhui region. In the spatio-temporal scan results, we found that SHI cases clustered around Chongqing in 2018. During that year, Chongqing experienced rapid development, and the accompanying increase in noise risk may be the primary factor contributing to this SHI clustering [10]. Furthermore, SHI prevalence is higher in southern cities compared to northern cities, which may be associated with geographical environmental factors and variations in healthcare quality. These findings underscore the necessity for targeted public health interventions and policies to address SHI, particularly in underdeveloped and economically disadvantaged regions. Enhancing access to healthcare services, implementing routine hearing screenings, and improving public awareness about hearing health are essential strategies. Additionally, investing in healthcare infrastructure and resources in these areas will help mitigate the risks and improve the quality of life for individuals with hearing impairments. Policymakers should prioritize these measures to reduce health disparities and address the growing burden of SHI among the elderly.

Strengths and limitations

CHARLS is a large-scale national study that has received widespread recognition. This study is the first nationwide investigation to use both cross-sectional and 8-year longitudinal research to observe the temporal changes and spatial distribution of SI prevalence among middle-aged and elderly people in China, and to validate the factors influencing SI prevalence over time. Our findings not only provide evidence on SI prevalence among the elderly in China but also serve as a reference for future research in other countries, particularly developing countries. Lastly, this study examined the spatiotemporal variations in the prevalence of different types of SI, providing a basis for comparison. Of course, our study has certain limitations. Since all variable results were obtained through questionnaires, the evidence is less robust compared to objective examination indicators, and may be subject to recall bias and cognitive bias. In addition, the CHARLS dataset does not differentiate between congenital and acquired sensory impairments, which may lead to misclassification in interpreting DSI. For example, a congenital impairment in one sense combined with an acquired impairment in another may not be fully preventable through public health interventions. Moreover, interactions between impairments could further complicate causal inference. Secondly, changes in the same population over the 8-year survey period were not specifically examined in this study; instead, statistical methods were used to eliminate confounding effects. Additionally, spatiotemporal scan analyses may also be affected by regional differences in data reporting, healthcare accessibility, or policy implementation. Moving forward, more systematic studies with larger sample sizes are needed—both cross-sectional prevalence studies and longitudinal incidence studies, as well as investigations into how geographical and environmental factors affect SI prevalence in specific regions.

Conclusion

Based on CHARLS data from 2011 to 2018, this study systematically analyzed the spatiotemporal distribution of SI individuals aged 45 years and older in China. We found a declining trend in SVI, while SHI and DSI increased in several provinces, with higher clustering in western and central-western regions and lower prevalence in developed eastern provinces such as Shanghai. Regression analyses identified age, low education, and rural residence as stable risk factors for DSI; gender, diabetes, digestive system diseases, and arthritis were associated with SVI; age and gender were significant for SHI. These findings highlight that progress has been achieved in visual health prevention, but hearing health management remains inadequate and regionally uneven. Strengthening early screening, telemedicine, chronic disease management, and access to assistive devices, particularly in high-burden regions, is essential to reduce disparities and improve health and quality of life in China’s aging population.
