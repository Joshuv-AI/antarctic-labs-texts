Assessment of airborne and surface microbes on leather cultural relics in museums of arid regions represented by xinjiang, China
Authors: Yuanyuan Wang, Yu Wang, Lilong Hou, Liwen Zhong, Hailiang Yang, Xiaojing Kang, Yang Zhou, Jiao Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the airborne microbial contamination in three museums located in the dry region of Xinjiang region, China—Bayingolin, Hami, and Turpan. Airborne microbial concentrations in these museums were found to be relatively low, ranging from 7.5 to 38.3 CFU/m³, which is advantageous for the preservation of cultural relics, especially in comparison to humid regions where higher microbial concentrations have been reported. The microbial communities were dominated by bacteria, with Firmicutes being the most abundant phylum, followed by Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes. Notably, Pseudomonas sp., Bacillus sp., and Staphylococcus hominis were identified as potential threats to the degradation of leather cultural relics. Additionally, Mycobacterium sp., Pantoea sp., and Priestia aryabhattai were first identified in the context of cultural heritage conservation. Metagenomic sequencing revealed a significant presence of salt-tolerant, spore-forming bacteria, which are characteristic of dry environments. Antibacterial tests showed that 0.5% K100 exhibited the best antimicrobial effect. This study provides valuable insights into the microbial ecology of museums in rid climates and suggests the need for targeted preservation strategies to mitigate microbial-induced biodeterioration, particularly through the use of antimicrobial agents and environmental management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19013-7.Keywords: Cultural relics preservation, Leather artifacts, Microbial ecology, Metagenomic sequencing, Antimicrobial effectSubject terms: Environmental biotechnology, Biological techniques, Microbiology, Microbial communities, Environmental microbiology

Introduction

Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, located in the arid region of northwest China, is renowned for its dry climate, low precipitation, and low humidity. Benefiting from its unique geographical location, natural environment, and historical context, Xinjiang has preserved China’s richest collection of leather cultural relics1. As a key hub of the ancient Silk Road, Xinjiang has long served as a crossroads for cultural exchange and trade between East and West2. Leather products were widely manufactured, utilized, and circulated as significant commodities in this region. These leather cultural relics in Xinjiang were not only utilized for crafting clothing, footwear, and containers but also served as durable and portable mediums for recording documents, transmitting cultural knowledge, and fulfilling religious purposes3.

Additionally, the dry climate and low humidity of Xinjiang have provided natural advantages for the long-term preservation of leather cultural relics, enabling them to remain relatively intact for millennia4. Moreover, the region’s abundant livestock resources and advanced leather processing techniques have laid a solid foundation for the production and dissemination of leather cultural relics. These cultural relics not only serve as tangible evidence of historical and cultural developments but also offer invaluable material for studying cultural interactions and technological exchanges along the Silk Road5.

Leather cultural relics are a typical type of bio-material primarily composed of collagen, fats, and other organic substances6. These characteristics make them particularly susceptible to environmental factors during preservation. Collagen and fats, as natural macromolecules, are easily identified and utilized as nutrient sources by microorganisms under certain conditions, leading to a series of degradation processes7. Microbial degradation represents one of the most complex and challenging threats to the preservation of leather cultural relics8. Such damage is often accompanied by structural deterioration and chemical changes9. For example, microorganisms secrete enzymes that degrade collagen, causing fiber rupture and material loosening, which compromises the mechanical integrity of the cultural relics. Furthermore, microbial metabolism produces acidic or alkaline by-products that accelerate aging and deterioration. The growth and metabolic activity of microorganisms can also lead to the formation of stains or deposits on artifact surfaces, altering their appearance and potentially obscuring their original cultural information10. In the dry air environments of museums, certain xerophilic and halophilic microorganisms can persist and remain metabolically active in localized microenvironments. These microorganisms possess robust metabolic capabilities and secrete enzymes capable of gradually degrading leather materials even in low-humidity conditions11.Therefore, microbial threats to leather cultural relics remain significant, even in the dry environments of museums in Xinjiang region.

The eight main sources of airborne microorganisms in buildings include ambient environment, resuspended dust, mold, humans, pets, plants, duct systems and heating ventilation and air conditioning systems (HVAC)12. Furthermore, research findings indicate that relative humidity exerts a more significant influence on the quantity of airborne microorganisms compared to temperature. Specifically, fungi generally exhibit more favorable growth in environments where the relative humidity ranges from 34.1 to 48.4%, as opposed to bacteria13,14.

The conservation of leather cultural relics in Xinjiang museums is a task of profound significance, as it is critical to the preservation of cultural heritage and the transmission of historical information. This study focuses on the museums of Bazhou (Bayingolin Mongolian Autonomous Prefecture), Hami, and Turpan, systematically analyzing the microbial communities present in dry air environments and their effects on leather cultural relics. It investigates the mechanisms of microbial deterioration under such conditions to provide a scientific basis for museum environmental management and artifact conservation strategies. By elucidating the interactions between microorganisms and artifact materials in dry environments, this research aims to offer insights applicable to the conservation of other types of cultural heritage in similar contexts. Furthermore, this study underscores the importance of protecting the unique cultural heritage of Xinjiang and contributes to the advancement of innovative conservation technologies.

Materials and methods

Sampling site

On August 15, 2023, this study involved a visit to the Turpan Museum to collect air samples in front of the display case exhibiting leather artifacts, as well as air sampling. The leather artifacts displayed in the museum had undergone tanning processes; particularly noteworthy were the leather saddles, filled with deer hair, which were both soft and durable, as well as intricately crafted leather coats and trousers. The primary focus was on leather artifacts excavated from the Yanghai Cemetery in Shanshan County, dating back to the Western Zhou period, and the Shengjindian Cemetery from the Han dynasty. The temperature inside the museum on the sampling day was measured at 24.5 °C, with a relative humidity of 39.2%, indicative of a consistently low-humidity environment.

On August 16, a visit to the Hami Museum exhibition hall and storage facilities was conducted to collect air samples in front of the display case exhibiting leather artifacts. This study primarily focused on the Wubao and Aisikexia’er burial sites from the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, both of which have yielded a significant number of leather artifacts, with the latter being particularly significant. Tomb M1 of the Aisikexiaer Cemetery is hypothesized to be the burial site of a leather craftsman, as it contained numerous leather scraps, tools for leatherworking, and adhesive material used for leather binding, solidified within a small stone mortar.

On August 17, research on desiccated leather artifacts from Bazhou Museum(Museum of Bayinguoleng Mongol Autonomous Prefecture). The museum houses a diverse collection of nationally significant artifacts, including bronzes, ceramics, textiles, mummies, gold and silver items, jade objects, and murals. Particular attention was given to leather artifacts from the Xiaohe Cemetery, a key prehistoric site in Xinjiang, is notable for its early date, large scale, and rich cultural significance, providing crucial insights into the region’s archaeological framework.

Sampling protocol

For air sampling, we utilized the Zr − 2050 air sampler (manufactured by Junray, China). This is an effective single - stage porous impact sampler operating on the Anderson impact principle. With an impact velocity of 10.8 m/s, it can capture particles larger than 1 μm in the air. The sampler draws air through a porous sampling head and deposits it onto a 90 - mm - diameter petri plate, where airborne microorganisms are trapped on the culture medium.

The LB (Luria - Bertani) medium for culturing airborne bacteria consisted of 10.0 g of tryptone, 5.0 g of yeast extract, 10.0 g of NaCl, 20.0 g of agar, and 1 L of distilled water. The pH of the LB medium was adjusted to 7.2 using 5 mol/L NaOH, and then it was autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min. The PDA (Potato Dextrose Agar) medium for airborne fungi was made up of 200.0 g of potato, 20.0 g of glucose, 20.0 g of agar, and 1 L of distilled water. The PDA medium was autoclaved at 115 °C for 20 min. The nutrient media used in this research were prepared in the laboratory following the above - mentioned formulas.

We selected 2, 3, and 2 sampling sites for Bazhou, Hami, and Turban museums, respectively. The specific sampling locations are shown in the attached material images, and each sampling site is sampled once. Each time, one LB and one PDA petri dish were exposed. The air flow rate was set at 100 L/min, and the exposure time was 2 min. Subsequently, the media were transported back to the laboratory. The LB medium was incubated at 37 °C for 2 days, while the PDA medium was incubated at 28 °C for 3 days. Based on characteristics such as colony color, size, shape, edge, luster, texture, and transparency, different strains were isolated in pure culture.

Molecular identification of the purified culturable microorganisms was carried out by sequencing 16 S rRNA gene for bacteria and ITS for fungi. Finally, the concentration of airborne microorganisms was determined as colony - forming units per cubic meter (CFU/m³), and a distribution map was created.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

The DNA of airborne bacteria and fungi was extracted via the hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. For the amplification of bacterial 16 S rRNA genes, the 341 F/907R primers were employed. The 341 F primer has the sequence 5′ - CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG − 3′, and the 907R primer has the sequence 5′ - CCCCGTCAATTCATTTGAGTTT − 3′. To amplify the fungal ITS regions, the ITS1/ITS4 primers were utilized. The ITS1 primer sequence is 5′ - TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG − 3′, and the ITS4 primer sequence is 5′ - CCTCCGCTTA TTGATATGC − 3′.

The total volume of the bacterial PCR mixture was 25 µl. It contained 3 µl of genomic DNA, 2.5 µl of 10× reaction buffer, 2 µl of 2.5 mM deoxy - ribonucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) mix, 1 µl of 10 μm forward primer, 1 µl of 10 μm reverse primer, 0.5 µl of 5U/µL TransTaq - T DNA polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), and 15 µl of ddH2O. The fungal PCR mixture had a total volume of 50 µl. It consisted of 2 µl of genomic DNA, 5 µl of 10× reaction buffer, 4 µl of 2.5 mM dNTP mix, 2 µl of 10 μm forward primer, 2 µl of 10 μm reverse primer, 0.5 µl of 5U/µL TransTaq - T DNA polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), with ddH₂O added to reach the final 50 - µl volume.

The PCR products were detected through electrophoresis in 1% agarose gels. The PCR products were then sequenced by GENEWIZ (GENEWIZ, Beijing, China). The obtained sequences were analyzed using the BLAST program provided by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Each isolate was compared with known taxa in the GenBank database. In the Nucleotide Blast, the blastn suite was used. The algorithm parameters included general parameters: Max target sequences was set to 100, for Short queries, “Automatically adjust parameters for short input sequences” was selected, the Expect threshold was 0.05, the Word size was 28, and Max matches in a query range was 0. All the obtained sequences were deposited in the NCBI GenBank under the accession numbers PV650668-PV650686 for bacteria and the accession numbers PV652786-PV652821 for fungi.

Metagenomic sequencing analysis technology

This study involved the collection and analysis of surface microbial samples from leather artifacts unearthed in the Xinjiang region, primarily focusing on woolen fabrics and leather goods currently preserved at the Cultural Relics and Archaeology Institute of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. These artifact samples originate from the Eski Kyzyl South Cemetery in Wubao, Hami, Xinjiang. In the latter half of 2009, some of the tombs were looted, prompting a rescue excavation by the Cultural Relics and Archaeology Institute of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region in November 2010. The excavation revealed a significant number of pottery items, as well as textiles, including woolen fabrics and leather goods, found on the deceased.

For leather fragments exhibiting visible microbial colonization, we carefully collected 50–100 mg samples using a sterile surgical scalpel, ensuring both fungal mycelia and adjacent leather material were included. Each sample was immediately transferred to a sterile 2 mL centrifuge tube and sealed with parafilm for transport to the laboratory.

Upon arrival, samples were processed as follows: First, they were vortexed for 2 min in 1 mL of PBS-Tween 20 (0.05%) to dislodge surface-adhered particles. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was subjected to mechanical lysis using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications. Cell disruption was achieved through bead-beating (FastPrep-24, 6 m s-1 for 60 s) with 0.1 mm glass beads. DNA was subsequently purified through the kit’s silica-column system and eluted in 50 µL of pre-warmed (70 °C) elution buffer. Negative extraction controls, consisting of empty tubes processed identically to samples, were included in each batch to monitor potential contamination.

DNA was quantified (Qubit dsDNA HS), inspected on 1% agarose, and accepted if A260/280 ≥ 1.8 and A260/230 ≥ 1.5 (NanoDrop). 50 ng DNA was sheared to 350 bp (Covaris M220) and processed with the NEB Next Ultra II kit, including UMI adapters. Libraries were size-selected (300–450 bp), qPCR-quantified, pooled, and sequenced PE150 on Illumina NovaSeq (~ 6 Gb per sample).Adapter/quality trimming (fastp), host-read removal (Bowtie2), and chimera-checked denoising (DADA2 in QIIME2) generated ASVs; OTU clustering (97%) was performed with vsearch. Taxonomy: SILVA 138.1 (16 S) and UNITE v8.0 (ITS). Functions were predicted with PICRUSt2 (KEGG)15,16 and annotated with eggNOG-mapper17 v2.1.10.Raw reads are deposited in NCBI SRA under BioProject PRJNA1047174.

Optical microscope observation

The morphology of the airborne fungi was observed via the small - chamber culture method under a Light microscope. Employing aseptic techniques, 1× 1 cm small pieces of solid PDA medium were cut with a sterile dissecting knife. These pieces were then placed on a slide within a sterilized Petri dish. The dominant strains were inoculated around the solid medium slice using an inoculating loop, and a coverslip was gently placed on top. Next, 1 ml of sterile glycerol was carefully dropped onto the filter paper inside the sterilized Petri dish, and the dish was covered with its Lid. The samples were incubated at 28 °C for three days. Following incubation, the slides were taken out and observed under a high - magnification microscope.

An optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E200, Japan) was utilized to observe the mycelia and spore morphology at a magnification of 400×. Each micrography features a 20 μm scale bar.

Determination of the collagen fiber degradation capacity of dominant airborne microorganisms

By cultivating the dominant microorganisms that potentially cause degradation in a culture medium where bone gelatin serves as the sole nitrogen source, we can assess the degradation risk to shadow puppet cultural relics18.

This culture medium was prepared with 20 g of bone gelatin, 5 g of glucose, 0.1 g of NaCl, 0.2 g of KH2PO4•7H2O, 20 g of agar, and 1 L of distilled water. The pH was adjusted to the range of 7.2–7.5, and then the medium was sterilized under high pressure at 121 °C for 20 min.

Fungi cultured in this medium were incubated at 28 °C, while bacteria were incubated at 37 °C. After three days, the growth of the microorganisms was observed. Microbial strains that grew and multiplied successfully demonstrated collagen - degrading activity. Such activity might hasten the degradation of shadow puppet cultural relics.

Inhibition assays with dominant airborne microorganisms

In recent years, a growing number of novel antimicrobial agents have been applied in the microbial management of cultural heritage conservation, demonstrating significant effectiveness. Isothiazolinones are synthetic biocides known for their strong antimicrobial activity against a wide range of bacteria and fungi. These compounds are commonly employed in the treatment of materials susceptible to biodeterioration, including paper, textiles, and wood19. Carvacrol is a naturally occurring monoterpene phenol found in plants such as oregano, has emerged as a promising biocide due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties. It has been reported to effectively inhibit the growth of fungi, bacteria, and other microorganisms that cause biodeterioration of cultural materials20; As an essential oil, Geraniol is relatively safe for both humans and the environment, which makes it an appealing option for the conservation of cultural heritage materials that require minimal chemical intervention21. Discussion

This study shows that the airborne microbial concentrations in the museums of Bazhou, Hami, and Turpan were 16.7, 7.5, and 38.3 CFU/m³, respectively. The temperature in these three museums were 25.5 °C, and the relative humidity was 39.2%. In comparison to previous investigations of airborne microbial contamination in humid museum exhibition halls fungal samples were collected from an exhibition hall of Jingzhou Museum, and the results showed fungal concentrations ranging from 17 to 74 CFU/m³22. The authors suggested that the temperature and relative humidity in June and July promote fungal growth. Tang Huan et al. monitored airborne microorganisms in the exhibition halls of Chongqing Three Gorges Museum over a two-year period, with microbial concentrations fluctuating between 78 and 616 CFU/m³23. The results indicated that the drier environment of the museum led to significantly lower microbial levels, which is favorable for artifact preservation. Gutarowska et al. surveyed the airborne microbial contamination in six different museums and archives in Poland, finding concentrations ranging from 1.5 × 10² to 7.0 × 10³ CFU/m³24. The most commonly identified fungi included Aspergillus, Penicillium, Cladosporium, and Chaetomium species, while common bacteria included Staphylococcus aureus. Scarlat et al. investigated microbial contamination in museum workplaces and found concentrations ranging from 200 to 1.4 × 10⁴ CFU/m³25.

Pseudomonas species were found in both Bazhou Museum and Hami Museum, which warrants attention26. It can grow in moist, organic-rich environments and degrade the material of cultural relics by producing enzymes, acidic metabolic products, and other metabolites27. Current research has shown that these bacteria cause varying degrees of damage to cultural heritage, particularly to organic and inorganic materials such as stone, wood, and bone28,29. Bacillus species were also detected in the metagenomic sequencing of leather artifacts, posing significant challenges to heritage conservation due to their strong resistance and biodegradation capabilities30. These bacteria are commonly found on the surfaces of organic material artifacts and cause both internal and surface degradation. S. hominis is another common pathogenic species in heritage conservation, frequently found on human skin and mucous membranes31. In relatively humid environments, it may corrode organic material artifacts, and it has also been identified in previous heritage surveys. In terms of fungi, Aspergillus species were the most dominant in this investigation. Aspergillus is a common fungal genus, widely distributed in natural environments, especially in humid and organic-rich conditions32. Due to its ubiquitous presence in air, water, and soil, Aspergillus is frequently found in museums, archives, historical buildings, and other cultural heritage sites. Certain Aspergillus species, particularly A. flavus, A. niger, and A. terreus, have strong biodegradation capabilities and can cause significant damage to cultural heritage33.

The Mycobacterium sp. and Pantoea sp. isolated from Bazhou Museum, as well as P. aryabhattai isolated from Turpan Museum, to the best of our knowledge, these species have not been previously reported in the field of cultural heritage conservation, thus presenting novel findings in this context. Mycobacterium species are well-known for their environmental persistence, particularly in harsh conditions. Although typically associated with human and animal pathogens, some non-pathogenic Mycobacterium strains are capable of surviving in diverse environmental niches, including water, soil34. Their presence in museums could be attributed to contamination from external sources such as outdoor air, museum staff, or visitors, especially those who may carry these microorganisms on their clothing or personal items. Pantoea sp., a genus belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae, is commonly found in plant-associated environments, but has also been isolated from human and animal sources35. Although there are no direct reports of its involvement in the degradation of cultural materials, its robust metabolic capabilities and potential for biofilm formation could imply a role in the degradation of organic materials or the formation of biofilms on artifacts. P. aryabhattai, a bacterium typically found in soil, could be introduced into museums through several routes. As a common soil bacterium, it is likely to be carried into the museum by external sources, such as dust or soil particles on shoes, clothing, or artifacts that have been transported from outdoor environments. If the museum houses collections with organic materials such as wood, leather, or bone, these could serve as potential substrates for the bacteria, where it might colonize and potentially interact with these materials.

The metagenomic sequencing results from the artifact samples in this study provide valuable insights into the microbial communities inhabiting these cultural relics. The results show that the microbial community is predominantly bacterial, with Firmicutes being the most abundant phylum, followed by Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes. These findings align with previous studies that have reported a similar dominance of Firmicutes in various museum and artifact samples, suggesting that this phylum plays a significant role in the microbial ecology of cultural heritage materials36. Notably, the genus Virgibacillus was identified as the most abundant genus, with Lentibacillus, Oceanobacillus, and Bacillus also present in substantial amounts, highlighting the prominence of salt-tolerant and spore-forming bacteria, which are commonly found in microbial communities associated with dry and museum environments37. In terms of functional annotation, the KEGG database results reveal that genes related to metabolism are the most abundant, followed by genes involved in environmental information processing and genetic information processing. This suggests that microbial communities in the artifacts are actively engaged in metabolic processes, which could be involved in the degradation of organic materials in the artifacts. The presence of metabolic pathways related to energy production and conversion is also consistent with the idea that these microbial communities may be utilizing organic compounds from the artifact materials as their primary carbon and energy sources. Furthermore, the eggNOG database annotation results highlight the relative abundance of genes associated with replication, recombination, and repair, as well as amino acid transport and metabolism. These findings suggest that the microbial communities in the artifacts are actively replicating and repairing cellular damage, which is indicative of an active and thriving community of microorganisms. The presence of genes related to amino acid metabolism may also suggest that the microbial communities are utilizing proteins and peptides in the artifacts as nutrient sources, further supporting the notion that microbial activity could be contributing to the biodeterioration of cultural heritage materials38. Therefore, understanding the microbial functional pathways and the environmental conditions that support their growth and activity is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate microbial-induced biodeterioration.

The high relative abundance of polysaccharide metabolism pathways (CAZy database, Fig. 4E) reflects the metabolic specialization of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria dominating the leather artifacts. These taxa possess robust enzymatic machinery, including glycoside hydrolases (GHs) and carbohydrate esterases (CEs), which primarily target polysaccharide components in leather artifacts39their activity could indirectly facilitate biodeterioration by breaking down protective polysaccharide matrices (exposing collagen fibers), generating microbial growth substrates, and altering leather’s physicochemical properties. Additionally, the detection of antibiotic resistance genes (CARD database, Fig. 4F) underscores a significant challenge in conservation efforts. Future strategies should integrate metagenomic surveillance with targeted antimicrobial approaches to mitigate these biodeterioration risks.

The experimental results of this study indicate that all isolated strains can grow on a collagen-based medium with bone gelatin as the sole nitrogen source, which confirms that they are capable of utilizing collagen for their growth and metabolism. Under certain conditions, these airborne microorganisms may settle on the surfaces of leather cultural relics in museums, leading to corrosion and degradation of leather materials. Finally, the antibacterial test results showed that 0.5% K100 exhibited the best antimicrobial effect. However, further experiments are needed to test its efficacy on actual cultural relics.

In subsequent experiments, we will focus on the application of antimicrobial agents on cultural relics, particularly leather artifacts, to assess the long-term effects of K100 in real-world conservation conditions. We will also investigate the optimal concentration of K100 for effectively inhibiting microbial growth without compromising the integrity of the cultural relics. Additionally, more comprehensive studies will be conducted to explore the interactions between these microorganisms and various materials commonly found in museum collections, including textiles, wood, and paper, to provide more targeted recommendations for heritage conservation. Furthermore, it would be important to evaluate the environmental factors, such as temperature and humidity, that influence microbial colonization and degradation, to develop effective preservation strategies tailored to specific museum environments.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the microbial concentrations in the dry environments of museums in Xinjiang are significantly lower than those found in more humid regions, providing a more favorable condition for the preservation of cultural relics. The presence of potentially harmful bacteria and fungi, including newly identified species such as Mycobacterium sp., Pantoea sp., and P. aryabhattai, underscores the need for continuous monitoring of microbial contamination in museum environments. The study further suggests that microbial activity, particularly by Pseudomonas sp., Bacillus sp., and Aspergillus sp., could contribute to the degradation of organic materials such as leather, textiles, and wood. Antibacterial testing revealed that 0.5% K100 showed promising results in inhibiting microbial growth, although further research is needed to confirm its efficacy on actual cultural relics. Future studies should focus on the application of antimicrobial agents in museum settings, the optimization of environmental conditions (e.g., temperature, humidity, and airflow), and the development of comprehensive preservation strategies tailored to specific materials and environments. By enhancing our understanding of the microbial threats to cultural heritage, this research lays the groundwork for more effective conservation efforts in museums worldwide.
