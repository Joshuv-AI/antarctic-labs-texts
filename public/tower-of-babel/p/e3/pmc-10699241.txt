Evaluation of Two-Year Follow-Up of Patients with BI-RADS 3 Breast Ultrasound Lesions in a Single Private Ultrasound Study
Authors: Atousa Adibi, Zahra Asadollahi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:This study assessed the frequency and progression rate of BI-RADS 4 or BI-RADS 2 during a two-year follow-up of patients with BI-RADS 3 on ultrasound screening.Materials and Methods:This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted on patients with BI-RADS 3 lesions who entered to study from 2013 to 2018 and underwent an ultrasound on the months of 6th, 12th, and 24th after the first ultrasound. After assessment of the size, if the size of the lesion in the two dimensions were stable or decreased, BI-RADS 2 would be considered. If BI-RADS 4 were detected, the lesion would undergo a biopsy.Results:The study included six hundred-four patients with BI-RADS 3 lesions at baseline. Participants’ mean ± SD (median, min-max) age was 413 ± 9.7 (40, 19-75). By month 24, BI-RADS 3 changed to BI-RADS 2 in 585 (96.6%) and BI-RADS 4 in 19 (3.1%) cases.Considering the size of breast lesions, the breast sensitivity and specificity for predicting size progression and changing to BI-RADS 4 were as follows: For long axis diameter, 12 mm at baseline with 72.22% sensitivity and specificity of 71.53% and 16 mm in 6 months follow-up with sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 83.78%.For short axis diameter, 6 mm at baseline with a sensitivity of 83.33% and specificity of 62.89% and 8 mm in 6 months follow-up with a sensitivity of 66.67% and specificity of 83.48%. Conclusion:The breast lesion size was the most crucial factor in altering the BI-RADS 3 to 4.

INTRODUCTION

Sonography is now a well-established tool in breast imaging thanks to recent advancements in ultrasound technology, allowing diagnosis of breast masses occult on mammography, notably in women under 50.[1] The American College of Radiology (ACR) created a breast imaging reporting and data system (BI-RADS) lexicon for breast sonography to standardize the description of sonographic breast lesions, given the increased use of ultrasound.[2] BI-RADS system allowed the monitoring of findings and quality improvement in breast imaging.[3]

Although BI-RADS had a prominent role in categorizing breast imaging findings for physicians and patients, diagnostic confusion remained regarding the probably benign (BI-RADS 3) category[234] BI-RADS 3 characteristics included a solid mass with an oval shape, homogenous echotexture parallel orientation, circumscribed margin, and no suspicious malignant characteristics.[56]

The malignancy rate of probably benign breast lesions in the US varies from 0% to 0.80%.[7] So the ACR management proposed a short-interval imaging follow-up, usually at 6, 12, and 24 months as a convenient alternative to biopsy.[7]

Putting patients in the category of BI-RADS 3 may help the radiologist feel more secure by recording the stability of the lesion, but being in this group leads to anxiety for patients who are still under the intensive care of a suspected lesion.[8]

Supplemental breast ultrasound imaging may boost cancer detection rates in women with dense breast tissue by 2.3–4.6 per 1000.[9] It has been shown that malignant and benign masses may be distinguished using BI-RADS ultrasound descriptors with high positive and negative predictive values, respectively.[2]

Given that ultrasound was the critical adjunct to mammography in breast cancer diagnosis. Some studies believed that ultrasound screening in women with dense breast tissue might have benefits due to the increase in false-negative cases in mammography.[10] No comprehensive study was conducted regarding the two-year follow-up of patients with the probably benign breast lesion on screening ultrasound in our country. This study assessed the frequency of significant changes in size during a two-year follow-up of patients with BI-RADS 3 on ultrasound screening who needs tissue diagnosis (BI-RADS 4).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample selection

This cross-sectional, retrospective study included consecutive patients referred to a single center for any reason, including screening or diagnostic ultrasound, who had solid breast lesions between 2013 and 2018. Conventional ultrasound findings were assessed using the BI-RADS™ classification system.[11] Exclusion criteria were patient dissatisfaction, incomplete fewer than 24 months of follow-up, and breast lesions with BI-RADS 4 and 5, which needed surgery.

Procedure

Each patient signed an informed consent form before the examination, and all ultrasounds were performed by one radiologist who has been an expert in breast ultrasound for 20 years under the standard protocol and the study approved by the Ethics Committee of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. The same radiologists performed the follow-up examinations.

All patients underwent sonography using ultrasound equipment (Voluson 730 expert, general electric) and a 5–12 MHz linear probe. The examination was done in the supine oblique position.

According to sonographic judgment, patients with BI-RADS 3 lesions entered the study, and then these patients underwent ultrasound at the 6, 12, and 24-month follow-up.

BI-RADS 3 lesions were defined as solid masses with an oval or softly lobulated form, limited boundary, and parallel orientation. In follow-up, the lesion was considered as BI-RADS 4 if it displayed one or more of the suspicious findings listed below: irregular shape, microlobulated, indistinct, angular, or spiculated margin, nonparallel orientation, complex echogenicity, duct extension or microcalcifications within the mass. The lesion was considered a BI-RADS 2 lesion if it was a benign-appearing cyst, an intramammary lymph node, or exhibited uniform or strong hyperechogenicity on follow-up US or if the size of the lesion remained stable for 24 months.[12]

The changes in lesion size were assessed as below:

D% = (d2-d1)*100/d1

If the lesion size in the two dimensions were stable or decreased, consider BI-RADS 2. If the lesion size in any of the dimensions were increased by more than 20%,[12] BI-RADS 4 would be considered. If BI-RADS 4 were detected, the lesion would undergo a biopsy. The reports of ultrasound follow-up and masses with the final decision of BI-RADS 2 and BI-RADS 4 were collected, and the results of the patient's biopsy were documented during the study.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and frequency (percentage). The normality of continuous variables has been evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q-Q plot. Categorical variables were compared between groups using the Chi-squared test. The within and between group changes in the short and long axis at months 6, month 12, and month 24 across BI-RADS type 2 and 4 were evaluated using repeated measure ANOVA. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the predictive ability of the short and long axis at months 6, month 12, and month 24 in differentiating BI-RADS types 2 and 4. The best cutoff values were detected during ROC analysis, with the highest value of sensitivity and specificity and area under the curve (AUC). P value <0.05 was assumed significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Ill., USA) STATA 11.2 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LP. StataCorp.).

According to Table 1, most lesions were hypoechoic masses without any vascularity. Moreover, most lesions had an oval shape and well-defined margins. Most lesions were on the right side, mostly in the superolateral quadrant.

In 6 months of follow-up: 66 (10.9%) of cases changed to BI-RADS 2 and 5 (0.8%) cases to BI-RADS 4. In month 12, most BI-RADS 3 breast lesions were changed to BI-RADS type 2 (239, 39.6%), and eight (1.3%) patients changed to BI-RADS type 4. Finally, in the last phase of the study (month 24), the majority of BI-RADS 3 breast lesions changed to BI-RADS type 2 (387, 64.1%), and five (0.8%) patients changed to BI-RADS type 4. In addition, after the biopsy was taken from patients with BI-RADS type 4 breast lesions, the results showed that most of them (13, 2.2%) were benign, and only one case (0.2%) showed suspicious pathology as atypical ductal hyperplasia.

There were no significant differences between BI-RADS type 2 and 4 breast lesions in shape, margin, side, location, and echogenicity (P > 0.05). Also, there were significant differences between the vascularity of BI-RADS type 2 and 4 in months 12 and month 24 (P = 0.00).

There was a significant difference between BI-RADS type 2 and 4 breast lesions in terms of the mammographic density group (fatty, scattered, heterogeneously dense, and extremely dense) (P = 0.005). Also, mammographic reports such as focal asymmetry, well-circumscribed mass, or normal studies showed significant differences between BI-RADS type 2 and 4 (P < 0.05) [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Although mammography is the best screening method for breast cancer, breast US is now well-established as a valuable imaging technique because of its high negative predictive value.[13] Various studies have shown sonographic appearance to be beneficial in distinguishing between malignant and benign solid breast tumors.[214] In combination with mammography, a final ACR BI-RADS breast US assessment in a clinical setting can indicate cancer risk.[15]

In this study, 19 (3.1%) lesions changed to BI-RADS 4 and 13 (2.2%) benign fibroadenomas after the biopsy. Still, just one lesion was suspicious atypical ductal hyperplasia (0.2%), which is 1/10 of indicated malignancy rate for BI-RADS 3 lesions (2%)[1617] which could be due to more sensitivity of the operator. In a similar study focusing on the difficulty of using BI-RADS 3 for breast ultrasound by radiologists, 14.6% of screening mammograms were determined to be BI-RADS 3. When the ultrasound examinations were reinterpreted, 19.3% of cases had a change in diagnosis. Of the 225 patients with BI-RADS change, 2123 were changed to BI-RADS 2, and 12 were upgraded to BI-RADS 4.[18]

Our findings showed no differences in shape, margin, side, location, or echogenicity between BI-RADS 2 and 4 breast lesions at the end of follow-up. Although descriptors showed significantly different features of mass margin, shape, orientation, lesion boundary, echo pattern, and posterior acoustic features between benign and malignant breast masses in a study on benign versus benign, malignant solid breast masses,[19] it's acceptable because most of our study's BI-RADS 4 lesions were benign.

In addition, because the relevance of vascularity in the characterization of breast masses is yet unknown, the BI-RADS ultrasonography lexicon does not include a description of vascular characteristics.[20] Interestingly, our study found significantly different vascularity between BI-RADS type 2 and 4 breast lesions during our follow-up period, so the vascular features of breast lesions could help to diagnose the higher possibility of changing to BI-RADS 4. The same study found that contrast-enhanced breast ultrasound may distinguish malignant and benign lesions. They declared that more than half of malignant lesions had rich vascularity. Enhancement degree also proved statistically accurate in differentiating between them (sensitivity: 89.6%; specificity: 48.6%). This technique, in particular, might make distinguishing between lesions in BI-RADS 3 and 4 easier, reducing the number of core-cut biopsies needed for benign lesions.[21]

It is critical to regularly follow-up the potentially benign lesions with ultrasound, especially during the first six months, because this allows for the diagnosis of fast-growing cancers.[16] Based on the results of the US examinations of patients with BI-RADS type 3 breast lesions in the baseline, a size higher than 12 mm on the long axis and a size higher than 6 mm on the short axis showed the most sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratio for diagnosing the tendency to altering BI-RADS 3 to 4. Also, in the second follow-up (month 6), a size higher than 16 mm on the long axis and higher than 8 mm on the short axis need further investigation. A study on the BI-RADS 3 on screening breast ultrasound depicted that an increase in diameter of a BI-RADS 3 mass of more than 20% in six months (44% in 12 months) should prompt a biopsy.[22]

It is important to consider breast lesion size at baseline and 6-month follow-up for biopsy condition because the patient's compliance rate could decline for the next follow-up. Therefore, if the patient cannot return for a follow-up examination for any reason or an imaging follow-up is not possible, an urgent biopsy should be conducted within these breast lesion sizes.

There were a few limitations to this research. First, the lesions with BI-RADS 3 were assessed, so we could have a few patients entering the study each month. Second, the study's inter-observer variability was not assessed.

CONCLUSION

Greater caution is warranted for a BI-RADS 3 assessment when, in the baseline, a size higher than 12 mm on the long axis and a size higher than 6 mm on the short axis is seen. Also, in the second follow-up (month 6), a size higher than 16 mm on the long axis and higher than 8 mm on the short axis need further investigation. In addition, the vascularity pattern might make distinguishing between lesions in BI-RADS 3 and 4 easier.
