Executive dysfunction and employment in epilepsy
Authors: Lisa E Hauger, Christoph Helmstaedter, Morten I Lossius, Kristin Å Alfstad, Ellen Molteberg, Halvor Torgersen, Annette H Skogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExecutive dysfunction, affective symptoms, and unemployment are prevalent in patients with epilepsy, yet the relation between these variables remains poorly understood. The present study examined: (1) The relationship between epilepsy‐related variables, affective symptoms, and executive functions (EFs); and (2) how these variables may be associated with participation in employment or education.MethodsRetrospective study including 559 patients admitted to the Norwegian National Centre for Epilepsy. EFs was assessed using EpiTrack, and affective symptoms were evaluated with the 7‐item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD‐7) and Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory for Epilepsy (NDDI‐E).ResultsImpaired EFs were observed in half of the patients, and were associated with a higher antiseizure medication (ASM) load, even after adjusting for variables related to disease severity. Approximately 36% of the patients reported symptoms indicating major depression and 19% reported symptoms indicating generalized anxiety. Half of the sample was unemployed, and these patients showed poorer EFs than those engaged in employment or education (F(3, 530) = 12.29, p < .001, ηp 2 = .07), with no group differences in seizure frequency, anxiety, or depressive symptoms.SignificanceIn patients at a tertiary epilepsy center, executive dysfunction is prevalent along with heightened symptoms of depression and anxiety. Executive dysfunction is associated with a higher ASM load as well as increased likelihood of unemployment. Our findings suggest that job retention may depend on more than the burden of epilepsy alone, with executive functioning playing a critical role. These findings emphasize the importance of monitoring EF during treatment adjustments and support minimizing total ASM load whenever possible to promote better functional outcomes.Keywords: DRE, employment, EpiTrack, executive function, mental health

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a prevalent neurological disorder that impacts multiple facets of an individual's life. Approximately one‐third of individuals with epilepsy do not achieve seizure freedom despite treatment trials of two well‐tolerated, appropriately selected, and correctly administered antiseizure medication (ASM), thereby meeting the criteria for drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE). 1 Seizures represent just one component of the complex and heterogeneous picture of DRE. In addition, ASM side effects, mental health issues, and cognitive deficits increase the burden of epilepsy and negatively affect quality of life, educational attainment, and employment. 2 , 3 , 4

Employment is closely linked to self‐esteem and personal identity, whereas unemployment can undermine autonomy, feelings of self‐worth, and quality of life. 3 Moreover, high unemployment and disability rates pose a significant socioeconomic burden. 5 A systematic review of 95 studies reported that the employment rate for people with epilepsy ranged from 14% to 89%, with a mean of 58%. 6 Furthermore, women with epilepsy have been shown to have lower employment rates and higher rates of psychiatric comorbidity compared with men. 7 Studies focusing on the causes of unemployment have identified seizure severity as the primary factor contributing to unemployment, 8 although some studies report that individuals with uncontrolled seizures had employment rates that were similar to those with controlled seizures. 6 This suggests that factors beyond seizure frequency may also play important roles in determining employability. It is known that epilepsy is a barrier for employment, often due to factors beyond the patient's control. 9 For instance, one survey found that future employers regard epilepsy as the least favorable medical disorder. 10 In addition, cognitive impairment and mental health issues are likely to impact everyday functioning, work status, and quality of life in people with epilepsy. 4 , 11

Executive functions (EFs) are among the cognitive domains commonly affected in patients with DRE. 12 EFs, including attentional control, working memory, inhibition, self‐regulation, and cognitive flexibility, are essential for effective planning, problem‐solving, and adaptation to everyday life. The etiology of cognitive dysfunction in patients with DRE is complex and multifactorial, including underlying neuropathology, the types and frequency of seizures, interictal epileptic activity, and psychosocial factors. 13 A recent systematic review highlights that poorer seizure control is linked to worse cognitive performance and higher expression of psychiatric symptoms. 14 Certain cognitive impairments associated with the underlying neuropathology tend to be relatively stable, such as general cognitive ability. In contrast, other cognitive functions such as EFs are more susceptible to external influences, including side effects of ASM, and may therefore fluctuate in relation to treatment. ASM treatment may influence cognitive function either negatively through side‐effects or positively through seizure reduction/freedom. It is well known that both ASM monotherapy and polytherapy may negatively affect cognitive functions, such as processing speed, language, memory, and EFs, with the risk of impairment being greater among patients receiving polytherapy. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

In addition to cognitive impairment, depression and anxiety are common comorbidities in people with epilepsy, 19 both associated with reduced quality of life. 4 A population‐based study reported a lifetime prevalence of 24.4% for mood disorders and 22.8% for anxiety disorders among individuals with epilepsy, in contrast to 13.2% and 11.2%, respectively, in individuals without epilepsy. 20 Similar to cognitive impairment, the etiology of psychiatric comorbidity in patients with DRE is multifactorial, and a bidirectional relationship between epilepsy and affective symptoms has been proposed. 21 However, the causal links between these factors are not well established, and research examining DRE, cognition, mental health, and employment is still limited.

Identification of comorbid mental health issues and cognitive impairment is crucial in the clinical management of patients with DRE. 22 Furthermore, it is essential to try to disentangle the different factors contributing to cognitive and affective symptoms, as this can have implications for diagnostic and treatment considerations and rehabilitation strategies. In clinical practice, conducting comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and mental health evaluations for all patients is not feasible, highlighting the need for sensitive screening tools. EpiTrack is considered a feasible tool for assessing attention and EFs in patients with DRE, 23 used for both screening and/or monitoring in relation to changes in ASM treatment. Among the different instruments available for screening for depression and anxiety in patients with epilepsy, the Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory for Epilepsy (NDDI‐E) and the 7‐item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD‐7) scale are often used in combination, and are considered feasible and recommended tools. 24 , 25

This retrospective cross‐sectional study of a large sample treated at a tertiary epilepsy center examined: (1) The relationship between epilepsy‐related variables (epilepsy type, etiology, duration of epilepsy, seizure frequency, and ASM load), affective symptoms, and EFs; and (2) explored how these variables may predict participation in employment or education.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Standard protocol approvals and registrations

This study included data from existing institutional data, the hospitals quality register, and patient's records, approved by the hospital data security authority and local patient's rights representative (22/02589).

Participants and procedures

The present study is retrospective and included data from 559 patients admitted to the National Centre for Epilepsy in Norway between 2017 and 2023. Our center is the only third‐line referral center for specialized diagnostics and treatments of epilepsy, complex epilepsy, and DRE in Norway.

Epilepsy‐related variables

Seizure frequency was categorized as follows: seizure‐free (no seizures during the past year), yearly (<1 per month), monthly (1–3 per month), weekly (1–5 per week), and daily (>6 per week). Epileptic seizures and epilepsies were classified according to the updated International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classifications. 20 , 21 The classification was conducted by the treating epileptologists and recorded in the patient's journal and the hospital's quality register. Etiologies were grouped as follows: (1) structural, (2) genetic, and (3) unknown.

Employment status

Participation in employment or education was grouped as follows: (1) full disability pension/unemployed/sickness‐leave, (2) part time, (3) full time, and (4) student. Eight patients were retired and seven had an unknown work status. In all the analyses relating to work status except for the regression analysis, the patients were grouped as follows: (1) Unemployed (including disability leave, sickness leave, or unemployment), (2) student, and (3) part‐time and (4) full‐time employment. Retired patients (n = 8) were excluded from the analyses including employment status as a variable, due to the small group size.

Executive function

EFs were assessed using the EpiTrack, which is a brief neuropsychological screening tool designed specifically for people with epilepsy. 26 Participants were assessed with EpiTrack by indication for change in ASM treatment and/or if they reported executive dysfunction. Not all patients evaluated at the center undergo screening with EpiTrack. Patients must have general functioning able to accomplish EpiTrack, which generally excludes patients with moderate to severe intellectual impairment. A total of 226 of the patients completed EpiTrack as a part of a more extensive neuropsychological examination, the majority as part of a preoperative neuropsychological evaluation. EpiTrack was administered by psychologists or trained nurses, and the administration takes ≈15 min. EpiTrack consists of six subtests requiring attention, cognitive tracking, psychomotor speed, and working memory: an interference test; the Trail Making Test (Parts A and B); a test of response inhibition; digit span backward; word fluency by writing; and a maze test. Scores for each subtest range from 1 to 7. Normative data include an age‐corrected total score and cutoffs yielding the following classifications: ≥39 = good performance; 32–38 = average/unimpaired performance; 29–31 = mildly impaired and ≤28 = significantly impaired. A detailed description of subtests, administration, and standardization can be found elsewhere. 26 Due to the small number of patients in our sample with “good performance” on the overall EpiTrack (n = 24), they were categorized as “average/unimpaired performance” in analyses beyond descriptives.

General cognitive ability

General cognitive ability was assessed with Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fourth Edition (WAIS‐IV) or Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI). In the present study we used the General Ability Index (GAI) as it is considered less sensitive to the influence of working memory and processing speed than the full‐scale IQ from WAIS‐IV. GAI scores were available for a subgroup of the patients (n = 226) who completed a more extensive neuropsychological examination. Comprehensive information on the subtests and standardization of the WAIS and WASI is available elsewhere. 27 , 28

Affective symptoms

Neurological Disorder Depression Inventory in Epilepsy (NDDI‐E) was designed to quickly screen for major depression (MD) in individuals with epilepsy. 29 It includes six items that focus on depressive symptoms, specifically chosen to avoid overlap with the side effects of ASM and cognitive impairments commonly seen in this population. Patients rate how often they have experienced each symptom over the past 2 weeks using a scale from 1 (never) to 4 (always or often), yielding a total score between 6 and 24. The NDDI‐E has been validated in multiple languages for use in patients with epilepsy and remains the most widely used screening tool for depression in this patient population. 30 However, the recommended cutoff values differ across language versions, ranging from >11 to ≥17. 30 Evidence from meta‐analyses suggests that a threshold of >13 optimally identifies major depression. In contrast, other studies propose a cutoff of ≥17 to balance sensitivity and specificity, while recommending a lower threshold of ≥14 when prioritizing sensitivity—albeit with an increased risk of false‐positive results. 31 The present study refers to total scores between 14 and 16 as indicating “possible major depression” and NDDI‐E ≥17 as “likely major depression,” in line with previous research on patients in a tertiary epilepsy referral center. 32 NDDI‐E data were available for 427 patients.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD‐7) scale has been developed to identify probable cases of generalized anxiety disorder and to assess symptom severity, 33 and is the recommended anxiety screening tool in patients with epilepsy. 34 Patients are asked if they were bothered by anxiety‐related problems over the past 2 weeks by answering seven items on a 4‐point scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). According to the original authors, the total score may be categorized into four severity groups: minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and serious (14–20), with a score of 10 or greater considered a reasonable cut point for identifying cases of GAD. 33 GAD‐7 data were available for 426 patients. Patients with and without available NDDI‐E and GAD‐7 data did not differ significantly in age, gender, epilepsy duration, epilepsy type, seizure frequency, or ASM load (all p's > .05).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS statistic 30. We used analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) to test for group differences in EFs as measured by EpiTrack, with the total age corrected EpiTrack score as dependent variable and group as the independent variable. ANCOVA was also used to test for group differences in symptoms of anxiety (GAD‐7 total score) and depression (NDDI‐E total score). To control for epilepsy‐related variables, we used seizure frequency and epilepsy duration as covariates. Following the ANCOVA, Bonferroni‐adjusted post hoc analyses were performed to control for multiple comparisons and to determine which groups differed significantly from each other. Chi‐square tests were used to examine group differences in categorical data. Pearson correlation was computed to assess the relationship between overall EpiTrack score, NDDIE‐score, GAD‐7 score, and GAI. A binary logistic regression was conducted to ascertain the effects of age, gender, epilepsy type (focal/generalized), ASM load, seizure frequency, epilepsy duration, EFs (non‐impaired/impaired EpiTrack performance), anxiety symptoms (GAD‐7 under/over clinical cutoff), and depressive symptoms (NDDIE under/over clinical cutoff) on the likelihood of participation in education/employment (0 = unemployed, 1 = employed/education). The rationale for categorizing GAD‐7, NDDI‐E, and EpiTrack scores is described in Sections 2.2.3, 2.2.5. Group differences were considered significant at alpha < .05. When appropriate, Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was used. Partial eta‐squared (ηp 2) was used to calculate effect sizes for the ANCOVA, with the following interpretation introduced by Cohen; .01 ≈ small, .06 ≈ medium, .14 ≈ large. 35 For all the statistical analyses, preliminary assumption testing was conducted, with no serious violations noted.

Affective symptoms

With regard to generalized anxiety symptoms (n = 426), 50.9% of participants reported minimal symptoms, 30.0% reported mild symptoms, 12.9% reported moderate symptoms, and 6.1% reported severe symptoms, resulting in 19% of the sample scoring above the clinical cutoff. Regarding depressive symptoms (n = 427), 16.6% had NDDI‐E scores between 14 and 16 indicating that major depression is possible; 19.4% of the patients had NDDI‐E scores ≥17 indicating that major depression is likely, resulting in 36.1% of the sample scoring above the clinical cutoff. Having controlled for epilepsy duration and seizure frequency, we found no statistically significant (corrected alpha level .05/3 = .017) difference in NDDI‐E scores between the different etiological groups (F(2,416) = 2.66, p = .07, ηp 2 = .01), or between epilepsy types (F(2,416) = .385, p = .681, ηp 2 = .00). Furthermore, a higher percentage of women (41.3%) scored above the clinical cutoff on the NDDI‐E, compared to men (29.2%); the group difference was significant χ 2 (1, n = 427) = 6.69, p = .001. In addition, there was also a statistically significant gender difference in overall GAD‐7 scores (F(1,424) = 9.16, p = .003, ηp 2 = .02), where women (M = 6.0, SD = 4.8) had higher scores than men (4.59, SD = 4.6), but the effect size was small. A higher percentage of women (21.1%) scored above clinical cutoff on the GAD‐7, compared to men (16.3%), but the group difference was not statistically significant χ 2 (1, n = 426) = 1.54, p = .214.

Employment status and related variables

Fifty percent of the study sample were unemployed. Table 3 presents the distribution of work status across age groups. Overall, about an equal percentage of women (52.1%) and men (48.1%) were unemployed. A slightly higher share of women (14.0%) worked part time compared to men (8.9%), whereas more men (21.5%) were employed full time than women (12.1%). The relation between seizure frequency and work status was not statistically significant: χ 2 (12, n = 540) = 16.3, p = .177. As expected due to age, students had a shorter duration of epilepsy (mean = 10.2, SD = 6.2) compared to the other groups; unemployed (mean = 21.3, SD = 14.4), part time (mean = 17.8, SD = 12.8), and full time (mean = 20.5, SD = 14.9). There was no statistically significant difference in epilepsy duration between the latter three groups (F(2, 422) = 1.59 p = .206, ηp 2 = .007). Having controlled for epilepsy duration and seizure frequency, a statistically significant (corrected alpha level .05/4 = .013) group difference with a medium effect size was observed in mean GAI score (F(3, 215) = 4.47 p = 005, ηp 2 = .06) between patients not working (mean = 92.8, SD = 14.2), patients who were students (mean = 96.7, SD = 16.9), patients working part time (mean = 100, SD = 16.2), and patients working full time (mean = 103, SD = 17.5). Post hoc analysis showed that there was only a statistically significant difference between unemployed patients and those engaged in full‐time employment. DISCUSSION

In this retrospective study of 559 patients treated at a tertiary epilepsy center, we examined (1) Associations between epilepsy‐related variables, affective symptoms, and EFs; and (2) how these variables may be associated with participation in employment or education.

Executive function, affective symptoms, and epilepsy‐related variables

More than half of the patients in our sample demonstrated impaired EFs as measured by EpiTrack. Patients with generalized epilepsy demonstrated slightly poorer EFs compared with those with focal epilepsy. Although this difference reached statistical significance, the effect was very small, suggesting limited clinical relevance. Consistent with findings from previous studies in both children and adults, 15 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 our results show that impairment in EFs is associated with a higher ASM load. Although the effect size was relatively small, the adverse effect of ASM load on EFs persisted after adjusting for variables related to disease severity, including seizure frequency. This highlights that executive dysfunction in patients with epilepsy cannot be attributed solely to disease‐specific variables, and must be seen within a multifactorial and neurodevelopmental model. 40 At the individual level, the relative impact of different factors should be evaluated longitudinally, in repeated standardized assessments, where EpiTrack is considered a feasible monitoring tool. 23 Although EpiTrack was developed originally to monitor the adverse effects of ASMs, it can also be applied to assess the impact of other treatment interventions. A recent study reported that vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) may enhance attention and executive functions, with greater benefits observed in patients on fewer ASMs. 41 Altogether, this emphasizes the importance of monitoring EFs during treatment adjustments and highlights the need to minimize total ASM load whenever possible.

Approximately 36% of the patients reported symptoms indicating major depression and 19% reported symptoms indicating generalized anxiety, consistent with previous research demonstrating that affective symptoms are more prevalent in patients with epilepsy compared to the general population. 20 We found a gender effect, indicating that female patients with DRE are more susceptible to depression and anxiety than male patients. However, the percentage of men reporting affective symptoms is higher than in the general population, 20 underscoring the importance of screening regardless of gender. With respect to affective symptoms and epilepsy‐related variables, no differences were observed based on epilepsy type or etiology. We found a significant, but weak correlation between affective symptoms and attention and EFs. These findings emphasize that although both affective symptoms and cognitive impairment are frequent in patients with epilepsy, they are not necessarily interrelated, and both need to be considered in the treatment context. Although analyses did not reveal any significant associations between affective symptoms and participation in work or education in our sample, it is well known that mental health issues in general are related to reduced quality of life and cognitive function. 4 Moreover, psychiatric disorders are a risk factor for suicidality, 42 and linked to mortality in people with epilepsy. 43 Thus, a positive screening, whether for depression, suicidality or anxiety, should be followed by a thorough exploration and referral for mental health evaluation to confirm the diagnosis and initiate treatment. 22

Employment and education

In our sample, ≈50% were unemployed, whereas 21.7% were students, 11.8% were employed part time, and 16.2% held full‐time employment. We found no clear gender differences in terms of employment status, except that more men were employed full time compared to women. In the 35–44 age group, the within‐group unemployment rate was 66.7%, accompanied by a higher prevalence of part‐time employment than full‐time employment. Although multiple factors are likely involved, childcare responsibilities may be one factor contributing to reduced work capacity. Regarding epilepsy‐related variables, our findings revealed no significant relationship between seizure frequency, epilepsy duration, and employment status. Although epilepsy‐related variables, including seizure type and frequency, may be detrimental in certain types of work, particularly blue‐collar occupations, our results indicate that work capacity may also be influenced by factors beyond epilepsy. In line with this we observed a strong and significant association between EFs and participation in employment or educational activities. The majority of unemployed patients demonstrated impairments in EFs, in contrast to those engaged in work or education. Furthermore, the regression analysis demonstrated that patients with no impairment in EFs had 2.5 fold higher odds of participation in education or employment compared to those with impaired EFs. In line with previous studies 39 , 44 we found a strong correlation between general cognitive ability (GAI) and EFs (EpiTrack) in our sample, but differences in employment status could not be explained by impairment in general cognitive ability. This suggests that GAI alone is an inadequate predictor of employability. Furthermore, no significant association was found between symptoms of generalized anxiety or major depression and engagement in employment or education. Taken together, these findings highlight the critical role of EFs in facilitating participation in work or educational activities. This interpretation is plausible, as impairments in these functions can hinder an individual's ability to manage tasks, adapt to changing demands, and maintain productivity, thereby reducing employability and job retention. These findings emphasize the importance of addressing EFs in the clinical management of patients with epilepsy.

Treatment considerations/future directions

Improved cognitive functioning is associated with enhanced quality of life and daily functioning in patients with epilepsy. 41 In addition to optimizing ASM treatment, rehabilitation programs have the potential to enhance EFs by implementation of effective compensatory strategies. Goal Management Training (GMT) 45 is a commonly used rehabilitation approach that has demonstrated moderate, lasting improvements in planning, attention, and self‐regulation. 46 Although GMT has been well studied in traumatic brain injury, stroke, and psychiatric population, 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 evidence in epilepsy remains limited despite the high prevalence of executive dysfunction. The is a need for large‐scale, controlled studies to establish the efficacy of interventions addressing EF difficulties and functional outcomes in epilepsy, including long‐term work retention and quality of life. Furthermore, there is still considerable work to be done in addressing the stigma and discrimination faced by individuals with epilepsy in professional and educational settings. Despite modern advancements, employment integration is in many cases still hindered by insufficient knowledge and a lack of inclusive programs. 8 The EpilepsyPOWER project works to address these challenges and aims to dispel myths and improve workplace inclusion for people with epilepsy. Hopefully, similar initiatives will become more prominent in the future.

LIMITATIONS

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the result of the present study. First, the cross‐sectional design does not allow claims regarding causality; concurrent assessment of mood, cognition, and employment outcomes precludes determination of temporal or causal relationships. In addition, there may be pre‐existing factors or other confounding factors contributing to the observed associations, or lack of associations. Second, as the majority of participants in the present study had focal epilepsy, the findings may not be fully generalizable to individuals with generalized epilepsy. Third, the ASM load did not take individual doses or medication class details into consideration. It has, however, previously been shown that both the number of concurrent ASMs and defined daily doses (DDDs) are related to cognitive performance. Analyses further indicated a stronger negative correlation between cognitive performance and the number of concurrent ASMs, whereas the total DDD did not provide additional predictive value. 15 Our sample contained some missing data, primarily concerning symptoms of anxiety and depression. This occurred because screening for these symptoms had not yet been implemented when the initial patients completed the EpiTrack. Patients with and without available NDDI‐E and GAD‐7 data did not differ significantly in any clinical variables. Consequently, this was not considered a source of response bias or a threat to the validity of the results. Furthermore, data on general cognitive ability were available only for patients who completed the full neuropsychological evaluation (n = 226). Finally, longitudinal studies assessing EFs before and after ASM therapy are necessary to better understand the impact of changes in ASM load, as well as the initiation and discontinuation of specific ASM type.

CONCLUSIONS

In the present study, patients with DRE frequently exhibit executive dysfunction, along with heightened symptoms of depression and anxiety. Unemployment rates are high (50%), and our findings suggest that job retention may depend on more than the burden of epilepsy alone, with executive functioning playing a critical role. Moreover, our results demonstrated that executive dysfunction is associated with a higher ASM load, even after controlling for disease severity–related variables. Altogether, our findings emphasize the importance of addressing EFs and affective symptoms in the clinical management of patients with epilepsy and support minimizing total ASM load whenever possible to promote better functional outcomes.
