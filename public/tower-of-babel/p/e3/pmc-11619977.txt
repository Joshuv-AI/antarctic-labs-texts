Efficiency maximization of fuel cell electric bus using asymmetric multi-stack fuel cells
Authors: The Nam Ngo, Minjae Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) have been widely studied as alternative ecofriendly vehicles based on multi-stack fuel cell (MFC) systems. An MFC system has the advantages of considerable efficiency, reliability, and durability compared with a single-stack fuel cell system. In this study, a novel MFC concept with an asymmetric structure is proposed to maximize the efficiency of a fuel cell electric bus (FCEB). The proposed concept was designed with a cascade electrical architecture composed of differently rated power stacks, and the optimal energy distribution between the stacks was determined to leverage the asymmetric structure. This facilitates the use of high-efficiency areas of the fuel cell stacks throughout most of the output power range. To confirm the performance of the proposed mechanism, dynamic programming, a global optimization technique, was used as the energy management strategy, and the FCEB was tested on Manhattan, Hanoi, and Seoul bus driving cycles. As a result, a significant reduction in the total fuel consumption of the proposed MFC electric bus compared with that of the conventional FCEB was verified.

Introduction

Enormous environmental issues arise from the exhaust of vehicles with internal combustion engines, leading to an urgent need for ecofriendly vehicles. Electric vehicles represent a potential solution, as they produce zero emissions, demonstrate more economical maintenance and offer higher performance than internal combustion engine vehicles [1]. Fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) have attracted considerable attention because they use hydrogen to generate electricity, and achieve significant efficiency while discharging only distilled water and warm air [2]. Moreover, they can be fueled in less than 5 min for operating over more than 300 miles without noise [3,4]. These advantages have been reflected in the pilot launches of fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs) [5]. In the Clean Urban Transport for Europe project, 36 FCEBs were developed to achieve more than 140,000 h of driving for an accumulated distance of over 1.3 million miles [6]. FCEBs are being increasingly deployed in transportation systems worldwide, indicating their potential for future development.

Two common fuel cell systems are used in FCEBs: single-stack and multi-stack systems. A single-stack fuel cell (SFC) system maintains low electrical efficiency for most of its operation time, with deficient durability and short-term reliability [7,8]. Moreover, FCEBs using SFCs exhibit a prolonged response time and parasitic load. SFC systems cannot respond immediately to power demands (typically requiring 2–7 s) owing to the polarization characteristics of the electrochemical reactions. In particular, a parasitic load requires an SFC to generate more power than is required to operate the auxiliary balance of plant (BOP) components, such as the fuel supply, pumps, blowers, and compressors, which considerably reduces the efficiency of the SFC system [9]. Both issues affect the overall efficiency of FCEBs, particularly under low load demands. Compensation for such late dynamics and parasitic loads can be achieved using batteries [10,11]. However, directly using a fuel cell can be more efficient than relying on a battery because discharging the battery for power use requires subsequent charging, and this charging and discharging process incurs energy losses. To overcome these barriers, multi-stack fuel cell (MFC) systems offer a practical solution owing to the high modularity of fuel cells in terms of response time and efficiency. These systems can activate degradation-mode operation to reinforce the redundancy and stability of the system when one or more stacks fail [12].

Overcoming these slow dynamics and parasitic loads and improving the fuel economy in MFC systems are important research topics. Palma et al. [12] proposed a modular fuel cell that laid the foundation for research on MFC systems. A prototype design for a segmented stack system in series was presented for a 12 V/150 W system with three-section proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell stacks and a DC/DC converter topology. Garcia et al. [13] used an offline power-splitting algorithm to optimize the efficiency of an MFC model with five fuel cell stacks of 500 W. Their simulation was verified using the rated power of the fuel cell stacks without considering the load power demand from the actual road profile. It can be seen from Ref. [14] that the efficiency of these systems depends on the power variation caused by the load, terminal voltage of the fuel cell, auxiliary equipment, and environment; therefore, validation of real driving cycles needs to be conducted. An online identification method was used in a previous study to reduce the fuel consumption of the MFC system [15]. To achieve this, a simulation was conducted on a test bench consisting of two PEM fuel cell stacks with the same rated power of 300 W and a stack controller, DC/DC converter, and DSP controller. However, the load demand power was obtained from the electric load in this system; therefore, it was difficult to analyze the effectiveness of the proposed method when used in a vehicle. Generally, most previous studies on MFC systems have considered their architectures, degradation-mode operation, and converter topologies [16], while the slow time response and parasitic load of these systems have not been adequately examined in the context of their application as fuel cells for electric vehicles. Also, research on asymmetric MFC systems and their large-scale application in the transportation industry (more than 100 kW), especially buses, has not yet been achieved despite their significant effect on system efficiency.

This study proposes a novel concept for a city bus called an asymmetric MFC electric bus (AMFCEB). The proposed system considers the power response delay and BOP components when developing an asymmetric MFC (AMFC) system composed of several fuel cell stacks. It is worth noting that these stacks have unequal rated power and are connected in a cascade architecture. The number and rated power of the stacks in the system were determined based on driving data from city cycles. Subsequently, a self-developed energy distribution algorithm based on vectorization was implemented to maximize the efficiency of the AMFC system [17].

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the powertrain, mathematical modeling, and energy management strategy of the FCEB. Section 3 introduces the concept description, fuel cell system architectures, and energy distribution of the AMFCEB and conventional FCEBs. In Section 4, the simulation results are compared with those of the conventional FCEBs. Finally, the conclusions of this study are summarized in Section 5.

Fuel cell system

This study uses PEM fuel cells, which show outstanding performance over other types of fuel cells in transport applications, such as a high power density, compact structure, excellent dynamic response, and low operating temperature [19]. Also, they have a high power density, and satisfy power requirements with a rapid response [19]. Additionally, they are lightweight, easy to handle, inexpensive, and exhibit long lifespans. PEM fuel cells use a water-based acidic polymer membrane electrolyte with platinum-based electrodes that conduct protons from the anode to the cathode [20].

Hydrogen is supplied to the anode of the cell, where the catalyst facilitates the separation of electrons from protons. The protons traverse the membrane towards the cathode, while the electrons travel through an external circuit, generating electrical energy. At the cathode, the protons, electrons, and oxygen react to form water as the final product. Battery

The efficiency of the fuel cell system is very low at low load demands owing to the slow response time and parasitic BOP load. Therefore, the fuel cell system cannot generate power for the BOP components when the power demand is too low. To address this problem, batteries are used as secondary energy sources. As an energy storage system, a battery can store the surplus energy produced by the fuel cell system and regenerative braking energy. Consequently, more efficient energy management strategies can be adopted to improve the overall performance of the designed buses.

Energy management strategy

Managing the energy flow between energy sources is crucial for improving the fuel economy of FCEVs. Utilizing an energy management strategy (EMS) helps FCEVs adjust their power and enhance efficiency, thereby matching the power demand and increasing the overall performance of the vehicles [28]. This study used two EMSs to distribute the motor power demand for the fuel cell system and battery. The optimal output power of the AMFC system and battery was determined using the global optimal dynamic programming (DP) method. When sharing the output power of the AMFC system among the four stacks, the optimal distribution method was used to handle the discrete optimization problem, as explained in Section 3.2. In general, the entire process was implemented using backward-to-forward simulations. This process consisted of four steps, all of which are conducted on the assumption of a known bus velocity and identical motor power demand values in both the forward and backward models (see Fig. 8 below). The optimal distribution algorithm initially generates the equivalent data map of the AFMC system, which is then transmitted to the DP algorithm. This algorithm calculates the system efficiency based on the hydrogen consumption map of each stack, and then adjusts the output power for the stacks such that the system efficiency is maximized at a certain required output power. Second, the DP algorithm is implemented offline to specify the output power of the AMFC system and battery. This algorithm uses an equivalent hydrogen consumption data map, battery characteristic data, and motor power demand, which are calculated using a backward model. The operating points from the data map are adjusted over time until the DP algorithm can determine the minimum hydrogen consumption value and the best power ratio between the AMFC system and battery every second of the driving cycle. The complete results of both strategies are then stair-processed with a 1 s time step before being transferred to the Simulink control unit as look-up tables. Finally, AMEsim utilizes the command signals from these lookup tables in the forward model and returns the final results.Fig. 8Overall process of current study.Fig. 8

Overall process of current study.

DP is a systematic technique that effectively determines and uses the required information. DP solves optimization problems by breaking them down into simpler subproblems to obtain global optimal solutions based on Bellman's principle of optimality [29]. Designing the AMFCEB and energy distribution between stacks

The efficiency of a fuel cell system is poor at low output power, and previous studies have overlooked this problem. In this study, we resolved this problem using asymmetric multi-stacks (hardware) and considering the energy distribution between the stacks (software). As a result, the efficiency of the FCEB could be significantly increased.

AMFCEB concept

This study proposes the AMFCEB, where the AMFC system includes four PEM fuel cell stacks with rated powers of 20, 30, 50, and 80 kW (a total of 180 kW) (see Fig. 10). The word “asymmetric” describes the different output power of each fuel cell stack, which is a key point in the performance of the proposed AMFCEB. As shown in Fig. 10, the efficiency of the fuel cell stack considering the BOP components is poor in the low output power area, and this tendency becomes more severe as the capacity of the fuel cell stack increases. Accordingly, using a small-rated-power fuel cell stack when low power is required is more advantageous. In addition, the use of multiple fuel cell stacks with an optimal energy distribution between the stacks is more favorable as the output power increases. A high-efficiency with the low output power(cf. fuel cell efficiencies in Fig. 10) means that charging and recharging the battery to overcome the low efficiency problem at a low output power is not necessary. This is because the efficiency of the fuel cell itself is very high despite the low output power.Fig. 10Efficiency characteristics of different fuel cell stacks.Fig. 10

Efficiency characteristics of different fuel cell stacks.

To examine the performance of the proposed AMFCEB, the AMFC system was compared with symmetric MFC (SMFC) and SFC systems that were used in two FCEB designs: a symmetric MFC electric bus (SMFCEB) and a single-stack FCEB (SFCEB). These designs were assumed to have the same powertrain system, support components, and operating conditions. For a better comparison, the three fuel cell systems were also assumed to have similar behavior and dynamic characteristics under the same operating conditions. Fig. 11(a) shows diagrams of the cascade configurations of the AMFC and SMFC systems, while the configuration diagram of the SFC system is shown in Fig. 11(b).Fig. 11(a) Cascade configuration of the asymmetric and symmetric MFC systems. (b) Configuration of the SFC system.Fig. 11

In AMFCEB and SMFCEB, the MFC system links multiple stacks through electric, fluidic, and thermal architectures. We focused on the electric architecture; the fluidic architecture was assumed to use a parallel connection owing to the net power production [16], and the thermal architecture was ignored. The electric architecture can be set up using series, parallel, series-parallel, or cascade configurations. In this study, the connection between the stacks was designed using a cascade architecture to achieve individual control of each stack, lower stress on the converter, high reliability, and low cost compared with other architectures [16]. Therefore, each stack was connected to a boost DC/DC converter before its power was provided to the DC bus and loaded through the inverter. The AMFCEB and SMFCEB were powered by PEM fuel cell systems with the same maximum gross power of 180 kW. The AMFCEB has an asymmetric MFC system with four PEM stacks of different rated powers (20, 30, 50, and 80 kW), whereas the SMFCEB involves a symmetric MFC with four PEM stacks of the same rated power (45 kW). The SFCEB was powered by a single PEM fuel cell stack with the maximum gross power of 180 kW. Detailed comparisons of these three concepts are presented in the following sections.

Energy distribution among the fuel cell stacks

The required power of the AMFC system obtained from the DP algorithm was sent to the fuel cell system where an optimal distribution method was developed by adopting linear programming (LP) and discrete optimization methods to specify the output power of each stack. In addition, equal and sequential distribution methods were used to prove the effectiveness of this method. The equal distribution method divides the required power of the AMFC system equally between the stacks, whereas the sequential distribution method (or daisy-chain method) splits the required power into the rated power order of each stack. These two methods are explained and implemented in detail in Ref. [32].

The optimal distribution method is based on the LP method and uses a vectorization technique to solve discrete optimization problems. LP simplifies complex relationships using a linear function and determines the optimum. The objective function obtained from the mathematical model is maximized or minimized in the feasible region, which consists of all points that satisfy the specified linear equality and inequality constraints. This method ensures that an optimal solution is linked to the defined objective function owing to the convex space of the feasible region in the LP.

The algorithm was implemented to solve discrete problems using a vectorization technique or array programming for ease of understanding, fast computation, and easy data and constraint calibration. Array programming allows the simultaneous access, manipulation, and operation of data in an entire set of values. Therefore, it is feasible to store multiple datapoints of the same type simultaneously using vectors, matrices, and multidimensional arrays. The detailed implementation of the algorithm is given in Fig. 12. First, the algorithm initializes the parameters, including the number of stacks, rated power of each stack, and operation modes of the AMFC system (Fig. 13). The total number of operation modes of the AMFC system was determined by the number of available operating cases for each stack. In this study, four stacks were used for the AMFC system, and the total number of operation modes was determined as follows. The four single modes (for only one operating stack at a time) contained only Stack 1, only Stack 2, only Stack 3, and only Stack 4. The six dual modes (for only two operating stacks at a time) contained Stack 1 vs. Stack 2, Stack 1 vs. Stack 3, Stack 1 vs. Stack 4, Stack 2 vs. Stack 3, Stack 2 vs. Stack 4, and Stack 3 vs. Stack 4. The four triple modes (for only three operating stacks at a time) contained Stack 1 vs. Stack 2 vs. Stack 3, Stack 1 vs. Stack 2 vs. Stack 4, Stack 1 vs. Stack 3 vs. Stack 4, and Stack 2 vs. Stack 3 vs. Stack 4. The full mode (all stacks operating at a time) contains Stacks 1, 2, 3, and 4. Only one mode was used at different required powers of the AMFC system at each time step. The optimal distribution algorithm selected the operation mode at each time step to satisfy two main conditions: the currently chosen mode must meet the required stack power (indicated by Pmfcstack in Fig. 12) and must demonstrate the highest fuel cell system efficiency (indicated by ηmfcsystem in Fig. 12). If there was more than one mode satisfying the above two conditions, stacks with smaller rated power had more frequent operations, and the power of each stack was discretized in power steps of 125 W between 0 W and their rated power. The BOP power, hydrogen consumption, and net power of each stack were interpolated and calculated using the previously determined discrete stack power. The multidimensional array containing all characteristics of the 15 operation modes of the AMFC system was vectorized and saved for later use. This array was used to calculate the system efficiency, and the results were added to the array to form the final array. Finally, the maximum efficiency value was returned for each required power step of the system. The operating mode of the AMFC system and the output power (reference power) of each stack corresponding to the maximum efficiency value were also obtained.Fig. 12Algorithm flowchart.Fig. 12Fig. 13Operation mode of the AMFC system.Fig. 13

Discussion

The newly developed AMFCEB achieved the lowest energy consumption in all driving cycles, and the AMFC system had the widest efficient stack power range (3–110 kW) where the efficient ranges in the other systems were 100–110 kW (SFC) and 9–110 kW (SMFC). Owing to the configuration and optimal energy distribution, the proposed AMFC system could be highly efficient at low and medium powers and the AMFC system only operated at high power during peak power demand. Also, using a small-rated power fuel cell stack is an advantage of the proposed AMFCEB. They presented an outweighing efficiency in the low output power area and mostly maintained an efficiency of more than 40 %. Therefore, the efficiency of the fuel cell system when charging the battery could be increased. Finally, the proposed AMFC system produced a smaller parasitic load. When composing stacks with small rated powers, the parasitic load from the BOP components considerably lowers the efficiency of the entire AMFC system. The proposed method minimized the parasitic load in all power ranges with a novel configuration and energy distribution, where the optimal energy distribution algorithm in this configuration ensured that all stacks satisfy the total required power.

Conclusion

This study proposed a novel concept for an MFC system using differently rated power stacks in a cascade architecture for application in an FCEB. The FCEB demonstrated inefficiency under low power when confronted with delayed responses and the supplementary loads imposed by its BOP components. In this study, an asymmetric MFC system structure was proposed to overcome these issues. Two types of energy management strategies were adopted to verify the effectiveness of the proposed concept. The first is the DP method to determine the global optimal values between the MFC system and battery, and the second is the optimal energy distribution method to share power between the stacks. As a result, the total hydrogen consumption of the proposed AMFCEB was reduced by 14.36 % and 2.05 % in the Manhattan driving cycle, 17.11 % and 2.39 % in the Hanoi driving cycle, and 6.92 % and 0.93 % in the Seoul driving cycle compared with those of the SFCEB and SMFCEB, respectively. All the results were obtained using MATLAB/Simulink and AMEsim model exchange simulations. In the future, more detailed and realistic models that consider complex dynamics will be investigated using real-world experiments to validate the proposed model. Additionally, the use of an ultracapacitor in the MFC system will be considered to support the ESS and decrease the time delay to enhance vehicle fuel economy. The proposed system can be used in various future applications that use fuel cells as power sources. Therefore, this mechanism should be evaluated for use in other applications, such as boats, homes, and industrial applications, to increase the efficiency of their systems.
