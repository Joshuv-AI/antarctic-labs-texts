Time-series transcriptomic analysis of Nile tilapia reveals the crucial roles of long non-coding RNA and transcription factor in response to Aeromonas veronii infection
Authors: Zhenzhang Lu, Anyi Li, Qianglong Sheng, Xiang Ma, Yanqiong Tang, Juanjuan Li, Xue Chi, Zhu Liu, Hong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) is a vital aquaculture species worldwide, prized for its rapid growth and high productivity. However, the increasing prevalence of Aeromonas veronii infections has led to significant economic losses in the Nile tilapia industry. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the host immune response, we performed transcriptome sequencing of Nile tilapia spleen tissues at 3, 7, and 10 days post-infection. Furthermore, weighted gene co-expression network analysis was employed to identify key genes and pathways, and a regulatory network was subsequently constructed to elucidate regulatory relationships. Our results revealed that long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) and transcription factor (TF) played crucial roles in responding to A. veronii infection, particularly lncRNA LOC112842004 and TFs TFAP2B and SP2. The differentially expressed targets of these lncRNAs and TFs, along with other differentially expressed mRNAs, were predominantly enriched in immune-related pathways, progressively shifting from upstream genes to downstream genes within pathways as infection advanced. Additionally, specific pathways were activated at each time point, with the MAPK signaling pathway remaining continuously active during infection. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying Nile tilapia’s response to A. veronii infection and provide valuable insights for improving disease management strategies in aquaculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11930-1.

Introduction

Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), a freshwater fish species native to Africa, is renowned for its rapid growth, high yield, omnivorous diet, and prolific breeding capabilities. It has been extensively farmed commercially in over 100 tropical and subtropical countries and is recognized as a top-quality species for aquaculture by the United Nations [1]. However, the widespread adoption of intensive aquaculture for Nile tilapia, coupled with escalating water pollution, has led to frequent disease outbreaks of Nile tilapia [2]. Notably, Aeromonas veronii poses a significant economic threat to the Nile tilapia industry due to its highly contagious nature and lethal effects [2–4]. A. veronii is a gram-negative anaerobic bacterium that thrives in aquatic environments and exhibits remarkable adaptability [5]. Infection with A. veronii leads to septicemia in Nile tilapia [6], characterized by symptoms such as protruding eyes, ulcerated epidermis, detached caudal fins, and internal organ redness and swelling [7]. In recent years, there has been an increasing number of cases worldwide documenting A. veronii infection in Nile tilapia [8–10]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying Nile tilapia’s response to A. veronii infection are not yet fully understood.

Transcriptome profiling is a powerful method for investigating host’s response to pathogen infection. Qi Li et al. revealed the important role of neuropeptides-related genes in the immune response of Nile tilapia by analyzing the brain transcriptome during Streptococcus agalactiae infection [11]. Neeraj Sood et al. identified crucial immune signaling pathways, such as the MAPK and JAK-STAT pathways, involved in Nile tilapia’s response to Tilapia lake virus [12]. Although substantial progresses have been made in Nile tilapia through transcriptome analyses, current focus has primarily centered on mRNA expression. The roles of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and transcription factors (TFs) remain largely unexplored.

lncRNAs typically exceed 200 nucleotides (nt) in length, lack open reading frames (ORFs), and do not encode proteins [13]. Historically regarded as transcriptional ‘garbage’, recent research has unveiled their pivotal roles in diverse biological processes, including the regulation of host innate immunity [14]. For instance, lncRNA TARL in Miichthys miiuy promotes the expression of transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), thereby inhibiting immune escape of Vibrio anguillarum and V. harveyi [15]. Additionally, lncRNA LTCONS8875 in M. miiuy up-regulates the expression of interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) at both the mRNA and protein levels, significantly increasing the production of inflammatory factors in response to LPS stimulation [16]. While most lncRNAs demonstrate low evolutionary conservation, these two lncRNAs exhibit high sequence conservation across M. miiuy, Larimichthys crocea, and Sciaenops ocellatus. TFs also play a crucial role in immune regulation by recognizing and binding to promoter regions of target genes [17]. For instance, forkhead box TF (FTF) regulates lymphocyte homeostasis in response to the invasion of pathogen, contributing to the establishment and maintenance of natural immunity [18]. Recent research has uncovered a complex regulatory interplay between lncRNAs and TFs in humans, mice, and other mammals, with both potentially co-regulating the same genes or pathways [19]. But similar analyses have yet to be conducted in Nile tilapia.

In this study, we sequenced and analyzed transcriptomic data from Nile tilapia infected and uninfected with A. veronii at days 3, 7, and 10. Differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) and differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) were identified at each time point. Our findings demonstrated a progressive activation of DEmRNAs and DElncRNAs over time. Specific pathways were observed at different time point, with their diversity increasing with infection duration. Notably, the MAPK and Calcium signaling pathways emerged as pivotal, primarily regulated by TF SP2 as well as DElncRNAs LOC102083157, LOC109203593, LOC102075756, and LOC112846962. These results significantly contribute to our understanding of how Nile tilapia respond to pathogen infection.

Materials and methods

Sample processing

The Nile tilapia used in this study were obtained from Hainan Baolu Aquatic Science and Technology, with an average weight of approximately 50 g ± 1 g. All experiments were performed under the guidelines evaluated and approved by the ethics committee of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hainan University. Prior to infection experiments, the Nile tilapia were acclimated in a recirculating freshwater system for one week.

A. veronii was cultured in 5 mL of Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium and incubated overnight with shaking at 30℃, 200 rpm. The mixture was then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 3 min. After removing the supernatant, the bacterial pellet was resuspended in 0.9% saline. Subsequently, the suspension was concentrated to achieve a final concentration of 2 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL.

Forty-eight healthy Nile tilapia were randomly selected and divided into two groups of 24 fish each. Both treatment and control groups received intraperitoneal injections: the experimental group received 500 µL of A. veronii (2 × 108 CFU/mL), and the final dose per fish was 1 × 108 CFU. While the control group received an equal volume of 0.9% saline. After 3, 7, or 10 days of infection, the Nile tilapia were euthanized using the rapid ice-cooling method. Spleen tissues were collected from eight Nile tilapia in each group, resulting in a total of 48 samples. These samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

RNA extraction, cDNA library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from spleen tissues of Nile tilapia using EZ-10 DNAaway RNA Mini-Preps Kit (Sangun Biotech, Shanghai, China). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). 1% agarose was used for gel electrophoresis to observe RNA integrity. 5 µg of total RNA was utilized for library construction. RNAs were enriched and fragmented with Fragmentation Buffer. Using the fragmented RNAs as a template, single-stranded cDNA was synthesized using a six-base random primer. Subsequently, double-stranded DNA was synthesized by adding buffer, dNTPs, and DNA polymerase I (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), followed by end repair, addition of adenine (A) overhangs, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The resulting cDNA fragments were purified. Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) to determine fragment size distribution and quantify library concentration. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina novaseq 6000 platform (Wuhan SeqHealth Tech Co., Wuhan, China). Raw reads from this study have been deposited into the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1145602.

Data processing

Adaptors and low-quality reads were removed using Trimmomatic [20]. The resulting clean reads were checked for quality using the FastQC, and further were aligned to the reference genome (accession number: GCF_001858045.2) using HISAT [21]. The number of reads mapped to each gene was quantified using FeatureCounts [22]. The Reads per Kilobase per Million Reads (RPKM) was calculated using R EdgeR package [23]. Functional annotation was performed based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

Weighted gene co‑expression network analysis

To conduct weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [24], genes were initially clustered based on their expression patterns across all samples using the TOMsimilarityFromExpr function from R WGCNA package. Module eigengenes were then calculated using the moduleEigengenes function. Similar modules were merged using the mergeCloseModules function with a cutHeight threshold of 0.15 (equivalent to eigengene correlation > 0.85). Each resultant module consisted of a minimum of 400 genes. The thresholds of cutHeight = 0.15 and minModuleSize = 400 were selected to preserve biologically distinct co-expression modules while reducing computational redundancy. The largest module was defined solely by its gene count using the table(moduleColors) function. In this largest module, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified at days 3, 7, and 10 post-infection, encompassing both DEmRNAs and DElncRNAs. DEGs were determined using the stringent criteria:|log2fold-change| >2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.001, as calculated by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, using R EdgeR package [23].

Target prediction of TFs and DElncRNAs

To identify TF targets within the largest module, position frequency matrices of vertebrate TFs were downloaded from the JASPAR database [25]. The MAST tool within MEME suite [26] was employed to screen the Nile tilapia genome for TF binding sites, using a threshold of p < 5 × 10−8. According to TF regulatory principles, genes harboring TF binding sites within 1000 bp upstream were designated as TF targets, with both their transcribed mRNAs and lncRNAs considered under TF regulation. Moreover, lncRNAs whose gene bodies contained TF binding sites were also classified as TF targets. For DElncRNA, targets were identified based on cis-regulation principles [27]. Initially, genes located within 100 kbp upstream and downstream of DElncRNA were screened as potential targets. Subsequently, Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) values between DElncRNAs and these genes were computed, with genes displaying|PCC| >0.9 considered cis-targets regulated by DElncRNA. KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted for DEmRNAs, TF targets and DElncRNA targets using R clusterProfiler package [28].

Construction of regulatory network

To construct the regulatory network for TFs and DElncRNAs, the connectivity between any two genes within the largest module was calculated based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM). Regulatory relationships with a connectivity greater than 0.4 were retained for TFs and their targets, as well as DElncRNAs and their targets. For miRNA-mediated regulation relationships, mature miRNA sequences of Nile tilapia were retrieved from the miRBase database [29]. miRNA-mRNA/lncRNA/TF interactions were predicted using miRanda with the stringent parameters: binding score cutoff ≥ 2000 and free energy threshold ≤ −200 kcal/mol. The resulting regulatory network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.0 [30].

Our transcriptomic analysis revealed a robust and dynamic host response to A. veronii infection, characterized by two distinct but complementary patterns of gene regulation. First, we observed significant activation of previously silent genes (RPKM ≤ 1), with lncRNAs exhibiting the most dramatic response (270.2% increase at day 3, peaking at 346.2% by day 10), followed by mRNAs (48.8–62.1% range) and TFs (33.3–42.4%) (Fig. 1A). Second, constitutively expressed genes (RPKM > 1) exhibited marked upregulation patterns following infection (Figure S1). Most notably, among the top 50 genes (ranked by ANOVA, between treatment and control groups), 90% (45/50) exhibited upregulation (Fig. 1B), with log2fold-change values predominantly ranging from 1 to 6 (Fig. 1C). These results collectively indicate a robust response of Nile tilapia to A. veronii infection.

Key genes and pathways responsive to A. veronii infection

To identify key genes and pathways responsive to A. veronii infection, we conducted a WGCNA based on the expression data of mRNA, lncRNA, and TF. A soft-thresholding power of 18 was chosen for the analysis. As a result, 10 modules representing groups of co-expressed genes were identified. To eliminate redundant information, modules showing over 85% correlation were merged, consolidating them into eight distinct modules (Fig. 2A). The module sizes varied greatly, ranging from 583 to 29,495 genes. Among them, the purple module encompassed the largest number of genes, encoding 21,991 mRNAs, 7,270 lncRNAs and 234 TFs. After A. veronii infection, the correlation between the purple module and the trait shifted from negative to positive (Fig. 2B). Our subsequent focus centered on investigating the genes within the purple module.

In the purple module, the number of DEmRNAs and DElncRNAs increased progressively with the duration of infection. Specifically, at day 3, there were 390 DEmRNAs and 105 DElncRNAs. By day 7, these numbers increased to 577 and 157, and by day 10, they further escalated to 733 and 164. Notably, 201 mRNAs and 52 lncRNAs showed differential expression across three time points (Fig. 3A). The 201 DEmRNAs were significantly enriched in 16 pathways (p < 0.05), including MAPK signaling pathway, ErbB signaling pathway, Gap junctions, Insulin signaling pathway, and Autophagy-animal (Fig. 3B). Target prediction for the 52 DElncRNAs yielded a total of 132 differentially expressed targets. These targets exhibited significant enrichment in eight pathways (p < 0.05), including MAPK signaling pathway, Hedgehog signaling pathway, and Mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis, aligning with those enriched in DEmRNAs (Fig. 3B).

Given that TFs can significantly influence on gene expression even with minimal changes in their abundance, we expanded our analysis to include the targets of all TFs, not just those that were differentially expressed. A total of 164 differentially expressed targets were obtained, significantly enriched in 12 pathways (p < 0.05), including GnRH signaling pathway, Calcium signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, Apelin signaling pathway, ABC transporters, and Gap junction (Fig. 3B). Notably, the MAPK signaling pathway and Cytoskeleton in muscle cells demonstrated dual regulation, being significantly co-enriched among targets of both DElncRNAs and TFs (Fig. 3B). This coordinated targeting highlight the synergistic roles of lncRNAs and TFs in orchestrating the host response to infection.

Dynamic changes in activated genes and pathways over time

Apart from the commonly observed DEmRNA and DElncRNA across three time points, time-specific DEmRNA and DElncRNA were identified during infection: 82 DEmRNAs and 19 DElncRNAs at day 3; 194 DEmRNAs and 64 DElncRNAs at day 7; and 370 DEmRNAs and 71 DElncRNAs at day 10 (Fig. 3A). This trend implies an increasing number of specifically activated genes in pathways as the infection progressed, as exemplified in the MAPK and Calcium signaling pathways.

In the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4A), the genes encoding CACN, GF, RTK, PKA, Ras, ERK, MKK7, Gap1m, RafA, MNK1/2, Crk II, MEKK2/3, and PP2CA showed sustained high expression after A. veronii infection, with notable increases observed in CACN, RTK, and GF nearing tenfold. The genes encoding Cdc42/Rac and DLK were highly expressed at day 3, with DLK peaking at day 7. By day 7, the genes encoding PPP3C and Cdc25B specifically exhibited heightened expression, while the genes encoding PKC, JIP1/2, and NLK specifically showed high expression at day 10,. Similarly, in the Calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 4B), the genes encoding NCX, CAV1/2, ROC, RYR, GPCR, RTK, GF, Gq, PLCβ, and PKA maintained consistently high expression after A. veronii infection. The genes encoding ADCY, FAK2, CALM, and CaN specifically showed high expression at day 10. By analyzing these pathways, we observed a gradual shift in highly expressed genes from upstream to downstream pathways as the infection progresses.

Furthermore, the specific activation of pathways also varied dynamically with the infection time: Galactose metabolism pathway at day 3, N-glycan biosynthesis and Intestinal immune network for IgA production pathways at day 7, and Melanogenesis, Gap junction, GnRH signaling, ABC transporters, Retinol metabolism, and Vascular smooth muscle contraction pathways at day 10 were notably activated. The results underscore the dynamic changes in gene expression and pathway activation in response to A. veronii infection over time.

Integrated regulatory networks of DElncRNA, TF, DEmRNA, and miRNA

To identify key regulators during A. veronii infection, we constructed a comprehensive regulatory network (Fig. 5A). This network encompassed 36 TFs, 83 DElncRNAs, 207 DEmRNAs, and 24 miRNAs. They were connected through 70 TF→DEmRNA, 4 TF→DElncRNA, 6 DElncRNA→TF, 226 DElncRNA→DEmRNA, 26 miRNA→DEmRNA, 5 miRNA→DElncRNA, and 1 miRNA→TF edges. In this network (Fig. 5A), TFs TFAP2B and SP2 emerged as central roles, exhibiting high degree and betweenness centrality. They demonstrated significant regulatory activity across infection stages. For instance, at day 3, TFAP2B governed the genes encoding CSMD2, HIS4, MUC1, and TMEM132. By day 7, TFAP2B collaborated with lncRNAs (LOC102081865 and LOC102082706) to regulate the genes encoding TSPAN8, PCNX4, and PPM1A. While it also coordinated with TF RUNX2 to regulate the gene encoding KRAB-ZFPs. By day 10, TFAP2B controlled the genes encoding CALM4B and ABCB11B. Among DElncRNAs, LOC112842004 emerged as a pivotal hub, regulating the genes encoding MYO1H, TNXBA, and GNA11B at day 10 (Fig. 5A). LOC106098758 served as a regulatory nexus, influenced by 14 miRNAs while itself modulating 4 mRNA. Notably, LOC106098758 was modulated by the key miRNA oni-miR-10,818, which targeted 3 lncRNAs and 21 mRNAs (Fig. 5A).

Furthermore, we extracted genes associated with the MAPK and Calcium signaling pathways from the global network, forming two functionally specialized subnetworks (Fig. 5B and C). The MAPK subnetwork comprised 3 TFs, 4 DElncRNAs, 10 DEmRNAs and 3 miRNAs. They were connected through 7 TF→DEmRNA, 4 DElncRNA→DEmRNA and 6 miRNA→DEmRNA edges (Fig. 5B). Similarly, the Calcium subnetwork involved 3 TFs, 8 DElncRNAs, 11 DEmRNAs, and 15 miRNAs. They were connected via 6 TF→DEmRNA, 8 DElncRNA→DEmRNA, 2 miRNA→DEmRNA, and 15 miRNA→DElncRNA edges (Fig. 5C). Notably, TF SP2 still served as a pivotal hub in both subnetworks. A notable example in the MAPK subnetwork was MAP3K12, which received coordinated input from SP2, DElncRNA LOC102083157, and miRNA oni-miR-184d. In parallel, the Calcium subnetwork revealed complex regulation of NMDA3B by TF PIGM together with three DElncRNAs (LOC112842606, LOC112843727 and LOC112846274). These results underscore the complexity of regulatory networks during A. veronii infection.

Discussion

Nile tilapia, a globally important aquaculture species valued for its high-quality protein and nutritional benefits, has experienced significant production growth in recent decades. However, intensive farming practices characterized by high stocking densities have substantially compromised water quality and fish welfare, creating favorable conditions for bacterial disease outbreaks [6, 7]. Among emerging pathogens, A. veronii has gained increasing attention as a formidable threat to Nile tilapia aquaculture due to its dual impact: substantial economic losses from its high infectivity and pathogenicity in fish populations, coupled with potential zoonotic risks to human health through foodborne transmission. Despite the growing significance of A. veronii infections, research efforts have predominantly focused on S. agalactiae, leaving critical knowledge gaps in our understanding of A. veronii pathogenesis and host defense mechanisms. Current limitations in the field are threefold: (1) an overreliance on mRNA-centric approaches that overlook the critical regulatory functions of lncRNAs and TFs; (2) a narrow temporal focus restricted to the acute phase, failing to capture the complete trajectory of immune responses; and (3) insufficient investigation into the complex regulatory networks governing host-pathogen interactions.

To address these limitations, we implemented a comprehensive temporal transcriptomic analysis spanning three critical time points (3, 7, and 10 days post-infection). Recognizing that genome assembly quality fundamentally impacts both expression quantification and regulatory network inference, we carefully evaluated our reference genome (Nile tilapia GCF_001858045.2) prior to analysis. The assembly demonstrated exceptional quality metrics: a scaffold N50 of 38.8 Mb, 99.4% BUSCO completeness, and 42,644 annotated genes with 95.5% RNA-seq support. These superior parameters substantially mitigate concerns about missing regulatory regions or annotation errors that could compromise TF binding site predictions in fragmented genomes. Combined with our stringent analytical pipeline, these genomic resources provide high confidence in both our expression measurements and regulatory relationship inferences.

Our findings revealed that lncRNA and TF were crucial in immune response. DElncRNA LOC1128424 regulated the genes encoding MYO1H, TNXBA, and GNA11B. MYO1H coordinates macrophage phagocytosis [31]. TNXBA plays a key role in both adaptive and innate immunity in fish [32]. GNA11B activates T cell receptor signaling in response to bacterial toxins in humans [33]. Notably, DElncRNA LOC106098758 may function critical sponge, by competitively binding to miRNAs (e.g., oni-miR-10818), thereby preventing their degradation of target mRNAs. More importantly, LOC106098758 served as a central hub in the miRNA→lncRNA→mRNA regulatory cascade, amplifying its functional impact on downstream pathways. This multi-tiered regulation suggests that LOC106098758 may act as a molecular buffer, modulating the intensity and duration of immune responses.

TF TFAP2B activated the genes encoding CSMD2, HIS4, and MUC1 at day 3, TSPAN8 at day 7, CALM4B and ABCB11B at day 10. CSMD2 is known for its role in complement cascade reaction in the immune system, while HIS4 enhances innate immune cell responses and increases intracellular Ca2+ concentration [34]. MUC1 contributes to immune homeostasis on ocular surfaces by modulating TLR-mediated innate immunity [35]. The homolog of TSPAN8 is involved in the innate immune response of Macrobrachium rosenbergii infected with A. hydrophila [36]. CALM4B facilitates cellular repair by accumulating at membranes of damaged cells [37]. ABCB11B mitigates liver inflammation induced by lipopolysaccharides in mice [38]. TFAP2B appears central in coordinating immune responses during A. veronii infection, activating stage-specific immune genes. The identification of specific TFs and their targets provides insights into the temporal regulation of immune responses, which is especially important as it reveals how TF orchestrates the immune response over time.

Our analysis revealed that these multi-layer regulatory mechanisms collectively converged on core immune signaling pathways. For instance, we observed sustained activation of the MAPK signaling pathway throughout A. veronii infection, regulated by miRNA, DElncRNA and TF. This aligned with observations in Channa argus infected with Nocardia seriolae, where the MAPK signaling pathway also showed persistent activation [39]. c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK), a key component of the MAPK cascade, can be directly activated by MKK4 and MKK7 [40]. Mice lacking MKK7 fail to activate JNK, ultimately leading to the end of inflammatory response [41]. In this study, DElncRNA LOC102082706 consistently enhanced the expression of PPM1A. PPM1A inhibits MKK7 dephosphorylation, thus further activating JNK [42]. Additionally, on day 10 of infection, DElncRNA LOC102083157 and TF SP2 worked synergistically to enhance the expression of MAP3K12, thereby sustaining JNK activity. miRNA oni-miR-184d also had a synergistic regulatory effect on MAP3K12, however, due to the lack of expression data, the exact timing of this regulation remains unclear. Additionally, oni-miR-184d regulated the expression of NLK, which plays a crucial role in regulating the immune tolerance of T cells [43]. These findings highlight the crucial role of the MAPK signaling pathway in response to A. veronii infection, with particular emphasis on the complex regulation of the JNK cascade.

Except for the MAPK signaling pathway, Nile tilapia also employs sophisticated Ca²⁺ signaling mechanisms to combat A. veronii infection. Previous studies have reported that an increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration promotes immune cell chemotaxis, adhesion, and cytokine secretion, thereby helping vertebrates to combat pathogen invasion [44]– [45]. In Nile tilapia, two mechanisms may contribute to the elevation of cytosolic Ca²⁺ levels. On one hand, the influx of extracellular Ca²⁺ is facilitated, for example, through the dual regulation of NMDA3B by TFs and lncRNAs. On the other hand, the efflux of Ca²⁺ from the endoplasmic reticulum is promoted, potentially through the regulation of key proteins like PLCβ, TACR1A, HTR2A, GNA11B, and FGF18A by lncRNAs [46]. These findings not only confirm the conservation of Ca²⁺-mediated immunity from vertebrates to teleosts, but also uncover the dual regulatory role of lncRNAs in both extracellular influx and intracellular release pathways. This sophisticated two-pronged mechanism enables precise control of Ca²⁺ homeostasis during immune challenge.

In conclusion, this study elucidated the pivotal roles of miRNA, lncRNA, and TF in Nile tilapia’s response to A. veronii infection, emphasizing their involvement in activating critical immune proteins and pathways, such as the MAPK and Calcium signaling pathways. These findings not only advance our understanding of Nile tilapia’s immunity but also identify potential molecular targets for improving disease control in aquaculture. However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the poly(A)-selected RNA-seq approach, while optimal for mRNA/lncRNA profiling, does not capture miRNA expression, precluding direct correlation analysis between miRNA and mRNA/lncRNA levels. To compensate for this, we applied extremely stringent thresholds in miRanda predictions to enhance the reliability of miRNA-mRNA/lncRNA interactions. Second, as most lncRNAs show lower evolutionary conservation than protein-coding genes (e.g., the well-characterized lncRNAs TARL and LTCONS8875 were undetectable in Nile tilapia) [47], our findings require validation across other teleost species. Future studies should combine small RNA-seq with poly(A)-seq to experimentally verify miRNA-mRNA/lncRNA interactions and assess the cross-species conservation of identified lncRNAs. These improvements will strengthen the evolutionary and functional interpretation of non-coding RNA networks in teleost immunity.
