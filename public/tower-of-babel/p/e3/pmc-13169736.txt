Attitude and subjective norms as predictors of intention of married men and women to accept caesarean section delivery in Lagos, Nigeria
Authors: Titilayo Abike Olaoye, Boluwatife Adebambo, Blessing Osie-Efetie, Ololade Ogunsanmi, Clarita Panuel-Egwakhe, Saratu Ajike, Juliana Olwatoyin Elebiju, Favour Ayobola Ojo, Nnodimele Onuigbo Atulomah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite its life-saving benefits, there is reluctance among Nigerian women and their husbands to have a caesarean section (CS) when indicated. This study assessed attitude and associated subjective norms involved in resolving whether to accept CS delivery among married men and women in Lagos, Nigeria.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional survey. A validated questionnaire was used to collect data from 422 consenting respondents who were selected through a systematic sampling technique. Attitudinal disposition was measured on15-point rating scale; subjective norms influence was on a 27 point-rating scale and intention to accept CS was measured on 10-point-rating scale. Data were transformed from response items into weighted-aggregate scores for moderating variables. Descriptive and Multiple regression analysis was conducted. All tests were conducted at p ≤ 0.05 level of significance.ResultsThe mean age of the respondents for males and females were 38.7 ± 11.6 years and 37.4 ± 9.8 years respectively. The male and female respondents had a mean score of 7.78 ± 2.82; 8.56 ± 3.08; 15.41 ± 6.97 and 13.66 ± 7.10 computed for attitudinal disposition and subjective norms respectively. About 30% of male respondents and 20% female respondents were likely to accept CS if the need arose. Fear of being mocked by friends (male: 42.0%; female: 42.9%) and risk/complications of the method (male: 30.3%; female: 23.7%) were barriers the respondents reported. There was a significant association between the subjective norm considerations of both the male (β = 0.19; p = 0.007) and female (β = 0.29; p = 0.001) respondents regarding their intention to accept CS. Male and female respondents with a master’s degree showed higher intention to accept CS compared with those with primary education (β = 0.27, p = 0.026; β = 0.23, p = 0.044).ConclusionThe respondents had positive attitude toward CS, with moderate subjective norm considerations in accepting CS. Education, Spouse, family, friends, and cultural beliefs played a major role in decision-making, reinforcing societal expectations regarding childbirth delivery options. These factors, in turn, contributed to intention to accept CS delivery among the respondents in the study. Integrating CS education into antenatal care, while ensuring that partners are also engaged, may enhance overall acceptance of caesarean section.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02318-9.Keywords: Attitude, Caesarean Section, Couples, Intention to Accept, Subjective norms

Introduction

The caesarean section (CS) is a vital surgical intervention for preventing maternal and perinatal mortality, particularly in high-risk situations such as obstructed labour, fetal distress, and breech presentation [1–3]. Globally, CS rates have risen sharply, accounting for over 19% of all births, with projections suggesting this trend will continue [4–6]. While the World Health Organization recommends an optimal population-level rate of 10–15% to maximize positive outcomes, this global increase is often driven by non-medical factors, leading to concerns about overuse in some contexts [6, 7].

In contrast, Nigeria faces a critical challenge of CS underuse. A systematic review and meta-analysis report the national prevalence of 2.7%, a figure far below the WHO recommended optimum [8]. This low rate signals a severe unmet need for safe emergency obstetric care and constitutes a significant contributor to the country’s high maternal and perinatal mortality [8]. The gap is not primarily due to a lack of medical intervention but rather to a complex interplay of limited access, health-system inadequacies, and, crucially, profound sociocultural barriers to acceptance [8, 9]. In many communities, CS is stigmatized and women who deliver via CS may be perceived as weak, “reproductive failures,” or even cursed, creating a powerful disincentive for its acceptance [10, 11].

Sociocultural considerations compel some women to opt for traditional birth attendants over CS for childbirth despite benefits it offers [12]. Decision-making around childbirth is rarely done by women alone; however, male partners often have significant authority. Existing research largely focus on women’s views, leaving a gap in understanding of how the male partners who have a major role in child birth decision-making [10, 13, 14]. The Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), developed by Fishbein and Ajzen [15], provides a rational theoretical framework to investigate this critical gap. TRA posits behavioural intention to proxy voluntary behaviour and is shaped by two key factors of attitude towards a specified behaviour and subjective norm considerations. In this context, a married man and woman acceptance or refusal of CS intervention reflects the shared beliefs about the procedure and the influence of family and community expectations. The overall disposition reflects the interplay of their personal attitudes towards the efficacy of surgical intervention and the norms imposed by their social environment, including extended family and community, who may hold strong traditional views on childbirth (Fig. 1).

Adopting TRA in this study provides an opportunity to elucidate the distinct dynamics linking psycho-cognitive components and decision-making process of married men and women in Lagos, Nigeria. Findings in this study will provide critical evidence to inform policymakers, programme planners, and healthcare professionals in designing targeted interventions that engage both partners, support informed decision-making, and ultimately help reduce preventable maternal and neonatal mortality.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design that collected data from consenting participants selected from six communities in Ikorodu Local Government Area.

Study location

This study was conducted in Ikorodu Local Government Area (LGA). Ikorodu lies 36 km North of Lagos and is bordered on the South by the Lagos Lagoon, on the North by a border with Ogun State, and on the East by a border with Agbowa-Ikosi. In 2003, the existing Ikorodu LGAs were split for administrative purposes into Local Council Development Areas (LCDAs). These lower-tier administrative units are now six (6): Imota, Igbogbo/Bayeku, Ijede, Ikorodu North, Ikorodu West and Ikorodu Central with an estimated population of 527,917 comprising of 268,463 males and 259,454 females [16].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible respondents were married men and women who consented to participate in the study. Respondents, who were sick or not mentally stable, were excluded.

Sample size determination

A minimum sample size of 422 participants for this study was derived using the Cochran formula (n = Z²pq/d²) [17]. The calculation was based on a 50% proportion (p) to maximize sample size due to uncertainty in the true population proportion, this choice was made due to uncertainty and variability in existing literature, as most available studies report caesarean section rates rather than the proportion of caesarean section acceptance among married men and women. In questionnaire-based surveys where no prior estimate is available, a 50% proportion is commonly used as a conservative and statistically safe assumption to ensure adequate sample size. A 5% margin of error (d), and a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96). A 10% addition was made to the computed minimum of 384 to account for non-response, yielding the final sample.

The study participants were selected using multistage sampling procedure. In the first stage simple random sampling by balloting was used to select three Local Council Development Areas (LCDAs) out of the six LCDAs. The three selected LCDA were Ikorodu Central, Ikorodu North and Igbogbo-Baiyeku. The second stage involved the selection of 6 wards (2 wards per LCDA) from the selected LCDAs using simple random sampling by balloting. Furthermore, the third stage involved purposive selection of the largest community per ward yielding 6 communities. In the selection of the participants, the number of streets and building per streets were considered. One street was selected per community and a proportionate sampling technique was used to select participants per street. An average of 2 participants, who met the inclusion criteria, was selected per building through balloting.

Variables

Explanatory variables and dependent variables

The explanatory variables considered were the socio-demographic characteristics as independent variables while attitudinal disposition (It reflects the person’s beliefs, feelings, and value-based judgments about CS, including perceptions of its safety, necessity, benefits, risks, and acceptability as a mode of childbirth) was measured as a moderating variable using a 15-point rating scale derived from five items assessed on a 4-point Likert-type response format (strongly agree, agree, disagree, strongly disagree), with item scores ranging from 0 to 3 and a total obtainable score of 15. Subjective norm consideration( refers to the perceived social pressure an individual feels from important people such as family members, partners, friends, or healthcare providers regarding whether they should accept or decline a caesarean delivery) was assessed using nine items on the same 4-point Likert-type scale, also scored from 0 to 3, yielding a maximum obtainable score of 27. The dependent variable was intention of accepting CS delivery. It assessed the participants’ readiness to use CS delivery when the need arises measured using five items assessed on a 3-point Likert-type response scale (likely, unlikely, not at all), with scores ranging from 0 to 2 per item, yielding a total obtainable score of 10. Thus, intention to accept caesarean section was operationalized on a 10-point rating scale. The response-items were transformed to weighted-aggregate scores to generate the overall scores to enable computation of summaries of descriptive statistics.

Instrument validity and reliability

The instrument was developed by the authors in English and translated into Yoruba because Ikorodu is home to the majority of Yoruba ethnic people (a major ethnic group of Nigeria living in the western states of the country). As a result, respondents could select the questionnaire in the language of their choice. The questionnaire was developed guided by TRA and provided the basis for construct validity of the instrument; also, literatures provided validation for the relevance of the variables in the study. The instrument’s internal consistency was measured by pre-testing the instrument with 42 participants in Sagamu. A Cronbach’s alpha score of 0.78 was obtained after necessary corrections.

Data collection procedure

Three community influencers who were community health officer with Higher National Diploma Certificate (HND) were recruited and trained as research assistants on the study processes. The recruitment of the participants took a period of 4 weeks (22nd Dec 2021 to 12th January 2022). Before administering the questionnaire, the study was introduced to the participants by the research assistants and informed consent was obtained by reading the informed consent form to the participants and those who could write signed the form while those who could not gave verbal consent as approved by the ethics committee. Further assistance was given to some participants who could not read.

Data analysis and test of significance

A coding guide was used to translate the responses obtained from the questionnaire. The attitudinal disposition, subjective norms and intention to accept CS data were transformed into a rating scale to derive standard measures. The IBM Statistical Package for Service Solution (IBM SPSS) version 29.0 was used for data analysis. Univariate descriptive analysis of socio-demographic, attitude, subjective norms and intention to accept CS variables were performed. Percentage mean score (Prevalence) were reported. Also, Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to test differences in the level of intention to accept CS across the socio-demographic profiles. Simple and Multiple linear regression was employed to evaluate the extent to which the TRA constructs (attitudinal disposition and subjective norms) predicted the intention to accept a caesarean section and was stratified by gender. Model was built using a stepwise backward elimination method, using variables with Univariable P-value of < 0.2. Some variables such as age, parity, occupation were chosen a priori. Data were summarized and presented on frequency tables with percentages. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The study enrolled 422 consenting participants, comprising 212 males and 210 females. Most respondents (61.3% of males and 63.3% of females) were aged 20–40 years, with a mean and standard deviation of 38.67 ± 11.67 years for males and 37.46 ± 9.88 years for females. Christianity was the predominant religion, with 51.9% of males and 57.6% of females. Yoruba individuals represented the largest ethnic group, comprising 63.7% of males and 62.4% of females. Regarding educational attainment, the highest proportion of males held a bachelor’s degree (31.1%), and while more than a quarter of females (31.0%) had an HND/OND (Higher National Diploma/Ordinary National Diploma). Less than half of the respondents were employed (males = 43.4%, female = 39.5%). Upon assessment, the lowest mean scores on the intention to accept CS delivery was found among male atheists (6.38), female followers of African Traditional Religion (5.92), Hausa males (6.23), Yoruba females (6.91), individuals with primary education (5.57 for males and 6.08 for females), unemployed males (6.25) and self-employed females (6.41) (Table 1).

TRA-constructs

Attitudinal disposition towards caesarean section

The findings presented here highlight attitudes toward CS birth. More than half of the male respondents (54.3%) and nearly half of the female respondents (47.6%) perceived CS as an unnatural way of childbirth. Likewise, approximately 7 in 10 males (68.9%) and 6 in 10 females (59.1%) believe that undergoing CS is a sign of weakness. Over half of the male respondents (54.3%) and a comparable proportion of females (57.2%) strongly believed that a woman should only have a CS with her husband’s approval. Additionally, nearly two-thirds of the male (66.5%) and female (65.2%) respondents perceived CS as profit making venture rather than a necessary medical intervention. Similarly, the belief that CS is unnecessary because childbirth should happen naturally is common among about 6 in 10 males (60.8%) and females (56.2%). Overall, the respondents had positive attitude towards CS (Table 2).

Subjective norms disposition

The findings showed that subjective norms played a key role in shaping respondents’ acceptance of CS delivery. Many respondents considered the opinions of their close social circles when deciding on CS. More than half of the male (59.4%) and female (59.5%) respondents would accept CS if their spouse supported it. Similarly, parental approval matters, with 68.4% of males and 66.7% of females more likely to accept CS if their parents viewed it as a good delivery method. Influence from in-laws is also significant, as nearly 7 in 10 males (69.4%) and 6 in 10 females (65.2%) would accept CS if their mother or father-in-law approved. Other family members also played a role, with 70.3% of males and 66.7% of females agreed that family support would make them more accepting of CS. Friends’ opinions affect acceptance as well, with 71.7% of males and 62.9% of females reported they would accept CS if their friends viewed it positively. However, healthcare professionals had slightly less influence, as only 58.5% of males and 51.4% of females would accept CS based on their doctor or nurse’s recommendation. Cultural and religious beliefs also shape acceptance of CS birth. CS was reported as culturally acceptable by 68.9% of males and 64.2% of females, also 64.6% of males and 63.3% of females reported that their religion permits it. Additionally, 67% of males and 64.7% of females would be more accepting of CS if they believed society viewed it as an acceptable method of delivery. Overall, these findings highlight the strong role of subjective norms influence in acceptance of CS with family, friends, and cultural beliefs playing a major role in shaping decisions (Table 3).

Outcome variable

Intention to accept caesarean section delivery

The frequency of responses on the intention to accept CS delivery in this study showed that only 7 out of every 10 male respondents and 8 out of every 10 females were likely to accept CS if the need arose. About 4 in 10 male and female respondents would accept CS as a less painful delivery method. Financial constraints influenced acceptance, as 56.1% males and 55.2% females were likely to opt for CS if they could afford it. Similarly, 57.5% males and 55.2% females were willing to accept CS if it is free. Overall, CS acceptance remains moderate, with financial and personal factors influencing decisions (Table 4).

Among female respondents, subjective norm was also found to be a significant predictor of intention to accept CS delivery. The intention to accept CS delivery increased by 0.29 points for every unit increase in subjective norm (β = 0.29, p = 0.001). With respect to educational level among female respondents, those with MSc qualification had a higher intention to accept CS delivery by 0.23 points compared with respondents with primary education (β = 0.23, p = 0.044). Discussion

This study assessed attitude and subjective norms as predictors of intention of married men and women to accept caesarean section delivery in Lagos, Nigeria.

Attitude of respondents towards caesarean section delivery

This study revealed that less than half of the respondents agreed that caesarean sections are an unnatural method of childbirth. Given that many respondents still believe vaginal delivery to be the best mode of childbirth; this finding corroborates previous findings where the respondents on the notion despite medical development [18]. Additionally, the respondents thought that having CS indicated that a woman was weak and unfaithful. A study in sub-Saharan Africa also reported that women who had CS frequently experience mockery and were viewed as “less of a woman“ [19].

Regarding decision-making, less than half of the respondents reported receiving spousal support for caesarean section (CS), particularly from their husbands. This contrasts with findings from rural South Western Nigeria, where high levels of general spousal support during pregnancy and childbirth were observed [20]. The discrepancy suggests that although husbands may offer emotional or financial support, they may be less willing to endorse specific medical interventions such as CS. Evidence from Nigeria indicates that men often hold primary decision-making authority in maternal healthcare [20]. This pattern is also evident in Ogun State, where partner involvement influenced family planning preferences, yet final decisions typically rested with the male partner [21]. Similar trends across reproductive health research show that male authority does not always translate into active support for all medical choices [21, 22]. The low level of CS related spousal support reported in this study aligns with broader findings from sub-Saharan Africa, where male partners exert substantial influence over reproductive decisions [23], shaped by sociocultural norms that frame reproduction as primarily a woman’s responsibility [24].

In some cultural contexts, women’s participation in the informed consent process for CS is further constrained by gender norms that require approval from a male family member before the procedure can proceed [25] For example, in Somalia, hospital protocols mandate that a paternal male relative not the pregnant woman must sign the consent form, giving male relatives, including husbands, significant control over women’s healthcare choices and sometimes limiting access to essential maternal services [26]. A comparable situation is seen in Malawi [27], where guardians frequently sign consent forms for caesarean sections, underscoring women’s limited autonomy in critical healthcare decisions.

Findings also showed that some respondents believed CS is used by doctors as a means of generating income. This perception should be understood within a broader context of financial insecurity and systemic mistrust. In Nigeria, the high financial cost of surgery is a well-documented reason for women’s reluctance to undergo CS [28]. This concern is intensified by a healthcare financing system characterized by substantial out-of-pocket payments for emergency CS and the frequent presence of informal charges, both of which erode trust in the health system [29]. Consequently, the belief that CS is profit-driven is not merely a misconception but reflects real financial pressures and limited transparency, which together discourage women from accepting medically necessary procedures. A study in Togo reported similar concerns, that pregnant women’s reluctance to undergo CS was partly due to mistrust in healthcare providers and fear of unnecessary medical interventions [30].

In addition, less than half of the respondents reported that CS is not necessary. This idea is the result of ignorance regarding delivery complications that require CS. A study in Ethiopia reported that women’s preference for CS was associated with factors such as dissatisfaction with previous intra-partum care and lack of knowledge about CS [30].

Subjective norms influencing caesarean section acceptance

Findings from this study showed that more than a quarter of respondents would accept a caesarean section (CS) if their partner thought it was a good method of delivery. This is consistent with quantitative evidence from Ghana, where spousal and family support emerged as the strongest predictor of women’s acceptance of elective CS [31]. Such patterns reflect broader socio-cultural norms; in Nigeria, patriarchal family structures and traditional gender roles often position male partners as primary decision-makers in health matters, thereby shaping and sometimes limiting women’s autonomy [32].

This dynamic is also evident beyond West Africa. In Malawi, guardians frequently sign most CS consent forms, while in Somalia, formal consent must be obtained from a paternal male relative [26, 27]. These examples highlight the powerful influence of spousal or family opinion on maternal healthcare decisions a trend widely documented across reproductive health research. For instance, studies from Lebanon similarly identify the spouse as a key motivator and an integral part of the socio-cultural norms that influence women’s choices regarding mode of delivery [33].

This study showed that the acceptability of CS is also significantly shaped by the involvement of parents. This mirrored the significant influence of parents on the acceptability of CS, resonate with the broader sociocultural context of reproductive decision-making in sub-Saharan Africa [26, 27].This aligns with studies in Nigeria, where intergenerational values and patriarchal norms systematically shape women’s health-seeking behaviors, often placing key decisions within the domain of the extended family rather than the individual woman [34, 35]. The role of paternal and maternal figures is not merely supportive but can be directive, governed by unspoken expectations about authority and familial duty, as observed in studies on surrogate medical decision-making [36]. Furthermore, the influence of parents, as indicated in this study, must be contextualized within a complex support ecosystem. Study from Kenya reveals that while family companionship during childbirth is common, the presence and influence of different family members from partners to female elders varies significantly and is shaped by personal preferences and fears of social judgment [37]. Therefore, the influence of in-laws on delivery techniques is a manifestation of deep-seated cultural norms that prioritize collective familial decision-making, sometimes at the expense of the individual woman’s autonomy.

In addition to spousal and parental support, friends and societal expectations also have a significant influence on the acceptability of CS. Rodriguez et al. [19] reported that societal expectations and peer influence had a 64.7% rate of CS acceptance in Middle Eastern countries. Maitanmi et al. [18] found that social media influenced CS perceptions, with both positive and negative narratives influencing decision-making. The study also revealed that cultural and religious beliefs had a significant impact on CS acceptance. These findings are consistent with the findings of Sadore et al. [38], who pointed out that religious leaders have a significant influence on maternal health decisions in conservative societies. This could be because religious teaching combined with faith-based intervention to foster acceptance of CS among church and society members [32, 38].

Respondent intention to accept CS delivery

The majority of respondents stated that they were likely to accept CS delivery if the need arises. This finding aligns with evidence from several studies conducted across sub-Saharan Africa and other low- and middle-income settings. Similar patterns of acceptance have been documented in Ghana, where women demonstrated a willingness to undergo CS when its medical necessity was clearly communicated, with counselling quality and family support playing a significant role in shaping this decision [31]. Comparable results have been reported in Nigeria, where initial fear and reluctance toward CS are common, yet women generally consent to the procedure once they understand its importance for preventing complications or safeguarding the life of the new born [28, 32]. Evidence from Kenya further supports this trend, showing that women are more likely to accept CS when the recommendation comes from trusted healthcare providers, particularly in emergency contexts [37].

Collectively, these studies suggest that CS acceptance tends to raise when the perceived health threat is substantial and when communication from healthcare workers is clear, supportive, and trustworthy. This pattern persists even in communities where cultural reservations, misconceptions, or stigma surrounding CS initially contribute to hesitancy.

The finding revealed that more than half of the respondents were likely to accept CS if the service is free and affordable. The high cost associated with CS is a significant deterrent, as corroborated by studies highlighting economic constraints as a barrier to CS utilization [39, 40]. The findings of this study revealed that educational level significant the intention to accept CS, although the effect varied across educational categories and between genders. Among male respondents, individuals with HND/OND and BSc qualifications demonstrated a higher intention to accept CS compared with those who had only primary education. However, respondents with SSCE and PhD qualifications did not show statistically significant differences when compared with the reference group. Similarly, among female respondents, those with MSc qualifications exhibited a higher intention to accept CS delivery relative to women with primary education. In contrast, other educational categories such as SSCE, HND/OND, and PhD were not statistically significant predictors of intention to accept CS.

The influence of educational attainment on the intention to accept CS may be explained by the role of education in enhancing health literacy, awareness of medical indications, and understanding of maternal health risks. Individuals with higher educational levels are more likely to appreciate the medical benefits of Caesarean delivery when complications arise and are therefore less likely to perceive it as a failure of natural childbirth. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in developing countries. For instance, Ajayi [41] reported that women with tertiary education were more likely to deliver by Caesarean Section compared with those who had secondary education or less. Similarly, Islam et al. [42] and Maitanmi et al. [18] reported a significant association between educational attainment and acceptance of Caesarean delivery.

The study also found that age, parity, occupation, and attitudinal disposition were not significant predictors of intention to accept CS among both male and female respondents. This suggests that these variables may not independently determine behavioural intention when other factors such as subjective norms and educational level are taken into consideration. The absence of a significant association between occupation and intention to accept CS may indicate that employment status alone does not necessarily translate into improved knowledge or acceptance of obstetric interventions. In certain contexts, cultural beliefs and prevailing social norms may exert stronger influence than occupational status in shaping reproductive health decisions. However, this finding contrasts with the report by Bam et al., [31] who found that age and parity were significant predictors of acceptance of CS.

Furthermore, the findings demonstrated that subjective norm was a significant predictor of intention to accept CS for both male and female respondents. This implies that individuals who perceived stronger social approval from significant others such as spouses, family members, healthcare providers, and community leaders were more likely to express willingness to accept Caesarean Section when medically indicated. This result supports the Theory of Planned Behavior, which posits that subjective norms play a critical role in shaping behavioral intentions by reflecting perceived expectations from important social referents [15, 43] In many African societies, reproductive health decisions are strongly influenced by family members and social networks, particularly husbands, mothers-in-law, and community elders. This finding is consistent with the report of Bam et al. [31], that spousal approval and family influence significantly affect women’s willingness to accept CS. These findings underscore the importance of social support and family involvement in maternal health decision-making.

The study also revealed that attitudinal disposition was not a significant predictor of intention to accept CS, which support the theory of planned behavior that attitude alone is not a major determinant of behavioral intention [15, 43]. One possible explanation for this outcome is that subjective norms may exert stronger influence than personal attitudes in collectivist societies, where social approval and family expectations play a dominant role in decision-making processes. Consequently, individuals may prioritize the opinions and expectations of significant others over their personal attitudes when making decisions about childbirth methods.

Therefore, there is need for a call to action on the urgent need to sensitize married couple, expectant mothers, youth and societies on the advantages and benefits of Caesarean section.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, it is important to launch comprehensive health education that will not only focus on pregnant women but also include their partners and in-laws. During such programs efforts should be made to debunk misconceptions about CS, especially the idea that it is a sign of weakness or an unneeded medical procedure. Key stakeholders, such as religious and traditional leaders, should be involved in these efforts in order to encourage a change in social norms and encourage well-informed decision-making. The adoption of CS is still significantly hampered by financial constraints, which calls for government initiatives to increase access to free or reasonably priced CS services.

Further, increasing health insurance coverage and offering financial aid for CS in public hospitals will help reduce worries about costs and guarantee that those who need medical operations will be more likely to accept such method of delivery. Additionally, incorporating thorough CS education into antenatal care service will raise procedure awareness and acceptance. During antenatal visits, it is imperative that healthcare professionals provide individualized counselling that explains the advantages and medical justifications for CS in order to reduce fear, anxiety and misinformation, before the need arises. Implementing public health strategies such as creating Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials can mitigate the sociocultural barriers to CS acceptance while contributing to improved maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study reported a positive attitude toward caesarean section (CS), with moderate subjective norm considerations in accepting CS. Spouse, family, friends, and cultural beliefs played a major role in decision-making, reinforcing societal expectations regarding childbirth delivery options. These factors, in turn, contributed to intention to accept CS delivery among the respondents in the study.

Limitation

The participants were not couples but were married men and women. Data collected were solely self-report. Also, findings may not be generalized to other geopolitical zones in Nigeria because the study location does not adequately represent other ethnic groups.
