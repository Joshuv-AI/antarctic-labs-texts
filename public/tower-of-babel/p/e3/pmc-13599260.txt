Meta-analysis of clinical trials assessing the safety of pharmacological treatments for muscular degeneration in Duchenne muscular dystrophy
Authors: Gerard Ompad, Petra Maria Saura, Nicole Sonne Heckmann, Andrea Rossi, Elena Olmastroni, Manuela Casula, Maurizio Sessa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCurrently, there is a lack of meta-analysis providing precise incidence estimates of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and associated risk differences (RDs) linked to pharmacological treatments for muscle degeneration in Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD).MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov (January 1, 2000–June 5, 2026) identified clinical trials evaluating safety of pharmacological treatments for muscle degeneration in DMD. A random-effects meta-analysis estimated ADR incidence, and RDs versus a common reference (givinostat) were derived from the pooled incidences rather than from conventional head-to-head treatment contrasts.ResultsThirteen clinical trials were included. Heterogeneity in ADR incidence across treatments (p < 0.05) was identified. Prednisone and prednisolone showed the highest proportions of serious ADRs. Discrepancies were found compared to Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC): deflazacort showed higher rates of erythema (+ 1.5%) and pollakiuria (+ 4.8%), while vamorolone showed increased Cushingoid features and appetite (+ 7.0%). Subgroup analyses showed dose- and duration-dependent ADRs for deflazacort and prednisone. A reference-anchored exploratory comparison of pooled incidences showed lower gastrointestinal and hematological ADR risks with corticosteroid monotherapy than with givinostat plus corticosteroids. Deflazacort showed lower risks of diarrhea (RD: − 0.3860), decreased appetite (RD: − 0.3160), and decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.4110).ConclusionPrecise ADR estimates can support decision-making in DMD care, helping clinicians balance safety and efficacy, address family concerns, and promote adherence by contextualizing treatment risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40360-026-01178-0.Keywords: Adverse drug reaction, Corticosteroids, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Givinostat, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe, progressive, X-linked neuromuscular disorder caused by mutations in the DMD gene (Xp21) that abolish functional dystrophin, rendering muscle fibres susceptible to contraction-induced injury and driving progressive muscle degeneration [1]. It is the most common inherited muscular dystrophy of childhood, with an estimated birth incidence of approximately 1 in 3,500 to 5,000 live male births [2]. Affected boys typically present in early childhood with delayed motor milestones, proximal muscle weakness, calf pseudohypertrophy and a positive Gowers’ sign, progressing to loss of independent ambulation during the second decade of life [1]. Because dystrophin is also expressed in the central nervous system, the phenotype frequently extends beyond muscle to neurodevelopmental and cognitive comorbidities, including a reduced intelligence quotient and learning and neurobehavioral difficulties, the severity of which is partly influenced by the position of the underlying mutation along the DMD gene [3, 4]. As the disease advances, patients develop respiratory insufficiency, progressive scoliosis and a dystrophin-deficient dilated cardiomyopathy with progressive left ventricular dysfunction, and respiratory and cardiac failure are the principal causes of premature death [1, 5–7]. Given this multisystem involvement, contemporary standards of care advocate coordinated, anticipatory management delivered through dedicated multidisciplinary Duchenne clinics that integrate neuromuscular, respiratory, cardiac, endocrine, bone-health, orthopaedic, neuropsychological and rehabilitative care; together with the systematic use of cardiopulmonary therapies, such management has substantially improved survival and quality of life [1, 5, 7].

Pharmacological management of muscular degeneration in DMD has historically centred on glucocorticoids, and current clinical guidelines recommend corticosteroid therapy such as prednisone, prednisolone, and deflazacort as the cornerstone of treatment, owing to their beneficial effects on muscle strength, prolongation of ambulation, preservation of pulmonary function, and delay of complications such as scoliosis and cardiomyopathy [1]. These effects are attributed to anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activity together with membrane-stabilising actions on dystrophin-deficient fibres. Chronic corticosteroid exposure, however, carries a substantial safety burden, including weight gain and a cushingoid habitus, growth suppression, delayed puberty, reduced bone mineral density with an increased risk of vertebral and long-bone fractures, cataracts, behavioral changes and adrenal suppression [1]. To improve the benefit–risk balance, vamorolone, a dissociative steroidal anti-inflammatory drug that retains transrepression activity while reducing the transactivation thought to mediate several glucocorticoid toxicities, has been developed and approved as an alternative to conventional corticosteroids, with the aim of preserving efficacy while attenuating effects on growth and bone [3, 8]. More recently, givinostat, a histone deacetylase inhibitor with a non-steroidal mechanism of action, has been approved to slow functional decline; it broadens the available pharmacological options while introducing a distinct adverse-event profile (e.g., thrombocytopenia, gastrointestinal effects and hypertriglyceridaemia) [9]. The coexistence of treatments with heterogeneous mechanisms and divergent safety profiles makes a robust comparative characterisation of their safety increasingly important for clinical decision-making.

Available clinical trials (CTs) of pharmacological treatments for muscular degeneration in DMD are typically small, which limits the precision of adverse drug reaction (ADR) incidence estimates and constrains direct safety comparisons across agents [4]. Recent systematic reviews have summarised the efficacy and safety of individual agents, vamorolone [8], givinostat [9], and the gene therapy delandistrogene moxeparvovec [10], and several studies have performed direct head-to-head comparisons of the efficacy and effectiveness of corticosteroids [11–16]. These reviews are nonetheless agent-specific and predominantly efficacy-oriented, and no analysis has integrated safety data across the full range of guideline-recommended pharmacological treatments for muscular degeneration alongside givinostat, nor provided indirect head-to-head safety comparisons where direct trial comparisons are lacking [17]. A comparative analysis is well suited to address this gap, as it allows ADR risks to be compared across treatments by combining direct and indirect evidence. In this study, we performed a meta-analysis to derive more precise incidence estimates of ADRs associated with pharmacological treatments for muscular degeneration in DMD using CTs data and compared these estimates among drugs and with the incidence proportions reported in the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPCs). We further conducted subgroup analyses to explore the association of dosage and treatment duration with ADR incidence and performed indirect head-to-head comparisons of ADR risk among treatments, using the risk difference (RD) as the effect estimator.

Methods

We performed a literature search and meta-analysis in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [12]. The protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) Registries (https://osf.io/w5kjh). No amendments to the registered protocol were made.

Search strategy

The literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov to identify relevant studies published between January 1, 2000, and June 5, 2026. Search terms included a combination of keywords connected by Boolean operators (AND, OR) to refine the results. Keywords related to the disease of interest, corticosteroids and givinostat, and safety outcomes were used. The specific search strategy for PubMed, which was subsequently adapted for Embase, is provided in Supplementary Material – Appendix 1. The PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in appendix 2.

The search strategy was developed by MS and independently performed and cross-checked by two reviewers (MS and PS). Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (GO). To complement the database search, we hand-searched the reference lists of all included trials and of relevant systematic reviews for additional eligible studies (backward citation searching). Grey literature was searched through clinical-trial registries, namely ClinicalTrials.gov.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized data extraction form. The data extraction form was designed to systematically capture relevant information from each study included in the systematic review. Key variables were recorded, such as the NCT identifier, PubMed ID (PMID), study reference, study type (e.g., CTs), drug under investigation, dosage (mg/kg/day), treatment duration (in days), therapeutic indication, ADRs, number of individuals experiencing the ADRs, study population exposed to the pharmacological treatment, and participant characteristics such as age, body mass index (BMI), and age at disease onset. The patient characteristics, including age, BMI, and age at onset, were selected for descriptive analyses as they were among the variables most consistently measured across the selected studies.

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (MS, PS) using the aforementioned standardized form. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (GO). Missing values were not imputed.

Risk of bias analysis

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers (MS, PS) using the RoB 2.0 for randomized trials and related tools. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (GO).

Meta-analysis of incidence

The meta-analysis focused on the incidence of ADRs reported in at least 2 different study arms of CTs included in the systematic review. Pooled incidence rates were estimated using a random-effects model with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator. Forest plots were generated to visualize individual study results alongside the overall pooled estimate, including 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and study weights. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the incidence of ADRs. The subgroup comparisons examined variations in incidence rates across different dosages and treatment durations. Differences in incidence estimates for each ADR between the SmPCs and our meta-analyses were calculated. The percentage of serious ADRs was calculated for each treatment. The seriousness of ADRs was assessed using the Important Medical Events (IME) list from the European Medicines Agency [13]. For ADRs reported in only one arm of a CT, the observed incidence in that arm was used directly to compute RDs, without pooling. No pre-specified sensitivity analyses were performed for the meta-analysis of incidence. No formal assessment of reporting bias (e.g., funnel plot asymmetry) was conducted due to the limited number of studies per outcome and heterogeneous outcome reporting.

Comparisons

To compare the risk of ADRs across treatments for muscular degeneration, RDs relative to a reference treatment (i.e., givinostat) were estimated using the random-effects pooled incidence estimates obtained for each treatment (Section “Meta-analysis of incidence”). For each ADR, the pooled incidence and its standard error for each treatment were entered pairwise against the reference treatment within a meta-analytic framework (netmeta package, R) to obtain the RD and its 95% CI. Givinostat was selected as the reference treatment because it is a novel, non-corticosteroid option with a mechanism of action distinct from that of the corticosteroids, and the treatment for which gastrointestinal and hematological reactions were most consistently reported, providing an informative comparator against which the corticosteroid profiles could be contrasted. Because all comparisons were anchored to this common reference and were computed from independently pooled incidences rather than from within-trial randomized contrasts, the estimates do not depend on the consistency assumption of contrast-based mixed-treatment comparison. Accordingly, these analyses are reference-anchored RDs between independently pooled incidence estimates rather than contrast-based mixed-treatment comparisons. Where an ADR was reported in the givinostat arm but not in a comparator arm (comparator incidence of zero), the RD necessarily equals the negative of the givinostat incidence, and its CI is determined by the givinostat estimate; such comparisons are identified in the Results. No pre-specified sensitivity analyses and no formal certainty-of-evidence assessment (e.g., Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation, GRADE) were performed.

Descriptive analyses

To provide an overview of the ADRs reported with the drugs under investigation, two types of plots were used. The first plot is a heatmap that visualizes the distribution of ADRs across System Organ Classes (SOC) and drugs. The second plot shows the percentage of serious ADRs (according to the Important Medical Event list, version 27.1) for each drug [14]. A heatmap was provided to visualize the incidence of ADRs across the drugs under investigation. R (Version 4.3.1, RStudio, 2024) was used as statistical software.

Ethics

Ethical approval or informed consent were not required for this manuscript.

In total, 13 CTs were identified as eligible and included for data extraction (Fig. 1). An overview of the 13 CTs [11, 12, 19–29] is provided in Table 1. Meta-analysis of incidence

The results of the meta-analysis of incidence revealed statistically significant heterogeneity in the incidence estimates of ADRs for deflazacort (p < 0.05), vamorolone (p < 0.05), prednisone (p < 0.05), prednisolone (p < 0.05), and givinostat (p < 0.05) among the CT estimates included. Incidence estimates obtained from the meta-analysis using random-effects models are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 2). When considering only serious ADRs, the results showed significant variation across drugs, with prednisolone and intermittent prednisolone exhibiting a percentage of serious ADRs exceeding 60% in CTs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Prednisolone (51.3%) and prednisone (57.3%) also showed the highest incidence of endocrine disorders (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Heatmap of ADR incidences across different drugs

Statistically significant variability was observed between the incidence estimates from the meta-analysis (random-effects models) and the incidence proportions reported in the SmPCs for deflazacort (p < 0.05 for each comparison). Specifically, the meta-analysis revealed higher incidences of erythema (+ 1.5%) and pollakiuria (+ 4.8%) compared to the SmPC (Supplementary Table 2). For vamorolone, higher incidences were observed for Cushingoid features (+ 7.0%) and increased appetite (+ 7.0%) (Supplementary Table 3). In the case of prednisone, the discrepancies between the meta-analytic estimates and the SmPC data were particularly notable for hypertension (+ 5.8%) and cataracts/glaucoma (+ 6.8%) (Supplementary Table 4). For prednisolone, Cushingoid symptoms were reported at a lower incidence in the meta-analysis (− 5.4%) compared to the SmPC (Supplementary Table 5). For givinostat, the meta-analysis showed higher incidences of nausea/vomiting (+ 18.1%) and abdominal pain (+ 13.2%) than those reported in the SmPC (Supplementary Table 6).

Subgroup analyses by dose and by treatment duration

For deflazacort, increasing treatment dose was significantly associated with a higher incidence of multiple ADRs, including central obesity, Cushingoid features, erythema, frequent daytime urination, headache, hirsutism, influenza, upper abdominal pain, and weight gain (p < 0.05 for each; Supplementary Figs. 3–11). For prednisolone, dose was significantly associated only with weight gain (p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 12). For vamorolone, a significant dose-related ADRs was observed solely for nasopharyngitis and pyrexia (p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 13). Subgroup analyses by treatment duration showed similar patterns. For deflazacort, longer treatment duration was significantly associated with increased incidence of several ADRs, including abnormal behavior, central obesity, constipation, cough, Cushingoid features, erythema, frequent daytime urination, hirsutism, increased appetite, influenza, pyrexia, upper abdominal pain, and weight gain (p < 0.05 for each; Supplementary Figs. 14–26). For prednisolone, only weight gain was significantly associated with longer treatment duration (p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 27). In the case of prednisone, extended treatment duration was significantly associated with higher incidence of abnormal behavior, Cushingoid features, headache, upper respiratory tract infection, and weight gain (p < 0.05 for each; Supplementary Figs. 28–32). For givinostat and vamorolone, treatment duration had a significant effect only on vomiting and nasopharyngitis, respectively (p < 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 33–34).

Comparisons

Pooled incidence estimates from the random-effects meta-analysis were used as input for the reference-anchored comparison described in Section “Comparisons”. ADRs reported in only one treatment arm were handled by treating the unreported comparator incidence as zero, so that the corresponding RD equals the negative of the reported (givinostat) incidence. For such ADRs, the observed incidence in that arm was used to compute the RDs (Supplementary Table 7). Figures 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 present the RDs of ADRs comparing monotherapy with deflazacort, vamorolone, prednisone, intermittent prednisone, prednisolone, and intermittent prednisolone with givinostat in combination with corticosteroids. Across comparisons, monotherapy was consistently associated with a lower risk of gastrointestinal disorders and hematological ADRs. Compared with givinostat plus corticosteroids, deflazacort was associated with lower risks of diarrhea (RD: − 0.3860; 95% CI: − 0.4568 to − 0.3152), decreased appetite (RD: − 0.3160; 95% CI: − 0.5250 to − 0.1070), decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.4110; 95% CI: − 0.4845 to − 0.3375), and soft feces (RD: − 0.3650; 95% CI: − 0.4408 to − 0.2892). Similarly, vamorolone showed reduced risks for diarrhea (RD: − 0.3090; 95% CI: − 0.3845 to − 0.2335), decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.3770; 95% CI: − 0.4527 to − 0.3013), and soft feces (RD: − 0.3650; 95% CI: − 0.4408 to − 0.2892). Prednisone was associated with lower incidence of diarrhea (RD: − 0.3650; 95% CI: − 0.4408 to − 0.2892), decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.4110; 95% CI: − 0.4845 to − 0.3375), and musculoskeletal pain (RD: − 0.3160; 95% CI: − 0.5250 to − 0.1070). Intermittent prednisone showed similarly favorable results, with reduced risks for decreased appetite (RD: − 0.3160; 95% CI: − 0.5250 to − 0.1070), diarrhea (RD: − 0.2520; 95% CI: − 0.3631 to − 0.1409), and decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.4110; 95% CI: − 0.4845 to − 0.3375). In the comparison between prednisolone and givinostat plus corticosteroids, prednisolone was associated with lower risks of decreased appetite (RD: − 0.3160; 95% CI: − 0.5250 to − 0.1070), diarrhea (RD: − 0.4040; 95% CI: − 0.4736 to − 0.3344), decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.4110; 95% CI: − 0.4845 to − 0.3375), and soft feces (RD: − 0.3650; 95% CI: − 0.4408 to − 0.2892). Lastly, intermittent prednisolone showed reduced risks for decreased appetite (RD: − 0.3160; 95% CI: − 0.5250 to − 0.1070), decreased platelet count (RD: − 0.4110; 95% CI: − 0.4845 to − 0.3375), and soft feces (RD: − 0.3650; 95% CI: − 0.4408 to − 0.2892).

Risk of bias analysis

Randomized CTs included in the review were assessed with the RoB 2.0 tool and were judged to be at low risk of bias. Across all five domains, bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in the selection of the reported result, every trial received a low-risk judgement, yielding an overall low risk of bias for each trial. No trial raised some concerns or was rated at high risk in any domain. The domain-level and overall judgements are summarized in the traffic-light plot (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Our results provide an analysis of the safety profiles and ADRs associated with various pharmacological interventions for muscle degeneration in DMD, including corticosteroids (deflazacort, prednisone, prednisolone, and vamorolone) and non-corticosteroid alternatives (givinostat). Additionally, this meta-analysis evaluated the incidence and risk RDs of ADRs associated with corticosteroids and givinostat in CTs, revealing significant heterogeneity in incidence estimates, dose- and duration-dependent effects, and comparative safety profiles.

The literature review identified a relatively small number of CTs, which is unsurprising given the rarity of DMD. The trials included in the review involved limited sample size, ranging from as few as 19 to just over 240 individuals. This constraint is inherently tied to the low prevalence of DMD, which presents challenges in recruiting larger cohorts for clinical studies [30].

The observed statistically significant heterogeneity in ADR incidence across drugs, notably for deflazacort, vamorolone, prednisone, prednisolone, and givinostat, underscores the variability inherent in CTs populations, dosing regimens, and study designs. This heterogeneity aligns with previous analyses of corticosteroid safety profiles, which have reported wide-ranging ADRs rates depending on indication and patient characteristics [31]. The discrepancies between meta-analytic incidence estimates and SmPC data, such as higher incidences of erythema with deflazacort and hypertension with prednisone, highlight the limitations of SmPCs as static sources that may underreport ADR frequencies observed in aggregated CTs data [32].

Overall, vamorolone exhibited lower incidence of ADRs across the treatments under investigation, suggesting a potentially favorable safety profile compared to other treatments. Accordingly, the meta-analysis by Ibrahim et al. [33] comparing vamorolone with glucocorticosteroids or placebo in DMD patients showed that vamorolone did not inhibit growth but increased the risk of weight gain, suppression of adrenal function, and insulin resistance, even if these effects were ameliorated by reducing the dose.

The clear dose-response relationship observed for deflazacort with multiple ADRs (e.g., central obesity, Cushingoid features) and weight gain with prednisolone and prednisone corroborate the well-established principle that corticosteroid toxicity increases with dose and prolonged exposure [20]. Notably, vamorolone showed limited dose-related ADR effects, consistent with its development as a dissociative steroid designed to minimize classic corticosteroid ADRs [34].

The reference-anchored comparison showed consistently lower risks of gastrointestinal and hematological ADRs with monotherapies than with givinostat combined with corticosteroids. Because these comparisons are indirect, anchored to a single common reference, and not derived from head-to-head randomized contrasts, they are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Corticosteroids, even when administered as monotherapy, exhibited a broader range of ADRs, including metabolic, dermatological, and hormonal disturbances. For example, deflazacort was linked to weight gain, Cushingoid features, and infections, while vamorolone showed ADRs like those of corticosteroids, including abnormal behavior, hypertension, and musculoskeletal pain. Prednisone and prednisolone both demonstrated significant risks for metabolic and immune-mediated ADRs, such as weight increase, hypertension, and infections, with intermittent use of these drugs still presenting considerable risks for Cushingoid features and other systemic effects. These findings underscore the differentiated ADR profiles between corticosteroids and givinostat, with the latter being associated with a more concentrated set of gastrointestinal and hematological risks.

Precise estimation of ADR incidence, comparative RDs across treatment options, and subgroup analyses hold substantial clinical value, particularly in the management of DMD, where long-term corticosteroid use remains the cornerstone of therapy. In this population, ADRs are among the most cited reasons for treatment discontinuation [35, 36]. As DMD progresses and patients become increasingly dependent on caregivers, decisions around therapy are deeply influenced by anticipated side effects, especially those affecting physical appearance, behavior, and metabolic health (e.g., Cushingoid features, weight gain, behavioral changes) [35, 36].

By quantifying absolute incidence proportion and RDs for specific ADRs across different corticosteroids and regimens, this study provides a foundation for evidence-informed shared decision-making between clinicians and families. Such information is not only critical for balancing efficacy and tolerability, but also for setting realistic expectations, proactively addressing concerns, and ultimately promoting treatment adherence. For example, the finding that vamorolone and deflazacort are associated with a lower incidence of certain gastrointestinal or hematological ADRs compared to combination therapies with givinostat may be particularly reassuring to families concerned about tolerability. Additionally, demonstrating that specific ADRs, such as weight gain or behavioral effects, are dose- and duration-dependent enables personalized adjustments to minimize harm while preserving therapeutic benefit. Moreover, fear of irreversible ADRs like cataracts or growth suppression has been shown to negatively influence family willingness to initiate or continue corticosteroid therapy, even when functional benefits are substantial [37]. By clarifying that the absolute excess risk may be manageable or less than perceived, clinicians are better equipped to help families contextualize and weigh these risks, thus strengthening adherence to long-term therapy plans. In this light, precise ADR estimates support not only clinical decision-making but also psychosocial resilience in patients and caregivers, ultimately contributing to better disease management outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of our study is the indirect head-to-head comparisons of ADR risks, addressing a critical gap in literature. Additionally, the study enhances the understanding of dose- and duration-dependent ADR patterns, which can inform more personalized treatment decisions. The low risk of bias across included studies supports the reliability of these findings.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not search for grey literature beyond ClinicalTrials.gov. Additional limitations include variability in ADR reporting standards, potential publication bias, and lack of individual patient data restricting subgroup analyses by demographic factors. Additionally, the analysis relies on data from published CTs, which may not fully reflect real-world patient populations and treatment conditions. Heterogeneity in study designs, follow-up durations, and ADR reporting standards could introduce variability in incidence estimates. Restriction to randomized CT, while ensuring internal validity, excluded observational studies and patient registries that provide crucial long-term safety data with larger, more diverse populations. Additional limitations: (1) No sensitivity analyses were performed; (2) No formal assessment of reporting bias was conducted; (3) GRADE certainty-of-evidence assessment and Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) were not applied; (4) Exclusion of grey literature beyond ClinicalTrials.gov; (5) We did not formally assess the transitivity assumption or quantitatively compare the distribution of effect modifiers across the contributing trials.

Several limitations specific to the comparative analysis warrant emphasis. The RDs were computed as differences between independently pooled incidence estimates anchored to a common reference (givinostat). Although direct head-to-head randomized comparisons among corticosteroids were available and included (for example, deflazacort versus prednisone, vamorolone versus prednisone, and dosing-regimen comparisons), these within-trial contrasts were not analyzed in a connected, contrast-based network model; RDs were instead computed against a common reference (givinostat) from independently pooled incidences. Givinostat was not randomized against any active comparator in the included trials and is therefore connected only indirectly. Consequently, for the reference-anchored comparisons reported here the consistency assumption does not apply and transitivity was not formally assessed. For ADRs reported in the givinostat arm but not in a comparator arm, the comparator incidence was set to zero, so the corresponding RD equals the negative of the givinostat incidence; where such events may have been unascertained rather than truly absent, this assumption can overstate the contrast, and these RDs should therefore be interpreted with particular caution. Accordingly, the comparative findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis highlights the heterogeneous and dose/time-dependent nature of ADRs associated with corticosteroids and givinostat in the treatment of DMD. By integrating data from randomized CTs and comparing findings with regulatory sources such as SmPCs, the study offers refined and clinically relevant estimates of ADR incidence and RDs. The comparative RDs between treatments are exploratory and rest on indirect, reference-anchored comparisons rather than head-to-head trials, and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. These findings have direct implications for treatment planning, enabling clinicians to better anticipate side effects, tailor regimens, and engage in transparent discussions with patients and families. Given that ADRs are a leading cause of treatment discontinuation in DMD, the ability to accurately quantify and communicate these risks is essential for balancing safety with efficacy. Ultimately, incorporating these data into shared decision-making may improve adherence, alleviate undue fears, and support more sustainable long-term care strategies in this vulnerable population.
