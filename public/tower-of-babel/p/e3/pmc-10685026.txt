Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation protein 1 inhibition alleviates intestinal impairment induced by chronic heat stress in finisher broilers
Authors: Huaiyong Zhang, Herinda Pertiwi, Maryam Majdeddin, Joris Michiels
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Heat stress (HS) in poultry has deleterious effects on intestinal development and barrier function, along with inflammatory outbursts. In the present study, chronic HS reduced body weight of broilers and activated mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation protein 1 (Malt1) /nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways to elicit the inflammatory cytokine response in jejunum. Subsequently, this study investigated the protective effects of the Malt1 inhibitor on the intestine of broilers under HS conditions. The 21-day-old male broilers were allocated to 8 pens housed in HS room (34°C for 7 h/d) until 28 d of age. During this period, 4 birds were selected from each heat-stressed pen and received intraperitoneal injection of 20 mg/kg body weight Mepazine (a Malt1 inhibitor) or the equivalent volume of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) every other day. When compared to PBS broilers, birds received Mepazine injection exhibited increased relative weight and higher villus height in jejunum (both P < 0.05). Mepazine treatment also increased (P < 0.05) the mRNA of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), claudin-1, and cadherin 1 of jejunum, which was companied by the reduced caspase-3 transcription under HS condition. Meanwhile, the gene expression levels of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), Malt1, NF-κB, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) in the jejunum were significantly downregulated by Mepazine administration (P < 0.05). Although there were no significant differences in the relative weight of the thymus and bursa, the transcription levels of T helper 1 (Th1)- and Th17-related cytokines were lower in thymus of birds injected with Mepazine. The cytokines of Treg cytokine transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and forkhead box protein P3 (Foxp3) in both the thymus and bursa were not influenced. These results suggest that inhibition of Malt1 protease activity can protect intestinal integrity by promoting the production of tight junction proteins and attenuating NF-κB-mediated intestinal inflammation response under HS conditions.

INTRODUCTION

The intestinal epithelium is composed of a single layer of cells that folds along its path through the gastrointestinal tract to generate villi and invaginations between villi (termed crypts), which are known to be susceptible to ambient stress (Slawinska et al., 2019). Exposure to high ambient temperatures can impair intestinal development, evidenced by shorter villi, increased crypt depth, or reduced ratio of villus to crypt length in poultry (Song et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2022a) and pigs (Pearce et al., 2013) and reduce nutrient absorptive capacity (Habashy et al., 2017). Increased gut permeability was also noticed in broilers subjected to chronic cyclic heat stress (HS) because of the downregulated expression of tight junction proteins (TJPs) such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and claudin-1 (Zhang et al., 2021). HS decreased the protein abundance of occludin and ZO-1 in the jejunum of broiler chickens when compared to thermoneutral birds (Song et al., 2014). Similarly, it was reported that the heat-stressed dairy cow exhibited lower protein levels of ZO-1 in the jejunum (Koch et al., 2019). In vitro studies have shown that heat directly downregulates the expression in protein and gene of occludin, ZO-1, claudin-6, and E-cadherin in intestinal epithelial cell-6 (IEC-6) (Huang et al., 2020). These deleterious effects of HS on the intestinal epithelium might be attributed to reduce intestinal blood flow causing intestinal ischemia, a key factor that leads to epithelial shedding (Rostagno, 2020). However, the complete mechanism by which HS alters the intestine has not been elucidated. Considering the fact that intestinal inflammation has been observed in bovines (Koch et al., 2019), pigs (Pearce et al., 2013), and broilers (Song et al., 2014; Santos et al., 2015) when exposed to mild or severe heat and its potential roles in intestinal dysfunction (Sejian et al., 2018), the inflammatory response may be responsible for part of the negative effects of HS on the intestinal epithelium. According to the review published by Robinson et al., the elevated gut interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) content was associated with an increased intestinal permeability by reducing occludin and ZO-1 protein levels (Robinson et al., 2015). Suppressing the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-17A could recover the ZO-1 protein level in the large intestinal tissues of mice upon the dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis (Lee et al., 2018).

Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) plays a key role in stress-induced inflammatory priming. When broilers were reared at 38°C ± 1°C for 6 h/d for 5 d, mRNA level of TLR-4 in the jejunum and ileum were increased (Varasteh et al., 2015). Activation of the TLR4 pathway may involve the secretion of heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) under heat challenge and/or microbial endotoxins infiltrating to the intestinal mucosa (Asea et al., 2000; Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010). Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), which acts downstream of TLR4 (Tang et al., 2021), can trigger the release of proinflammatory IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α to induce the occurrence of an inflammatory response (Kagan and Medzhitov, 2006). Of note, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation protein 1 (Malt1) plays an important role during NF-κB activation based on both its scaffold and protease functions. Upon activation, Malt1 serves as a scaffold in a complex with Carma1- and B-cell lymphoma/leukemia 10 (BCL10) and recruit the ubiquitin ligase tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6), which induces the activation of the I-kappa B kinase (IKK) complex leading to proteasomal degradation of IκB and translocation of the transcription factors to the nucleus (Afonina et al., 2015). Furthermore, through its protease activity, Malt1 also enhances the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway via specifically cleaning several negative regulators of NF-κB, including transcription factor RelB, A20, the ubiquitin ligase HOIL1 (Hailfinger et al., 2011; Klein et al., 2015). Although numerous studies on chickens have confirmed that HS caused increased mRNA and protein expression of NF-κB in the intestinal mucosa (Liu et al., 2016a) and liver (Liu et al., 2022b), the relationship between HS and Malt1 expression of gut is still unclear. Further, whether inhibition of Malt1 protease activity alleviates HS-induced intestinal damage also needs to be confirmed in broilers.

Therefore, this first experiment aimed to confirm that the expression of the Malt1 and NF-κB in jejunum of broilers in response to chronic cyclic HS is increased. In the second experiment, the Malt1 inhibitor Mepazine was used to address whether the suppression of NF-κB activation pathway mitigates HS-induced intestinal impairment and inflammatory response. This research may provide a novel insight into the molecular basis for intestinal injury and inflammation of broilers subjected to HS conditions, and more targeted interventions can be developed and applied in the poultry industry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and Management

Experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the care and use of animals and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Ghent University (approval No. 2022-044). Male Ross-308 male chickens were reared in floor pens (1.0 × 0.9 m). The temperature in the room was maintained at 32°C during the first 3 d and then gradually decreased to 22°C by 22 d. The continuous lighting schedule of 23 h light:1 h dark was maintained during 1 to 7 d, followed by 18 h light:6 h dark until the end of the trial. The birds vaccinated for Newcastle Disease and Infectious Bronchitis at d 1 of age and received the vaccination against Newcastle Disease at 18 d of age by spraying again. Trial Design

Experiment 1 was conducted to explore the expression of Malt1 and NF-κB signaling pathway of jejunum of broilers in response to HS. Birds were individually weighed (the average weight was 979 g) at 21 d, and then divided into the thermo-neutral group (TN) at constant 22°C or chronic cyclic HS group (HS). The temperature and humidity in the facilities were monitored automatically by the climate control system (Cygnus-V-5A, Hotraco Agri, the Netherlands), and accordingly temperature and humidity were adjusted following set values by heating, ventilation, and nebulization of water. Set values for the chronic cyclic HS group were 34°C for 7 h daily from 8:00 am to 15:00 pm with relative humility (RH) between 46 and 56% and the rest of day at 26°C. In addition, the temperature and humidity in facilities was recorded by Escort iLog loggers (Hohentegen, Germany) every 10 min. Each group had 7 replicates with 12 broilers per replicate. Experiment 2 aimed to determine whether inhibition Malt1 activity could attenuate HS-induced intestinal impairment and NF-κB mediated inflammatory response. On d 21, birds with mean body weight were of 982 g allocated to 8 pens and housed in the HS room. Each pen included 12 chickens. Birds with similar body weight were selected from each pen and injected 20 mg/kg body weight of Mepazine (Mepazine, 2 birds each pen) or the equal volumes of phosphorous buffer saline (PBS, 2 birds each pen) at 22, 24, 26, and 28 d of age by intraperitoneal injection. HS was carried out from 22 to 28 d as described above. The number of dead chickens in Mepazine and PBS groups was recorded from 22 to 28 d and the survival proportion was calculated.

Panting Frequency and Rectal Temperature

The impact of chronic cyclic HS in experiment 1 was evaluated through the panting frequency and rectal temperature of chickens as described in our previous study (Majdeddin et al., 2020). For the panting frequency, which was defined how many times of breaths per minute, over 1 min and at least 5 birds maintaining standstill was recorded using camera (Apple Inc., Cupertino, California), and the video was played with a computer at a very slow speed for clearly counting the respiratory rates. Moreover, 3 birds were randomly taken from each pen during the HS protocol. The painting frequency defined as the number of breaths per minute was determined on 22, 29, 34 d starting at least 4 h after inducing HS, based on video recordings. Rectal temperature was directly measured using a digital thermometer inserted to a minimum depth of 3 cm in the cloaca.

Data Collection and Sampling

In experiment 1, body weight by pen was recorded at 35 d. One bird with per pen with weight close to the average weight of the pen was sampled on d 35 (4 h after starting high temperature for birds from the HS room). After euthanasia using sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg of body weight) injected intravenously via the brachial vein, mid-jejunum mucosa was collected by scraping with glass slide, then snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored (−80°C) until analysis. For experiment 2, the birds that received Mepazine or PBS were weighed individually on 28 d, and average body weight was calculated on a per-pen basis. One bird per pen was subjected to sample collection after 4 h after starting high temperature. The bird was euthanized using sodium pentobarbital, and the thymus and bursa of Fabricius were excised and weighed immediately, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until the extraction of total RNA. The duodenum and jejunum were emptied of digestive content with saline solution. Afterward, their length and weight were determined using a flexible ruler and electronic balance, respectively. Segments of 1 cm length from the middle of jejunum and ileum were dissected and rapidly immersed in phosphate-buffered formaldehyde for histology analysis. The mid-jejunal mucosa (removing 1 cm right in the middle for histology analysis) was collected and stored at −80°C for gene expression determination.

Histomorphology of Intestine

The formaldehyde fixed intestinal samples were dehydrated by a series of gradients of alcohol and required xylene according to the standard paraffin-embedding procedure (Van Nevel et al., 2003). These samples were then embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-μm thick slices. Slices were conventionally stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The villus height and crypt depth were determined on 10 to 13 well-oriented villi per intestine segment using an Olympus BX61 microscope and image analysis software (Olympus, Aartselaar, Belgium). The corresponding average value of villus height and crypt depth, as well as the ratio of villus height to crypt depth for each bird were calculated.

Gene Expression Analysis

The expression of genes of tight junction protein, apoptosis, and inflammatory reaction was assessed by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from the jejunum, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius using the Trizol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, Overijse, Belgium). After quantifying, using the Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Merelbeke, Belgium), cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of RNA, combined in reaction with 4 μL of 5×PrimeScript Buffer, 1 μL of PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix, 1 μL of P Oligo dT primer, and 1 μL of Random 6 mers (Takara; Saint-Germain-en-Laye, France). The obtained cDNA was used to determine the mRNA expression of genes of interest by Lightcycler 480 II detection system (Roche), using Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, France). Each reaction consisted of 2.5 μL SYBR Green I Master, 2 μL of each primer and was made up to a 5 μL reaction using 0.5 μL cDNA. The cycling program was 95°C/15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C/5 s and 60°C/30 s, and a final melting curve analysis. Primers for RT-qPCR were designed using Primer3 and shown in Table 2. The presence of a single peak that was checked through melting curve analysis indicates that a single production was amplified by the associated primers. Statistical Analyses

All data are presented as mean and standard deviation. Normal distribution and homogeneity of variances were evaluated by the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene's test, respectively. An unpaired 2-tailed t test analysis was used to evaluate differences between TN and HS, or the Mepazine effect on PBS and Mepazine groups using JMP pro 13.0 (SAS Institute Inc.). DISCUSSION

Heat stress has been shown to impair gut integrity thereby increasing the translocation of bacteria and toxic compounds in pigs (Pearce et al., 2013), chickens (Zhang et al., 2021), and bovine (Koch et al., 2019), which elicits an inflammation of the gut. In turn, increased intestinal proinflammatory cytokines concentrations, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, were noticed to induce gut leak by reducing occludin and ZO-1 protein expression (Robinson et al., 2015). However, whether suppressing the gut inflammatory response directly caused by HS enhances intestinal integrity is unknown. In the present study, the main outcomes showed that inhibition of the activity of Malt1 could improve the disrupted jejunal barrier induced by thermal stress for broilers, and the Malt1/NF-κB activation is critical signaling cascades in this process of HS directly impairing gut integrity.

Birds suffering from HS have been noticed to be associated with impaired gut integrity, immune dysregulation, endocrine disorders, microbiota dysbiosis, etc., which result in poor performance (Majdeddin et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021). To accommodate heat dissipation, birds have to accelerate their respiratory frequency in the high temperature environment (Lara and Rostagno, 2013). This might induce alterations in blood acid-base status and integrity of muscle membrane to implicate meat quality in broilers (Sandercock et al., 2001). Analogously, the deleterious effects of HS were confirmed in this study by decreased body weight and higher respiratory rate. As an important digestive organ and the largest immune organ, gut development, and maturation is the basis of animal growth and health. Evidence showed that chronic HS (33°C ± 1°C for 10 h/d) decreased the relative weight of jejunum and the relative length of the ileum and jejunum in broilers (Wang et al., 2021). What's worse, HS was also confirmed to result in decreased villus height and villus height to crypt depth ratio in broilers (Zhang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022a). The reason why HS-induced intestinal damage might result from lower blood flow and ischemia in the gut. Under the circumstance of high ambient temperature, broilers are apt to increase peripheral blood flow to accelerate heat dissipation, which leads to blood redistribution and reduces the intestinal blood supply, thereby giving rise to gut ischemia-hypoxic injury (Rostagno, 2020). In this study, inhibition activity of Malt 1 using Mepazine injection increased in the weight and villus height, thereby elevating the ratio of villus height to crypt of jejunum under the HS condition, indicating that suppressing the activity of Malt1 might protect jejunum against damage induced by heat exposure in broilers. Similarly, mice receiving an intraperitoneal injection of MI-2 (30 mg/kg of body weight), a selective inhibitor of Malt1, showed reduced crypt damage and restored the structure of the colon induced by DSS (Lee et al., 2018). Some studies pointed out that the changes in morphology and structure were more obvious in the jejunum compared with the other segments of the gastrointestinal tract under HS, although these alterations could be observed in all segments of the small intestine (Yu et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2021). This may explain that the pronounced effects of Mepazine occurred in the jejunum but not in the duodenum in the current study. In addition, some alterations in body weight are expected based on the improvement in jejunum structure due to Mepazine treatment. However, we did not observe a more marked change in body weight of 28-day-old birds between PBS and Mepazine groups. This indicates that the changes in jejunal development are insufficient for the body growth of broilers under the thermal stress condition.

Focusing on jejunal TJPs variation, the negative effects of HS on intestinal TJPs have been demonstrated multiple times. For example, HS depressed the protein levels of ZO-1 in the jejunum of dairy cows (Koch et al., 2019), as well as the protein abundance of occludin and ZO-1 in the jejunum of chickens (Song et al., 2014). Such a downregulation in protein and gene expressions of occludin, ZO-1, claudin-6, and E-cadherin response to heat treatments was observed in IEC-6 (Huang et al., 2020). The inflammatory response may be responsible for part of the negative effects of HS on the intestinal epithelium. The elevated contents of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in intestine was noticed to be linked to an increased intestinal permeability by reducing occludin and ZO-1 protein levels (Robinson et al., 2015). The inhibition of inflammatory cytokines could normalize the ZO-1 protein level in the large intestinal tissues of DSS-treated mice (Lee et al., 2018). In the present study, HS significantly elevated the Malt1 and NF-κB mRNA expression and brought about intestinal inflammation in heat-stressed birds characterized by increased ratio of proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α to anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in jejunum. Accordingly, inhibition Malt1/NF-κB signaling might be a potential target to reverse the HS-induced intestinal impairment. It was reported that inhibited Malt1 activity using MI-2 treatment could recover the ZO-1 protein level in the large intestinal tissues of mice with DSS-induced colitis (Lee et al., 2018). In this study, gene expression analyses showed that Mepazine upregulated the transcription of ZO-1, claudin-1, and CDH, suggesting that Malt 1 inhibitors prevented the gut leak in broilers.

To understand whether some potential protective mechanisms were involved, genes related to intestinal protection and enterocytic apoptosis were studied. HSP70 is produced in response to HS and has a protective role for the intestinal barrier (Arnal and Lallès, 2016). GLP-2 produced in the intestine has been also shown to reduce gut permeability (Dong et al., 2014). The increased expression of GLP-2 after Mepazine treatment might partly explain the protective roles of Mepazine in intestinal integrity of jejunum in broilers under HS. Besides this, when animals are exposed to high temperatures, the limited blood flow could result in serious damage to villous epithelial cells and cause excessive apoptosis (Liu et al., 2009; Gao et al., 2013). Mechanistically, HS induces deposition of cytochrome c, increase in lysosomal membrane permeability, and/or reactive oxygen species-mediated the activation of lysosome mitochondrial apoptosis pathway (Yi et al., 2017), resulting in the overexpression of apoptosis-related genes, especially cathepsin B, caspase-9, and caspase-3. The increase of cathepsin B in the cytoplasm leads to caspase activation by degrading the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2. In addition, the released cytochrome c combined with the apoptotic protease activator 1 also induces the oligomerization and assembly of apoptotic bodies. Published data showed that the promoter caspase-9 and the apoptotic executive protein caspase-3 are sequential to cause the apoptosis of intestinal cells (Chen et al., 2021). In this study, Mepazine treatment tended to increase Bcl-2 transcription and decreased the mRNA levels of caspase-9 and caspase-3 in birds exposed to HS, indicating that Mepazine injection enhanced the barrier integrity of jejunum under heat load condition probably through inhibiting enterocytic apoptosis.

Under HS conditions, the increased HSP70 and microbial endotoxins infiltrating the intestinal mucosa may activate TLR pathways, triggering the release of cytokines and inflammatory reactions (Asea et al., 2000; Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010). TLR4 is thought to be the critical receptor that receives the various stress responses, including HS (Ju et al., 2014). A chronic cycle HS (38°C ± 1°C for 8 h/d for 5 d) stimulated the mRNA expression of TLR4 in the jejunum and ileum of chickens (Varasteh et al., 2015). Pigs under constant 35°C also displayed higher transcription levels of TLR4 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells on 1 and 7 d (Ju et al., 2014). Activation of TLR4 can initiate an intracellular signaling cascade to activate the transcription factor NF-κB (Kawai and Akira, 2010), which causes the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines. In the present study, treatment of heat-stressed birds with Mepazine attenuated the proinflammatory cytokine infiltration in jejunum, evidenced by the reduction in the mRNA levels of TRL4, NF-κB, IL-6, and TNF-α as compared to PBS birds. It was also reported that inhibiting Malt1 activity using MI-2 or Mepazine can reduce the activation of NF-κB signaling thus reducing the secretion of IL-1, IL-6, IL-18, IL-17a, and IFN-γ in DSS-induced mice colitis (Liu et al., 2016b). Moreover, HS-mediated activation of inflammatory pathways also involves alterations in immune tissues. Outcomes from our recent research showed that HS (34°C for 7 h/d for 18 d) significantly decreased the relative weight of the thymus but not the spleen and bursa of broilers (Zhang et al., 2021), whereas the decreased relative weight of the bursa in broilers that were exposed to 31°C (10 h/d for 7 d) was noticed (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010). More dramatically, broilers subjected to 36°C (10 h/d for 7 d) presented more remarkably reduced spleen weights in addition to that of the thymus and bursa (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010). The Mepazine injection did not alter the relative weight of the thymus and bursa in the current study. This discrepant effect of HS on the weight of immune organs might be attributed to the severity and duration of heat treatment. The comparable weight of the thymus and bursa between PBS and Mepazine group implies that the inhibition by Malt1 did not change the development of lymphoid organs. In addition, the biological roles of Th-related cytokine in inflammatory response through secreting various cytokines and regulating other types of immune cells have been investigated (Dong, 2021). Previous research has shown experimental colitis induces a strong Th1 and Th17 response in mice against DSS injection, and Malt 1 inhibitors suppressed the protein and mRNA levels of DSS-induced proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, IL-17a, and IFN-γ in the colon (Liu et al., 2016b). Consistently, the decreased mRNA of the Th1-related cytokines (IFN-γ and TNF-α), the Th17-related cytokines (IL-17a and IL-22) in the thymus, and the IL-17a in bursa was noticed in the current study. These data further confirm that inhibition of Malt1 could suppress Th1 and Th17 effector responses.

Importantly, it should be mentioned that the response of Treg cells to Malt1 deficiency (Malt1KO) and inhibition of Malt1 activity is different. Previous studies evidenced that the complete absence of Malt1 in knockout mice leads to a severe defect in the development of Tregs (Mc Guire et al., 2013), which exerts an important role in maintaining immune homeostasis (Vignali et al., 2008). A decrease in Tregs would be disadvantageous in any therapeutic setting that aims to inhibit Malt1/NF-κB-mediated pathological change (Gilis et al., 2019). However, inhibiting the activity of Malt1 using Mepazine has barely an influence on the frequency of Foxp3+ Tregs (Mc Guire et al., 2014). Coherently, this research found that the Malt1 protease inhibitor Mepazine did not affect Treg development in both the thymus and bursa, instructed by similar Foxp3 and TGF-β mRNA expression. This suggested that inhibition of Malt1 proteolytic activity, leaving its scaffolding function intact, does not affect the development of Tregs of broilers in this study. Moreover, a main limitation in the present study is there was a lack of comparison regarding the effects of the silence Malt1 gene and inhibition of Malt1 activity on Tregs development in heat-stressed broilers.

In conclusion, the present study showed that a chronic cyclic HS reduced body weight of broilers and activated Malt1/NF-κB signaling pathways to elicit the inflammatory cytokine response in jejunum. Administration of Mepazine, a Malt1 inhibitor, could decrease the apoptosis of enterocytes, improve jejunal tight junctions, and attenuate intestinal inflammatory injury by targeting Malt1/NF-κB pathway. Moreover, Mepazine suppressed Th1 and Th17 differentiation, along with similar Treg development. The findings of the study can enhance our understanding of the mechanism underlying HS-induced gut impairment and provide a basis for exploring new strategies for combating the negative consequences of HS in domestic birds.
