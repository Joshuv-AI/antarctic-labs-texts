Sustainable cooling strategies for workers in the ready-made garment factory industry in Bangladesh under simulated extreme heat: a randomised crossover trial
Authors: James W Smallcombe, Ruindu N Ranhotty, Aaron JE Bach, Jean P Palutikof, Fahim N Tonmoy, Ashikur R Joarder, Monir Hossain, Nathan B Morris, Anthony Capon, Yorgi Mavros, Ollie Jay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBangladesh frequently experiences extreme heat and humidity, which threatens the health, wellbeing, and productivity of ready-made garment workers. Scalable, sustainable, and low-cost cooling strategies are urgently needed to protect this workforce in low-income and middle-income countries. Therefore, we examined the effects of building-level and personal-level cooling alternatives to air conditioning on worker heat strain in a simulated Bangladesh ready-made garment factory.MethodsIn a randomised cross over trial (ACTRN12622000457741), healthy participants (aged 18–40 years) recruited from a convenience sample of volunteers from diverse ethnic backgrounds completed six 3-h gender-specific ready-made garment work simulations (males ironed and females sewed) in a climate chamber at the University of Sydney: (1) current factory (control [CON]=40°C, 38% relative humidity); (2) CON with fan (FAN); (3) FAN with drinking water (FAN+HYD); (4) modified rooftop (ROOF=37·5°C, 38% relative humidity); (5) ROOF with fan (ROOF+FAN); and (6) air conditioning (AC=24°C, 40% relative humidity). Primary outcomes were end-trial core temperature, heart rate, and sweat loss fluid deficit. Linear mixed models with fixed effects of intervention and sex and random effects of participant were used for analysis. Pre-planned contrasts compared each intervention to CON, with Dunnett’s correction for multiple testing.FindingsBetween March 16, 2022, and Nov 25, 2023, 42 participants (20 identifying as females, 22 as males) completed 247 trials. Compared to CON, male core temperature decreased with FAN+HYD (–0·27°C [99% CI –0·44 to –0·09]), ROOF (–0·35°C [–0·53 to –0·17]), ROOF+FAN (–0·28°C [–0·45 to –0·10]), and AC (–0·71°C [–0·89 to –0·53]), but in females, core temperature decreased only with AC (–0·42°C [–0·60 to –0·24]). Male heart rate was lower with FAN (–6 beats per minute [–12 to 0]), FAN+HYD (–13 beats per minute [–19 to –7]), ROOF (–8 beats per minute [–14 to –2]), ROOF+FAN (–11 beats per minute [–17 to –5]), and AC (–34 beats per minute [–41 to –28]), and female heart rate was lower with FAN+HYD (–7 beats per minute [–13 to 0]), ROOF (–8 beats per minute [–15 to –2]), ROOF+FAN (–10 beats per minute [–16 to –4]), and AC (–25 beats per minute [–31 to –18]). Male sweat loss fluid deficit was reduced with FAN+HYD (–704 g [–817 to –591]), ROOF (–205 g [–317 to –93]), and AC (–853 g [–966 to –741]), and in females with FAN+HYD (–439 g [–554 to –324]), ROOF (–131g [–246 to –16]), and AC (–513g [–628 to –398]).InterpretationSustainable cooling interventions can reduce physiological heat strain under peak heat stress conditions in a typical non-airconditioned Bangladeshi ready-made garment factory. Cooling benefits were greater in males, highlighting potential gender-based workplace heat stress inequalities.FundingWellcome Trust.

Introduction

Bangladesh is a lower-middle-income country with a tropical monsoon climate characterised by high ambient temperatures (>35°C) and frequent stifling humidity. Although this climate already poses substantial societal challenges including increased risks of heat-related illness, reduced labour productivity, pressure on energy and water resources, and disproportionate impacts on disadvantaged populations, Bangladesh has been identified as a country highly vulnerable to the impacts of global climate change, most notably due to the threat of future extreme heat and humidity.

The Bangladesh economy relies heavily on the ready-made garment industry, which employs 4 million people across approximately 5000 factories nationwide and accounts for approximately 15% of gross domestic product, and more than 80% of domestic exports.1 Women comprise approximately 60% of the ready-made garment workforce, which consists primarily of people from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds.2 Although some factories, such as Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design-certified green factories, offer better working conditions, many ready-made garment factories still present challenging environments. Many workers face output-based work quotas necessitating long working hours, often in excessively hot and humid environments.3,4 Additionally, limited access to potable drinking water and infrequent rest breaks further exacerbate heat stress for workers.5

We equipped a typical three-story ready-made garment factory employing more than 2000 workers in Dhaka, Bangladesh, with 33 temperature and humidity sensors to profile the indoor thermal environment for more than a year.4 Peak indoor temperatures exceeded 35°C on 37% of days and during the hottest months (ie, April to June), occasionally exceeded 40°C. Building modelling simulations were based on the Representative Concentration Pathway 8.5 climate scenario estimate6 that the working hours exceeding 35°C will double by 2050.4

In hot conditions, the capacity to perform physical work declines, leading to reduced productivity.7,8 Workers must subsequently work longer hours to receive the same pay or accept lower income. Highly motivated workers might maintain high work rates despite the heat but suffer an increased risk of illness and injury from elevated thermal, cardiovascular, and/or renal strain.9,10

The urgent need for effective heat stress mitigation in ready-made garment factories is evident. Although air conditioning provides highly effective cooling,11 its implementation in large ready-made garment factories is often deemed economically unfeasible due to high capital and operational costs. Additionally, the Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association has pledged to reduce the sector’s greenhouse gas emissions by 30% by 2030, and the carbon footprint of air conditioning is well documented. Therefore, identifying optimal, low-carbon-cooling solutions that do not rely on air conditioning is essential. Low-cost and sustainable cooling opportunities exist at both the building and personal level of the heat cascade.10 Different factory roof types, including green (ie, a layer of vegetation) and insulated reflective white rooftops reduce indoor factory temperatures by 2–3°C.4 But, how these temperature differences translate to reductions in physiological heat strain and improvements in thermal comfort and work performance remains unknown. Personal-level strategies (eg, personal hydration regimes and electric fan use) can potentially cool the body further but should not interfere with the otherwise optimal completion of work tasks or exacerbate other health risks.10 The efficacy of fan use in hot conditions is potentially dependent on the avoidance of dehydration.12 Employers are often reluctant to permit drink breaks due to concerns about lost productivity;13 it is possible though that these losses can be reclaimed by mitigating heat-related losses in work output while reducing physiological strain and discomfort. Before solutions are implemented and tested at scale in readymade garment factories, which requires substantial logistical and financial commitments and potentially excessive occupational disruptions, optimal candidate solutions can be identified using a laboratory-based approach. Evidence of the efficacy of potential solutions is likely to be an important factor in persuading factory management to support in-factory testing and subsequent implementation.

This study was conducted in a climate-controlled chamber which provided very precise control of temperature and humidity to replicate peak heat stress conditions previously recorded in a typical Bangladesh ready-made garment factory4 and assessed the impact of building-level and personal-level cooling interventions on workers’ physiological heat strain, thermal discomfort, and productivity. We hypothesised that when factory roof modifications (eg, installing a green or insulated white roof) are feasible, the resulting indoor temperature reductions would be most effective in improving physiological heat strain, discomfort, and productivity when combined with increased air movement from electric fans. In cases for which roof modifications are not an option, we hypothesised that the greatest benefits would be achieved by combining electric fan use with free access to drinking water while working.

Methods

Study design and participants

This randomised cross over trial was conducted in a climate chamber in the Heat and Health Research Centre at the University of Sydney (Sydney, NSW, Australia). Participants were recruited from the general population through advertisements in the community, word of mouth, and social media. Males and females aged 18 to 40 years and not current smokers were eligible. Participants with a history of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, or metabolic disorders, or heat-related illness were excluded. All participants completed a Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire and an American Heart Association Screening Questionnaire and were informed of all potential risks before providing written informed consent. The sex and racial or ethnic categories of each participant were self-nominated on the consent form. The options for self-reporting sex were female, male, intersex or another variation, or prefer not to say. The options for self-reporting race or ethnicity were: American Indian or Alaska Native Asian, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, White, other (please specify), or prefer not to say. Participants self-reporting to be pregnant on the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire form were ineligible.

Participation in the study involved three heat and work adaptation trials followed by six main trials, on separate days. The adaptation trials consisted of a 1-h work task (appendix pp 3–4) in standardised clothing (appendix p 5) on consecutive days in 40°C with 38% relative humidity. These trials were designed to familiarise participants to the high levels of heat and humidity, clothing, and work task simulated in the main experimental trials to help induce some level of heat acclimation before commencing the main trials. Participants completed six 3-h trials in a climate chamber on separate days, always at the same time of day. Depending on scheduling constraints, sessions were spaced 48 h or 72 h apart. The experimental trials were: (1) no intervention at 40°C and 38% relative humidity (CON); (2) electric fan use at 40°C and 38% relative humidity (FAN); (3) electric fan use with free access to drinking water at 40°C and 38% relative humidity (FAN+HYD); (4) the equivalent indoor temperature reduction due to a modified roof at 37·5°C and 40% relative humidity (ROOF); (5) electric fan use at 37·5°C and 40% relative humidity (ROOF+FAN); and (6) the equivalent of an air-conditioned environment at 24°C and 40% relative humidity (AC).

These conditions were selected as follows: 40°C and 38% relative humidity representing the peak conditions during the hottest week in an ready-made garment factory in Bangladesh;4 and 37·5°C and 40% relative humidity, reflecting a simulated reduction of up to 2·5°C in peak daytime indoor temperatures during operational hours on the factory’s top floor, derived from a validated building energy model using year-long multi-point sensor data, following simulated rooftop retrofitting with green or insulated white roofing—simulations were derived using DesignBuilder (version 7.01.006) and EnergyPlus software (version 9.4.0).4

The study was approved by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (2021/583) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A complete protocol document is provided in the appendix (pp 22–37).

Randomisation and masking

A balanced Latin square was used to counterbalance the treatment order of the six main trials for each participant. A team member (JWS) randomly allocated participant identification numbers (eg, 1–20) into two sex-specific blocks using Research Randomizer, with the trial order table stored on a password-protected computer. Additional slots were created for dropouts. Participants were unaware of the environmental conditions on each trial day, making the building-level intervention single-masked. Interventions (ie, fan use and drinking water availability) were visible and could not be masked.

Procedures

All ambient conditions were controlled within SD 0·1°C with SD 1% relative humidity in a climate chamber (Steridium, Brisbane, QLD, Australia). In the two fan trials, air speed was fixed at 1·5 m·s−1 using a 45 cm diameter pedestal fan (Ventair PED45, Carrum Downs, VIC, Australia) placed 1·25 m in front of the participant. This wind speed was selected to strike a balance between promoting additional sweat evaporation and enhancing thermal comfort, while minimising potential disruption to the factory work tasks due to excessive air turbulence. In the trial with drinking water access, 250 mL of room-temperature tap water was provided in a plastic bottle every 30 min, available for ad libitum consumption over the following 30 min.

Core temperature was measured continuously via a tele-metric pill (BodyCap e-Celsius, Caen, France) ingested six h before each experimental trial. Heart rate was monitored continuously via a chest-worn telemetric sensor (PE4000, Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). Sweat loss fluid deficit was determined via triplicate measurements of pre-trial and post-trial nude body mass using a high-precision digital scale (Mettler 1D1 Multirange, Greifensee, Switzerland) after accounting for any fluid intake. Visual analogue scales (0–100 mm) were used to assess thermal discomfort (from not uncomfortable to very uncomfortable) and thermal sensation (from cold to hot) at baseline and then at 30-min intervals throughout each trial. Participants were instructed to indicate their perceived thermal discomfort and thermal sensation by manually marking a line with a pen on separate 100 mm horizontal scales printed on paper—perceptual ratings were then determined by measuring the point at which the mark had been made (measured in mm). At the same timepoints, thirst was evaluated using three 100 mm visual analogue scales assessing three questions rating thirstiness, mouth dryness, and anticipated pleasantness of a drink, which were adapted from the work of Rolls and colleagues.14 Scores were then aggregated to provide a thirst score out of 300 at each timepoint.

Work performance was assessed by simulating the two core factory tasks observed in the ready-made garment factory sector4 of ironing (males) and sewing (females) and designed to mimic the physical and cognitive demands of each task (appendix pp 3–4). Briefly, male participants stood and ironed a series of 1 × 1 m canvas sheets following a pattern using a handheld iron (which, for safety reasons, was not turned on and therefore did not generate extra heat). A set of eight uniquely patterned garments were ironed, folded, and stacked throughout each trial. Female participants sewed by tracing with a clamped retractable marker pen (Sharpie, 1926876) a set of 16 unique line patterns (0·5 point thickness) while seated at a custom-built experimental rig designed to resemble a standard sewing machine. These work tasks were performed for the duration of each trial. Work performance was quantified using the total number of garments ironed (males) or sewn (females) in each trial, while for the sewing activity, the number of errors (defined as visible deviation completely from the pattern line) and the distance sewn was also measured.

Standardised clothing (appendix p 5) typical of work attire in Bangladesh was worn. Females dressed in traditional Shalwar Kameez with an accompanying headscarf (100% cotton and clothing insulation of approximately 0·7 clo),15 whereas males wore full-length chino pants and a short-sleeved open collar shirt (100% cotton and clothing insulation of approximately 0·5 clo).16

For 12 h before each trial, participants refrained from strenuous exercise, and ingesting caffeine17 or alcohol. Each participant completed all their trials at the same time of day. Upon arrival at the laboratory, pre-trial euhydration was verified by a urine specific gravity measurement of less than 1·025.18 If urine specific gravity exceeded this threshold, participants consumed 5 mL·kg−1 of room temperature water 30–60 min before entering the climate chamber. After dressing and instrumentation in a 23°C room, participants entered the climate chamber set at the appropriate temperature and humidity and undertook 10 min of seated rest before completing five 30-min simulated work bouts, each separated by 2 min of stepping (90 steps per min) to simulate general factory floor movement (appendix p 6). Participants could withdraw any time, and trials were terminated immediately due to these safety criteria: heart rate of less than 50 beats per minute or more than 80% of age-predicted maximum, core temperature greater than 39·0°C. Participants were provided with a refrigerated electrolyte-replacement drink upon exiting the chamber and had the option to take a cool shower before leaving the laboratory.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were the change in core temperature from baseline (final 5-min average of 10-min seated rest) to end-trial (average of last 10 min), end-trial heart rate (average of last 10 min), and end-trial sweat loss fluid deficit from baseline. These were the primary outcomes since they represent the triad of physiological heat strain that serve as leading indicators of heat-related illness risk.9 Secondary outcomes were work performance—defined as the number of ironed garments completed for males, and the distance sewn divided by the number of errors for females with the data expressed as Z scores from the grand mean within each sex, and mean thermal discomfort, thermal sensation, and thirst during each trial.

A high-level indicative cost–benefit assessment of each cooling strategy tested was conducted and was informed by peer-reviewed and grey literature, publicly available cost data, international reports, and field data, including stake-holder interviews, collected over 12 months in Dhaka-based ready-made garment factories. Interventions were evaluated across five categories—cost, benefit, environmental impact, drawbacks, and overall feasibility—based on real-world constraints. When cost or performance estimates varied, conservative and broadly applicable values were used.

Statistical analysis

Using G*Power 3 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düssel-dorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), an a priori power calculation was performed for the primary outcome of change in rectal temperature when comparing fan use versus control, which was anticipated to have the smallest effect size. An effect size of 0·8 was used based on a fan effect of a 20% difference relative to control, with a SD of the difference between trials of 25%.19 Assuming an α of 0·05 and a power of 90%, a minimum sample size of 40 participants stratified equally (n=20) between males and females was required to detect a sex-based interaction.

A full description of the statistical analysis plan is provided in the appendix (pp 38–45). Each primary outcome was analysed using a linear mixed model with intervention (six levels: CON, FAN, FAN+HYD, ROOF, ROOF+FAN, and AC) and sex (two levels: male and female) as fixed effects, and participant as a random effect for repeated measures. Pre-planned contrasts compared the five interventions to CON, with Dunnett’s method for multiple testing correction. Point estimates are provided with 99% CIs. Contrasts were performed within each sex group if a significant intervention × sex interaction was found. Secondary outcomes were analysed using the same linear mixed models and contrasts, with Dunnett’s method for multiple testing correction. Point estimates are presented with 95% CIs. As the study was not powered to detect sex-based interactions for secondary variables, this analysis was exploratory. Residual diagnostic plots were inspected for constant variance and normality of residuals (appendix pp 8–12). All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.3). The linear mixed models were fitted with the lme4 package, with model output extracted using the broom.mixed and sjPlot packages. To check model assumptions, residual diagnostic plots were generated using the ggResidpanel package with the estimated marginal means and pairwise comparisons made using the emmeans package. Model fit statistics were assessed using the lmerTest and car packages. Sensitivity analyses confirmed model robustness for primary and secondary outcomes. Sensitivity analyses were applied to the final models to avoid overfitting and included period (six levels: intervention order), season (four levels: spring, summer, autumn, and winter), and previous intervention (seven levels: including no-previous) as additional variables to test for order, seasonal, and carry-over effects. Parameter estimates from these models were compared with those from the final models, by examining absolute and relative percentage change, the direction of the effect and significance of associated p values (<0·05). The code used for all statistical analyses is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The study was registered prospectively on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12622000457741).

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Between March 30, 2022, and Nov 25, 2023, 53 participants were screened for eligibility, of which 49 (92%) were enrolled in the study. Six (11%) participants withdrew during familiarisation, one (2%) withdrew during the first trial and so did not provide any usable data, and two (4%) withdrew after completing some but not all experimental trials. Data from 42 participants were analysed (ie, 40 complete and 2 incomplete data sets), totalling 247 main trials. Figure 1 shows the CONSORT flow diagram of participant progression through the trial. Data collection was completed on Dec 20, 2023. No adverse events occurred in this study. Participant characteristics are shown in the appendix (p 7).

Discussion

This randomised crossover trial systematically evaluated the effects of scalable, low-resource cooling strategies on physiological and perceptual heat strain, as well as estimated work performance, in males (ironing) and females (sewing) exposed to peak conditions (ie, 40°C and 38% humidity) previously documented in Bangladesh’s ready-made garment sector.4 Among the strategies requiring no structural modifications, brief ad libitum drinking water breaks combined with electric fan use lowered core temperature and heart rate, reduced dehydration, and improved thermal comfort—all without compromising productivity. The estimated 2·5°C reduction in indoor temperature (from 40°C to 37·5°C) with the installation of an insulated white roof reduced core temperature, heart rate, and dehydration while improving thermal comfort and sensation. Fan use in these conditions further enhanced thermal comfort and sensation but did not reduce physiological strain.

The cooling effects of all interventions were more pronounced in males than females, highlighting potential gender-based workplace inequalities20 resulting from a combination of different workplace task requirements (ie, ironing vs sewing) and metabolic demands, cultural norms around clothing, and potential sex-based differences in thermal perception. These differences could be partly biological, as females have lower sweating rates than males.21 However, greater clothing coverage (including a full head-scarf) and differences in working postures and intensity of physical activity—eg, sitting at a bench for sewing versus standing for ironing—could likely have also reduced the effectiveness of increased air movement from fans or indoor temperature reductions from roof modifications. In the control condition though, females exhibited a 0·3°C smaller rise in core temperature and a 13 beats per minute lower heart rate than males, indicating a lower absolute physiological heat strain to mitigate due to the different intensities of the gender-specific work tasks. The benefits of each cooling strategy can be better understood by comparing their effects to the reductions achieved with air conditioning relative to the no-cooling control condition. In females, core temperature reductions reached up to 32% (ROOF) of the AC effect, whereas in males, reductions were as high as 49% (ROOF). For heart rate, reductions relative to AC were 40% (ROOF+FAN) in males and 37% (FAN+HYD) in females.

Unlike findings in resting young adults19 (ie, those aged 18–40 years) and older adults (ie, those aged >60 years) with and without heart disease22 at 40°C or lower with high humidity, fan use alone did not lower core temperature and only reduced heart rate in males. At ambient temperatures exceeding skin temperature (ie, approximately 35°C), the effectiveness of fans depends on increasing sweat evaporation beyond the additional sensible heat absorbed through convection.10 The humidity level (40% relative humidity) tested might have only impaired sweat evaporation modestly, limiting the potential benefits of increased airflow. Nevertheless, fans still improved thermal comfort, and no adverse effects were observed, supporting revisions to WHO’s recommended safety limits for fan use.23

Benefits of fan use were observed with parallel ad libitum hydration with reductions in thermal and cardiovascular heat strain broadly equivalent to the benefits observed with a 2·5°C reduction in air temperature with a change in factory roofing. Whether these benefits can be singularly attributed to the maintenance of euhydration is unclear as a hydration-only intervention was not tested. End-trial dehydration from sweating in the control condition was equivalent to 1% (in females) and 1·5% (in males) deficit in total body mass, which is sufficient to cause greater elevations in core temperature and heart rate during intermittent work.24 Avoiding such dehydration prevents moderate sweating impairments and maximises the likelihood of evaporative benefits with fan use.

The estimated 2·5°C reduction in indoor temperature with the modification of the factory rooftop (ie, installation of a green or insulated white roof) reduced physiological and perceptual heat strain. Using electric fans in parallel did not amplify these benefits but did further improve thermal comfort and sensation, which has important implications for general worker welfare. Similar reductions in indoor temperature (range –1·0 to –4·3°C) have been reported following roof modifications in other South Asian settings, including schools25 and urban slums26 in India and tropical residential settings in Sri Lanka.27 Our findings highlight the potential advantages of retrofitting existing factories with cool roofs, and the clear benefits of incorporating cool roof designs into future building projects. Employers are often reluctant to permit pauses for cooling and/or drinking because of potential disruptions to work productivity. However, heat-related reductions in work output are well documented,7,28,29 and we observed a 13% reduction in work performance in the no cooling (control) condition compared with air conditioning. None of the cooling strategies tested incurred any reductions in work performance, and approximately half of heat-related productivity losses observed in ironing (ie, the number of garments completed) were reclaimed in the ROOF and ROOF+FAN trials.

A high-level indicative cost–benefit summary of each cooling strategy tested is given in table 2. A green roof incurs substantially higher installation, operational, and maintenance costs than a reflective white roof, but potentially carries more positive environmental impacts by having a lower albedo, enhancing biodiversity, and serving as a local absorbent of CO2. Electric fans are comparatively easy to retrofit and cost-efficient and energy-efficient but cannot be used in settings with wind-sensitive tasks so site-specific assessments must ensure airflow speed and direction are compatible with factory activities.10 Providing clean drinking water faces several barriers to implementation. Reusable bottles and many drinking stations are required to minimise queuing times in factories with high staff numbers (up to 1000 per floor). Alternatively, factory management could opt to deliver drinking water to employees at regular intervals throughout the day. Female workers can be reluctant to drink water, despite heat stress, due to concerns about unhygienic or absent female toilet facilities.30 Factories must therefore be equipped with suitable toilet facilities for both genders and promote a culture of healthy water consumption.

Our study has several limitations. Participants were recruited from Sydney, Australia, and likely less acclimatised to heat than workers in Bangladesh. However, most trials occurred in the Australian summer, providing some acclimatisation,31 and participants also completed three preliminary adaptation trials to acclimatise to the conditions. The crossover trial design additionally ensured participants served as their own control, and any potential difference in heat acclimatisation status between our participant cohort and the worker population in Bangladesh is unlikely to have altered the observed effects of each intervention on the primary outcomes. Only healthy participants aged 18–40 years were included. Although this aligns with Bangladesh’s ready-made garment workforce (of whom approximately 70% are younger than 29 years),2 it does not reflect global ageing trends. Some ready-made garment workers also have pre-existing health conditions,13 making them more vulnerable to heat stress. However, this is unlikely to affect the potential efficacy of cooling interventions; indeed, fan use can provide effective cooling even in older adults (ie, those older than 60 years) with heart disease.22 Our study design did not allow for the independent assessment of drinking water at 40°C without fan use or the combined effects of roof cooling, fan use, and water drinking. Given the benefits of water drinking with fan use at 40°C, adding water drinking to the ROOF+FAN condition could further enhance physiological and perceptual responses. However, further research is needed to confirm this potential synergistic effect. Additionally, during the electric fan trials, only a wind speed of 1·5 m·s−1 was tested, therefore, whether or not the physiological and perceptual effects of this intervention would have been modified by increasing or decreasing the rate of air flow is unknown. The tested interventions were co-designed with factory owners for factory-wide application, targeting all workers. As a result, other potentially effective cooling strategies, such as skin wetting22 or a cooling oasis32 were not tested. Furthermore, although sewing and ironing represent the most common factory tasks the efficacy of personal-level interventions such as fans might not apply to workers that are more mobile. Finally, although male participants used a standard hand-held iron during their work task simulation, for safety reasons, the iron was not turned on and, therefore, did not generate extra heat or generate steam, which workers in ready-made garment factories likely encounter. Although we optimised our approach to ensure that the findings would be applicable to the ready-made garment industry in Bangladesh, further research is needed to validate these results in real-world settings.

We show that sustainable cooling interventions at both the building and personal levels can reduce physiological strain, enhance thermal comfort, and maintain worker productivity under peak heat stress conditions observed in a typical Bangladeshi ready-made garment factory. When roof modifications are feasible, the resulting 2·5°C indoor temperature reduction lowers physiological and perceptual heat strain while improving thermal comfort and sensation. Fan use in these conditions further enhances comfort and thermal sensation. The most effective personal-level intervention, in the absence of indoor temperature reductions via roof modification, was electric fan use combined with free access to drinking water. Short work breaks for water intake do not impair—and might even modestly improve—work performance.

Supplementary Material

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The ready-made garment industry is vital to South Asia’s economy; however, factory workers face extreme heat and humidity during working hours. In 2021, our team assessed heat stress in a Dhaka ready-made garment factory, where factory indoor temperatures hit 40°C with 38% humidity. These conditions endanger workers, raising the risk of heat-related illnesses and reducing productivity. We searched PubMed and the Cochrane Library for reviews on sustainable cooling strategies for occupational heat strain published from database inception to March 5, 2025. Only articles written in English were included for review. A 2020 umbrella review synthesising 36 articles highlighted the effectiveness of air conditioning but noted its high cost and emissions. It also assessed personal cooling strategies, many of which show promise but remain untested in a low-middle income country ready-made garment factory setting. Emerging research suggests that building modifications such as reflective rooftops could reduce indoor temperatures, however whether these temperature reductions are sufficient to alleviate physiological strain now and in a warmer future while maintaining productivity remains unknown.

Added value of this study

Using a state-of-the-art climate chamber, we tested sustainable cooling interventions for ready-made garment factory workers under conditions replicating the peak heat stress conditions measured in a Dhaka ready-made garment factory. Targeting both building-level and personal-level heat mitigation, we found significant benefits. When factory roof modifications are feasible, a 2·5°C reduction in indoor temperatures from installing an insulated white roof or green roof, both with and without fan use (1·5 m·s−1 air speed), significantly decreased thermal and cardiovascular strain, enhanced comfort, and preserved work productivity. In cases for which roof modifications are not an option, electric fan use combined with free access to drinking water lowered core temperature and heart rate, reduced dehydration, and improved thermal comfort. The brief work breaks to drink water did not impair work productivity. Across all building-level and personal-level interventions, benefits were less pronounced in females compared with males, highlighting further potential gender-based workplace inequalities in hot climates.

Implications of all the available evidence

There is an urgent need for scalable, sustainable, low-cost cooling strategies to protect ready-made garment factory workers in low-income and middle-income countries, such as Bangladesh. Our findings show that, in a controlled laboratory simulation reflecting the conditions experienced by workers in Bangladesh’s ready-made garment sector, both building-level and personal-level interventions can effectively reduce physiological and perceptual heat strain when energy-intensive air conditioning is unavailable. These robust findings can be tested in real-world settings and can guide the retrofitting of existing factories or the design of future heat-resilient buildings and implementation of sustainable personal cooling strategies. Our evidence supports policies that enhance occupational health standards and promote sustainable solutions in the ready-made garment sector, which employs more than 60 million workers across the south Asia region.
