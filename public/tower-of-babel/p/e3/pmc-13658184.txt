Urinary caffeine and caffeine metabolites, biological aging, and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: deciphering association, mediation, and mechanism via epidemiology and bioinformatics
Authors: Jie Liu, Wenshan Shi, Shuhui Wan, Da Shi, Jiahao Song, Le Hong, Feifei Wang, Yani Xiong, Wendi Shi, Qing Liu, Zhiying Huo, Jinqiang Li, Pengfei Chen, Yongfang Zhang, Jiamin Xu, Weihong Chen, Bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssociations of caffeine (the most routinely consumed bioactive compound worldwide) and caffeine metabolites (CAF/CAFMs) with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome (a newly-introduced high-burden clinical condition) and role of biological aging and underlying mechanisms are uncharted territories warranting pressing decryption. Among 3020 adults, 15 urinary CAF/CAFMs were determined to investigate their single-analyte (by weighted logistic regression) and mixture (by Bayesian kernel machine regression [BKMR] and weighted quantile sum [WQS]) relationships between advanced CKM syndrome. Role of emerging biological aging metric—Gompertz-Law-Based Biological Age Difference (GOLD BioAgeDiff)—was explored by mediation analysis. Underlying mechanisms were explored by network pharmacology. Specific CAF/CAFMs (1-methylxanthine, theophylline, paraxanthine, and theobromine) were negatively linked to advanced CKM syndrome (P and false discovery rate < 0.05). WQS and BKMR uncovered negative CAF/CAFMs mixture-advanced CKM syndrome association (odds ratio = 0.782; 95% confidential interval: 0.670, 0.912). Exploratorily, GOLD BioAgeDiff mediated 13.85–20.06% of the CAF/CAFMs-advanced CKM syndrome associations. Metabolic, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt), and apoptosis signaling pathways were exploratorily enriched in the CAF/CAFMs-CKM syndrome relationships. Overall, several CAF/CAFMs and, especially, their mixture showed protective associations with CKM syndrome, which was exploratorily mediated by delayed biological aging, while metabolic, MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and apoptosis signaling pathways might be involved in the underlying mechanisms. Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

As an emerging complex clinical disorder, cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is distinguished by the close interconnection and mutual influence among factors spanning the cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic domains1. As emphasized in the Global Burden of Disease 2021 report, the CKM syndrome imposes a substantial and growing global health and economic burden2. Concurrently, given the complex, multi-system nature of CKM syndrome and the severe burden which leads to significant disability and mortality3, identifying modifiable environmental and lifestyle factors is crucial and priority for developing effective prevention strategies.

Caffeine, predominantly obtained from coffee, tea, and soft drinks, is a globally consumed bioactive compound with a profound cultural significance in numerous dietary traditions4. Consequently, accurately evaluating the health effects of caffeine intake/exposure is a major public health concern. Following consumption, caffeine undergoes hepatic biotransformation primarily facilitated by the cytochrome P450 enzyme system. This process generates a lot of bioactive caffeine metabolites (for instance, theophylline [1,3-dimethylxanthine, 1,3-DMX], theobromine [3,7-dimethylxanthine, 3,7-DMX], and paraxanthine [1,7-dimethylxanthine, 1,7-DMX])5, which are subsequently excreted through urine6. Serving as dependable biomarkers of caffeine intake/exposure, urinary caffeine and caffeine metabolites (CAF/CAFMs) are capable of well representing the amount of caffeine intake/exposure superior to dietary survey, a subjective methodology typically subject to recall bias and data inconsistency caused by the varied caffeine concentrations in diverse products6. Published research has documented the beneficial effects of urinary CAF/CAFMs, like 1,7-DMX and 3,7-DMX, separately on the system of cardiovascular, kidney, or metabolic7–10. However, their links with CKM syndrome, as well as the underlying mechanisms, remain poorly understood. Therefore, further elucidating the links and the latent mechanisms between CAF/CAFMs and CKM syndrome is therefore pressingly needed to inform targeted dietary strategies and advance prevention approaches for this high-burden condition.

By leveraging the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; a nationally representative survey), the present research was carried out to systematically assess the links between urinary CAF/CAFMs and CKM syndrome. Given the proposed anti-aging properties of caffeine11–13, coupled with the reported potential link between biological aging (which reflects the progressive decline in systemic physiological integrity14) and CKM syndrome15, we employed the emerging biological aging metric—Gompertz-Law-Based Biological Age Difference (GOLD BioAgeDiff)16—to explore its potential mediating role in associating CAF/CAFMs with CKM syndrome. Overall, we aimed to test the hypotheses that several individual CAF/CAFMs, and particularly the CAF/CAFMs mixture, may be protectively (inversely) related to CKM syndrome, and that delayed biological aging may account for part of these observed associations. Furthermore, a network pharmacology approach was incorporated to bioinformatically explore the potential molecular mechanisms/pathways underlying the protective associations of CAF/CAFMs with CKM syndrome.

Relationships of urinary CAF/CAFMs with advanced CKM syndrome

Results of single-analyte analyses are depicted in Fig. 1A. After adjusting for relevant confounders, we uncovered significant negative relationships of 1-MX (odds ratio [OR] = 0.797; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.670, 0.947), 1,3-DMX (OR = 0.793; 95% CI: 0.683, 0.921), 1,7-DMX (OR = 0.778; 95% CI: 0.673, 0.899), and 3,7-DMX (OR = 0.812; 95% CI: 0.713, 0.925) with advanced CKM syndrome (all with P and false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05). These relationships remained robust after excluding smokers (Fig. S2), when modeling crude urinary CAF/CAFMs (uncorrected for urinary creatinine [Cr]) while with urinary Cr included as a covariate in the model (Fig. S3), and after additionally adjusting for body mass index (BMI) (Fig. S4).

As illustrated in Fig. 1B-C, the entire mixture of CAF/CAFMs exhibited a significant inverse link with advanced CKM syndrome, consistent across both weighted quantile sum (WQS) and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) regression frameworks. In the WQS regression model, per quartile increment in the mixture was associated with a 21.8% decrement in the odds of advanced CKM syndrome (OR = 0.782; 95% CI: 0.670, 0.912). The chemicals substantially contributing to the mixture association, as indicated by weight ≥ 1/15 (0.067), were 3,7-DMX (0.330), 1,7-DMX (0.248), 1,3-DMX (0.172), AAMU (0.123), and 1-MX (0.103). The significant inverse association was robust across alternative training/validation splits: 60/40 (OR = 0.715; 95% CI: 0.592, 0.863) and 70/30 (OR = 0.672; 95% CI: 0.539, 0.839). Besides, the top three components driving the mixture trend (3,7-DMX, 1,7-DMX, and 1,3-DMX) consistently identified with the highest weights across all allocation scenarios (Fig. S5). In the BKMR model, compared with participants at the 50th quantile of the mixture, those at the 35th, 30th, and 25th quantiles of the mixture manifested significantly increased odds of advanced CKM syndrome, while those at the 55th and 60th quantiles of the mixture manifested significantly decreased odds of advanced CKM syndrome. The posterior inclusion probability (PIP) with value ≥ 0.5 indicated that the mixture effect was primarily driven by 1-MX (1.000), 1-MU (1.000), 1,3-DMX (0.978), 1,3,7-TMX (0.936), and 7-MU (0.910) (Table S1). The bivariate exposure-response plots outputted by the BKMR model suggested potential pairwise interactions among CAF/CAFMs (Fig. S6). For instance, the non-parallel curves in Fig. S6 indicated that there might exist pairwise interactions between 1-MU and 1,3,7-TMX, 7-MU and 1,3-DMX, and 7-MU and 1,3,7-TMX.

Role exploration of GOLD BioAgeDiff

Among urinary CAF/CAFMs, 1-MX (β = −0.371, 95% CI: −0.616, −0.126), 1,3-DMX (β = −0.437, 95% CI: −0.713, −0.162), and 1,7-DMX (β = −0.375, 95% CI: −0.641, −0.109) were discovered to be negatively linked to GOLD BioAgeDiff (all P and FDR < 0.05) and caffeine/1,3,7-TMX (β = −0.287, 95% CI: −0.514, −0.059) was nominally (P = 0.015 and FDR = 0.057) linked to GOLD BioAgeDiff (Fig. S7). GOLD BioAgeDiff was further uncovered to be positively linked to advanced CKM syndrome (OR = 1.116; 95% CI: 1.081, 1.151) (Table S2). Moreover, this relationship remained stable and significant after further adjusting for BMI (OR = 1.115; 95% CI: 1.081, 1.150) (Table S3). Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that GOLD BioAgeDiff accounted for part of the associations of 1-MX (proportion mediated: 17.42%), 1,3-DMX (20.06%), 1,7-DMX (15.84%), and the entire mixture of CAF/CAFMs (13.85%) with advanced CKM syndrome (Table 2). Exposure-mediator interaction was not discovered.

Latent mechanisms exploration

Network pharmacology was employed to explore potential molecular pathways underlying the observed associations of CAF/CAFMs with CKM syndrome. Target prediction identified 288 proteins associated with CAF/CAFMs, which were cross-referenced with 9750 targets related to advanced CKM syndrome, yielding 258 overlapping proteins (Fig. 2A, B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these common targets suggested significant (FDR < 0.05) enrichment in biological processes such as metabolic processes, molecular functions including catalytic activity, and cellular components like the extracellular region (Fig. 2C, Table S4). Subsequent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis suggested several key pathways (FDR < 0.05), and the top 5 pathways included metabolic pathways, pathways in cancer, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt) signaling pathway, and apoptosis (Fig. 2D, Table S5). By leveraging the STRING database, we developed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network based on the intersecting targets (Fig. 2E). Topologically significant nodes within this network were exploratorily identified by applying nine different algorithms and proteins ranked within the top 10 across these algorithms were suggested as candidate hub targets, including RAC-alpha serine/threonine-protein kinase (AKT1), matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), caspase-3 (CASP3), proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase (SRC), and heat shock protein HSP 90-alpha (HSP90AA1) (Fig. 2F, Table S6). Collectively, metabolic pathways, MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and apoptosis, along with hub proteins AKT1, EGFR, SRC, CASP3, HSP90AA1, and MMP9, were exploratorily suggested as key pathways and targets potentially linking CAF/CAFMs to CKM syndrome.

Discussion

Leveraging a nationally representative sample of American adults, we discovered that several individual urinary CAF/CAFMs (1,7-DMX, 1,3-DMX, 3,7-DMX, and 1-MX) as well as the entire mixture of 15 urinary CAF/CAFMs were negatively linked to advanced CKM syndrome. Additionally, GOLD BioAgeDiff, an emerging biological aging metric, was exploratorily identified as a potential mediator of the relationships of 1-MX, 1,3-DMX, 1,7-DMX, and the mixture with advanced CKM syndrome. Moreover, results of exploratory network pharmacology suggested that metabolic pathways, MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and apoptosis, along with the involvement of AKT1, EGFR, SRC, CASP3, HSP90AA1, and MMP9, might be involved in the latent mechanisms underlying the negative associations of CAF/CAFMs with CKM syndrome.

To our knowledge, our research provides the first evidence for the protective associations of CAF/CAFMs (particularly 1,7-DMX, 1,3-DMX, 3,7-DMX, and 1-MX) and their mixture with CKM syndrome, a complex clinical multisystem condition featured by intricate cross-talk among cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic disorders1. Although there is lack of prior evidence, previous research has separately revealed the protective cardiovascular, renal, or metabolic effect of CAF/CAFMs. For example, methyl xanthine caffeine, including 1-MX, has been shown to reduce cardiovascular atherogenesis in rodents17. Two studies involving over 800 Swiss adults uncovered cross-sectional inverse relationships of urinary CAF/CAFMs such as 1,3-DMX and 1,7-DMX with systolic blood pressure18 and arterial stiffness19, while an NHANES-based cross-sectional study of 1006 American adult women found that urinary caffeine metabolites (including 1,3-DMX) were negatively linked to cardiovascular disease (CVD)7. A UK Biobank cohort study enrolling over 0.1 million adults discovered that habitual coffee/caffeine consumption was linked to a lower risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity, including coronary heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes8. Besides, coffee intake was uncovered to be linked to lower risks of type 2 diabetes, obesity, and metabolic syndrome (MetS) in a cohort study of 93179 Danish adults9, and the negative coffee-MetS link was further confirmed by a meta-analysis encompassing 13 observational studies20. Additionally, urinary caffeine metabolites such as 1,7-DMX and 3,7-DMX were inversely associated with the kidney function indicator serum uric acid in a cross-sectional study of 1252 American adults21. Levels of CAF/CAFMs, including 3,7-DMX, 1,7-DMX, and 1,3-DMX, were also found to decrease with chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression among 145 Chinese CKD patients22. CKD incidence risk decrement in relation to coffee consumption was further demonstrated by a meta-analysis integrating 4 large cohort studies10. Collectively, the foregoing research indeed backs up our present discoveries, albeit CKM syndrome is far from a simple aggregation of cardiovascular, metabolic, and kidney disorders.

It is noteworthy that since CAF/CAFMs coexist in the body, the assessment of mixed exposure is particularly important, despite the fact that no research to date has examined the link between such mixed exposure and CKM syndrome. While single-chemical analyses captured only 4 CAF/CAFMs (1,7-DMX, 1,3-DMX, 3,7-DMX, and 1-MX) individually associated with advanced CKM syndrome, both the WQS regression and the BKMR models revealed a statistically significant protective association for the entire mixture of 15 CAF/CAFMs. This phenomenon could be largely explained by the complicated interaction effects, particularly synergistic effects, existing among these CAF/CAFMs, as partially supported by the bivariate exposure-response plots indicative of pairwise interactions in the BKMR model. However, it is important to acknowledge that current mixture methodologies, including BKMR, are primarily limited to assessing pairwise (1 × 1) interactions; the presence and nature of higher-order (5 × 5, for instance) interactions remain unresolved and warrant further investigation through advanced analytical frameworks or specifically designed experimental studies23. Additionally, the two mixture models offer complementary insights into identifying key drivers within the mixture (weight ≥ 1/15 in WQS; PIP ≥ 0.5 in BKMR) for advanced CKM syndrome. Components identified to be important in one mixture model but not in the other (3,7-DMX, 1,7-DMX, and AAMU in only WQS, while 1-MU, 7-MU, and 1,3,7-TMX in only BKMR) can be explained by the distinct methodology between the two mixture models. Specifically, WQS regression, which constructs a weighted index from component quantiles, reveals critical components driving the overall mixture trend; while BKMR employs a flexible kernel function to accommodate non-linear and non-additive effects, highlighting factors that may be critical in complex biological scenarios24. Beyond methodological disparities, these discrepancies can be biologically explained. Specifically, 1,3,7-TMX has been shown to upregulate proteins associated with the PI3K/Akt/AMPK/mTOR pathway25. Meanwhile, 1-MU exhibits antioxidant activity by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS)17,26, which likely suppresses MAPK-driven inflammatory cascades—a process closely linked to oxidative stress-induced organ damage27. Although the biological activity of 7-MU has not yet been reported, we speculate that 1-MU, 7-MU, and 1,3,7-TMX act synergistically on the PI3K/Akt/AMPK/mTOR pathway, which may explain their identification as key drivers by BKMR. Importantly, both models consistently highlighted 1,3-DMX and 1-MX as major contributors driving CKM syndrome advancement. From a scientific perspective, it allows future intervention research to target specific bioactive metabolites rather than caffeine as a whole. From a public health standpoint, this knowledge may inform the development of more targeted dietary guidance and biomarker-based research strategies.

Accumulating evidence has revealed that caffeine exerts protective effects against aging and biological aging11–13. In vitro study has demonstrated that caffeine treatment delays cellular senescence and aging after caffeine treatment12, and an American adults-based survey reported that daily dietary coffee intake was cross-sectionally and negatively linked to biological aging parameters, including phenotypic age (PhenoAge) and Klemera-Doubal method-derived age (KDMAge), in 13384 adults13. Our present research supports and further extends this anti-aging knowledge, suggesting that not only caffeine but also its metabolites 1-MX, 1,7-DMX, and 1,3-DMX in urine were inversely linked to the emerging biological aging metric GOLD BioAgeDiff. Compared with traditional biological aging clocks such as PhenoAge or KDMAge, GOLD BioAgeDiff offers distinct advantages in the context of CKM syndrome. Mathematically, it is grounded in the Gompertz law of mortality, capturing the exponential trajectory of systemic physiological decline more accurately than linear correlation-based models28. Pathophysiologically, the biomarkers utilized to construct the GOLD metric, including markers of renal function (serum creatinine and albumin), metabolic regulation (plasma glucose), and systemic inflammation (white blood cell count), directly map onto the clinical pillars of CKM syndrome29. This multi-systemic integration renders GOLD BioAgeDiff uniquely sensitive to the interconnected dysfunction across the cardiorenal-metabolic system. Importantly, GOLD BioAgeDiff was uncovered to be positively linked to advanced CKM syndrome. This observation aligns with the findings by Qi et al., whose study of 3102 American adults showed that individuals with advanced CKM syndrome exhibited higher KDMAge and PhenoAge acceleration15. Our subsequent mediation analysis integrated these two strands of evidence (CAF/CAFMs-aging and aging-CKM syndrome) with the mediating role of GOLD BioAgeDiff partly discovered in the links between CAF/CAFMs and advanced CKM syndrome. This provides a novel mechanistic clue hinting that CAF/CAFMs may be linked with lower odds of advanced CKM syndrome partially through the possible attenuation of biological aging. Mechanistically, caffeine and its bioactive metabolites may enhance systemic resistance to oxidative stress, a key mechanism contributing to attenuated biological aging30. This attenuation of systemic aging, in turn, likely reduces the cumulative damage and dysfunction across cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic systems, thereby potentially mitigating the integrated organ injury that defines advanced CKM syndrome.

Our discoveries exploratorily suggested that signaling pathways of metabolic, PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and apoptosis along with hub proteins AKT1, EGFR, SRC, CASP3, HSP90AA1, and MMP9 might be involved in the underlying molecular mechanisms linking CAF/CAFMs to CKM syndrome. Coffee consumption has been shown to enhance cellular metabolic reprogramming and strengthen antioxidant defenses31. Improvements in metabolic efficiency are critical for slowing biological aging, as they may counteract the metabolic exhaustion and dysregulate nutrient-sensing, which are key drivers of systemic biological decline32. By optimizing energy homeostasis, CAF/CAFMs maintain a more youthful metabolic state, potentially serving as a primary defense against the initiation of CKM syndrome. Prior research reported that caffeine could increase the expression of Akt and PI3K proteins, potentially upregulating the PI3K/Akt/AMPK/mTOR pathway25. The PI3K-Akt pathway, chiefly regulated by AKT1 and critically modulated by upstream nodes such as SRC and the receptor tyrosine kinase EGFR, is a key regulator of glucose metabolism and insulin signaling. Its activation may enhance insulin sensitivity33 and counteract oxidative stress and renal inflammation34, which are core drivers of the kidney and metabolic components of CKM syndrome. The MAPK signaling pathway serves as a critical interface for oxidative-stress-driven aging. As study argued, ROS act as essential messengers in cellular growth and apoptosis, but their deregulation leads to accumulated oxidative damage35. By antagonizing adenosine A2a receptors (A2aR), caffeine is thought to reduce excessive ROS production and prevent the ROS-MAPK inflammatory cascade from escaping cellular control, thereby potentially protecting the heart and kidneys from accelerated senescence and fibrosis that occur when redox homeostasis is disrupted36,37. Apoptosis occurs throughout the human body, and caffeine has been shown to regulate this process to reduce tissue damage in organs such as the pancreas, supporting the relevance of the enriched apoptosis pathway in our model38. Notably, the identified hub protein HSP90AA1 could play a pivotal role in regulating cellular homeostasis associated with apoptosis and aging. As a molecular chaperone, HSP90AA1 promotes autophagy by regulating the nuclear localization of transcription factor EB (TFEB) and slows down the aging process induced by oxidative stress, thereby extending lifespan39. Its direct interaction with AKT1 constitutes a key mechanism for resisting acute kidney injury, suggesting potential relevance to renal pathology in CKM syndrome40. Additionally, research indicated that activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway could upregulate MMP941. MMP9 might contribute to cardiovascular resilience by modulating macrophage phenotypes and inhibiting atherosclerosis42. By preventing the pathological remodeling of the vasculature, these molecular actions may help preserve structural integrity of the cardiovascular system, counteracting the progressive structural decay associated with advanced biological age. Overall, CAF/CAFMs may confer protective effects by simultaneously targeting critical nodes to positively influence interconnected pathway networks closely associated with biological aging processes. This mechanistic network may attenuate systemic biological decline, as captured by GOLD BioAgeDiff, thereby helping to reduce the risk of developing advanced CKM syndrome characterized by integrated organ damage.

The primary strength of this study lies in addressing a critical knowledge gap by employing mixture modeling approaches (WQS and BKMR) to perform the first comprehensive evaluation of both individual and combined associations of urinary CAF/CAFMs with CKM syndrome in a relatively large, nationally representative sample of American adults, providing innovative and potent epidemiological insight. Besides, it is also the first to formally establish biological aging (GOLD BioAgeDiff) as a potential mediator and to explore underlying mechanisms through network pharmacology, offering innovative and suggestive mechanistic insights. Several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, the cross-sectional design, combined with reliance on single spot urine measurements, introduces within-person variability, limiting the assessment of long-term exposure and precluding causal inference. Consequently, the observed associations remain susceptible to reverse causality; for instance, pre-existing CKM conditions might alter caffeine consumption behaviors and/or influence renal excretion of CAF/CAFMs, although reverse causation would more likely bias the observed associations toward non-specific alterations in substance consumption and/or renal excretion of metabolites rather than generate consistent metabolite-specific association patterns observed in our analyses. Therefore, these associations should be interpreted with caution, and future prospective studies with repeated sampling and measurements are needed to confirm our findings. Besides, although urinary Cr normalization for the levels of urinary CAF/CAFMs is appropriate, there is no universal consensus on whether to use the urinary Cr-corrected analyte levels (µmol/g Cr) or the crude analyte levels (mg/dL) with Cr included as a covariate for model adjustment. Potential measurement error remained and over-/under-estimation of levels of CAF/CAFMs and their associations with CKM syndrome may occur after applying these methods43,44, though both methods revealed consistent and robust results. Second, our findings rely solely on urinary excretion data of caffeine. While urinary biomarkers are reliable indicators of caffeine exposure, they primarily reflect the excretion phase. It remains uncertain whether identical associations with CKM syndrome would be observed using blood-derived caffeine measurements, which might better reflect systemic circulation. Future studies incorporating parallel caffeine measurements from both blood and urine are warranted to validate our findings. Third, socioeconomic status in this study was assessed using the poverty-income ratio (PIR), which is based on the traditional federal poverty line (FPL). The FPL calculation does not account for modern shifts in living expenses. Because public-use NHANES data lack geographic identifiers to protect participant privacy, we were unable to apply state-specific cost-of-living adjustment, which may lead to an incomplete representation of actual economic hardship. Fourth, the CKM syndrome was dichotomized into advanced (stages 3–4) and non-advanced (stages 0–2) categories following clinical guidelines to identify high-risk populations. However, this categorization may introduce clinical heterogeneity by combining individuals with high predicted 10-year risk of CVD with those who have established clinical CVD. Fifth, the findings from our mechanistic explorations, both the mediation and network pharmacology analyses, should be interpreted with caution. Regarding the mediation analysis, although we meticulously adjusted for a comprehensive range of confounders, the observational nature of our study means that unmeasured residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded45,46. Similarly, the network pharmacology component is based solely on computational predictions, carrying an inherent risk of identifying non-specific pathways. Therefore, both sets of results should be interpreted cautiously and treated as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive proofs, necessitating future validation in longitudinal cohorts and in vitro/in vivo experiments. Finally, although the study is based on a nationally representative sample, generalizability to other populations with differing genetic backgrounds, dietary habits, and environmental exposures remains to be established.

Based on this nationally representative study integrating epidemiological and network pharmacology evidence, we deciphered for the first time that urinary CAF/CAFMs (especially 3,7-DMX, 1,7-DMX, 1,3-DMX, and 1-MX) and their mixture showed negative associations with CKM syndrome, which was exploratorily found to be mediated in part by delayed biological aging, while metabolic pathways, MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and apoptosis, along with AKT1, EGFR, SRC, CASP3, HSP90AA1, and MMP9, may be involved in the underlying mechanisms. Our discoveries highlight the value of considering CAF/CAFMs and biological aging deceleration in strategies aimed at preventing CKM syndrome. Future prospective studies and experimental models are encouraged to confirm our discoveries.

Methods

Data sources and study subjects

Orchestrated by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the NHANES serves as a continuous initiative developed to monitor the nutritional and health conditions of the non-hospitalized U.S. civilian population47,48. This work is accomplished through a combination of clinical examinations, participant interviews, and laboratory measurements. All individuals involved provided written informed consent. This analysis included data from three consecutive NHANES cycles (2009–2010, 2011–2012, and 2013–2014), during which urinary CAF/CAFMs measurements were available. From an initial pool of 30468 individuals, the following exclusions were applied: pregnancy (n = 190); lacking data for CKM syndrome staging (n = 20,691); without urinary CAF/CAFMs measurements (n = 6556); and lacking data for GOLD BioAgeDiff computation (n = 11). Consequently, the final study population consisted of 3020 subjects (Fig. S8).

Measurement of urinary CAF/CAFMs

To determine the levels of urinary CAF/CAFMs, an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alta, CA) was employed. The analytical process integrated stable isotope-labeled internal standards and strictly adhered to the NHANES standardized laboratory guidelines for CAF/CAFMs measurements49. Briefly, 50 µL of urine was diluted with 450 µL of water. A 100-µL aliquot was combined with 120 µL of 0.2 N NaOH containing stable isotope-labeled internal standards and incubated for at least 30 min at room temperature for the alkaline conversion of unstable uracil metabolites. Samples were then acidified with 30 µL of 2.0 N HCl and 250 µL of a 1:9 methanol/water solution containing 0.1% formic acid before filtration and analysis.

Quantitation utilized an 11-point calibration curve (reportable ranges spanning 0.003-600 µM, depending on the specific metabolite). Laboratory quality control included three levels of bench QC materials (low, medium, and high) per run, with batch effects and instrument drift monitored via a multi-rule system. The use of stable isotope-labeled internal standards for each analyte ensured high recovery and compensated for matrix effects, maintaining robust inter- and intra-assay precision. Spike recovery experiments validated the accuracy and precision across multiple days. The 15 measured metabolites, along with their abbreviation, mean recovery rates, and standard deviations (SD), included: 1-methyluric acid (1-MU; 103%, SD 4.93%), 3-methyluric acid (3-MU; 104%, SD 2.84%), 7-methyluric acid (7-MU; 101.0%, SD 3.73%), 1,3-dimethyluric acid (1,3-DMU; 103%, SD 4.39%), 1,7-dimethyluric acid (1,7-DMU; 98.2%, SD 1.82%), 3,7-dimethyluric acid (3,7-DMU; 93.8%, SD 4.40%), 1,3,7-trimethyluric acid (1,3,7-TMU; 98.1%, SD 1.72%), 1-methylxanthine (1-MX; 100%, SD 4.88%), 3-methylxanthine (3-MX; 102%, SD 4.36%), 7-methylxanthine (7-MX; 96.6%, SD 2.83%), 1,3-dimethylxanthine (1,3-DMX; 95.1%, SD 1.55%), 1,7-dimethylxanthine (1,7-DMX; 99.3%, SD 4.04%), 3,7-dimethylxanthine (3,7-DMX; 99.4%, SD 1.18%), 1,3,7-trimethylxanthine (1,3,7-TMX; 103%, SD 3.18%), and 5-acetylamino-6-amino-3-methyluracil (AAMU; 86.9%, SD 3.7%).

Measurement data from 2011 to 2012 were processed using the metabolite regression equations recommended on the official website. For measured concentrations falling beneath the lower limits of detection (LLODs), we substituted them with values computed as the LLODs divided by √2. To account for variations in urine dilution/concentration, CAF/CAFMs were normalized to urinary Cr50,51, with results reported in units of µmol/g Cr. A detailed overview of urinary CAF/CAFMs’ LLODs, detectable rates, and concentration distributions is provided in Table S7.

Assessment of CKM Syndrome

Following the framework established by the American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory29, CKM syndrome was classified into 5 stages: stage 0 (nonexistence of any CKM risk factor); stage 1 (characterized by dysfunctional or excessive adiposity); stage 2 (existence of metabolic risk factors or moderate-to-high CKD risk); stage 3 (distinguished by very high CKD risk or elevated ten-year CVD risk); and stage 4 (presence of clinical CVD). For CKM syndrome staging, relevant demographic variables (race, sex, and age) were obtained from the demographics dataset; physical metrics (for instance, BMI and waist size) were collected during physical examinations; diagnoses of hypertension, prediabetes, diabetes, MetS, and CKD were based on a combination of laboratory results and self-reported questionnaire data; in accordance with the 2024 KDIGO framework, CKD staging was determined by estimating the glomerular filtration rate through the race-neutral 2021 CKD-EPI Cr formula52; history of CVD and statin use were ascertained from questionnaire data; and ten-year CVD risk was calculated using the AHA’s Predicting Risk of CVD EVENTs equations53. Additional details on CKM syndrome staging are provided in Table S8. Consistent with prior research54, advanced CKM syndrome, the primary outcome of interest in this study, referred to stages 3 and 4.

Estimation of GOLD BioAgeDiff

GOLD BioAgeDiff, representing the deviation between biological age and chronological age, provides an integrated measure of systemic physiological status and is an emerging metric of biological aging superior than other clocks (Levine’s PhenoAge, for instance). It has been validated across various cohorts, including NHANES16. GOLD BioAgeDiff was estimated on the basis of the Gompertz Law with nine easily accessible clinical blood parameters, including white blood cell count, red blood cell distribution width, mean cell volume, lymphocyte percentage, albumin, γ-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, and glucose. Assessment of covariates

To ensure robust multivariable adjustment, candidate covariates were identified through a combination of literature review and exploratory data analysis, grounded in biological and statistical rationale55. We took covariates covering demographic characteristics (age, sex, and ethnicity), socioeconomic factors (marital status, education, and PIR), and lifestyle behaviours, including physical activity, HEI-score, and smoking/drinking status, into account. In accordance with published research54,56, BMI was initially considered but finally did not enter into the model for adjustment, as BMI serves as a critical covariate leveraged for assessing the study outcome while further adjustment for BMI resulted in overcorrection and biased estimates55. PIR, an indicator of a family’s socioeconomic status relative to the FPL, was estimated as the ratio of total family income to the applicable annual federal poverty guideline and further categorized as into ≤ 1 (at or below poverty), 1–4 (low to moderate income), and ≥ 4 (relatively higher income)57. Smokers were defined as subjects who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime57,58. Drinkers were defined as those who had consumed at least 12 alcoholic drinks/year58. Weekly metabolic equivalent (MET)-minutes were used to assess physical activity, with participants classified as sedentary (no regular activity), insufficient (< 500 MET-min/week), moderate (500–1000 MET-min/week), and high (> 1000 MET-min/week)59. Dietary quality was evaluated using the HEI-2015 score, consistent with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans60.

Statistical analysis

Following NHANES analytical protocols, we incorporated sample weights into all statistical analyses. Continuous variables are presented as weighted mean ± standard error, while classified variables are reported as counts (weighted percentages). Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess bivariate correlations among urinary CAF/CAFMs. Data of CAF/CAFMs were ln-transformed to approximate normality for regression modeling. All statistical analyses were operated in R (version 4.4.1) and all regression models were adjusted for race, sex, age, marital status, education, PIR, drinking status, smoking status, physical activity, and HEI score, with a two-tailed α level set at 0.05.

We utilized weighted logistic regression models, implemented via the R “survey” package, to quantify the links between advanced CKM syndrome and individual urinary CAF/CAFMs. Effect estimates are reported as ORs with 95% CIs. To control the potential bias from multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied. Furthermore, to evaluate the robustness of our primary findings, the links were further re-evaluated by sensitivity analyses of (1) excluding smokers to preclude the possible influence of smoking-related shifts on caffeine metabolism and urinary levels of CAF/CAFMs as well as its potential bias on the observed associations; (2) modeling crude urinary CAF/CAFMs (uncorrected for urinary Cr) while with urinary Cr included as a covariate in the model to preclude the possible influence of methodology discrepancy on accounting for urine dilution/concentration; and (3) additional adjustment for BMI, which was not adjusted in the primary models due to the issue of overadjustment, to preclude the potential residual confounding from BMI.

The association between mixed exposure to 15 urinary CAF/CAFMs and advanced CKM syndrome was assessed leveraging WQS regression model (R package “gWQS”) and BKMR model (R package “BKMR”), two models widely applied to evaluate the health effects of exposure to the mixture of multiple chemicals as detailed in a previous review24. For the WQS regression model, the total population was split into a training set (40%) and a validation set (60%), with 1000 bootstrap resamples applied. The estimated weight of each chemical with a value ≥ 1/n (n is number of the total chemicals) outputted by the WQS regression model indicates chemical importance24. To address potential concerns regarding data allocation and to verify model stability, alternative training/validation splits of 60/40 and 70/30 were conducted as additional sensitivity analyses. For the BKMR model, 30000 iterations were run to ensure robustness, with a PIP threshold of ≥ 0.50 for inferring chemical importance24,61.

To explore whether GOLD BioAgeDiff serves as a potential mediator linking CAF/CAFMs (exposure) to advanced CKM syndrome (outcome), the exposure-mediator relationship and mediator-outcome relationship were first evaluated using weighted linear regression and weighted logistic regression models, respectively. To verify the stability of the association between the mediator and the outcome, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by additionally adjusting for BMI. The mixture data of urinary CAF/CAFMs utilized for mediation analysis were constructed via the WQS model. Subsequently, mediation analysis was performed leveraging a bootstrap method based on 1000 resamples. The mediation analysis decomposed the total effect (TE) of exposure on outcome into the average causal mediation effect (ACME) interpreted by mediator and the average direct effect (ADE) not interpreted by mediator, with exposure-mediator interaction considered, and further estimated the proportion mediated as the percentage of ACME relative to TE62–64.

Network pharmacological analysis

Canonical SMILES for the 15 CAF/CAFMs were retrieved from the PubChem platform (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Potential protein targets of CAF/CAFMs were retrieved from the PharmMapper (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html) platform and the Swiss Target Prediction (https://swisstargetprediction.ch/) platform. Disease-related targets for advanced CKM syndrome were acquired from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). As direct queries for “cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome” yielded sparse results, we expanded our search to include “chronic kidney disease” and “cardiovascular disease”, which is in accordance existing literature and justified by the fact that advanced CKM syndrome is fundamentally characterized by CKD and CVD65. To enhance specificity, we only preserved genes with a GeneCards Inferred Function Score (GIFtS) greater than 15 and a relevance score greater than the median. Overlapping targets between the analytes and disease sets were identified and visualized using a Venn diagram generated with the R package “VennDiagram”. These overlapping targets were subsequently submitted to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) for PPI network construction, as well as GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses. KEGG pathways were selected based on Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.0566. Among the pathways with FDR < 0.05, the top 5 with those clearly irrelevant excluded were emphatically presented and interpreted, following the recommendation of previous studies67,68. The PPI network was visualized and analyzed in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1), where key hub proteins were identified and ranked based on nine topological algorithms (Degree, Radiality, Closeness, Betweenness, Bottleneck, Maximal Clique Centrality, Edge Percolated Component, Maximal Neighborhood Component, and Stress)69,70. Selecting converging nodes that consistently ranked in the top 10 across these multiple parameters is a robust consensus method to filter out algorithm-specific noise and accurately isolate the most essential biological targets for network stability68,69,71.
