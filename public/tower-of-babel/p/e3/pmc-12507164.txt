Lamina cribrosa curvature index as a biomarker of non-glaucomatous optic neuropathy in obstructive sleep apnea syndrome
Authors: Serhat Ermis, Ece Ozal, Idil Celen Arabaci, Ulku Dubus Hos, Cengiz Gul, Murat Karapapak, Sadık Altan Ozal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The aim of this study was to evaluate retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), radial peripapillary capillary plexus (RPCP), and lamina cribrosa (LC) parameters in patients diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) and healthy controls and to investigate the relationship between these parameters and OSAS severity.Methods:This prospective case-control study included 124 eyes from 124 OSAS patients (mild: n = 24, moderate: n = 45, severe: n = 55) diagnosed by polysomnography and 104 eyes of 104 healthy individuals. RNFL thickness, ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness, RPCP vessel densities (VD), LC thickness (LCT), LC depth, LC anterior width, LC curvature depth, and LC curvature index (LCCI) were assessed using swept-source optical coherence tomography and optical coherence tomography angiography. Comparisons among groups and correlation analyses were performed.Results:RNFL thickness and RPCP VD were significantly lower in OSAS patients compared to healthy controls (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed among groups in GCIPL thickness. Among LC parameters, LCCI was significantly higher in severe and moderate OSAS groups compared to healthy controls (P < 0.05). Moreover, a positive correlation was found between apnea-hypopnea index and LCCI, while a negative correlation was noted between AHI and LCT (P = 0.013 and P = 0.005, respectively).Conclusion:LC parameters, particularly LCCI, may serve as important indicators reflecting disease severity and potential structural alterations at the optic nerve head in OSAS patients. LCCI may have clinical utility in assessing early optic neuropathy risk in OSAS. Further large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is a disease more common in middle-aged adults, characterized by repetitive upper airway obstructions during sleep.[1] These recurrent partial or complete obstructions reduce airflow, leading to intermittent hypoxia.[2] The hypoxic condition manifests as excessive daytime sleepiness, cognitive impairment, and chronic fatigue.[1] Obesity is the most significant predisposing factor for OSAS; other contributing factors include male gender, advanced age, smoking, alcohol consumption, muscle relaxants, endocrine disorders (such as hypothyroidism and acromegaly), and increased neck circumference.[23]

OSAS is diagnosed by polysomnography (PSG) and sleep studies. Apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) quantifies apnea and hypopnea events per hour. Apnea is defined as airflow cessation for ≥10 seconds with ≥90% airflow reduction; hypopnea is defined as airflow reduction ≥30% lasting ≥10 seconds, accompanied by ≥3% oxygen desaturation or arousal.[34] Respiratory events are classified as obstructive if associated with snoring, inspiratory flattening in nasal pressure waveform, or paradoxical breathing; otherwise, they are categorized as central.[3] OSAS severity is classified based on AHI as mild, moderate, or severe.[5] OSAS is diagnosed when OSA is accompanied by excessive daytime sleepiness, typically evaluated using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), with scores ≥10 considered significant for diagnosis.[3]

OSAS has been associated with various ocular diseases, including floppy eyelid syndrome, non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, normal-tension glaucoma, primary open-angle glaucoma, optic disc edema, and central serous chorioretinopathy.[12] Previous studies have demonstrated increased prevalence of glaucoma in OSAS patients.[1] Chronic nocturnal hypoxemia, vascular dysregulation, and increased sympathetic activity associated with OSAS are thought to cause optic nerve hypoperfusion and neurodegeneration.[6] Elevated vasoactive hormones, such as catecholamines, angiotensin II, and arginine vasopressin, might further exacerbate vascular tone.[127] Moreover, a correlation between OSAS severity and retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thinning has been suggested, indicating progressive effects on ocular health.[189] Hence, screening for early optic nerve axonal damage may be advisable in this high-risk population.

The primary aim of this study is to compare optic nerve head (ONH) microvascular parameters, RNFL thickness, and lamina cribrosa (LC) morphological parameters between untreated OSAS patients and healthy controls and to evaluate potential effects of OSAS severity on these structural parameters.

Methods

Study population and design

This prospective, cross-sectional, case-control study was conducted between January 1, 2025 and February 1, 2025 at the Department of Ophthalmology and Sleep Disorders Center. The study protocol was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (IRB Approval: E-96317027-514.10-265686386). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

All patients underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) at the sleep laboratory to confirm OSAS diagnosis and determine disease severity. Newly diagnosed, untreated OSAS patients were included. Participants with a total sleep time below 4 hours were excluded from the study. Individuals with an AHI ≥5 were diagnosed with OSAS. The severity of OSAS was classified based on AHI as mild OSAS (5–15 events/hour), moderate OSAS (15–30 events/hour), and severe OSAS (≥30 events/hour).[5] Participants were categorized into four groups: mild OSAS, moderate OSAS, severe OSAS, and healthy controls. The healthy control group was composed of individuals who presented for routine ophthalmic examinations, had an Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) score <10, and had a STOP-Bang score ≤2.[3]

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were established to ensure participants in both OSAS and control groups had similar ophthalmologic and systemic parameters. Participants were required to have a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of ≥6/6, a spherical equivalent refractive error <±3.0 diopters, an intraocular pressure (IOP) <21 mmHg, and an axial length (AL) between 21 and 24 mm.

Participants not meeting these criteria were excluded. Other exclusion criteria included a history of systemic vascular diseases (such as diabetes mellitus or hypertension), ocular media opacities interfering with optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging (e.g., cataracts, vitreous hemorrhage), smoking history, and ocular or neurological diseases potentially affecting ONH or RNFL (e.g., uveitis, glaucoma, significant ocular trauma, optic neuropathy, retinal-choroidal diseases). Additionally, patients with rheumatologic or autoimmune diseases and those with a history of intraocular surgery (cataract surgery, vitrectomy) were excluded.

Examination protocol and measurements

All participants underwent neurologic and ophthalmologic examinations. Ophthalmologic examination included automated refractometry measurement (KR-8900, Topcon Corporation, Japan), BCVA assessment, anterior segment biomicroscopy, IOP measurement by Goldmann applanation tonometry, central corneal thickness (CCT) measurement (Oculus Pentacam; Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), and axial length measurement via optical biometry (Tomey Optical Biometry OA-2000 Opt-Meas V.4E).

All eyes underwent non-mydriatic ONH vessel density (VD) analysis using OCT angiography (OCTA) in a dark environment. Ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness and LC parameters were also recorded. All measurements were performed between 09:00 and 11:00 AM by a blinded examiner (S.E) who was unaware of the clinical information of participants to minimize circadian variations.

Swept-Source OCT ımaging protocol

Swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) imaging was performed using a Topcon DRI OCT Triton device (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), which utilizes an adjustable laser source with a wavelength of 1050 nm and an A-scan speed of 100,000 scans per second. For OCT scanning, a raster scan pattern with a 6 mm × 6 mm scan area centered on the optic disc was employed. An internal eye tracking system and artifact removal algorithm integrated into the device were used to minimize motion and projection artifacts. The minimum acceptable OCT quality score was set at 90.

Swept-Source OCT angiography ımaging protocol

OCTA images were obtained using a 4.5 mm × 4.5 mm field centered on the optic disc. Each OCTA scan cube consisted of 320 clusters of B-scans, and automatic segmentation of all vascular plexuses was performed by IMAGEnet6 software. Manual segmentation corrections were performed if needed. Radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) segments were analyzed, defined as the vascular structures between the internal limiting membrane (ILM) and the lower boundary of the RNFL. The minimum acceptable OCTA quality score was set at 70.

Lamina cribrosa and structural parameters

Lamina cribrosa parameters were obtained using horizontal and vertical SS-OCT scans centered on the optic disc. Lamina cribrosa curvature index (LCCI) was calculated using lamina cribrosa curvature width (LCCW) and lamina cribrosa curvature depth (LCCD). LCCW was defined as the distance between two points on the anterior LC surface. These points corresponded to the intersections of lines drawn perpendicularly from the termination points of Bruch’s membrane to the Bruch’s membrane opening reference plane. Lamina cribrosa depth (LCD) was defined as the vertical distance from the plane connecting the Bruch’s membrane openings to the anterior surface of the lamina cribrosa. LCCD was defined as the vertical distance between the anterior laminar surface and the reference line [Fig. 1]. LCCI was calculated as (LCCD/LCCW) × 100. Lamina cribrosa thickness (LCT) was measured as the distance between the internal and external borders of the hyperreflective region at the vertical center of the optic nerve head.

The anterior and posterior surfaces of the lamina cribrosa are indicated by red dashed lines. The solid yellow line represents the reference line connecting the Bruch’s membrane openings. Lamina cribrosa curvature width (LCCW) is defined as the distance between two points on the anterior lamina cribrosa surface, corresponding to the intersections of lines drawn perpendicularly from the termination points of the Bruch’s membrane to the reference plane. Lamina cribrosa curvature depth (LCCD) is defined as the vertical distance between the anterior laminar surface and the reference line. Lamina cribrosa depth (LCD) refers to the vertical distance from the plane connecting the Bruch’s membrane openings to the anterior surface of the lamina cribrosa. The lamina cribrosa curvature index (LCCI) is calculated using the formula LCCI = (LCCD/LCCW) × 100

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum-maximum), while categorical variables were described as numbers and percentages. The normality of numerical data was evaluated by Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. For comparisons among more than two groups, either one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used depending on data distribution. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Pearson’s Chi-square test was employed for categorical variables. Correlations between continuous variables were analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Box plots were used for visual data presentation.

IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28) software was utilized for statistical analyses. A significance level of 95% was adopted, and results with a P value ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

In total, 124 eyes from 124 OSAS patients (mild OSAS: n = 24, moderate OSAS: n = 45, severe OSAS: n = 55) and 104 eyes of 104 healthy controls were included. There was no statistically significant difference between OSAS groups and controls in terms of age or sex (P > 0.05). However, neck circumference, mean oxygen saturation (SpO₂), lowest SpO₂, oxygen desaturation index (ODI), body mass index (BMI), total sleep time, and average abnormal respiratory event counts significantly differed among OSAS groups and controls (all comparisons, P < 0.05). Discussion

In this study, significant differences were observed among OSAS patients with different severity levels in terms of neck circumference, mean and lowest SpO₂, ODI, BMI, and total sleep duration. The repetitive upper airway obstructions characteristic of OSAS are closely related to intermittent hypoxia and oxidative stress, leading to vascular and neurodegenerative alterations.[10] It has been shown in the literature that systemic inflammation and vascular complications increase with OSAS severity, and these processes play crucial roles in underlying retinal and ONH alterations.[11] Consistent with these data, higher ODI values and lower SpO₂ levels detected in OSAS patients in our study reflect chronic hypoxia and may help explain the potential structural changes observed in retinal tissues.[10]

In our study, RNFL thicknesses of OSAS patients were significantly lower compared to healthy controls. Especially superior quadrant RNFL thickness showed marked thinning in severe OSAS patients relative to controls. Although a thinning tendency was noted in all quadrants, statistically significant differences were only observed between severe OSAS patients and healthy controls. Similar RNFL thinning in OSAS patients was also reported by Kargi et al.[8] and Mojon et al.[9] For instance, a previous study reported significantly lower superior and nasal quadrant RNFL thicknesses in OSAS patients compared to controls.[1] In another study, significant RNFL thinning particularly in superior and inferior quadrants was demonstrated in OSAS patients.[10] Similarly, Ngoo et al.[11] further supported this association, reporting progressive RNFL thinning in parallel with increasing OSAS severity. The underlying mechanisms of RNFL thinning are not yet fully understood, but chronic hypoxia and vascular dysregulation associated with OSAS may lead to ischemic damage in the optic nerve, potentially resulting in RNFL thinning. Additionally, previous studies have reported higher glaucoma prevalence in OSAS patients, indicating an increased risk of glaucoma development in this patient population.[89] Recent meta-analyses also support these findings, showing that OSAS is significantly associated with increased risk and progression of glaucoma.[121314] Additionally, recent studies and meta-analyses have highlighted that OSA may not only increase the risk of glaucoma development but also contribute to its progression over time, emphasizing the need for close follow-up in this patient group.[1516] Therefore, the RNFL thinning observed in our study emphasizes the necessity of careful clinical assessment for potential optic neuropathy development in OSAS patients.

In this study, although differences were observed in GCIPL thicknesses across all four segments among OSAS patients and healthy controls, these differences did not reach statistical significance. The literature on GCIPL thickness changes in OSAS is controversial. While some previous studies reported significant thinning in GCIPL thicknesses due to retinal ganglion cell ischemia associated with oxygen deprivation and vascular alterations,[117] other studies—including ours—found no significant relationship between GCIPL thickness and OSAS severity.[110] Additionally, some studies also reported no significant GCIPL thickness changes, indicating that OSAS effects on GCIPL remain unclear.[910] These varying results might be explained by the use of different OCT devices, differences in segmentation methods, and heterogeneous patient populations across studies.[110] As supported by our findings, GCIPL thickness alone might not sufficiently reflect OSAS severity and should be interpreted alongside additional parameters.

In our study, a notable reduction in RPCP VD was observed in OSAS patients compared to healthy controls, yet significant differences were observed only in the superior and temporal segments. Differences in other quadrants did not reach statistical significance. Recent OCTA studies have reported significant reductions in superficial and deep VD in optic disc and parafoveal regions of OSAS patients compared to healthy individuals, correlated with disease severity.[10] Yu et al.[18] highlighted a reduction in peripapillary capillary densities in accordance with OSAS severity, suggesting that such changes may stem from vascular dysregulation caused by chronic hypoxia at the optic nerve head. These findings support the significant reduction we observed in superior and temporal RPCP quadrants, indicating that OSAS might directly affect retinal microvascular structures.[101819] RPCP changes might have implications for glaucoma and other ocular complications in OSAS patients, emphasizing the clinical importance of RPCP as a biomarker.[182021]

In this study, LC parameters were compared between OSAS patients and healthy controls. While no significant differences were observed in terms of LCT and LCD, statistically significant differences were identified for LCCW, LCCD, and especially LCCI. LCCI values were significantly higher in severe and moderate OSAS groups compared to controls, while no significant difference was noted between mild OSAS and control groups. LCCI is an important parameter reflecting structural deformation at the ONH, especially in glaucoma and other optic neuropathies. Recent studies have demonstrated the clinical importance of LCCI as a biomarker for glaucoma diagnosis and progression, with elevated LCCI values linked to disease progression.[2223] Additionally, increased LCCI has been associated with reduced optic nerve head blood flow, suggesting a potential role in the pathophysiology of optic nerve damage.[2425]

Chronic hypoxia, vascular dysregulation, and repeated hypoxic episodes associated with OSAS may lead to microstructural deformation within the LC.[2526] Elevated LCCI values found in severe OSAS patients in our study highlight a potential risk of neurodegenerative changes in the optic nerve head. Early detection of optic nerve damage in OSAS patients is essential for clinical follow-up. Recent studies suggest OSAS may contribute to glaucoma progression, highlighting a potential clinical implication for patients observed with elevated LCCI values in our study. Severe and moderate-to-severe OSAS have been reported to accelerate structural glaucoma progression,[2728] though some studies did not confirm this association.[29] Therefore, the precise role of elevated LCCI in glaucoma progression warrants further large-scale, prospective studies.

In our study, correlations among OSAS severity marker AHI, RNFL, LCT, and LCCI were investigated. No significant correlation was found between AHI and RNFL thickness. Although previous studies demonstrated RNFL thinning associated with OSAS severity,[89] the direct relationship with AHI remains unclear.[15] Conversely, a significant negative correlation was found between AHI and LCT, and a positive correlation between AHI and LCCI. Moreover, a significant negative correlation was observed between LCT and LCCI. These findings suggest structural deformations in LC might relate to OSAS severity. Particularly, Lee et al.[22] and Lee et al.[23] emphasized that elevated LCCI values are predictive of both structural deformation and disease progression in glaucomatous eyes. However, the absence of correlation between RNFL and LCCI observed in our study suggests LCCI changes might occur through a mechanism independent of RNFL thinning. Considering that glaucoma and non-glaucomatous optic neuropathies—such as ischemic optic neuropathy—share common pathophysiological mechanisms including chronic ischemia and vascular dysregulation, elevated LCCI in OSAS patients may reflect structural deformation of the optic nerve head without being specific to a particular disease entity. Similar debates exist in glaucoma literature; some studies have reported correlation between LCCI and RNFL,[2223] while others found no clear relationship.[2530] Therefore, LCCI might represent structural optic nerve head damage in OSAS independently from RNFL thinning, necessitating further prospective studies.

This study’s main strength is that it is one of the first detailed evaluations of LC parameters, especially LCCI, in OSAS patients. The potential clinical importance of LC parameters in assessing optic nerve head damage in OSAS enhances the originality and clinical value of our findings. Additionally, homogeneous patient selection criteria and a comprehensive evaluation protocol strengthen the reliability of our results. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Primarily, the cross-sectional study design limits interpretation of long-term outcomes, and the single-center nature may limit generalizability. Another limitation is that cases with a family history of glaucoma were not specifically excluded, which might introduce a potential bias in evaluating structural optic nerve alterations. Future multicenter, prospective studies with extended follow-up periods would provide deeper insights into OSAS’s ocular impacts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that LC parameters, especially LCCI, may serve as valuable indicators reflecting disease severity and potential structural changes at the optic nerve head in OSAS patients. The elevated LCCI values detected particularly in severe OSAS patients suggest a possible association between disease severity and optic nerve head deformation. Therefore, LCCI might be clinically useful in the early assessment of optic neuropathy risk in OSAS patients. These findings should be confirmed through future large-scale, prospective, and long-term studies.
