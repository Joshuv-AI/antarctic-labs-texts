Associations between breathlessness and individuals’ satisfaction with sexual life: a nationally representative internet survey
Authors: Max Olsson, Jacob Sandberg, Slavica Kochovska, Sungwon Chang, Diana Ferreira, Steven Pantilat, Magnus Ekström, David Currow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSexual life is important for many people and may be limited by breathlessness. We evaluated associations between breathlessness and individuals’ perceived satisfaction with sexual lives and explored mediating factors in this relationship.MethodsA cross-sectional, online, population-based survey of Australian adults with key demographics (age, sex, rurality, state/territory) reflected the 2016 national census. Assessments included: demographics, breathlessness (modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale); satisfaction with overall sexual life; whether breathlessness had affected overall sexual life and physical, social and emotional functioning. Binomial and ordinal logistic regression evaluated associations; a structural equation model evaluated direct, indirect and total associations.ResultsOf 10 033 respondents (52% women; mean age 45.4 (SD 18.6)), 4245/10 033 (42%) reported mMRC ≥1, 1214/10 033 (12%) reported being very dissatisfied with their overall sexual life and 943/10 033 (9%) reported that breathlessness had impacted their overall sexual life. Compared with mMRC 0, there was an unadjusted association between mMRC 1 and satisfaction with overall sexual life which remained after adjusting for age, sex and body mass index (adjusted OR (aOR): 1.50; 95% CI 1.29 to 1.74).Breathlessness severity was associated with worse unadjusted and adjusted impact on overall sexual life: mMRC 0 versus mMRC 1 (aOR: 1.84; 95% CI 1.36 to 2.47). The associations increased stepwise for each higher mMRC level. There may be moderation of the effect of breathlessness on satisfaction with sexual life through emotional functioning.ConclusionsIncreasing breathlessness severity increases the likelihood of a person’s overall sexual life being impacted negatively, potentially mediated partly through emotional functioning.Keywords: Perception of Asthma/Breathlessness

Introduction

Breathlessness is the subjective experience of breathing discomfort.1 This chronic symptom is experienced by 4%–10% of the general population in high-income countries,2 with higher rates in low-resource countries3 and 25% of the middle-aged population, with rising proportions in older populations.4 Chronic breathlessness limiting exertion (hereafter breathlessness) causes significant disability across a range of domains from self-care to social participation.5 The symptom causes suffering for the affected individual and their families, including decreased physical capacity, poorer emotional functioning and limiting social lives.6 7 Cardiorespiratory diseases, cancer, neurologic conditions, anxiety/depression and obesity are common underlying causes of breathlessness in high-income countries.7 8

Sexual life, important for the well-being of many people, can often be limited by breathlessness.9 Previous population-based studies have sought to codify lifestyle changes for people with chronic breathlessness. A significant proportion of people identified that breathlessness had limited their sexual life, with those reporting severe breathlessness (modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) breathlessness scale 3–4) nominating it as the second most commonly affected everyday activity, after high intensity sports.10 In older men who experienced breathlessness, the most commonly reduced or ceased activity was physical activity (48%) followed by sexual activity (41%), with proportions increasing for those reporting more severe breathlessness, where 51% (mMRC 3) and 61% (mMRC 4) reported that their sexual life had been affected.7 This stood in stark contrast to those reporting no breathlessness (mMRC 0), where only 6.5% reported such impact.

There are few data on how the symptom may be associated with individuals’ perceived satisfaction with sexual life. Here, sexual life refers to a person’s sexual activities and experiences, encompassing various aspects of a person’s sexuality.11 Sexual satisfaction is an important, multi-faceted aspect of sexual well-being.12,14 There is also limited understanding about possible effects on breathlessness and a person’s sexual life that are mediated through physical, emotional or social functioning. To better meet the needs of people with breathlessness, we need to understand the extent to which breathlessness may be affecting individuals’ perceived satisfaction with their sexual lives.

The aim of this study was to evaluate any associations between breathlessness and individuals’ perceived satisfaction with sexual life. Specifically, it sought to examine whether physical, social or emotional functioning mediates this relationship, thereby exploring the mechanisms through which breathlessness may influence perceived sexual satisfaction.

Methods

Study design

This study was a cross-sectional, online, population-based survey study conducted using Qualtrics software (2021 Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA; available at https://www.qualtrics.com/). Respondents were recruited from a panel of 800 000 Australians maintained by Qualtrics market research. The questionnaire design and content, and study sample size were determined exclusively by the researchers, with no input from Qualtrics. A flow diagram of recruitment in this study is presented in online supplemental figure 1.

Settings and respondents

This study was open to Australians aged ≥18 years with proficiency in English. The sample was designed to be representative of the most recent Australian national census available at the time of the study (2016).15 To achieve this, the research team set target quotas for four demographic factors including age, sex, rurality (metropolitan/non-metropolitan) and state or territory of residence, creating cells with predetermined sizes to reflect the national census population. Respondents were assigned to specific demographic ‘cells’. If a respondent qualified for a cell that had not yet reached its predetermined target number, they proceeded to the full questionnaire. If the cell had reached its required quota, the respondent was directed away from this particular survey. The survey was pilot tested in June 2021 with 110 Qualtrics panel members, and no modifications were made as a result. The main data collection occurred from mid-July to early August 2021. The full questionnaire is available at https://osf.io/zwgfc/. There were no exclusion criteria for this study.

Measures

All assessments relied on self-report. Respondents’ demographics included: age, sex, postcode (to calculate rurality and state/territory of residence) and height and weight (to calculate body mass index (BMI)).

Individuals’ perceived satisfaction with sexual life was assessed with two questions (online supplemental table 1). Question 1 asked about the person’s satisfaction with their overall sexual life (very dissatisfied=5; moderately dissatisfied=4; about equally satisfied and dissatisfied=3; moderately satisfied=2; very satisfied=1; prefer not to say; not applicable). Question 2 asked whether breathlessness had affected the person’s overall sexual life (yes; no; prefer not to say; not applicable). “Prefer not to say” and “not applicable” were coded as missing values for the analyses. The questions were adopted from Rosen et al12 13 and earlier open-ended questions asked of the population.10

Physical, emotional and social functioning were assessed using three questions from the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule V.2.0 12-item16 (online supplemental table 1). Physical functioning used the question ‘How much difficulty did you have in walking a long distance such as a kilometre (or equivalent)?’. Emotional functioning used the question ‘How much have you been emotionally affected by your health problems?’. Social functioning used the question ‘How much of a problem did you have joining in community activities (eg, festivities, religious or other activities) in the same way as anyone else can?’. Response options for all three questions ranged from ‘none’ to ‘extreme or cannot do’.

Breathlessness was measured using the ordinal mMRC breathlessness scale, ranging from 0 (‘I only get breathless with strenuous exercise’) to 4 (‘I am too breathless to leave the house or I am breathless when dressing/undressing’)17 (online supplemental Table 2). Respondents who selected mMRC 1–4 were asked to report their understanding of what the primary cause of breathlessness was: lungs (eg, emphysema, smoking, asbestosis, work related, asthma), heart (eg, ischaemic heart disease, valve disease, abnormal heart rhythms), disorder of nerves or muscles (eg, motor neuron disease, multiple sclerosis), cancer, other (with <free text>), don’t know or prefer not to say.

Statistical analysis

Respondents’ characteristics were reported descriptively. The frequency and percentage of reported satisfaction with overall sexual life and impact of breathlessness on overall sexual life were cross-tabulated by breathlessness severity. Due to the low number of respondents with mMRC 4, they were merged with mMRC 3 into one category for the analytic statistics. The characteristics of respondents with missing data on satisfaction with their overall sexual life were compared with respondents with complete data due to the high number of missing data on this variable (30%; online supplemental Table 3).

Binomial logistic regression was used to evaluate any associations between breathlessness severity (mMRC 1, 2 or 3–4) and impact of breathlessness on overall sexual life, with no breathlessness (mMRC 0) as the reference. The binomial logistic regression was first conducted as an unadjusted analysis and then adjusted for age, sex and BMI. Ordinal logistic regression was used to evaluate any associations between breathlessness severity and satisfaction with overall sexual life and, separately, between impact of breathlessness on overall sexual life and satisfaction with overall sexual life, first unadjusted and then adjusted for age, sex, BMI, rurality (metropolitan or rural) and primary self-reported cause of breathlessness. To evaluate the proportional odds assumption of the ordinal logistic regression models, for example, that the relationship between predictor variables is the same across all categories of the ordinal outcome, a Brant-Wald test was used. If a p value is equal to or above 0.05 for a variable on the Brant-Wald test, the proportional odds assumption is considered to be violated for that variable. For the logistic regressions, the strength of association was reported with OR with 95% CIs.

A structural equation model (SEM) was used to evaluate if physical, emotional or social functioning mediated any association of breathlessness severity and satisfaction with overall sexual life.18 SEM can be used to estimate the indirect association between factors, such as underlying mechanisms of the relationship between a symptom and consequences of the symptom. For example, to what degree is breathlessness associated with satisfaction with overall sexual life indirectly through its association with physical function. Beta coefficients were used as the measure of strength of association for the SEM model.

SEM was used to examine whether breathlessness was linked to factors that may affect a person’s sexual life, by testing indirect pathways defined by the researchers. For example, instead of breathlessness directly lowering satisfaction with sexual life, it might first reduce physical functioning, which in turn might lower satisfaction with sexual life (figure 1). We hypothesised that breathlessness could affect sexual life potentially directly and indirectly: directly by reducing satisfaction with sexual life, and indirectly by lowering physical, emotional and social functioning, which then affects satisfaction with sexual life. We also ran the same analyses using the impact of breathlessness on sexual life as the outcome. All SEM models were adjusted for sex, age, rurality (metropolitan or rural) and primary self-reported cause of breathlessness. The strength of these associations is expressed as beta coefficients, which indicate how much the outcome changes with each increase in breathlessness severity, through the mediating effects of physical, emotional or social functioning. For example, a beta coefficient of one between breathlessness score and satisfaction with sexual life means that an increase of one score on satisfaction with sexual life (range: 0–3) is expected for every increase of breathlessness score (range: 0–4). The total effect of the SEM was calculated as the direct effect plus the indirect effects multiplied.

The structural equational model conceptual model used in this study shows potential direct and indirect effects of breathlessness severity on (1) satisfaction with sexual life or (2) impact of breathlessness on sexual life, with social, emotional or physical functioning as mediators.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by defining breathlessness as ‘chronic’ using the recently created International Classification of Disease definition (breathlessness lasting for >8 weeks).19 Because 30% of BMI data were missing, additional sensitivity analyses were also conducted by imputing the mean BMI for the logistic regressions.

All analyses were conducted using the software R, V.4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Except for the sensitivity analyses, no data were imputed and participants with missing data on variables used in the regressions and SEM models were excluded from those specific analyses (complete case analysis).

Ethics and reporting

Before participating, information relating to the study was provided to potential respondents. All respondents provided informed consent on three separate occasions: when first signing up to a Qualtrics panel; before commencing this particular survey online and by continued participation in the survey.

The study is reported in accordance with the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology guidelines for observational studies.20

The mean (SD) age was 45.4 (18.6) years; mean BMI was 27.1 (7.1) (table 1). A total of 4245 (42%) people reported mMRC ≥1 and a total of 1106 (11%) people reported mMRC ≥2.

Irrespective of the mMRC level, 1214 (12%) reported being very dissatisfied with their overall sexual life and 943 (9%) reported that breathlessness had impacted their overall sexual life (table 1). Participant characteristics by category of satisfaction by sex and breathlessness impacting sexual life are presented in cross-tables (online supplemental Tables 4 and 5).

The percentage of people who reported being very dissatisfied with overall sexual life increased for each level of mMRC: mMRC 0 (11%), mMRC 1 (14%), mMRC 2 (15%) and mMRC 3–4 (21%), as did the perceived impacts of breathlessness on overall sexual life (% yes): mMRC 0 (4%), mMRC 1 (13%), mMRC 2 (28%) and mMRC 3–4 (33%) (table 1).

Participants with missing data on satisfaction scale with overall sexual life (n=3008, 30%) had more women and were much older (online supplemental Table 3).

Associations of breathlessness severity with satisfaction with sexual life

Compared with no breathlessness (mMRC 0), there was an unadjusted association between mMRC 1 and satisfaction with overall sexual life (OR 1.65 (95% CI 1.51 to 1.81)) for an increase of one step of the Likert satisfaction scale. This association decreased when adjusting for age, sex and BMI: compared with no breathlessness (mMRC 0), there was an association between mMRC 1 and poorer satisfaction with overall sexual life (aOR 1.50 (95% CI 1.29 to 1.74)) for each one step increase in the Likert satisfaction scale (table 2). A similar adjusted association was also seen for mMRC 2 (aOR 1.35 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.72)) and mMRC 3–4 (aOR 1.76 (95% CI 1.13 to 2.76)).

Associations of breathlessness severity with impact on overall sexual life

Breathlessness severity was associated with greater impact on overall sexual life. In the unadjusted model compared with mMRC 0, mMRC 1 had an OR 4.04 (95% CI 3.40 to 4.80), mMRC 2 OR 13.00 (95% CI 10.50 to 16.20) and mMRC 3–4 OR 19.20 (95% CI 13.90 to 26.50). When adjusting for age, sex, BMI, rurality and primary self-reported cause of breathlessness, the associations remained significant: mMRC 1 aOR 1.84 (95% CI 1.36 to 2.47), mMRC 2 aOR 3.91 (95% CI 2.68 to 5.68) and mMRC 3–4 aOR 5.30 (95% CI 3.03 to 9.27; table 2). The proportional odds assumption was met for mMRC 1 (p=0.14) and mMRC 2 (p=0.07) but violated for mMRC 3–4 (p=0.04) in this model (online supplemental Table 6).

Analysis of factors potentially mediating satisfaction with sexual life and impacts on sexual life

The association between breathlessness severity and satisfaction with overall sexual life may be mediated through emotional functioning (beta coefficient: 0.04; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.05), but not social functioning (beta coefficient: −0.01; 95% CI −0.02 to 0.00) or physical functioning (beta coefficient: 0.00, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.01; table 3; figure 2). The percentage mediated through emotional functioning was 25% of the total association (beta coefficient: 0.16; 95% CI 0.11 to 0.22) corresponding to the direct and indirect associations. This suggests that approximately 25% of the total effect of breathlessness on satisfaction with sexual life may potentially be mediated through an indirect pathway, indicating that poorer emotional function may partly explain the association between breathlessness severity and worse satisfaction with overall sexual life. There was no mediating factor between breathlessness severity and impact of breathlessness on sexual life (beta coefficient: 0.00; 95% CI 0.00 to 0.00).

Sensitivity analysis

The direction and magnitude of estimates did not change when limiting the definition of breathlessness to ‘chronic’ (ie, >8 weeks in duration).19 After imputing the mean BMI for missing values, the ORs of the regressions were almost identical but with narrower CIs for all logistic regression models.

Discussion

The main finding of this novel study is that reporting worse breathlessness is associated with poorer satisfaction with sexual life. The high prevalence rates of dissatisfaction with overall sexual life and perceived impacts for people with breathlessness align with high rates of sexual dissatisfaction and impacts on sexual health reported by people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),21 asthma22 and poorer physical fitness.23

In this exploratory study, there may also be an indirect relationship between breathlessness and perceptions related to overall sexual life potentially mediated by emotional functioning, something not found for physical nor social functioning. This latter finding needs to be interpreted with care given the complex interactions between breathlessness and aspects of emotion including mood, self-esteem and intimacy, each of which may contribute to a person’s sexual life.24

The interplay between a person’s perceived satisfaction with their sexual life and a range of social and health factors is complicated.25 A wide variety of factors may underpin a person’s sexual life (eg, hormonal, physical, physiological, psycho-social), any of which may be compromised in people with chronic, complex conditions.26

Multiple co-morbidities and chronic conditions also increase the likelihood of sexual dysfunction.25 Our study was unable to explore the role of co-morbidities on overall sexual life. However, given that people with breathlessness often have more than one contributing cause for their symptom, the current findings align with the evidence and should be investigated further.

This study builds directly on a previous study in older Australians which showed similar direction and magnitude of relationship between breathlessness and, in the case of the earlier study, sexual inactivity. As breathlessness became more severe, the greater the likelihood that people had longer periods of sexual inactivity.9 The findings also reflect population surveys that indicate that almost one half of people with breathlessness are more likely to cease or reduce sexual activities.7 This is especially the case in people with mMRC 3–4.7 10

Strengths and limitations

This is a large, population-based study that reflects key demographic characteristics of the Australian population, independent of health service contact. Especially for people with moderate to severe breathlessness, an internet-based approach allows potentially a more representative population to participate. As the associations between breathlessness and satisfaction with sexual life decreased after adjusting for confounders such as rurality and self-reported cause of breathlessness, it is likely that these factors include associations that we did not measure and could be further explored, including the respondent’s socioeconomic status and other symptoms associated with the underlying causes of breathlessness.

A study of two highly subjective aspects of personhood – breathlessness and one’s sexual life – requires self-report. The web-based platform allows people to be more honest than they may be in face-to-face interviews for such sensitive issues. Future work can now be justified to explore people’s sexual lives in more detail.

This study used the unidimensional mMRC breathlessness scale, which is extensively used in population research and, as such, facilitates comparison between studies. Population studies have shown that higher mMRC scores correlate with higher scores in multidimensional breathlessness scales (Dyspnea-12 and Multidimensional Dyspnoea Profile).4

The SEM is a novel contribution as it sought to assess the primary relationships between breathlessness severity and perceived satisfaction with sexual life and any modulating effects of three key domains: physical, emotional and social function. Given the use of ordinal scales to evaluate breathlessness and functioning, beta coefficients might not be optimal for interpretation of these variables in the SEM as some categories are less reported. However, given the large sample size, the categories of the ordinal scales are well represented in the data. SEM relies on assumptions such as adequate sample size and distributional properties of the data, which may affect parameter estimates and model fit. In addition, the cross-sectional observational design limits any causal inference. Given that the proportional odds assumption was violated for some of the variables in the ordinal logistic regression, this particular result should be interpreted with caution. Physical functioning was not a significant mediator in the SEM analyses, and this may be due to the fact that mMRC already reflects more directly some physical consequences of breathlessness and is, therefore, less affected by a factor with which it is highly associated.

A limitation of the present study is that we only evaluated overall sexual life and satisfaction with it. Sexual life itself is a multi-faceted concept. The current findings justify a more detailed exploration of people’s sexual lives that should include specific elements relating to intimacy, sexual desire, people’s physical relationships and any impact on intimate partners.

We had no data in the current study from, or about, intimate partners.27 Qualitative findings indicate that the sexual well-being of caregivers of people with COPD is as affected as that of the person living with the illness because of difficulties experienced during sexual activities (including breathlessness and fatigue) as well as the risk of emotional distancing.28 29 In the context of advanced cancer, family caregivers who are the patients’ partners report lower sexual satisfaction compared with family caregivers who are not such partners.14 An association between higher sexual satisfaction and higher emotional functioning was also observed for family caregivers in this context.14 The impact of breathlessness on intimate partners of people experiencing chronic breathlessness should be investigated in qualitative and quantitative research. Understanding the perspectives of intimate partners of people whose sexual life has been affected will be pivotal in future work.

Implications for research, policy and practice

This study codifies another impact of breathlessness on the adult population. Although the presence of breathlessness can negatively affect sexual life,9 clinicians often do not ask people experiencing breathlessness about how satisfied they are with their sexual life.30 In people with COPD, 90% had never discussed their sexual function with their clinician, with more than one in three expressing a preference to have such discussions.21 Effective responses to managing chronic breathlessness and improving satisfaction with people’s sexual lives often require new perspectives and approaches for the patient, their intimate partner(s) and their team of health professionals31 32 if these complex issues are to be addressed sensitively and effectively. Comprehensive care focused on the whole person requires that in people with breathlessness or those who are at risk of experiencing breathlessness (given its invisibility),33 clinicians should ask about the symptom and, in the light of the current findings, any impacts on a person’s overall sexual life. Early identification of concerns and associated psychological distress relating to a person’s sexual life can guide timely interventions, including counselling, pulmonary rehabilitation and practical strategies for intimacy. This should be underpinned by a supportive and responsive person-centred approach to sensitively and competently address these complex issues.31

There are sufficient signals in this study about impairment to sexual life to expand the details sought in future studies. Given the contribution of sexuality to many people’s overall life satisfaction, following people longitudinally to explore how sexual life and satisfaction change over time could add a more nuanced understanding of how breathlessness progressively affects a person’s life and how this may impact their relationships overall.

Conclusions

Increasing breathlessness severity is associated with worse perceived satisfaction with sexual life. Breathlessness was more frequently reported to impact on people’s overall sexual lives with more severe breathlessness. Emotional functioning may mediate part of the relationship between breathlessness and perceptions about an individual’s satisfaction with sexual life.
