Aligning observation to action: Integrated monitoring for tropical seascape conservation and restoration
Authors: Susana Carvalho, Walter A Rich, Yusuf C El-Khaled, Darren J Coker, Eva Aylagas, Morgan Bennett-Smith, Michael L Berumen, Ronald Cadiz, Joao Curdia, Burton H Jones, Ute Langner, John K Pearman, Florian Roth, Rodrigo Villalobos, David J Suggett, Raquel S Peixoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTropical coastal ecosystems are rapidly degrading due to climate change and local pressures, while current monitoring efforts remain fragmented, habitat specific, and disconnected from management. This paper advocates for integrated, seascape-scale observatories that link biodiversity with ecosystem function and translate observations into scientific-driven management actions. It identifies key gaps in existing monitoring systems, including limited scope, methodological inconsistency, and weak policy relevance, and proposes a transferable framework built around core ecological indicators, adaptable technology tiers, and a FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) web-Geographic Information System platform for early warning, policy application, and community engagement. The framework includes a phased roadmap connecting local pilot sites to regional and global networks, embedding governance mechanisms for long-term sustainability. By aligning monitoring with restoration planning and adaptive management, this proposed model enhances restoration success and innovation. Global implementation, especially in developing regions, will require sustained funding, capacity building, and coordinated leadership from international organizations such as the United Nations to ensure equitable, durable outcomes.Subject areas: environmental science, nature conservation, ecology, aquatic science, oceanography

Introduction

Tropical coastal ecosystems, including coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and mangrove forests, provide essential goods and services such as coastal protection,1,2,3 climate regulation,3,4 water quality,5 enhanced biodiversity,6,7,8,9 fisheries,10,11 and carbon sequestration,12 directly and indirectly supporting the livelihoods and wellbeing of over a billion people.10,11,12,13,14,15 Collectively, these ecosystems form part of the tropical seascape,16,17,18 complex mosaics of interconnected and often interdependent natural habitats known as one of the richest vaults of marine biodiversity. Over the past decade, increasing attention has been paid to habitat connectivity within these seascapes,19,20 highlighting how ecological linkages among reefs, seagrasses, and mangroves enhance functioning and the delivery of goods and services.18,21,22

Despite their value to humanity, tropical coastal marine ecosystems are experiencing unprecedented degradation,23,24,25,26 driven independently or synergistically by climate change, habitat destruction, pollution, and overharvesting. Projections indicate that coral reefs may no longer sustain positive net carbonate production by 2100,3,27 while biodiversity loss will restrict the ocean’s ability to deliver food provision or maintenance of water quality and coastal protection.28,29 Securing a more positive future requires a dual strategy: mitigating global CO2 emissions and local stressors while implementing targeted restoration initiatives.30,31 Critically, both strategies necessitate a fundamental shift that accounts for inherent ecosystem connectivity.

Substantial investments have been directed toward active ecosystem recovery (spanning restoration, mitigation, and remediation approaches, sensu Suggett et al.,32 aiming to counteract the current trajectory of degradation30,33,34,35,36,37,38). However, these efforts are often conducted in isolation, focusing on single habitats or species. Such fragmented approaches fail to account for the seascape connectivity, and, therefore, opportunities to amplify conservation and restoration outcomes are missed.18,39 Degradation in one habitat can propagate across the seascape, magnifying impacts elsewhere.21,29,40,41 For example, the loss of coral cover and structural complexity following mass bleaching reduces wave attenuation, increasing hydrodynamic energy, sediment resuspension, and turbidity in nearshore environments.42,43 These changes directly impair adjacent seagrass meadows by reducing light availability, constraining photosynthesis and productivity, shifting microbial communities, and impacting seagrass cover44,45,46 and ultimately carbon sequestration.21,36,47 Similar cascading effects have been documented where mangrove deforestation and poor wastewater management interact with coastal development, further degrading connected habitats.48 These examples underscore the limitations of segregated management efforts and emphasize the need for ecosystem-based marine spatial planning (EB-MSP) frameworks supported by integrated monitoring and observation systems capable of capturing seascape-scale processes.

Here, we address persistent shortcomings in current tropical monitoring efforts that limit decision-making capacity to guide restoration and conservation effectively. We propose a scalable, flexible framework for integrated seascape observatories that combine classical and advanced monitoring tools, tailored to local capacity and underpinned by a core of ecological indicators. These indicators, when linked to FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles and user-friendly web-Geographic Information System (GIS) platforms, enable visualization, early warning detection, and policy-relevant reporting. A phased roadmap connects pilot observatories to regional and global networks, embedding governance mechanisms to ensure long-term sustainability. By aligning monitoring outputs with restoration planning and adaptive management across connected habitats, this framework enhances the likelihood of durable restoration outcomes and strengthens resilience at scale.

From fragmented actions to EB-MSP: The role of long-term seascape observatories

When applied to marine observatories, EB-MSP offers a pathway to overcome habitat fragmentation in management and conservation by integrating ecological processes, cumulative pressures, and societal objectives across space and time. However, in many tropical regions, EB-MSP is constrained by discontinuous monitoring, inconsistent indicators, limited long-term datasets, and weak coordination across institutions and actors. As a result, interventions are often opportunist rather than evidence led, limiting the ability to prioritize sites, evaluate trade-offs, and adapt management as conditions change.

Integrated, long-term seascape observatories provide the empirical backbone needed to operationalize EB-MSP. By generating spatially explicit indicators of biodiversity, habitat condition, ecosystem functioning, and cumulative land-sea pressure across connected habitats, observatories support three core planning functions: (1) identify priority areas for protection and risk mitigation; (2) select feasible and cost-effective restoration sites with the highest likelihood of persistence; and (3) update zoning, thresholds, and permitted activities through adaptive management. In this context, monitoring becomes a decision-support system linking observations to management triggers and policy action.

While effective ecosystem recovery strategies begin with monitoring,49 persistent barriers limit the effectiveness of monitoring programs in many tropical systems. Historical baselines are frequently absent, human and financial resources are limited, and existing efforts often vary in protocol, spatial coverage, and temporal discontinuity, which results in the production of patchy databases that hinder trend detection, comparability, connectivity assessments, and robust risk analyses.49,50,51 As a result, restoration trajectories and success cannot be fully evaluated50,51,52,53 such that fragmented habitat-specific frameworks developed for coral reefs,49 seagrasses54 and mangroves55 remain difficult to translate into coordinated seascape management. Importantly, evidence from tropical systems shows that restoration outcomes improve when monitoring, spatial planning, and governance are aligned. Mangrove conservation efforts that integrated spatial planning, sustained monitoring, and community engagement have slowed mangrove loss and enhanced carbon permanence (reviewed by Friess et al.56), whereas coral restoration efforts implemented without parallel water-quality management or spatial prioritization frequently fail to deliver durable outcomes.57,58 Together, these contrasts underscore the urgency of embed restoration within EB-MSP frameworks supported by integrated, long-term observatories. While, recognizing the importance of tracking impact, conservation practitioners and scientist have developed tools like the mangrove restoration tracker tool (MRTT)—a platform incorporating multiple layers of information that can be used for evidence-based restoration decisions59—similar platforms incorporating multiple habitats are needed.

Despite clear need for long-term programs, they are notoriously difficult to sustain; even where initiatives expand (e.g., from one to many sites), continuity is often uneven, and instrument failures commonly introduce gaps. For example, a western Pacific in situ temperature series that began in 1999 with one site, as of 2020, has expanded to 70 stations spanning 700 km,60 yet most sites have not been monitored for the entire duration. Similarly, NOAA’s Pacific RAMP program has surveyed dozens of reefs across US territorial possessions in the western Pacific Ocean since 2000 but with irregular site coverage, producing patchworks of data across years. Even continuous time series of physico-chemical variables are often riddled with gaps due to instrument failure or recovery issues. Larger consortia, such as CARICOMP network in the Caribbean, highlight additional difficulties in coordinating diverse institutions to standardize methodologies, leading to inconsistencies across coral reef, mangrove, and seagrass datasets within the same umbrella and region.61 Moreover, many regional efforts remain disconnected from global programs targeting marine observations, such as the Global Ocean Observing System (GOOS; https://goosocean.org), Coral Reef Ecosystem Integrated Observing System (CREIOS; https://doi.org/10.5270/OceanObs09), the Marine Biodiversity Observation Network (MBON), or even the MarineGeo (https://marinegeo.si.edu), reducing their value for cross-scale synthesis.

A combined approach is, therefore, essential: top-down standardization of core variables (to ensure comparability across sites and scales), protocols, and data formats (to ensure interoperability), avoiding “patchwork” fragmented efforts, complemented with bottom-up flexibility that accommodates local management needs and ecological contexts, as well as logistical and resource constraints. This hybrid model improves feasibility, continuity, and stakeholder engagement while enabling regional and global synthesis. Recent advances in community-based monitoring further demonstrate how such integration can support both efficiency and sustainability of observing systems,62 evidenced by cases such as the Great Reef Census63 and MERMAID (https://datamermaid.org/).

Moving from concept to implementation requires coordinated investments in pilot observatories, harmonized indicator sets, and governance mechanisms that explicitly link monitoring outputs to spatial planning decisions and management thresholds. Without this integration, restoration risks remaining reactive, fragmented, and vulnerable to the cumulative pressures it seeks to mitigate. The Seychelles provides a real-world example of an EB-MSP transformative blue economy model implemented at a national scale in a tropical seascape64 (also refer to the study by Hunt and Hilborn65). A key component of this approach is a debt-for nature swap (i.e., the partial cancellation of a country’s external debt in exchange for governmental commitment to redirect those financial resources toward local conservation and environmental management initiatives) and a subsequent issuance of a blue bond. The country committed to protecting 30% of its exclusive economic zone (EZZ; ∼1.35 million km2), integrating biodiversity conservation, fisheries, tourism, and climate adaptation within a single, science-informed and participatory spatial planning framework (Seychelles Marine Spatial Plan Initiative 202066). Such achievements were enabled by strong political leadership, international partnerships, and innovative financing mechanisms, including the establishment of the Seychelles Conservation and Climate Change Adaptation Trust (SeyCCAT; Conservation and Climate Adaptation Trust of Seychelles Act, 2015).

The Seychelles’ example similarly highlights persistent challenges for EB-MSP in tropical contexts, including maintaining the political momentum across electoral cycles, clarifying institutional mandates, building public-sector capacity, ensuring stakeholder participation, and sustaining adaptive management beyond initial financing cycles.64,67,68 Thus, while EB-MSP can successfully align conservation ambition, finance, and spatial governance, its long-term effectiveness, particularly for coral reef resilience, depends on sustained, integrated monitoring, explicit consideration of cumulative land-sea pressures, and continued investment in local institutional capacity. Without these enabling conditions, restoration and conservation efforts risk remaining fragmented, reactive, and vulnerable to cumulative pressures,69 with some authors downplaying the positive effects of the blue finance model applied.65 Compounding these challenges, communication and perception gaps, driven by limited transparency, highly technical framing, and unclear articulation of local benefits have reduced stakeholder ownership of the blue economy agenda, underscoring the need for clearer narratives, accessible data, and participatory communication strategies alongside EB-MSP implementation.64

The challenge: Breaking the silos toward integrated ecosystem-based restoration of coastal habitats

Despite growing investment in active restoration, major gaps remain between available funding, ecological needs, and realized outcomes. Effective global marine conservation is estimated to require ∼USD $174.52 billion annually, yet current commitments remain far lower (∼USD $25.5 billion).70 Active restoration alone is unlikely to scale sufficiently under current approaches: restoring even 10% of degraded coral reefs would cost around USD $1 billion (at ∼USD 6,000 per hectare), nearly four times the global investment to date.71 Consequently, alternative approaches are needed, namely embedding restoration within broader ecosystem-based management frameworks that integrate protection, impact mitigation, and spatial prioritization.

Restoration practice remains dominated by single-habitat and species-specific interventions, despite increasing evidence that seascape-scale, multi-habitat, and multi-species strategies enhance resilience and recovery.32,33,72,73,74 For example, the creation of oyster reefs promoted seagrass cover within five years of restoration.75 Yet, fewer than 25% of projects target multiple habitats, and <1% jointly address seagrass and coral reefs or coral reefs and mangroves.38 Even within habitats, limited species diversity is often used, potentially reducing ecological functioning and resilience.76 In contrast, studies have shown that functional complementarity and multi-species approaches can improve restoration outcomes, or even the natural colonization of untargeted species, including in seagrass,77,78,79 and coral transplantation efforts.80

Effective restoration at seascape scales requires explicit consideration of habitat connectivity, land-sea interactions, and climate-driven stressors, alongside strategies that combine passive recovery with targeted interventions. For example, improvements in water quality can stimulate seagrass recovery—an effect further strengthened when combined with oyster and seagrass restoration—leading to broader habitat stabilization and ecosystem recovery.81 However, robust monitoring before, during, and after restoration remains essential to track ecological trajectories beyond simple survival or growth metrics, capturing biodiversity-ecosystem function relationships and ecosystem service delivery across connected habitats.49,82,83,84 Many projects still lack adequate baselines, controls, field-based pilot experiments, and long-term monitoring, limiting the ability to distinguish restoration success from natural recovery.32,49,85,86

Systemic limitations in robustly documenting successful restoration contributes perceptions of highly variable outcomes.87 Current databases (until 2020) for coral restoration projects suggest that >30% of all projects are unable to demonstrate success due to inadequate goal and target setting, poor planning, insufficient maintenance, adequate monitoring plans, and/or failure to mitigate local stressors.49,71,82 Seagrass restoration similarly often underperforms due to poor site selection.77,88,89 Strengthening decision frameworks, including habitat risk assessments and differentiated strategies,90,91,92 is critical for improving feasibility and effectiveness across the seascapes.

Ultimately, advancing ecosystem-based restoration will depend on coordinated planning, sustained monitoring, and stakeholder engagement supported by decision-ready data. Embedding observatories within EB-MSP frameworks can move restoration and conservation beyond fragmented interventions toward adaptive seascape-scale solutions that safeguard biodiversity, ecosystem services, and human wellbeing.

Recognizing biodiversity-function relationships at seascape scales, including both positive and negative ecological spillovers, is critical for ensuring that restoration delivers durable outcomes. Comprehensive monitoring programs spanning organism-to-ecosystem processes provide actionable evidence needed to anticipate emerging threats and guide adaptive management. For example, conservation of seagrass-associated green turtles can inadvertently accelerate habitat degradation, with cascading consequences for fisheries and ecosystem services93 (and references therein). Conversely, recent slowdowns in global mangrove loss have been linked to enhanced monitoring, localized management, and increased community engagement.94,95 Such monitoring is also critical for early detection of invasive species, which are often overlooked until establishment, despite global economic costs exceeding USD $10.3 trillion from 1970 to 2020.96 Effective observatories must, therefore, capture ecological interactions and ecosystem service flows, rather than focusing solely on species survival or habitat extent. Integrating robust socio-environmental reporting, stakeholder participation, and Indigenous knowledge can further enhance legitimacy, learning, and long-term continuity. Although emerging platforms such as MERMAID (https://datamermaid.org/) and Great Reef Census (https://greatreefcensus.org/) provide valuable tools for reef monitoring, they are reef specific and constrained by limited historical baselines. Ultimately, transitioning toward integrated, ecosystem-based restoration requires coordinated seascape observatories that explicitly quantify Biodiversity-Ecosystem Functioning (BEF) dynamics across connected habitats.

Unifying biodiversity and function: The next generation of marine observatories across the seascape

Marine monitoring programs have traditionally relied on classical monitoring tools and indicators of change such as coral cover or isolated physico-chemical parameters, likely due to the availability of suitable tools for long-term, cost-effective, and widespread data collection (Figure 1). In turn, such metrics often frame how success is viewed in restoration activities, with most programs targeting growth and survival of re-introduced taxa (for corals82). In a recent meta-analysis, the whole spectrum of “survival” of re-introduced marine habitat-forming species was assessed as the most common indicator of restoration success.87 While results of this meta-analysis presents successful marine ecosystem restoration practice, it obscures restoration meaningfulness in terms of ecosystem functioning and service provision. Overlooking such an important aspect may be problematic, as, for example, the functioning of coastal ecosystems is intrinsically linked to its associated biodiversity. However, the comprehensive understanding of this relationship across biomes is not always straightforward. For example, in coral reef ecosystems, Brandl et al.97 proposed eight complementary ecological processes: calcium carbonate production and bioerosion; primary production and herbivory; secondary production and predation; and nutrient uptake and release. In light of ecosystem functioning, these processes are not limited to coral reefs but extend to other habitats. Both seagrasses and mangroves have been rendered as critical players in global blue carbon balances,44 considered as the functional equivalent to “calcium carbonate production” in coral reefs. We contend that the tools and measures to detect and quantify these ecological processes are not limited in their application to a single ecosystem. Instead, they can easily be utilized to assess the functioning and biodiversity in connected ecosystems across habitats, which is essential to consider net service provision across the connected seascape (Figure 1), collectively forming a framework for what we consider, and term here, as “marine observatories” for sustained ecosystem assessment.

Overview of classical (top) and technology-reliant and innovative monitoring and restoration tools (middle) regularly applied in marine ecosystems

Classical monitoring tools listed here generally (1) ensure long-term feasibility due to lower complexity, required manpower, and/or monetary capacities and effort but (2) provide less information output. Technology-reliant and innovative monitoring tools, including ecosystem restoration and intervention approaches, are generally more complex, require higher efforts and capacities, and are often less feasible for long-term monitoring programs despite a higher information output. A combination of both classical and technology-reliant and innovative tools is profound to establish observatories across the seascape (bottom) and to address critical biodiversity and ecosystem function knowledge to support scientifically sound decision-making.

We propose that marine observatories should include a range of tools that require distinct levels of resourcing including (1) classical monitoring techniques and (2) technology-reliant and innovative monitoring tools (Figure 1). Generally, classical monitoring tools offer increased feasibility over long periods of time (decades) due to the selection of basic assessments such as visual surveys, basic remote sensing, or environmental parameters and water chemistry investigations. Such tools provide valuable baseline information about the respective marine habitats, while requiring limited equipment only, hence offering great opportunities, particularly for remote observatories with limited funding and capacity. Where historical monitoring records are absent, marine observatories can establish retrospective baselines by harnessing paleoecological proxies from sediment cores and other archives as well as historical and local ecological knowledge to reconstruct prior ecosystem states. Such approaches have been widely applied in marine systems to fill data gaps and inform reference conditions for restoration and management where long-term monitoring is lacking (e.g., employing sedimentary proxies to extend ecological records beyond instrumental data and integrating qualitative historical sources to contextualize ecosystem change).98,99,100 Traditionally, classical monitoring efforts have been based on in situ observations and sample collection (in some cases supported by satellite imagery) to construct estimates of biodiversity (e.g., reef fishes and invertebrates, and seagrass species composition and cover) and its potential drivers (environmental variables and direct pressures). Monitoring coastal communities through time provides a key tool to identify changes and, therefore, estimate rates and predict trajectories and magnitudes of change. As such, classical monitoring tools are the backbone of marine observatories and, hence, the core to many innovative global monitoring programs (e.g., MERMAID, Great Reef Census, and Global Mangrove Watch; https://www.globalmangrovewatch.org/).

While classical methods of data acquisition are important to understand changes within marine observatories over time, they often have a limited scope due to a defined output of information and low taxa resolution. Technology-reliant and innovative monitoring tools provide opportunities to overcome limitations of more classical methodologies. For example, using advanced remote sensing tools, such as remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) or drones, allows for the investigation of inaccessible (e.g., remote) habitats—both above and below water—with minimum disturbance.101,102 Together with artificial intelligence (AI) and automated image recognition, benthic biodiversity and its changes over time can be assessed on scales that are beyond the capacities of classical monitoring tools.59,103,104 Monitoring frequency within marine observatories is inherently dependent on the scale and process. Continuous measurements (e.g., temperature, light, oxygen, or carbon flux sensors) can capture high-frequency environmental variability, whereas biodiversity surveys, functional assessments, and habitat mapping typically require periodic campaigns that may be seasonal or annual depending on life histories and management objectives. In practice, optimal observatory design often integrates continuous environmental sensing with recurring ecological surveys, enabling both near-real-time change detection and long-term trend assessment across habitats.

While ROVs open previously inaccessible and, hence, “hidden” ecosystem locations, traditionally overlooked “hidden” biodiversity (i.e., microbial, early life stages, or cryptic diversity) can be targeted further by using cutting-edge sequencing technologies. Being scalable and replicable, the use of environmental DNA (eDNA) to target specific species (via digital PCR105) or communities (via DNA metabarcoding106) can provide an integrated assessment of habitat biodiversity across the Tree of Life,107 with enhanced resolution for the detection of biodiversity patterns across relatively small spatial scales.108 One of the advantages of eDNA is the relatively easy collection of data in the field since a low amount of environmental material (sediment or water samples) is needed to isolate eDNA, followed by now widely standardized “standard operating procedures.” Further, a combination of non-destructive physical collecting devices such as autonomous reef monitoring structures (ARMS) and eDNA sequencing approaches have enabled a deep characterization of the cryptic diversity of rocky reefs.109,110,111 eDNA has been successfully used across marine habitats to characterize communities and track biodiversity changes,112,113,114 and it can be particularly useful in areas of difficult access or low visibility. Such tools can (1) support the investigation of cryptic biodiversity/communities across different habitats; (2) potentially reveal overlooked biodiversity reservoirs and identify regions of high conservation importance; and (3) help in early detection of species, such as marine pests, that can compromise the success of restoration and rehabilitation efforts.115,116,117 Moreover, a direct assessment of the functioning of certain key processes offers a repetitive, non-invasive investigation of tropical marine habitats.

Quantifying organism metabolism using bio-optical systems118,119 or multi-probe in situ arrays (e.g., community in situ metabolism and energetics [CISME120]) allows the acquisition of physiological data that provide a better link to critical ecosystem service functions, including primary production, calcification, and thermal thresholds. In effect, such tools “take the pulse of environmental status” of any given ecosystem, with obvious seascape application across coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and mangrove forests under natural or disturbed scenarios.118,121,122,123

When combined, classical tools and modern technology-reliant and innovative approaches have proven useful in identifying changes that build or erode resilience to alternative ecosystem states124,125 and can, thereby, aid as early warning indicators of ecosystem collapse. Ultimately, the combination of both classical and modern and technology-reliant approaches is obvious by their complementary uses and their ability to provide invaluable information regarding the status of the marine environment,110,123,126,127 and they can be chosen depending on monetary capacities, research interest, expertise, or human power. Therefore, marine observatories should be built upon diversity and ecosystem functioning approaches, rather than focusing on biodiversity or functionality alone by using approaches that are suitable and applicable across habitats and ecosystems (Figure 1). Environmental and climate change, as well as mitigation, restoration, and recovery efforts, need to be incorporated into biodiversity and ecosystem functioning to tackle future and modern-world issues, determine patterns, and forecast trajectories.

Overall, marine observatories can also work as test beds to test innovative approaches. An example in which such efforts are integrated into a marine observatory is the Red Sea coral probiotics village.128 This local pilot observatory offers a unique opportunity for the deployment, monitoring, and improvement of coral rehabilitation tools, as it offers not only the local baseline ecological data of the reef but also a realistic, monitored, well-controlled, and automated field laboratory where these strategies can be tested and evaluated at a reef scale. For example, several combinations of restoration approaches (or “tools”) that have been chosen to consider a more holistic coral reef restoration approach are currently being investigated. Coral arks (Figure 1) stand out as particularly promising innovation. They offer a versatile platform for integrating a range of restoration and monitoring tools (as outlined in Figure 1), including the deployment of primed ARMS. Combining multiple restoration approaches (or “tools”) may enable more holistic, ecosystem-based intervention strategies, shifting the focus beyond single-species targets, but realizing this potential requires appropriate tools, monitoring, and governance to translate strategy into effective restoration. Taken together, outcomes and ongoing research of this platform can be used and/or replicated worldwide, which can contribute to the development of customized and efficient recovery strategies. Thereby, these outcomes are crucial to meet the urgent need to advance the development, optimization, and scaling of these nature-based solutions, ensuring that they are deployed in a tailored and context-specific manner that reflects the unique historical, ecological, and environmental characteristics of each target area.129,130,131

Monitoring tools are mapped against different biological organization levels, from sub-organism to ecosystem (Figure 2), showing their range of applicability. Visual surveys and basic remote sensing primarily capture ecosystem- and habitat-level attributes, while water chemistry extends to sub-organism responses. Advanced remote sensing (e.g., satellites and autonomous platforms) broadens coverage across the ecosystem and habitat levels. Airplanes and drones extend monitoring to finer spatial scales, and soundscapes provide insights into community diversity and ecosystem function. DNA-based assessments link habitat, population, organism, and sub-organism levels, providing high-resolution biodiversity and functional insights. AI supports multi-level integration and automated analyses. Tools focused on functioning and health address organism-to-ecosystem scales, while ARMS are specialized for cryptic biodiversity and habitat-level processes. This framework emphasizes that no single method captures all dimensions of the seascape biodiversity and function; instead, a multi-tool, cross-scale approach is essential to understand and manage ecosystem status effectively. These indicators, either used in isolation or combined for an ecosystem-based approach, could be integrated into marine observatories following standardized protocols to promote interoperability. Together, these indicators enable a consistent cross-habitat assessment of biodiversity, ecosystem function, and environmental drivers at seascape scales, while remaining adaptable to local monitoring capacity and management priorities. In this context, initiatives, such as the Marine Assessment Framework (MAF) Toolbox (https://open-research-europe.ec.europa.eu/collections/maf-toolbox/about), illustrate good practice by providing open- and easy-access, standardized protocols and operational tools that support the early implementation of marine seascape observatories. Such resources are valuable especially because monitoring methods and guidance are often fragmented and rarely shared consistently across interconnected coastal habitats.

Fostering science-based decision-making: Data archive and online GIS dashboards

Compelled by the need for clearer, faster, and more efficient communication not only among scientists but also with stakeholders and policymakers, centralized and searchable databases linked to web-GIS platforms can provide the operational backbone for integrated observatories. These systems connect multi-source observations across tropical seascapes (including biodiversity, ecosystem functioning, environmental stressors, and water quality) with local and Indigenous knowledge streams, such as participatory mapping and community-defined indicators (Figure 3). Together, these inputs can be synthesized within a shared dashboard environment to support mapping, visualization, scenario analyses, and the delivery of policy-relevant information as web services (web maps and apps).

Most existing platforms remain habitat segregated (e.g., MERMAID for coral reefs; The Mangrove Alliance for mangroves; and Seagrass Watch for seagrasses, https://www.seagrasswatch.org), highlighting the need for seascape-scale integration that explicitly links connected ecosystems within EB-MSP frameworks (Figure 3). Easy access to harmonized information is especially critical in high-urgency contexts requiring a rapid response. With the advancements of underwater communication153 and the growing deployment of autonomous underwater vehicle and sensor networks, automated data transmission to centralized dashboards increasingly enables near real-time tracking of environmental parameters. When paired with defined decision thresholds, such systems can generate early warnings that activate reactive management responses, support emergency actions (e.g., pollution events and introduction of a potential invasive species), and guide mitigation strategies. Embedding FAIR data principles, as encouraged by the UN Ocean Decade, further ensures that observatories contribute not only to local decision-making but also to the global knowledge infrastructure, reinforcing their value for coordinated conservation and adaptive management at multiple scales.

In many regions, in situ monitoring remains fragmented across teams, objectives, and governance mandates. A centralized visualization and decision-support hub (Figure 3) can offer unique opportunity to align efforts through standardized protocols, identify knowledge gaps, share metadata practices, and the prioritization of core indicators that remain comparable across sites and time frames. This reduces the risk of becoming data rich but insight poor by ensuring that monitoring outputs are interoperable, accessible, and explicitly linked to management needs.

Analyses conducted across spatial and temporal scales can reveal meaningful ecological patterns, such as long-term shifts in habitat extent (e.g., seagrass or mangrove coverage154,155) or fluctuations in fish biomass across habitats,156,157 as well as stability and resilience in coastal ecosystems.158,159,160 These correlations provide important evidence of change but, on their own, do not necessarily identify the underlying causes. Stronger links with drivers require the integrating ecological datasets with environmental variables, including indicators of human pressure such as fishing intensity, pollution, or coastal population density. For instance, linking photo-quadrat-derived benthic community composition with mapped fishing pressure strengthens the weight of evidence attributing shifts in community structure to anthropogenic impacts,161 while water quality data can help explain broader ecosystem responses shifts.162,163 Such integrative analyses enable observatories to move beyond descriptive monitoring toward identifying likely drivers of change, supporting cumulative impact assessments that connect species and habitat sensitivity with human activities and ultimately providing actionable evidence for coastal policy and management (Figure 3).

Equally important is ensuring that observatory outputs remain accessible and usable for non-expert audiences. User-friendly dashboards and communication tools can support transparency, community engagement, and shared ownership of monitoring outcomes, including through smartphone-based contributions, trust, and equitable access, ensuring that observatories function not only as data repositories but also as inclusive early warning and decision-support systems that link observations directly to action.

Opportunities and challenges of integrated marine observatories in increasingly threatened coastal environments

As climate extremes and local pressures continue to accelerate the degradation of coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and mangroves, integrated marine observatories are emerging as essential infrastructures for tracking change, supporting restoration, and informing decision-making across connected seascapes. Realizing this potential, however, requires overcoming persistent barriers related to funding, capacity, data governance, and long-term continuity. Recognizing the need for effective restoration solutions that can operate across basins and jurisdictions, and, critically, for mechanisms that translate scientific advances into tangible management action, recent European Union (EU) Horizon Research and Innovation calls provide a strong example of this shift. In particular, the “living labs for co-creating solutions for ecosystem restoration” framework explicitly promotes coordinated interventions across habitats and environmental gradients, supported by the active involvement of multiple stakeholders. These initiatives emphasize the adaptation of monitoring tools to local contexts in order to ensure robust assessments of environmental condition and to track restoration progress over time. Such approaches are essential for demonstrating the effectiveness of developed solutions and facilitating their dissemination and uptake by practitioners (HORIZON-CL6-2026-01-BIODIV-01-two-stage; European Commission Decision C (2025) 8493).

Opportunities

Outreach and promote education

Environmental awareness and education activities are of utmost importance, especially in regions where access to formal environmental education remains limited. To ensure broad and inclusive communication, outreach materials should be developed in both the local language and English, complemented by interactive and multi-lingual online platforms. Similar approaches in marine conservation programs (e.g., Reef Check Mediterranean Sea; https://www.reefcheckmed.org/espa%C3%B1ol/) have proven effective in increasing public understanding, participation, and long-term engagement. These efforts will raise awareness, strengthen community engagement, and motivate local stewardship of coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and mangrove ecosystems. While it is vital to engage people across all age groups, education and capacity building should target efforts with younger generations to ensure that the benefits of environmental education persist over time.164 Partnerships with local public schools, educational institutions, and training centers shall support curriculum-linked activities, hands-on learning, and career awareness in marine and environmental sciences. Importantly, English language classes have been identified by local communities as essential to their full engagement in marine conservation initiatives,165 particularly given the dominance of English language communication in international science and conservation. Such capacity building is especially relevant for public engagement and participation during transitions from destructive to non-destructive livelihoods (e.g., from fishing to tourist-based activities). Community-based activities, including beach clean-ups, fish-market surveys, and presentations to local audiences can provide practical and accessible opportunities for participation and learning. Comparable coastal and marine programs have demonstrated that participatory approaches not only enhance scientific data collection but also strengthen community ownership of conservation outcomes (e.g., Seagrass-Watch166 and Reef Check167). Engagement will be further strengthened through the involvement of trusted public figures, such as local community leaders, educators, and cultural icons, who can act as local champions. Evidence from marine conservation initiatives shows that engagement led by respected figures locally (e.g., Selayar Island, South Sulawesi Province, Indonesia)168,169 and globally (e.g., Sylvia Earle TED Prize winner and the Mission Blue) can significantly amplify outreach messages and conservation efforts,168 bridging the gap between science and society and fostering a stronger sense of shared responsibility for coastal habitat protection.

Community science

Community science activities have been expanding worldwide and are key approaches to increasing the frequency and coverage of observations regionally and globally. Some of the programs, such as the Reef Life Survey (https://reeflifesurvey.com), Reef Check (https://www.reefcheck.org), and Great Reef Census (https://greatreefcensus.org) focus on monitoring of reef health, while others are dedicated toward restoration (e.g., Rescue a Reef170). Some of these programs have already generated meaningful results about long-term changes in coral reefs.171 Involving community members directly in research programs decreases the gap between researchers and society, empowers them to protect coral reefs, improves science communication, and develops environmental literacy. In coordination with the tourism and recreation industry and supported by existing apps, information for flagship species such as manta rays, sea turtles, dugongs, and sharks can be uploaded and used to support research on their distribution, aggregation spots, and nesting areas that are critical to guide informed management and conservation actions. Community members can also play a critical role in the early detection of invasive species and bleaching observations, which can help direct resources toward areas of relevance. Research has reported that community science platforms can indicate the presence of invasive species, even earlier than or in the time frame as official channels.172 Advances in machine learning and the increasing availability of underwater digital devices will foster interactions between citizens and scientists as well as the accuracy of the data generated.

Limited effective protection of marine resources

Many countries are likely to fall well short of commitments for biodiversity preservation, which is inevitably tied to their capacity to effectively monitor and report (Kunming-Montreal173). In many cases where Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) are established, enforcement is limited or inefficient.174 In addition, a careful design and implementation of MPAs require a sound understanding of, for example, biodiversity changes across spatial and temporal scales and genetic connectivity, which is also often not available. The dual nature of local and global pressures that cause the degradation of coastal habitats and the limited physical barriers require collective and concerted actions across multiple jurisdictions. Unfortunately, unlike some concerted efforts observed in temperate regions facilitated by the existence of political structures, such as those provided by the EU, tropical regions largely lack cooperative research programs and transboundary consortiums dedicated to marine conservation. This absence of regional coordination may compromise the success of conservation plans established at national levels. Notably, within the EU, two key policy instruments, the Marine Strategy Framework Directive (MSFD; Directive 2008/56/EC) and the Water Framework Directive (WFD; Directive 2000/60/EC), have played a central role in harmonizing environmental monitoring and management across member states. Both directives aim to achieve good environmental status (GES) of marine and transition waters by addressing multiple descriptors, including different aspects of biodiversity (e.g., phytoplankton and zooplankton communities, macroalgae, fish, marine invertebrates, and non-Indigenous species), as well as temporal and spatial variability of ecological parameters (European Parliament and Council, 2000, 2008). One of the strengths of the MSFD and WFD lies in their centralized coordination under EU governance, ensuring consistency and comparability through intercalibration exercises and shared methodological frameworks among signatory countries. Moreover, the effectiveness of these directives has been significantly improved by dedicated EU funding mechanisms, such as the LIFE program and Horizon Europe, which are critical to support long-term monitoring, capacity building, and implementation of ecosystem-based management strategies. These initiatives exemplify how transnational governance combined with sustained financial and technical support can facilitate large-scale, integrated environmental management–an approach that remains largely underdeveloped in tropical contexts.

Technological development

Advances in marine monitoring technologies increasingly enable integrated, real-time assessment of ecosystem state across biological and physical dimensions, with data products moving from periodic reporting toward near real-time decision support where telemetry is available. Tools can be grouped into three complementary categories (1) biologging and animal-borne sensing,175 (2) molecular early-warning (eDNA),127 and (3) autonomous and networked observing platforms. Biologging, including animal-borne tags equipped with miniaturized multi-variable sensors (e.g., acoustic, chemical, and optical), can provide animal-directed sampling of the environment while simultaneously informing animal behavior, habitat use, and exposure history. Coordinated networks such as AniBOS/MEOP show how animal-borne observations can contribute to broader ocean observing efforts176 (also the MEOP consortium, https://www.meop.net/). Molecular approaches, including eDNA assays and metabarcoding, can support cost-effective surveillance for biodiversity change and the early detection of rare or invasive taxa (but we note the need for careful QA/QC and interpretation.127,177,178 At broader spatial scales, autonomous and networked systems, including sensor-equipped buoys, gliders, seabed platforms, and cabled observatories, provide sustained measurements and long-time series that are critical for detecting climate-driven change and supporting forecasting.179,180 These systems commonly rely on instrument nodes that aggregate multiple sensor streams and transmit data to shore via fiber, satellite, or cellular links, enabling near real-time visualization and adaptive management in some settings (e.g., OOI Cabled Array; Ocean Networks Canada; and NOAA Global Drifter Program, https://www.sofarocean.com/products/spotter). Ongoing advances in underwater communication and networking further improve reliability and latency constraints for subsea sensing and mobile platforms,181 strengthening the practical pathway from observation to action.

The resulting datasets support both short-term decision-making (for example, event response and adaptive management) and the development of long-term time series needed to detect climate-driven change and evaluate restoration trajectories. As telemetry, networking, and underwater communication continue to improve, more platforms have become available that can deliver data closer to real time, strengthening early warning workflows where thresholds and response pathways are defined. Collectively, these advances expand the spatial, temporal, and ecological resolutions of marine monitoring, providing a practical foundation for proactive ecosystem management and restoration planning.

Drivers, incentives, and pathways for adoption

The widespread adoption of standardized marine observatory frameworks requires clear incentives that extend beyond scientific coordination alone. For governments, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and research institutions, standardization reduces duplication of effort, enhances interoperability across regions and habitats, and increases the policy relevance and longevity of monitoring investments. Participation in shared observatory frameworks can also facilitate compliance with national and international reporting obligations related to biodiversity conservation, climate adaptation, and ecosystem restoration, while enabling benchmarking and comparability across jurisdictions and projects. Beyond voluntary uptake, concrete policy and financial levers can further drive adoption. These include linking eligibility for public funding, climate adaptation finance, concessional loans, or blended finance instruments to the use of standardized indicators, monitoring protocols, and open data reporting. Certification and accreditation schemes for ecosystem restoration, nature-based solutions, or blue carbon projects could similarly require observatory-aligned monitoring as a prerequisite for validation, thereby embedding standardized data collection and sharing into project lifecycles. Such conditionality would create strong incentives for disparate actors to converge on common monitoring standards while maintaining flexibility in implementation.

Challenges

High cost, technology requirements, and know-hows associated with complementary tools used for biodiversity quantifications (ARMS, eDNA, hydrophones, and structure from motion), as well as innovative methods to assess environmental health (e.g., AUVs and sensors) may prevent the establishment of such an integrated framework in developing countries or the countries lacking solid research infrastructure or highly trained staff. It can also limit the regional cover. However, as with most technologies, the costs are quickly coming down over time. Developing countries may also apply for funding through world associations such as UN environmental programs and WWF, but regional coordination is critical.

Securing funding

Securing sustained financing remains one of the most persistent barriers to implementing integrated restoration and long-term observatory frameworks, particularly in developing tropical regions.182 In addition, funding is often unevenly distributed across habitats, reflecting differences in investment priorities. For example, mangrove restoration has received substantially greater support (€479 million) than coral reefs (€258 million) and seagrasses (€75 million), largely driven by large-scale, government-backed blue carbon initiatives such as Indonesia’s “Mangroves for Resilience” program.38 This imbalance not only shapes the restoration practice but also influences the availability of supporting planning tools: mangroves benefit from dedicated restoration potential maps, whereas coral reefs rely on fragmented sources regarding distribution (e.g., Allen Atlas), threats, and potential restoration areas.183 Crucially, these sources lack integration with comprehensive feasibility studies, cost-benefit analyses, and climate change adaptability assessments. Information gaps are even more severe for seagrasses, significantly hindering integrated, seascape-scale planning and coordinated investment.

International financing mechanisms, therefore, play a critical role in supporting long-term monitoring, restoration, and governance capacity. Climate adaptation finance, notably through the Green Climate Fund, has increasingly supported ecosystem-based coastal resilience projects that integrate habitat restoration, sustained monitoring, and community capacity building. Complementary reef-focused blended finance initiatives, such as the UN-led Global Fund for Coral Reefs (GFCR), mobilize grants, concessional finance, and private capital to support reef resilience while catalyzing sustainable local economies. In parallel, project preparation facilities such as IUCN’s Blue Natural Capital Financing Facility help develop pipelines of viable coastal ecosystem investments, enabling monitoring and management to extend beyond short-term project cycles.

More broadly, international ocean finance draws on a diverse portfolio of public, philanthropic, and private instruments tailored to different risk-return profiles and conservation objectives. Debt-based mechanisms can be particularly relevant where restoration delivers high public and ecological value but limited direct financial returns.184 The Seychelles Blue Bond, developed with the World Bank and Global Environment Facility (GEF) support,185 exemplifies that sovereign-backed debt can mobilize capital for sustainable fisheries and marine conservation. Similarly, debt-for-nature swaps have enabled the establishment of long-term financing structures such as SeyCCAT, providing sustained support for MAPs, climate adaptation, and monitoring.184

Concessional and impact-oriented funding from official development assistance (ODA) agencies, NGOs, multi-lateral institutions (e.g., GEF, Green Climate Fund, UNDP, and the World Bank), and philanthropic organizations (Bezos Earth Fund, Bloomberg Philanthropies, and Packard Foundation) has also become increasingly important for conservation and monitoring, as well as community-based projects.186 To mobilize larger financial flows, blended finance mechanisms that strategically combine public and private capital can help de-risk investments, as demonstrated by GFCR and locally anchored trust funds (e.g., SeyCCAT in Seychelles). At the commercial end of the spectrum, private finance is increasingly being directed toward ocean sectors capable of generating competitive returns, including sustainable aquaculture, eco-tourism, and marine technology. Across these mechanisms, effective deployment depends critically on credible science, spatial planning, and long-term monitoring to demonstrate impact, manage risk, and ensure that international capital supports locally defined conservation and development priorities rather than external agendas.

Human power and continuity

Time series initiatives are costly, require substantial labor, and, therefore, are difficult to maintain, especially at national scales, with many efforts resulting mainly from discontinuous, short-term surveys, rather than continuous time-series observations.187 Limited human resources and lack of centralized operational hubs often constrain the ability to deliver data as fast as some emergency events require. Limited institutional capacity, including constraints in staffing and financial resources, has been shown to undermine the establishment and enforcement of effective and equitable management thresholds of MPAs; if left unaddressed, these limitations may compromise the conservation outcomes of future expansions aligned with the global target of protecting 30% of marine areas by 2030.188 Moving toward automated processes promoted by the developments in AI will overcome some of these limitations. Capacity building and knowledge transfer are some of the main challenges. Engaging local research institutions and leveraging international collaborations are essential to support long-term initiatives, training locals in the acquisition of data and its analysis, and promote the translation of knowledge into decision-making.

Jurisdictions

In addition to hesitancy regarding submitting data to internationally accepted repositories, sharing of data externally can be limited by national level policies and agreements with sponsors, and this limitation will have to be addressed to make the best use of data at a regional (multi-national) scale. Similarly, national-scale legislation and multi-national differences in resource availability and resource priorities determining what can be collected, where, and when may dictate how cohesive a seascape observatory can be framed as well as its utility over space and time. Multi-national agreements and commitments will be essential.

A roadmap toward integrated marine observatories for the “people” and the “planet”

Despite the growing recognition of the need for long-term monitoring of tropical coastal ecosystems, current efforts remain fragmented, inconsistent, and difficult to sustain. The absence of standardized methodologies, continuous funding, and coordinated networks has produced persistent data gaps across habitats, time frames, and geographic locations, limiting our ability to detect trends, identify drivers of change, and evaluate restoration outcomes. These limitations are particularly concerning as disturbances continue to intensify and restoration initiatives expand globally, especially in the context of international commitments to conserve and restore at least 30% of degraded ecosystems by 2030. Without robust baseline data and sustained observatory infrastructure, decision-making is weakened, including the selection of effective restoration strategies and the capacity to track meaningful ecological recovery.

To move beyond isolated, short-term monitoring effort, we advocate for integrated, seascape-scale observatory frameworks implemented through a phased, tier-based approach that can scale from local pilots to globally coordinated networks (Figure 4). Early-stage observatories should prioritize a core suite of standardized ecological, functional, and social indicators, measured through a combination of classical surveys and emerging technologies, and be applied consistently across both reference and restored areas (Table 1). To remain feasible in low-resource settings, observatories must avoid indicator overload by focusing on decision-relevant metrics that are scientifically robust yet logistically achievable, with additional indicators incorporated as capacity expands. This tiered structure ensures that monitoring remains scalable, comparable, and aligned with the adaptive management needs over time.

From pilots to global networks: A phased roadmap for integrated marine observatories

Phase 1 (“pilot & harmonization,” 1–5 years) focuses on establishing pilot observatories with standardized protocols, mapping global gaps, defining core indicators, and integrating local and Indigenous knowledge under guidance from the United Nations (UN) and the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO). Phase 2 (“scaling & regional coordination,” 5–10 years) expands pilots into regional networks, supported by governance bodies such as the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), the Caribbean Community (CARICOM), and the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority (GBRMPA), while securing regional funding and aligning cross-sector mandates. Phase 3 (“global integration & adaptive management,” 10+ years) consolidates these into a globally coordinated system, ensuring interoperability of datasets, inclusivity, and links to global policy platforms such as the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD). This phased approach provides a pathway for sustainable, scalable, and adaptive monitoring of tropical coastal ecosystems. The figure was produced with Biorender.

Achieving this vision will require not only technical harmonization and inclusive governance but also sustained financial mechanisms that support long-term monitoring infrastructure across interconnected habitats. Without secure and equitable investment, observatory networks risk remaining short-lived, habitat biased, and incapable of delivering integrated evidence needed for restoration and EB-MSP. For observatories to be both effective and enduring, persistent barriers including chronic funding constraints, logistical limitations, institutional fragmentation, and challenges in transnational coordination must be addressed. Moving from concept to implementation requires concrete steps: piloting observatories in priority regions, harmonizing indicator sets across existing programs, securing regional funding streams, and aligning mandates across institutions through shared governance structures and interoperable data platforms (Figure 4). A practical example from temperate systems demonstrates how Tier-1 observatories can be operationalized using the EU-supported MAF Toolbox. This open-access framework provides standardized protocols and decision-support tools, facilitating the rapid adoption of monitoring approaches while lowering technical barriers during the initial stages of implementation (https://open-research-europe.ec.europa.eu/collections/maf-toolbox/about; https://open-research-europe.ec.europa.eu/articles/5-359; https://open-research-europe.ec.europa.eu/articles/5-272).

Crucially, social sustainability must be treated as a foundational pillar rather than an add-on. As illustrated in Figure 5, local and Indigenous knowledge should be incorporated as a formal observatory data stream within FAIR web-GIS decision-support systems through participatory mapping, co-designed community indicators, and tiered access permissions that respect knowledge sensitivity, consent, and data sovereignty. Community governance structures, including local advisory groups and trained data stewards, support long-term custodianship, ensure that monitoring priorities remain locally relevant, and help translate ecological observations into actionable thresholds and early warning triggers. Embedding these processes within Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) and Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics (CARE) principles, alongside transparent benefit-sharing mechanisms (e.g., paid jobs, training, recognition, and shared data ownership), strengthens trust, legitimacy, and sustained engagement.

Social sustainability and governance pathway for integrated observatories and FAIR web-GIS decision support

The first step is co-producing information with local stakeholders and Indigenous people toward documenting local and Indigenous knowledge. “Participatory mapping” captures place-based knowledge such as culturally important areas, seasonal use, access routes, fishing grounds, restoration priorities, and perceived biodiversity and stress hotspots, generating spatial layers that can be incorporated into the web-GIS. “Ecological and social indicators” refer to locally defined measures of ecosystem condition and change (e.g., perceived declines in fish availability, changes in water clarity, biodiversity and coral cover, mangrove dieback, seagrass loss, or outbreak observations), social indicators (e.g., population structure and income- or job-related positions) to support day-to-day decision-making. FPIC and CARE principles ensure that knowledge is contributed ethically and transparently, guiding stewardship of Indigenous data and preventing extractive data use. “Access controls” define what information is public versus restricted, recognizing that sensitive ecological locations, cultural knowledge, or livelihood data may require tiered permissions and data governance agreements. These knowledge streams are operationalized through shared governance structures that connect science to management (“community governance”). A local advisory group represents community voices, practitioners, and stakeholders to set monitoring priorities and interpret outputs. Data stewards (local institutions or trained community members) support data quality, metadata completion, secure upload, and long-term custodianship of datasets within the FAIR framework. A science-management link ensures continuous communication between monitoring and conservation teams and decision-makers (e.g., environmental agencies, protected area authorities, and restoration programs), enabling evidence to inform permitting, enforcement, and intervention planning. A set of locally defined criteria set the base for “decision rules” that translate the monitoring outputs into action by establishing explicit trigger points (e.g., “if turbidity exceeds threshold for X days” or “if bleaching prevalence crosses Y%”), creating a transparent pipeline from observation→ alert→ response. Equitable benefit-sharing mechanisms must be in place to guarantee tangible and fair returns for communities contributing time, access, expertise, and data. Among these are “paid jobs,” supporting local communities (e.g., fishermen re-qualified into eco-tourism if fisheries are banned or restricted) and building long-term observational capacity. “Training and certification” (e.g., standardized survey protocols and restoration techniques) enable career pathways and strengthen local ownership of monitoring infrastructure. At the same time, “financial incentives” (local and global) will support community-led monitoring extensions, local mitigation actions, or small-scale restoration pilots aligned with observatory priorities. Finally, it is critical to recognize intellectual contributions and the knowledge of the Indigenous people, ensure credit and traceability of knowledge, and support transparency in how local data inform publications, policy, and investment decisions through “co-authorship and data ownership.” The integrated system produces actionable outputs through the web-GIS platform, from early warning alerts highlighting emerging risk conditions (e.g., heat stress, hypoxia, pollution spikes, and disease signals) to management actions (e.g., restricting damaging activities, deploying rapid response monitoring, prioritizing restoration sites, and activating enforcement or mitigation), while maintaining a transparent communication (community updates) through accessible reporting. Collectively, the figure highlights how socially grounded governance and knowledge co-production strengthens accountability, trust, and long-term sustainability, ensuring that FAIR data systems do not simply store information but also actively support equitable decision-making and conservation outcomes across tropical seascapes. The figure was created with Biorender.

Together, the phased roadmap (Figure 4) and the governance pathway (Figure 5) highlight how integrated observatories can evolve from pilot monitoring sites into enduring seascape-scale infrastructures that support early warning systems, adaptive management, and equitable conservation outcomes. Ultimately, observatories will only deliver transformative conservation impact if they are designed not only as monitoring systems but as socially grounded, decision-ready infrastructures that connect biodiversity change to governance action across tropical seascapes. This shift from fragmented habitat monitoring to coordinated observatory networks grounded in both ecological evidence and community ownership is essential for safeguarding tropical coastal ecosystems and the services they provide to the “people” and the “planet.”
