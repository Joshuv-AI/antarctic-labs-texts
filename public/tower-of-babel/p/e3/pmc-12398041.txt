Predicting one-year outcomes after TAVI: evidence from a two-site Swiss registry
Authors: Charlie Ferry, Dorian Garin, Roberto Corti, Aurélien Clerc, Mario Togni, Peter Wenaweser, Stefan Stortecky, Stephane Cook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe number of patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is rising. While TAVI improves survival, symptoms, and quality of life, ESC guidelines highlight the lack of validated tools to predict futile outcomes. Around 20% of intermediate- and high-risk patients die within one-year post-TAVI, underscoring the need for robust risk stratification.ObjectivesTo develop and validate a multivariable model predicting one-year mortality after TAVI, using real-world data from two Swiss centers.MethodsWe analysed 2,053 patients who underwent TAVI between 2014 and 2022 at two centers in the Swiss TAVI Registry. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality at one-year. Candidate variables were selected based on a 2024 scoping review, clinical expertise, and data availability. Variables associated with mortality (p < 0.2) in univariate Cox regression were included in a multivariate Cox model. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curves and Youden’s index.ResultsAt 30 days, independent predictors of mortality were reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, COPD, peripheral artery disease, and age. At one-year, mortality was associated with atrial fibrillation, lower LVEF, mitral regurgitation grade, age, corticosteroid use, coronary artery disease, insulin-dependent diabetes, and reduced glomerular filtration rate. The model achieved an AUC of 0.814 for 30-day mortality and 0.763 at one-year, outperforming the STS score for short-term prediction (AUC 0.785).ConclusionThis study demonstrates the feasibility of building a one-year mortality prediction model after TAVI using standard clinical variables. With 2,053 patients from two centers, the model is a promising step toward Swiss-specific risk tools. Incorporating frailty or functional status may further improve performance. This model may support heart teams in shared decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05114-8.

Introduction

To treat aortic stenosis (AS), the current guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) recommend transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) for patients aged ≥ 75 years, those at high surgical risk (STS-PROM or EuroSCORE II > 8%), and individuals deemed inoperable (Class I, Level A) [1]. Several studies have shown the benefits of TAVI, particularly in terms of survival [2], symptoms [3], and patients’ quality of life [4].

Despite these well-established indications, the guidelines acknowledge a major gap: the absence of validated stratification tools to predict the risk of futility [1]. This issue is critical, as TAVI is frequently performed in elderly [5] or high-risk patients [6], and may, in some cases, provide only minimal or short-lived benefit [1]. Studies suggest that approximately 20% of intermediate- and high-risk patients die within one-year following TAVI [7–9].

The concept of futility in medicine is complex [10, 11] and debated [12], yet one-year survival is widely accepted as a practical benchmark [13]. There is currently no consensus on how to reliably identify patients at risk of futile outcomes before undergoing TAVI [13]. This uncertainty creates a clinical dilemma: some patients are denied access due to a perceived poor prognosis [14], while others receive the intervention without achieving significant benefit.

Several predictive models have been proposed, but most are limited to short-term endpoints such as 30-day mortality [15–17]. Only recently have efforts emerged to develop specific one-year mortality models [18–20]. However, these models were developed outside Switzerland and may not reflect local clinical practice. Furthermore, racial, ethnic, and geographic disparities in TAVI outcomes have been reported [21, 22], raising concerns about the generalizability of existing tools.

This study aims to develop a multivariable risk model to predict one-year mortality after TAVI, using real-world data from the Swiss TAVI Registry.

Methods

Study population

We selected all consecutive patients treated by TAVI at our 2 institutions from the SwissTAVI registry between 2014 and 2022. The SwissTAVI (NCT01368250) is a national prospective cohort study, which is mandated by the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health to include all consecutive patients undergoing TAVI at approved sites in Switzerland since 2011.

Outcomes and variables

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at one-year, the most widely accepted definition of futility after TAVI [23]. Thirty-day mortality was also assessed as a secondary endpoint.

Variable selection was based on a prior study by the authors on TAVI futility [13], expert input from experienced TAVI operators, and availability in the Swiss TAVI registry. The 38 variables included: Gender (Male); Age; Body Mass Index (BMI); Atrial Fibrillation (AF); Diabetes; Dialysis; Hypertension (HTA); Dyslipidemia; Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD); Stroke or Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA); Previous Pacemaker Implantation; Coronary Artery Disease; Previous Aortic Valvuloplasty; History of Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI); History of Myocardial Infarction; History of Cardiac Surgery; Peripheral Artery Disease; Dyspnea (NYHA Classification); Angina Pectoris (CCS Classification); Cockcroft-Gault Creatinine Clearance; Hemoglobin (g/L); Thrombocytes (g/L); Statin Use; ACE Inhibitor or Angiotensin Receptor Antagonist Use; Beta-blocker Use; Calcium Channel Antagonist Use; Diuretics Use; Steroids Use; Insulin Use; Aspirin Use; P2Y12 Antagonist Use; Use of Marcoumar or Sintrom; Use of Oral Anticoagulants or Novel Anticoagulation Drugs; Mean Gradient (mmHg); Aortic Valve Area (cm²); Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) (%); Aortic Regurgitation Grade (categorical); Mitral Regurgitation Grade (categorical).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using counts and percentages for categorical variables and means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables, as appropriate. Normality was assessed via histogram inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared using Student’s t-test; non-normal variables with the Mann–Whitney U test.

Associations with all-cause mortality were assessed using univariate Cox proportional hazards regression. Variables with p < 0.200 were retained for multivariate analysis using Cox regression with Lasso penalization (penalizer = 0.001). Variables with p < 0.05 in the multivariate model were considered significant. A bootstrap validation with 1,000 iterations was performed to strengthen confidence intervals. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying missing data imputation (drop, mean, median). All multivariate models were run at 30-day and one-year timepoints. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared via log-rank tests (see Supplementary Figs. 4–12). Score performance was assessed using ROC curves, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity at various thresholds. The optimal threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden index. ROC analysis was also performed using the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score at 30 days as a reference standard.

Analyses were performed in Python 3.13.0 using NumPy (1.24.3), pandas (2.0.1), psycopg2 (2.9.10), statsmodels (0.14.4), scikit-learn (1.6.1), lifelines (0.30.0), matplotlib (3.10.1), seaborn (0.13.2), and SciPy (1.15.2). The workflow was managed in Visual Studio Code (v1.95) with a PostgreSQL 14.15 database accessed via DBeaver (v24.3.4).

Results

Between 2014 and 2022, 2,053 patients with aortic stenosis were treated by TAVI and included in the analysis. During approximately the same period, 14,110 patients were registered in the SwissTAVI registry across all participating centers. Our cohort represents approximately 14.6% of all patients registered in SwissTAVI during the same period. Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The cohort was gender-balanced (52% male), with 56% aged 76–85 years, 33% over 85 years, and 1.5% under 65 years. Most patients were in NYHA class II (26%) or III (36%). The most prevalent comorbidities were arterial hypertension (78%), coronary artery disease (53%), and dyslipidemia (50%). The most common preprocedural medications were diuretics (53%), statins (52%), and beta-blockers (44%). Mean STS score was 3.6 ± 2.8%, mean aortic valve area was 0.84 ± 0.32 cm², and mean LVEF was 59.2 ± 13.0%. The transfemoral approach was used in 96% of cases. Overall death and by categories

In the multivariate Cox regression for 30-day mortality, significant predictors included LVEF (HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.94–0.99; p = 0.013), COPD (HR 3.74; 95% CI 1.32–10.55; p = 0.013), peripheral artery disease (HR 2.82; 95% CI 1.15–6.91; p = 0.023), and advanced age (HR 1.10; 95% CI 1.03–1.17; p = 0.003). Bootstrap analysis confirmed the robustness of these associations. In sensitivity analyses using median or mean imputation for missing data, only LVEF and age remained significant at 30 days (Supplementary Tables 1–4).

At one-year, predictors of increased mortality included atrial fibrillation (HR 2.69; 95% CI 1.52–4.74; p = 0.001), LVEF (HR 0.98; 95% CI 0.96–0.99; p = 0.001), mitral regurgitation grade (HR 1.32; 95% CI 1.03–1.68; p = 0.024), age (HR 1.06; 95% CI 1.018–1.11; p = 0.005), corticosteroid use (HR 1.98; 95% CI 1.05–3.72; p = 0.034), coronary artery disease (HR 1.87; 95% CI 1.13–3.12; p = 0.015), insulin-treated diabetes (HR 2.33; 95% CI 1.06–5.13; p = 0.035), and lower creatinine clearance (HR 1.007; 95% CI 1.0007–1.015; p = 0.029). These findings were supported by bootstrap analysis. In sensitivity analyses, only LVEF and age remained significant predictors at one-year (Supplementary Tables 5–8).

For 30-day mortality, the STS score yielded an AUC of 0.785 (sensitivity 0.786, specificity 0.658) (Fig. 1). A model developed via Cox regression using variables with p < 0.20 in univariate analysis achieved an AUC of 0.814 (sensitivity 0.750, specificity 0.802) (Fig. 2). At one-year, the derived model showed an AUC of 0.763, with both sensitivity and specificity at 0.72 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to develop a multivariable predictive model of mortality following TAVI based on a Swiss cohort. Unlike existing models derived from French, Japanese, or American populations [18, 24, 25], our cohort presents distinct demographic and clinical features. For example, the Swiss population is generally younger than its Japanese counterpart, which itself is younger than the American cohort. Such differences in patient profiles, previously reported in comparative studies of TAVI outcomes [25], likely contribute to variations in predictive variables across models. We hypothesize that the specific characteristics of the Swiss healthcare system—such as minimal waiting times, high availability of cardiologists, and dense surgical infrastructure—may influence both patient selection and outcomes.

Several clinical factors were independently associated with 30-day and one-year mortality. Lower LVEF and advanced age consistently emerged as significant predictors across all models and sensitivity analyses. Early mortality was also linked to comorbidities such as COPD and peripheral artery disease, while one-year mortality was associated with atrial fibrillation, mitral regurgitation, coronary artery disease, insulin-treated diabetes, corticosteroids use, and impaired renal function. These findings align with existing literature and highlight the multifactorial nature of risk in TAVI patients [13]. Although RAAS inhibitors were included among the candidate variables, they were not independently associated with one-year mortality. However, their potential long-term benefits beyond one year, as suggested by Basile et al. [26]warrant further investigation.

Our model showed good discrimination, with an AUC of 0.814 for 30-day mortality and 0.763 for one-year mortality, outperforming the STS score for short-term prediction in this cohort. These results suggest that population-specific models may improve risk stratification over generalized surgical scores.

The model is intended to aid in preprocedural risk assessment, supporting clinicians in identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from more intensive follow-up, comprehensive geriatric evaluation, or reconsideration of treatment strategy. The inclusion of frailty markers could further enhance model performance, particularly for long-term outcomes. Frailty is a well-established predictor of prognosis in elderly TAVI patients [17, 27, 28], but was not systematically recorded in our dataset. Although the proposed model can be used manually by Heart Teams during multidisciplinary discussions, future integration with artificial intelligence (AI) tools could allow real-time processing of large datasets from electronic health records [20]. Nevertheless, algorithmic tools should complement—not replace—individualized clinical judgment. Personalized decision-making that incorporates patient values, goals, and quality of life remains fundamental in the care of TAVI candidates.

Study limitations

This study was based on data from two Swiss centers, which may limit generalizability. Although bootstrap internal validation was performed, the model was derived and tested on the same dataset, potentially overestimating performance. External validation in a larger, multicenter and contemporary cohort is necessary to confirm robustness. The relatively low number of events at one year compared to the number of candidate variables raises concerns about model stability, despite the use of Lasso penalization. We used simple mean or median imputation in sensitivity analyses but acknowledge that more sophisticated methods such as multiple imputation would better address potential bias, especially when data are missing at random (MAR). Additionally, key variables related to frailty and geriatric syndromes were not available in the registry, despite their known prognostic relevance in elderly TAVI populations.

We also acknowledge that we used all-cause mortality as the primary outcome, and detailed data on cause-specific mortality (i.e., cardiac vs. non-cardiac) were not consistently available across centers. While this may limit etiological insights, our aim was to predict global survival at one year in order to support shared decision-making and identify patients who may derive limited overall benefit from the procedure.

Finally, as data collection began in 2014, evolving TAVI technologies, operator experience, and expanded indications may impact the model’s relevance for contemporary practice. Further testing in more recent and representative cohorts is therefore warranted.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of developing a robust and clinically relevant risk prediction model for one-year mortality after TAVI in a Swiss population. By focusing on overall survival—a key benchmark in evaluating procedural futility—our model aims to support clinicians and patients in making more informed, individualized treatment decisions. These findings highlight the value of population-specific tools in identifying patients who may derive limited or short-lived benefit from TAVI. Future studies should include broader and more contemporary patient populations, integrate frailty and quality-of-life metrics, and externally validate the model across diverse clinical settings.
