Neural network tissue and myocytes co-culture system reveals effects of different myocytes on postnatal development of spinal cord neural network
Authors: Shangbin Yang, Haiyang Yu, Jing Xu, Chuangran Wu, Yu Cheng, Miao Tian, Ying Ding, Yue Yang, Zhen Chen, Mingyu Lv, Jiawei Sun, Yujian Lin, Kang Zhang, Xiangyu Liu, Yinan Guo, Ling Zhang, Jiafeng Fang, Biqin Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interaction between spinal cord neural networks and myocytes in postnatal mammals plays a crucial role in development, and the neural regulation of motor and visceral functions. Here, spinal cord neural network tissue (SC-NNT) was constructed with neonatal rat spinal cord neural stem cells (SC-NSCs) and co-cultured with skeletal muscle cells (SkMCs) and corpus cavernosum smooth muscle cells (CC-SmMCs). The mechanisms by which these two types of myocytes influence postnatal SC-NSC differentiation and SC-NNT formation were explored. Both SkMCs and CC-SmMCs promoted SC-NSC differentiation towards neurons rather than neuroglia cells. Excitatory neurons were predominant in co-culture, with significantly increased synaptic protein expression. CC-SmMCs more strongly promoted SC-NSC differentiation into DβH-positive neurons, while SkMCs more strongly promoted axonal extension of neurons. Moreover, SkMCs formed neuromuscular junction-like specialized structures, enabling the SC-NNT to control SkMC contraction. Although human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neuromuscular organoids avoid embryo-related ethical concerns, they remain subject to strict cell source regulations. Neonatal rat cells avoid such ethical issues. In addition, rat SC-NSCs differentiate into neurons more rapidly and establish neural control over muscles faster. They also offer insights into how different myocyte types affect the postnatal development and function of spinal cord neural networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05872-w.Keywords: Neural network tissue, Spinal cord neuro-myocyte interaction, Spinal cord neural stem cells, Corpus cavernosum smooth muscle cells, Skeletal muscle cells

Introduction

The spinal cord serves as a vital bridge connecting the brain and all the organs of the body, and its development and function are affected by other organs [1–3]. The interaction between the spinal cord neural network and myocytes is essential for the development and maturation of the nervous system, and is closely related to somatic movement and visceral functions [4, 5]. Skeletal muscle cells (SkMCs) are innervated by spinal cord motor neurons [2], and vascular smooth muscle cells in central nervous tissue and corpus cavernosum smooth muscle cells (CC-SmMCs) are also innervated by spinal cord neuron [6, 7]. There have been studies on the interaction between central nervous system (CNS) organoids and SkMCs, which simulate the impact of skeletal muscle on the development and functional formation of the CNS during the early stages of development [8, 9]. However, little is known about the mechanism by which SkMCs and CC-SmMCs, influence the postnatal development of the spinal cord neural network [10]. In contrast to the embryonic stage, postnatal development of the central nervous system relies more heavily on the refinement of synaptic activity and the feedback regulation of neural function by muscle activity [11]. Given that the maturation of neural circuits and neuromuscular junctions predominantly occurs during the postnatal period, investigating normal postnatal neuromuscular development is essential for understanding pediatric neuromuscular disorders such as cerebral palsy, spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), and muscular dystrophies [12]. Therefore, establishing a spinal cord neuro-myocyte interaction system through tissue engineering technology and exploring the effects of SkMCs or CC-SmMCs on the differentiation and maturation of the spinal cord neural network can help elucidate the mechanisms of postnatal neural development and regeneration or simulate neuromuscular diseases [13, 14].

Previous studies on the mechanism of interaction between the spinal cord neural network and myocytes have relied on observations of embryos [15, 16]. However, these are not suitable for genetic interventions or disease modeling [17]. In recent years, the rapid development of organoid technology based on induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [18], particularly the successful construction of neuromuscular organoids [19–22], has provided a novel model for studying neuromuscular development and neuromuscular diseases during early embryogenesis. Currently, there is a lack of models for neuro-smooth muscle organoids. Furthermore, it takes 30 days or longer to observe neuronal regulation of SkMCs contractions in spinal cord-SkMCs organoids, leading to increased experimental costs and strain on other resources [8, 22]. Construction of a spinal cord neuro-myocyte interaction system through tissue engineering in a shorter time, especially one that reflects the postnatal development of spinal cord, would more efficiently reveal the mechanism underlying the development, maturation, and functional formation of spinal cord neural network. Our previous research has successfully established spinal cord neural network tissue (SC-NNT) and SkMCs co-culture system [23]. However, the mechanisms by which CC-SmMCs influence the development of the spinal cord neural network remain to be elucidated, particularly regarding the neural regulation of blood vessels and visceral smooth muscle.

In this study, we seeded spinal cord neural stem cells (SC-NSCs) from newborn rats into a 3D collagen sponge scaffold, co-cultured with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs, to explore the effect of different myocytes on the differentiation of three-dimensional (3D)-cultured SC-NSCs and the formation of SC-NNT. This model was further used to investigate the mechanism underlying the functional maturation of postnatal SC-NNT, providing a reference for constructing postnatal spinal cord neuro-myocyte interaction systems as models for diseases and drug screening [24], or for constructing tissue-engineered neural transplants in spinal cord repair [25].

Construction of the co-culture system

The SkMCs displayed a spindle-shape and were confirmed as myogenin (MyoG)-positive (MyoG+, Fig. 1D). Co-culture with myocytes promotes the neuronal differentiation of SC-NSCs in SC-NNTs

To explore the effects of different myocytes on the differentiation of SC-NSCs in SC-NNTs, the proportion of differentiated cells of SC-NSCs in the NN-SmM group and NN-SkM group was compared with the NN group. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the proportion of neurons in both the NN-SmM (48.33% ± 6.81%) and NN-SkM groups (54.63% ± 5.79%) were increased relative to the NN group (31.04% ± 10.05%) (Fig. 2A1-A3 and 2D), while proportions of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes were decreased in both the NN-SmM (8.70% ± 5.57%; 16.22% ± 5.63%) and NN-SkM groups (3.45% ± 1.82%; 14.27% ± 4.97%) to the NN group (15.80% ± 5.63%; 23.69% ± 8.34%) (Fig. 2B1-B3, 2C1-C3 and 2D). Clustering heatmap analysis of differential expression genes (DEGs) related to the differentiation of SC-NSCs showed that the expression of neuronal differentiation-related genes was increased in the NN-SmM group and NN-SkM group and the two groups exhibiting distinct sets of upregulated genes, while the expression of genes related to astrocytes and oligodendrocyte differentiation was decreased (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Table 1).

Co-culture with myocytes promotes differentiation of SC-NSCs into excitatory neurons

To explore the impact of different myocytes on the differentiation of SC-NSCs in SC-NNTs into various subtypes of neurons, we detected the expression of neurotransmitter-related protein components in the SC-NNTs after co-culture by immunofluorescence staining. In the NN-SmM group, there was an increase in neurons expressing choline acetyltransferase (ChAT+) (46.40% ± 8.65%), dopamine β-hydroxylase (DβH+) (42.74% ± 6.04%), and vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (VGluT1+) (33.40% ± 3.42%), compared with NN group (28.47% ± 1.80%; 15.36% ± 4.00%; 23.02% ± 7.51%) (Fig. 3A1-A3, 3B1-B3, 3C1-C3, and 3E). In the NN-SkM group, there was also an increase in neurons expressing choline acetyltransferase (ChAT+) (41.83% ± 7.71%), dopamine β-hydroxylase (DβH+) (37.28% ± 7.23%), and vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (VGluT1+) (35.74% ± 2.99%), compared with NN group (Fig. 3A1-A3, 3B1-B3, 3C1-C3, and 3E). Furthermore, the number of DβH+ neurons in the NN-SmM group was greater than that in the NN-SkM group. However, numbers of neurons expressing glutamate decarboxylase (GAD 67+) were reduced in the NN-SmM (11.57% ± 3.54%) and NN-SkM groups (10.96% ± 2.14%), compared with NN group (19.72% ± 2.79%) (Fig. 3D1-D3 and 3E). The results suggest that these neurons differentiated from SC-NSCs may have the phenomenon of coexistence of multiple neurotransmitters in the early stage of differentiation [26, 27]. Co-culture with myocytes increased the proportion of excitatory neurons and reduced the proportion of inhibitory neurons in the SC-NNTs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that upregulation of gene expression associated with cholinergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic synapses is observed in both comparisons: when the NN-SmM group is compared to the NN-SkM group and vice versa (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Despite the lack of significant differences in the expression levels of several key neurotransmitters between the NN-SmM and the NN-SkM group as revealed by immunofluorescence staining, RNA-Seq analysis demonstrated distinct characteristics in the subtypes of gene expression profiles within each group. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) clustering analysis of neurotransmitters expression-related genes suggests that genes related to the Ras, PI3K-Akt, Phospholipase D, MAPK, cGMP-PKG, cAMP, Calcium and AMPK signaling pathways are all upregulated in the NN-SmM and NN-SkM groups. In the NN-SmM group, the genes related to Wnt, TGF-beta, and Rap1 signaling pathway were upregulated relative to the NN-SkM group. The genes related to Thyroid hormone, ErbB and Apelin signaling pathway, phosphatidylinositol signaling system and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction were significantly upregulated in the NN-SkM group relative to the NN-SmM group (Fig. 3F).

Effect of co-culture on synapse formation and axonal growth

Immunofluorescence staining revealed increased expression of synaptic markers, including synaptophysin (SYP) (0.3506 ± 0.0145) and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) (0.3522 ± 0.0145) in the NN-SmM group relative to the NN group (0.1499 ± 0.0120; 0.1349 ± 0.0158), while SYP (0.3616 ± 0.0165) and PSD95 (0.3422 ± 0.0262) in NN-SkM group relative to the NN group (Fig. 5A-D). In the NN-SmM group, neurofilament-positive (NF+) axons from the SC-NNTs are observed to establish connections with α-SMA+ CC-SmMCs (Fig. 5E). In the NN-SkM group, the colocalization of NF from the SC-NNTs with α-bungarotoxin (BTX) in SkM cells indicated the formation of a structure resembling neuromuscular junctions (NMJ) (Fig. 5F). To investigate the ability of SC-NNT control of SkM cells contraction after co-culture, we subjected optogenetic stimulation and glutamate stimulation to SC-NNTs after 7 days of coculture and the results showed that the the amplitude (23.11% ± 2.53%; 22.72% ± 4.10%) and frequency (40.16 ± 1.94; 38.00 ± 2.90) of SkM cells contraction notably greater than those before light (6.94% ± 1.33%; 27.00 ± 4.34) and glutamate stimulation (5.73% ± 1.47%; 24.00 ± 1.90) (Supplementary Fig. 4A-F and Supplementary Video 1–3). Furthermore, after treatment of tetrodotoxin (TTX) and dizocilpine (MK-801), SkM cells contraction (frequency: 3.16 ± 1.17; 3.16 ± 1.17) in the NN-SkM group was inhibited (Supplementary Fig. 4A-4B and 4D-E). The length of neuron axons to the myocytes in the NN-SkM group (74.88 ± 17.34 μm) was greater than that in the NN-SmM group (36.28 ± 11.65 μm) (Fig. 5G). A schematic diagram illustrating the similarities and differences in the changes of the SC-NNTs after co-culturing with CC-SmMCs or SkMCs is shown (Fig. 5H).

Discussion

In this study, we innovatively used tissue engineering technology to construct a co-culture system of SC-NNT interacting with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs. The system was used to elucidate the differences in mechanisms by which SkMCs and CC-SmMCs influence the differentiation of SC-NSCs from newborn rats and shape the development of the SC-NNT. This research not only provides insights for constructing models for postnatal neuromuscular diseases or drug screening, but also offers important references for building tissue-engineered neural transplants for replacing and repairing injured spinal cord.

The effect of SkMCs and CC-SmMCs on the differentiation of SC-NSCs and development of SC-NNT

Previous studies on neuro-myocyte interactions have mainly focused on the control of skeletal muscles by spinal cord motor neurons and neuromuscular junction function [9, 28]. These studies include early research using a 2D co-culture model of motor neurons and SkMCs or recent reports on neuromuscular organoids [9, 13, 29, 30]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that the development of the postnatal SC-NNT in the co-culture system is influenced by SkMCs [23]. Additionally, although vascular smooth muscle cells in central nervous tissue and CC-SmMCs are innervated by spinal cord neurons [6, 7], there are few reports comparing the effects of smooth muscle and skeletal muscle on the differentiation of SC-NSCs and development of SC-NNT.

In this study, SC-NSCs from newborn rats were implanted into a 3D collagen sponge scaffold using tissue engineering technology and co-cultured with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs. The results showed that regardless of co-culturing with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs, the proportion of neurons differentiated from SC-NSCs was significantly higher than in the NN group. Furthermore, when co-cultured with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs, the number of excitatory neurons differentiated from SC-NSCs including ChAT-, DβH-, and VGluT1-positive neurons was higher. Interestingly, the NN-SmM group showed significantly increased number of DβH+ neurons relative to the NN-SkM group. These results suggest that the differentiation of DβH+ neurons is critical for regulating smooth muscle function, as these neurons play a key role in modulating visceral functions mediated by smooth muscle, including airway constriction, blood pressure regulation, and gastrointestinal peristalsis [31]. Additionally, the proportion of GAD67+ inhibitory neurons was significantly lower in NN-SkM and NN-SmM groups than in the NN group. It is worth noting that, in contrast to the neuromuscular organoids, which are predominantly composed of neurons and rarely contain astrocytes and oligodendrocytes [13], the SC-NNT and myocytes co-culture system not only contains a majority of excitatory neurons but also includes a proportion of inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes. This more closely resembles the actual interaction of the spinal cord neural network with skeletal muscle or smooth muscle in the human body and has the advantage of use as a drug screening model and for disease simulation research.

RNA-Seq analysis revealed that the Ras, PI3K-Akt, cGMP-PKG, cAMP, Calcium, and AMPK signaling pathways play important roles in the co-culture systems of SC-NSCs with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs. In the NN-SmM group, the Wnt, TGF-beta, and Rap1 signaling pathways were significantly upregulated at the mRNA level, while there was no upregulation in the NN-SkM group. In the NN-SkM group, the thyroid hormone, ErbB and apelin signaling pathways, as well as neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction and phosphatidylinositol signaling system, were significantly upregulated, while there was no upregulation in the NN-SmM group. It is noteworthy that in the NN-SkM group, significantly higher upregulation of axon projection-related mRNA expression was observed relative to the NN-SmM group, consistent with the finding that SkMCs can induce neurons to extend longer projections and form neuromuscular junction-like structures to control muscle contraction. The aforementioned results for SC-NSC differentiation and the RNA-Seq analysis revealed that DEGs and their associated signaling pathways are consistent with the key regulatory signaling pathways reported in the literature for SC-NSC differentiation and SC-NNT development in animals after birth [32, 33]. These results suggest that CC-SmMCs and SkMCs differ in their mechanisms for promoting SC-NNT development, and that the co-culture system of SC-NNT and myocytes can serve as an in vitro model for investigating spinal cord neural network development and associated diseases.

The role of SkMCs and CC-SmMCs in functional development of SC-NNT

Traditionally, both the construction of tissue-engineered SC-NNTs and neural organoids are constrained by the problem of slow maturation of neuronal function [34–36]. Our results for the differentiation of SC-NSCs in the SC-NNT and myocytes co-culture system suggest that co-culturing with myocytes helps promote the maturation of neurons in SC-NNTs and their expression of synaptic-related proteins, achieving faster control of muscle contractions. Compared with self-organized neuromuscular organoids, for which observation of neural control of muscle contraction takes over 30 days [37–39], our co-culture system achieved control of SkMC contraction by spinal cord motor neurons after 7 days of co-culture, up to 21 days. Therefore, this co-culture system can be used as an in vitro model for studying postnatal neuromuscular diseases or for drug screening applications where long-term observation is necessary. Furthermore, in contrast to neuromuscular organoids subject to prolonged culture, which face challenges such as enlargement of organoid volume, increase in cell apoptosis, and significant developmental phenotypic differences that are detrimental to standardized construction and high-throughput testing [34, 40–42], our study reports the standardized construction of SC-NNT through standardized design of three tissue engineering elements—stem cells, biomaterials, and bioactive factors. The constructed system has controllable size and meets the high-throughput testing requirements of a 96-well plate format, enabling rapid achievement of neuromuscular dominance and control within a short period.

On the 7th day of co-culture, the results of GO analysis based on RNA-Seq indicate that in the NN-SmM and NN-SkM groups, the expression of genes related to neuron projection, synapse formation, and neurotrophic factor signaling pathways in the SC-NNTs was significantly higher than in NN group, which suggests that both CC-SmMCs and SkMCs play a positive role in the structure and functional development of the SC-NNTs. KEGG and GO interaction analyses suggest that CC-SmMCs and SkMCs promote the functional maturation of SC-NNTs through the secretion of bioactive factors (mainly including platelet-derived growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor, and insulin-like growth factor). Compared with CC-SmMCs, SkMCs secrete bioactive factors that promote the functional maturation of SC-NNTs through Trk-related signaling pathways and PDGF receptor signaling pathways, resulting in more pronounced axonal growth and projection in the NN-SkM group, which is also the reason for the formation of NMJ and the achievement of neural control over SkMCs contraction. Meanwhile, CC-SmMCs mainly regulate the maturation of SC-NNTs through the VEGF receptor signaling pathway.

Advantages and limitations of the application of the SC-NNT and myocytes co-culture system

Co-culturing with SkMCs or CC-SmMCs may accelerate the formation and functional maturation of SC-NNT; another key factor may be the selection of stem cells. Using SC-NSCs from newborn rats instead of iPSCs can reduce induction time and the need for a series of specific inductive factors [19, 43, 44]. This model may more accurately reflect postnatal spinal cord-myocyte interactions in mammals compared to neuromuscular organoids derived from iPSC-induced NSCs. It is therefore suitable for simulating the development of the spinal cord neural network in vitro after birth, as well as for investigating postnatal-onset neuromuscular disorders and conducting drug screening. However, it cannot replace research on neural-muscle interactions in early embryonic development [45, 46]. The iPSC-derived system is well-suited for modeling embryonic-onset neuromuscular diseases such as congenital myasthenic syndrome (CMS) and congenital myotonia syndromes (e.g., Thomsen’s disease) [47]. Additionally, although the SC-NNT co-cultured with two types of myocytes is mainly composed of excitatory neurons, it also contains inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes, which may provide a more realistic physiological response for disease or drug screening applications than neuromuscular organoids lacking neuroglia cells [13]. However, due to the role of immune cells in neuromuscular disease pathogenesis and the need for vascular endothelial cells in forming an inflammatory microenvironment, further optimization of the disease model is needed based on our current system [48–50].

It is also worth noting that SkMCs and CC-SmMCs promoted the differentiation of SC-NSCs into excitatory neurons as well as their production of neurotransmitters and formation of synapses, suggesting that the co-culture system can create SC-NNT composed mostly of excitatory interneurons that are suitable for transplantation to repair spinal cord injuries [51]. It is reported that SC-NSCs-derived neurons are more likely to form synaptic connections with brain-derived nerve fibers (such as CST, 5-HT fibers) when transplanted into spinal cord injury sites compared with neurons obtained from forebrain-derived NSCs [52–54]. It can be speculated that the excitatory SC-NNT constructed by co-culturing with myocytes transplanted into spinal cord injury sites is not only conducive to forming synaptic connections with brain-derived nerve fibers to transmit excitatory neural information from the brain but also beneficial for integrating with the caudal spinal cord neural network at the injury site to achieve brain control over skeletal and smooth muscles.

Experimental procedures

Cell culture and identification

SC-NSCs were prepared from 3-day-old wild-type Sprague‒Dawley (SD) rats or three-day-old transgenic mice [B6. Cg-Tg (Thy1-ChR2/EYFP) 18Gfng/J]; Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA], as previously described [55]. The number of SD rats and used transgenic mice for cultivating SC-NSCs was 40 and 15 respectively. Briefly, the rat spinal cord was removed intact and placed in D-Hank’s solution, with the spinal membrane and blood vessels removed. After cutting the spinal cord using sterile scissors, the tissue block was blown to form a single-cell suspension and the cells were resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 (1:1, Life Technologies, USA) containing B27 (Life Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and 20 ng/L basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, Life Technologies). Cells proliferated into neurospheres in the culture medium and neurosphere components were detected using Nestin and HoxD9 immunostaining.

CC-SmMCs were obtained from 4-week-old male rats and the number of rats used was 15. The rats were disinfected with 75% alcohol and transferred to the biosafety cabinet. The foreskin was removed and the penis was excised intact. The corpus cavernosum smooth muscle (CC-SmM) was cut into small pieces and digested with type II collagenase at 37 °C for 30 min. CC-SmM implants were planted in well plates with culture medium (40% DMEM (high glucose, Gibco) and 40% Ham’s F12 medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 100 U/mL streptomycin (Gibco)). When the cell density was above 80%, 0.25% trypsin was used for digesting cells and subjected the single-cell suspension to differential adhesion in a 6-well plate for 40 min, and then collected the supernatant for subculturing. CC-SmMCs were identified using α-SMA immunostaining.

SkMCs were obtained from 7-day-old wild-type or GFP-transgenic SD (Osaka University, Osaka, Japan) rats and the number of wild-type and GFP-transgenic SD rats used was 20 and 5 respectively. The skeletal muscle block was isolated from thigh and the blood vessels, fascia, and tendons were removed in D-Hank’s solution. The skeletal muscle was cut into 2 × 2 mm pieces and digested using type II collagenase at 37℃ for 15 min. Skeletal muscle implants was resuspended in culture medium (40% DMEM (high glucose, Gibco) and 40% Ham’s F12 medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 100 U/mL streptomycin (Gibco)), planted in well plates. When the cell density was above 80%, 0.25% trypsin was used for digesting cells and subjected the single-cell suspension to differential adhesion in a 6-well plate for 30 min, and then collected the supernatant for subculturing.

Generation of SC-NNT from SC-NSCs

The collagen sponge (BIOT Biology, Wuxi, China) scaffold was shaped into a square column (5 × 5 × 0.5 mm3). A total of 5 × 105 SC-NSCs per 10 µL of culture medium were injected to the collagen sponge scaffold to construct 3D SC-NNTs by pipette. To ensure complete cell adhesion during the culture of SC-NNTs, 50 µL of culture medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with B27 (Life Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, Life Technologies) was initially added for cultivation, followed by supplementation with an additional 250 µL of culture medium. Thereafter, the culture medium was replaced daily with 300 µL of medium.

Construction of co-culture system

For generation of the co-culture system, SkMCs and CC-SmMCs were cultured in 6-well plates. Once the cell confluence reached 80%, SC-NNTs that had been cultured for 1 day were transferred onto the surface of the myocytes in 6-well plates, facilitating contact between the neural cells and the myocytes. The co-culture system in NN-SmM group and NN-SkM group were maintained with 1 mL of DMEM/F12 culture medium supplemented with 1% FBS, which was changed daily. For the control group (NN group), the SC-NNTs were cultured in 6-well plates using the same method, with the well plates not containing myocytes.

Cell viability assay

The cell viability of cells in SC-NNTs, CC-SmMCs and SkMCs were detected by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CK04; DOJINDO) assay. The SC-NNTs in the three groups were removed with fine forceps after 7 days of culture and placed in 96-well plates. 5 × 105 SC-NSCs were added to a 96-well plate as a control group. After adding 100 µL CCK-8 solution to each well, they were cultured at 37℃ for 1 h. After removing the SC-NNTs, the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN Sunrise) at a wavelength of 450 nm. On the 7 day, the CC-SmMCs and SkMCs in the co-culture system as well as the CC-SmMCs and SkMCs cultured alone were subjected to CCK-8 assay. The reaction was carried out at 37℃ for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN Sunrise) at a wavelength of 450 nm.

The cell viability was also evaluated using a standard propidium iodide (PI) assay kit (Solarbio, China) with PI to assess the number of dead cells. The SC-NNTs in the three groups were removed with fine forceps after 7 days of culture and placed in 24-well plates. The SC-NNTs were thoroughly washed 2–3 times with 1 × buffer. The PI staining solution (1:200) was added to 24-well plates, incubated at 37℃ for 5 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope (NIKON Ti2-E). The CC-SmMCs and SkMCs before co-culture were also subjected to the above-mentioned PI dye staining in 24-well plates. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoe. The dead cells stained red with PI and the ratio of the number of dead cells to the total number of cells per field was calculated.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The samples from 3 groups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution, the samples were sealed with 10% goat serum diluted in PBS with 0.3% Triton-X100 for 30 min. The samples were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. After three washes with PBS solution, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at 37℃. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoe. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Dragonfly, CR-DFLY-202 2540; Andor Technology, Belfast, UK). Supplementary Table 3 shows the list of antibodies used for immunofluorescence analysis.

mRNA sequencing

After 7 days, SC-NNTs from three groups were mechanically isolated for mRNA sequencing. RNA extraction was performed according to standard protocols. RNA integrity was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis (28 S:18 S ≥ 1.5). RNA purity was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (optical density [OD] 260/280: 1.8–2.2), and RNA concentration was accurately quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (≥ 500 ng/mL). Samples were sent to Genergy Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for RNA-Seq analysis. Raw data generated from high-throughput sequencing were converted into sequencing reads using the CASAVA base-calling software. The original sequence counts of known genes in all samples were analyzed using StringTie. Expression levels of known genes were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped. DEGs between groups were identified using DESeq2 software with |log2(fold change [FC]) | values ≥ 1 and P-value ≤ 0.05.

Light and glutamate stimulation and blockade

In the experiments in which the NN-SkM was stimulated with light or glutamate, we imaged NN-SkM in the 6-well plates using automated microscopy (BioTek-Lionheart FX). We first recorded the contraction of SkMCs without stimulation. Then, light stimulation with 66 pulses of blue light (488 nm, each lasting 911 ms) was performed using an optical fiber-coupled LED directed to the SC-NNTs. The SC-NNTs were constructed using SC-NSCs isolated from transgenic mice [B6.Cg-Tg(Thy1-ChR2/EYFP) 18Gfng/J], allowing for stable neuronal expression of ChR2 with negligible off-target effects [56]. After photostimulation, the voltage-gated sodium channels of the SC-NNTs were blocked using 0.5 µM of TTX. Each contraction cycle was recorded for 60 s at a frame rate of 10 frames per second.

The contraction of SkMCs without stimulation was recorded as control. Then, we added glutamate to the culture medium of the NN-SkM to achieve a final concentration of 1 mM, which activates the glutamatergic neurons. Subsequently, we used MK-801 at a final concentration of 50 µM to block the NMDA receptors of the neurons. Each contraction cycle was recorded for 60 s at a frame rate of 10 frames per second.

The open-source ImageJ plugin MUSCLEMOTION (https://github.com/l-sala//MUSCLEMOTION) was used to quantify the frequency and amplitude of muscle contraction, which were determined by calculating the absolute change in muscle position between the final and reference frames. We selected 3 regions of interest (ROIs) in each imaging field and analyzed each ROI separately. In the analysis of the frequency of contraction, events with more than 3 median absolute deviations were considered contraction events. The muscle position from 50 frames before the stimulation was used as a reference for normalization to calculate the displacement over time.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (v 9.5.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-independent samples t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the least significant difference (LSD) test (equal variance assumed); otherwise, Dunnett’s T3 test (equal variance not assumed) was used. For all experiments, a P-value of less than 5% was considered to indicate statistical significance.

The biological influence of myocytes on postnatal SC-NNT development and the neural regulation mechanism of motor and visceral functions were revealed. Co-culturing with different myocytes promotes distinct subtype-specific neuronal differentiation and maturation, and enables rapid establishment of neuromuscular control.
