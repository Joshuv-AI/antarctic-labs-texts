Unveiling surface and subsurface atomic vacancies in MoS2 with lateral force microscopy
Authors: Oscar Gutiérrez-Varela, Aitor Zambudio, Pablo Ares, Julio Gómez-Herrero, Enrico Gnecco, Jaime Colchero, J G Vilhena, Cristina Gómez-Navarro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtomic-scale defects govern materials properties, with their influence amplified in low-dimensional systems. Understanding and controlling such defects is therefore essential for both fundamental science and technological applications. However, precise defect identification and localization remain major challenges. Lateral force microscopy (LFM), an atomic force microscopy mode that measures friction forces through the lateral torsion of the cantilever, is an appealing technique for probing surface properties. Nevertheless, it has conventionally been regarded as insensitive to structural features beneath the topmost atomic layer. In this study, we overturn this paradigm by demonstrating that LFM can not only detect but also classify atomic vacancies in MoS2 according to their depth. By combining experiments with molecular dynamics and Prandtl-Tomlinson (PT) modeling, we identify unique frictional fingerprints associated with each defect type: surface vacancies produce a characteristic ’drop-and-rise’ signature, whereas subsurface vacancies generate a pronounced exit barrier without an entry drop. While molecular dynamics validates these specific MoS2 signatures, the PT model reveals a generic, geometric lattice-disruption mechanism, extending the validity of our findings beyond MoS2. Furthermore, we apply these findings to compare the defect density and defect types in MoS2 flakes obtained via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and mechanical exfoliation.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials, Two-dimensional materials, Characterization and analytical techniques

Introduction

Chemical identification of single atoms at or even beneath solid surfaces remains one of the challenges in nanoscience1–8. Defects such as vacancies govern the mechanical4,9–13, electronic2,14–17, and chemical properties18,19 of functional materials, yet their detection and classification at the atomic scale is notoriously demanding. Scanning probe and transmission electron microscopies have delivered remarkable insights in this direction1,2,6,20–23. In particular, scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), non-contact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM), and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) can resolve atomic-scale defects with high fidelity1,2,7,21–23. However, these approaches generally require ultrahigh vacuum (UHV), cryogenic environments, or extensive sample preparation, which limits their routine applicability.

Lateral force microscopy (LFM) offers a compelling alternative4,5. In this contact-based mode of atomic force microscopy (AFM), a sharp tip is dragged across the surface, and the resulting dissipative forces are recorded. The lateral force signal reflects the stick-slip motion of the tip over the periodic energy landscape of the surface, producing peaks at the atomic scale24–26. While lattice resolution can thus be readily achieved, the ability of LFM to truly resolve atomic defects remains a matter of debate4,5,27,28. Only a few studies have reported atomic-scale defect signatures in LFM maps. Among them, it is worth to mention the cases of a KBr thin film grown in a wavy form on a NaCl surface27, and of a graphene surface with atomic-scale vacancies generated by ion bombardment4. Recently, Yang et al. confirmed the ability of LFM to quantify defects in semiconductors and insulators5. Nonetheless, a critical limitation persists across these studies: the precise identification of defect types has remained elusive, primarily due to the absence of atomistic modeling to interpret the experimental signatures. Furthermore, prevailing understanding in the field -rooted in the technique’s mechanical nature- assumes that LFM is inherently limited to probing the topmost atomic layer and is incapable of detecting subsurface structures.

Here, we address these two issues combining experimental LFM images of atomic defects on MoS2 with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and Prandtl-Tomlinson (PT) modeling. By studying defects on a variety of locations at/beneath the surface, we are able to demonstrate that LFM can not only detect but also distinguish between surface and subsurface vacancies based on unique frictional signatures. Specifically, surface defects are found to yield a distinctive drop-and-rise pattern in the lateral force, whereas subsurface defects manifest as a large lateral force barrier without any distinctive entry drop. These contrasting behaviors are then rationalized within the Prandtl-Tomlinson model, which strips away material-specific chemistry to reveal their origin in a universal lattice-disruption mechanism, thereby establishing the generality of our findings. In addition, using this approach, we directly compare the density and nature of defects in MoS2 flakes prepared by CVD and mechanical exfoliation. In this way, we prove that LFM can be used as a broadly accessible tool for routine atomic-scale defect characterization, extending its reach to both surface and subsurface defects on a representative 2D material and possibly beyond.

Results

Imaging atomic defects with lateral force microscopy

In LFM, the torsion of the scanning cantilever reflects the lateral force acting on the tip (Fig. 1a, b), producing characteristic stick-slip traces (Fig. 1c) that mirror the substrate lattice periodicity24. Atomic defects locally disrupt this periodicity in distinct ways, as schematically illustrated for pristine, surface, and subsurface defects in Fig. 1d–f, but how such disruptions manifest in LFM traces and whether it is sufficiently sensitive for reliable defect characterization remains an open question.

To address this point, we imaged MoS2 flakes in both ambient and high vacuum (HV) conditions (see Method and Supplementary Note 1). In large-area scans (500 × 500 nm2), the surface exhibited low-friction behavior with a friction coefficient of 0.0429.

Scans smaller than 100 × 100 nm2 revealed reproducible features with a characteristic size of ~ 1 nm, consisting of a sharp drop followed by an increase in the lateral force (LF) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Note 1). The nanometer-scale extent of these signatures is consistent with atomic-scale defects 4,5 and allows us to rule out alternative origins such as step edges or surface puckering, which typically produce frictional variations extending over several nanometers 30–34 (see Supplementary Note 2).

In addition, the sample cleaning procedure employed prior to the measurements, together with the persistence of the features under elevated-temperature conditions in HV (see “Methods” and Supplementary Notes 2, 3, and 4), excludes surface contamination as the source of the contrast. Furthermore, the observed density of these features (~ 1012 cm−2) is consistent with the expected natural density of single sulfur vacancies in pristine MoS2 flakes35. Lattice resolved stick-slip images (Fig. 2b, d) confirmed this behavior; a darker region indicating an initial larger drop in the lateral force, followed by a brighter region reflecting an enhanced lateral force when the tip exits the site. These features are referred to as Type 1 defects hereafter. At much lower densities (< 1 × 1011 cm−2), we identified a second type of atomic-sized LF disruption (Type 2 features, (Fig. 2c, e)), for which we observe only a localized increase in LF without an entry drop.

To test whether the distinct contrasts observed experimentally can be attributed to specific atomic vacancies, we performed all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of a four-layered MoS2 surface scanned by an amorphous SiO2 (a-SiO2) tip (see Supplementary Notes 5 and 6). We considered several vacancy types on the topmost MoS2 layer (structures in Supplementary Note 5; simulation details in Method) and focus here on two representative cases. Among these, the sulfur vacancy in the top sublayer produces a pronounced modification of the lateral force, with simulated traces that closely reproduce the experimental Type 1 signature. In both simulation and experiment, the tip experiences a marked drop in lateral force upon entering the vacancy, followed by a greater-than-average force required to pull it out (Fig. 2d).

These findings provide two key insights. First, the simulations demonstrate conclusively that LFM can resolve single-atom vacancies. Second, they show that Type 1 can be associated to sulfur vacancies in the topmost sublayer of MoS2. As detailed in Supplementary Note 7, this behavior is general to all defects involving the removal of a top-layer sulfur atom, making the corresponding vacancies indistinguishable from one another using LFM. The prevalence of Type 1 features in our experiments (Fig. 2a) is therefore in excellent agreement with the fact that sulfur vacancies are the most abundant intrinsic defects in MoS21.

Earlier reports of similar contrasts had linked them to single-atom vacancies but without atomistic validation, leaving open the possibility of alternative origins4,5,28. By combining experiments with simulations, we establish an unambiguous lateral-force fingerprint for surface sulfur defects.

Simulations reveal a further key result: LFM can also detect subsurface atomic vacancies. The simulated lateral force trace (Fig. 2e) for a subsurface molybdenum vacancy (Fig. 1f) closely matches the experimental Type 2 signature, reproducing its characteristic features. As with Type 1, the tip requires an abnormally large lateral force to exit the defect region, which is a distinctive fingerprint of an atomic vacancy4 (regardless of its Type). In contrast to Type 1, no larger drop in lateral force is observed upon entry. As detailed in Supplementary Note 7, this behavior is general to all subsurface defects involving the removal of a molybdenum atom.

These results extend LFM sensitivity beyond the topmost atomic sublayer, with the agreement between experiment and simulation establishing the Type 2 contrast as the fingerprint of subsurface vacancies. Moreover, they unveil earlier doubts about the possibility of detecting subsurface atomic defects with LFM5 and complement the identification of surface defects.

Lateral force signatures of different atomic vacancies

We now aim to characterize the different constrasts produced by the most common atomic vacancies (presented in Supplementary Note 5) using MD simulations. Importantly, these defects are structurally stable and, from a top view, each occupies an area equivalent to a single atom, even though some arise from the removal of multiple atoms (see Supplementary Notes 5 and 8). We classify them by the depth of the first missing atom: surface vacancies occur in the topmost sulphur sublayer (see Fig. 1e), whereas subsurface vacancies lie beneath it (see Fig. 1f).

For each vacancy type, the lateral force trace was obtained from MD simulations (see Supplementary Note 7). All traces display a characteristic saw-tooth shape, defined by a minimum (Fmin), a maximum (Fmax), and a mean (〈F〉) lateral force value, as shown in Fig. 3a, b.

The interpretation of ΔF (see Fig. 3b) is straightforward: a vacancy produces a detectable contrast when either ΔF value significantly deviates from 1, whereas ΔF = 1 corresponds to a trace indistinguishable from the pristine case.

Figure 3c shows ΔFmax for all vacancies considered. In general, both surface and subsurface defects exhibit ΔFmax values well above 1, confirming the capability of LFM to detect atomic vacancies. The only exception is the vacancy related with the removal of the sulfur atom in the bottom atomic sublayer (see Supplementary Note 7). This result suggests that, although LFM is capable of resolving subsurface defects, this sensitivity is limited to the first two atomic sublayers. Furthermore, this technique not only resolves single atomic vacancies, but it also makes it possible to distinguish between surface and subsurface types through ΔFmin. More specifically, as shown in Fig. 3d, only surface defects yield a ΔFmin value significantly above 1, while all subsurface defects remain within the standard deviation of the pristine material. So, while all defects show ΔFmax≫1, only surface defects also exhibit ΔFmin≫1. Therefore, the lateral force increase (ΔFmax≫1) can be considered as a defect-marker feature on the LFM trace that allows defect detection, whereas the lateral force dip (ΔFmin≫1) can act as a fingerprint to distinguish the defect intra-layer depth.

Physical origin of atomic vacancy LFM contrast

To understand why surface and subsurface atomic vacancies produce different lateral force signatures, we first recall the stick-slip mechanism in a pristine lattice. During the stick phase, the tip accumulates stress through cantilever torsion caused by the relative motion between the tip, pinned at the surface, and the driving support advancing at constant velocity. This build-up of stress is detected as the linear increase in the lateral force. The torsion causes the tip to form an angle θ with respect to a vertical line perpendicular to the surface (see Fig. 4a). Indeed, the lateral force is proportional to this torsion angle, FLateral ∝ θ. When θ reaches a critical value, θC, the stick phase ends and the slip phase begins, as represented in Fig. 4a. In the slip phase, the tip moves toward the next potential minimum, releasing part of the accumulated stress. This release of stress results in the abrupt drop of the lateral force. At the end of the slip phase, θ reaches its minimum value for a pristine sample, θP (see Fig. 4a). Then, the tip becomes pinned again, and the stick-slip process repeats.

In the presence of a surface atomic vacancy, the absence of the atom in the topmost sublayer locally disrupts the lattice, increasing twice the spacing the tip travels during slip. The longer slip distance allows the cantilever torsion to relax more, resulting in a smaller post-slip angle, θsur < θP (see Fig. 4a). Consequently, the lateral force shows a larger drop upon entering surface vacancies, resulting in a ΔFmin≫1. In contrast, for a subsurface vacancy, the topmost atomic sublayer remains intact, and the local lattice periodicity is unchanged. Therefore, the slip distance and post-slip angle, θsub, are similar to the pristine case, θsub ≈ θP (see Fig. 4a). So, subsurface defects do not show an additional drop in lateral force as the tip enters the defect, hence ΔFmin≈1.

We now express the previous reasoning in a quantitative manner employing the Prandtl-Tomlinson (PT) model. Solving Eq. (2) with the PES definitions in Eq. (3) reproduces the main features in the lateral force traces observed in both simulations and experiments (Fig. 4c). For surface atomic vacancies, the model yields a pronounced drop in lateral force upon entry, followed by a higher force required to exit the defect region. In contrast, subsurface atomic vacancies show no significant drop during entry but still require an increased force to exit due to the deeper potential well. The contrast difference originates from a geometric effect associated with surface lattice disruption. Specifically, the absence of a surface atom locally increases the effective lattice periodicity, allowing the tip to relax further during slip. This enhanced relaxation leads to a larger slip distance and, consequently, to the pronounced lateral force drop observed for surface vacancies (ΔFmin≫1). For both surface and subsurface vacancies, the increased force required to exit the defect region arises from a deeper effective potential well, which implicitly incorporates elastic, chemical, and other contributions to the tip-sample interaction. Importantly, this analysis relies on minimal, generic arguments within the PT framework and is independent of material-specific details. Hence, by identifying a geometry-driven lattice-disruption mechanism, the PT model clarifies the fundamental origin of the distinct frictional signatures and establishes their generality, extending beyond MoS2 to other TMDs and layered 2D materials.

At the same time, this generality also defines a fundamental limitation of the technique. Because the contrast associated with surface vacancies arises almost entirely from a purely geometric disruption of the lattice, any atomic defect that produces a similar local depression or effective hollow at the surface will generate a comparable frictional signature. A representative example is the substitution of a surface sulfur atom by an oxygen atom. Although less frequent than atomic vacancies, this is the most common type of substitutional impurity in MoS239. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S13 of Supplementary Note 10, the smaller oxygen atom relaxes inward, creating a partially exposed hollow at the surface, in agreement with previous works 39–41. Because this substitution is structurally analogous to a surface vacancy, reactive MD simulations confirm that it yields a mechanically indistinguishable lateral force profile (see Supplementary Note 10). These observations demonstrate that atomic-scale LFM contrast is governed primarily by local geometric and mechanical perturbations of the lattice rather than by the specific chemical identity of the defect. Consequently, while the method enables highly reliable detection and spatial classification of structural disruptions, readily distinguishing between surface and subsurface atomic vacancies, unambiguous chemical identification of the involved atoms remains challenging without complementary spectroscopy.

Robustness of atomic defect signatures under varying conditions

The ability to distinguish surface from subsurface defects in LFM relies on two contrast parameters: ΔFmax and ΔFmin. For ΔFmax, we provide clear evidence of a behavior that was hinted at in earlier studies: a feature as small as 0.2 nm (an atomic defect) can be resolved with a tip ten times larger (2 nm tip radius). The robustness of ΔFmax≫1 stems from the stronger tip-sample interaction at the defect site, amplified in the lateral force trace as unveiled by the PT model. Our unexpected finding is the capability of LFM to distinguish whether a vacancy lies at the surface or below it. This discrimination hinges on ΔFmin, which captures the weaker slip-halting effect caused by a missing surface atom. Unlike ΔFmax, we expect ΔFmin to be more sensitive to measurement conditions. To gauge this, we examine how ΔFmin changes with load, tip radius, and composition, identifying the conditions where ΔFmin≈1, the point at which surface and subsurface vacancies become indistinguishable.

Figure 5a–c show the effect of the load force on ΔFmin from experiments, MD simulations, and the PT model, respectively (see Method and Supplementary Note 11). Each load includes two independent experimental measurements, ensuring reproducibility. In experiments, surface defects robustly yield ΔFmin≈2.9±0.8 over the entire load range. For subsurface defects, ΔFmin remains near unity and within the standard deviation of the pristine region (gray shaded area in Fig. 5). These observations are in good agreement with the predictions from both MD simulations and the PT model. In both approaches, surface vacancies consistently give values close to 2 (2.1 from the PT model), while subsurface vacancies remain near 1 (0.86 from the PT model), all within experimental uncertainty. The absence of load dependence within the range studied (below 4 nN in the experimental case) suggests that the slip-halting mechanism quantified by ΔFmin is driven primarily by lattice disruption at the defect, as demonstrated in the previous section, rather than by the applied normal force. Moreover, both the experiments and the MD simulations show that the geometric signature of the vacancy persists even at zero normal load (Fig. 5a, b). This observation rules out a contrast mechanism based on the formation of transient bonds, since previous studies have demonstrated that a nonzero normal load is required for transient bonding at vacancy sites to significantly alter the lateral force trace 30,42.

The consistent value of ΔFmin≈2.9±0.8 for surface atomic defects across experiments, MD, and the PT model is not coincidental. As mentioned, the missing atom locally alters the periodicity of the lattice (a1 = 2a), effectively doubling the spacing that the tip must traverse during the slip. Since the lateral force is proportional to slip distance, the force drop is roughly twice that of the pristine lattice, making ΔFmin a robust and quantitative measure for counting and classifying atomic vacancies.

The effect of tip size on ΔFmin for surface vacancies is shown in Fig. 5d, based on MD simulations with amorphous SiO2 tips of radii ranging from 2 to 7 nm. To ensure comparable contact conditions, all simulations were performed under a constant normal pressure of ~ 1.3 GPa. However, at sufficiently low forces, the results indicate that the response becomes independent of the applied load. Small tips yield ΔFmin≈2, consistent with the geometric argument above. With increasing tip radius, ΔFmin decreases and eventually falls within the standard deviation of the pristine region (ΔFmin≈1). The larger contact area of bigger tips engages atoms well beyond the defect vicinity, including structurally intact regions that pin the tip and restrict extended slip. As a result, the lateral force drop (ΔFmin) becomes similar to that over pristine regions, and the ability to distinguish surface from subsurface atomic vacancies is lost.

These findings are also robust across different tip materials and geometries (see Supplementary Note 12). MD simulations with the different amorphous SiO2 geometries and diamond tips all show the same behavior. For both a representative surface and a subsurface atomic vacancy, the lateral force required to exit the defect region is consistently larger than in pristine regions, ensuring defect detection through ΔFmax. When the vacancy lies in the surface layer, a pronounced drop in lateral force upon entry enables the distinction between surface and subsurface defects. Importantly, this effect is restricted to tips with radii smaller than ~ 5 nm, sizes readily available from commercial suppliers. Although the actual tip radii of commercial AFM probes frequently differ from the nominal values specified by manufacturers, in many cases, the parameter that proves experimentally more critical than the nominal radius of curvature is the sharpness of the apex itself, which most likely arises from a nanoscale protrusion or feature located at the very end of an otherwise relatively blunt commercial probe.

In addition, it is important to emphasize that the characteristic features of the lateral force traces associated with surface and subsurface atomic defects remain robust across a wide range of material properties and conditions, including variations in layer stiffness, interlayer coupling, mechanical parameters, and the possible formation of transient chemical bonds (see Supplementary Notes 13, 14). In particular, the lateral force trace of a surface atomic defect consistently exhibits a more pronounced drop upon tip entry than that of a subsurface defect. In both cases, however, the lateral force required to extract the tip from the defect region exceeds that measured in the pristine area.

For subsurface vacancies, MD calculations reveal that their signature progressively diminishes as the material stiffness increases (see Supplementary Note 14), which may limit their detectability in sufficiently rigid systems. Furthermore, this mechanical robustness across varying conditions reinforces the conclusion that LFM contrast is dominated by local structural topography rather than chemical identity. Therefore, while the geometric signature of a surface “hole" is highly stable against load and environmental variations, distinguishing an atomic vacancy from a chemically distinct but structurally identical substitutional impurity (see Supplementary Note 10) remains an inherent limitation of the technique.

Sample quality assessment via LFM imaging

The reliability and practical applicability of LFM for visualizing and identifying distinct defect types is assessed by quantifying the defect density in two flakes of MoS2 obtained by different routes: one synthesized by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and the other mechanically exfoliated from a natural molybdenum disulfide bulk crystal.

Native defects are intrinsically present in nominally pristine MoS2, and their density is known to vary significantly depending on the initial crystal quality or the specific CVD growth conditions 1,43. Common characterization techniques employed to estimate defect densities in two-dimensional materials, such as Raman spectroscopy, lack the sensitivity required to detect the ultralow concentration of native defects typically found in MoS229.

To demonstrate the capability of our approach, LFM images were acquired over multiple spatially distinct regions of each sample, enabling a statistically robust evaluation of defect densities (see Supplementary Note 15). The extracted defect densities, together with their associated standard deviations, are summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 6. Our analysis reveals that Type 1 defects are markedly more abundant than Type 2 defects in both CVD-grown and mechanically exfoliated flakes. In addition, CVD-grown samples exhibit a higher density of Type 1 defects compared to exfoliated counterparts. The measured defect densities fall within the same order of magnitude as previously reported values obtained using different experimental techniques 35,44. Moreover, the higher defect density observed in CVD-grown materials, as well as the approximately one order of magnitude difference between Type 1 and Type 2 defects, are consistent with prior studies 45.

Discussion

In this work, we conclusively demonstrate that LFM resolves not only surface but also subsurface atomic defects. Supported by a combination of experiments, molecular dynamics and analytical (Prandtl-Tomlinson) modeling, we unveil a general mechanism that produces well-distinguishable friction contrasts over single-atomic defects. This mechanism establishes a direct connection between lattice disruption induced by an atomic defect (Type 1 defect) and the resulting frictional response. Specifically, atomic geometric holes on the lattice surface produce a characteristic drop-and-rise pattern upon entry and exit, whereas Type 2 atomic defects exhibit only an enhanced exit barrier. Although the experimental and MD investigations provide atomistic validation specifically for MoS2, the Prandtl-Tomlinson analysis proves that the underlying mechanism is geometry-driven and independent of specific chemical details. This complementary approach thereby extends the applicability of our findings to other transition-metal dichalcogenides and layered 2D materials. Likewise, our findings establish LFM as a robust and practical technique for the routine characterization of both surface and subsurface atomic-scale defects.

Methods

LFM Experimental details

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) experiments were carried out in a home-built AFM setup equipped with WSxM controller electronics, with the WSxM software used for both data acquisition and post-processing46,47. LFM experiments were conducted both in HV (10−6 mbar) and ambient conditions, obtaining comparable LF images and profiles in both situations. After preparation and before high-resolution LFM image acquisition, we conducted a gentle yet effective in situ surface cleaning procedure. It consists of scanning the samples with a regular AFM tip under moderate applied normal force (NF < 30 nN) for at least 2 h. Measurements were performed immediately after this cleaning step to minimize potential re-adsorption, using a new LFM tip. This gentle cleaning ensures the integrity of the MoS2 crystallinity, while no signatures indicative of significant surface contamination are observed in the LFM images (see Supplementary Note 4). All the LFM images and profiles shown in this manuscript were acquired using contact mode silicon tips HQ:CSC38/Al BS from MikroMasch®, with a nominal tip curvature radius below 7 nm. We also tested quartz-like tips qp-SCONT from NANOSENSORSTM with a nominal spring constant of 0.01 and a nominal tip curvature radius below 10 nm, obtaining comparable results. LFM images were force-calibrated using the Colchero method as described in previous works4,29. LFM acquisition was taken in a low normal force regime, applying loads between 0.1 nN to 2 nN. LF images and profiles were measured at scan speeds ranging from 20 to 800 nm/s (see Supplementary Note 4). No significant changes in defect signatures were observed at these speeds, ruling out the possibility of dynamic artifacts.

Sample preparation

MoS2 layers used in our experiments were obtained by 2 different approaches: Direct mechanical exfoliation from bulk MoS2 using the scotch tape method on top of previously cleaned SiO2 flat substrates; and few-layer MoS2 grown by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) on sapphire and transferred to similar previously cleaned SiO2 flat substrates. LFM images were acquired on MoS2 flakes ranging from 1 to 5 layers. Similar results were obtained in all cases, however substrate roughness associated features can hinder easy identification of defects for monolayer samples (see Supplementary Note 16). MoS2 thickness was verified using Raman Spectroscopy48.

Atomic level model and force field

The molecular systems under study consist of 4 layers of MoS2 in AB stacking with dimensions of 16.5 nm in the x-direction and 9 nm in y-direction if not specified otherwise, along with amorphous silicon oxide (a-SiO2) tips with radii of 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, and 7 nm. In addition, we also consider a diamond tip with a radius of 2 nm. A representative image of the atomic structure of the system is presented in Supplementary Note 5. The a-SiO2 tips are obtained from a SiO2 bulk that is amorphized using the protocol described in reference 49. The tips are treated as rigid, while the intramolecular interactions of MoS2 are modeled using a Reactive Empirical Bond-Order Potential (REBO), based on the parameterization by Liang et al. 50–52. This parameterization aligns well with the predictions from databases of crystalline properties obtained through DFT 52, particularly emphasized for specific structures involving Mo, S, and Mo-S.

For the intermolecular interactions, we use the 12-6 Lennard-Jones potential for both the interactions between different MoS2 layers and the interactions between the MoS2 layers and the tips, considering a cutoff radius of 1.1 nm. While advanced registry-dependent potentials provide a more accurate description of inter-layer dynamics in MoS253,54, a standard Lennard-Jones potential is sufficient to capture the fundamental contrast mechanism. Because the vacancy-induced frictional contrast investigated here is governed by highly localized tip-surface contact mechanics and top-layer geometric perturbations, rather than large-scale inter-layer shearing, this potential successfully captures the essential physics required to reproduce our experimental observations and analytical results from the Prandtl-Tomlinson model. The parameters between the a-SiO2 tips and the MoS2 layers are: ϵSi-Mo = 0.004 eV, σSi-Mo = 3.79 Å, ϵSi-S = 0.013 eV, σSi-S = 3.25 Å, ϵO-Mo = 0.002 eV, σO-Mo = 3.61 Å, ϵO-S = 0.006 eV, and σO-S = 3.17 Å. These parameters are obtained from reference 55. The interaction parameters between the different MoS2 layers are as follows: ϵMo-Mo = 0.00058595 eV, σMo-Mo = 4.20 Å, ϵMo-S = 0.0028 eV, σMo-S = 3.665 Å, ϵS-S = 0.02 eV, and σS-S = 3.13 Å. These parameters are taken from reference 51. The interaction parameters between the MoS2 layers and the diamond tip are ϵC-Mo = 0.00455 eV, σC-Mo = 4.391 Å, ϵC-S = 0.00164 eV, and σC-S = 3.64 Å. These parameters are taken from reference 56.

Molecular dynamics simulation details and protocol

All simulations are performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) 57. We apply periodic boundary conditions only in the x and y directions. Atomic positions of the MoS2 layers are relaxed, minimizing their potential energy using a conjugate gradient algorithm, where the energy and force tolerances are set to 10−15 eV and 10−6 eV/Å, respectively. Simultaneously, the simulation box is relaxed in the x and y directions to ensure zero external pressure.

Then, we thermalize the system at a temperature of 300K with an integration time step of 1 fs and considering a constant volume. A Langevin thermostat with a damping value of 1 ps is applied to all the atoms in the MoS2 layers. The initial distance between the tips and the top layer of MoS2 is set to 1.3 nm, which is beyond the cutoff radius to prevent any initial interactions. To simulate a substrate, the lowest MoS2 layer is constrained using a harmonic potential applied to each of its atoms (see Supplementary Note 6), centered at their initial positions, with a force constant of khar = 10 N/m. In addition to preventing undesirable movement of the tip, we apply the same harmonic potential with the same force constant to the atoms in the uppermost 0.5 nm layer of the tip. The thermalization process is carried out over 10 ns.

The indentation process begins after the thermalization phase. At this stage, the thermostat is removed from the top three MoS2 layers, allowing them to evolve without any artificial effects. The thermostat remains active only on the bottom layer of MoS2, serving as both the substrate and the temperature controller. For the tip, the harmonic constraint is removed in the z-direction (the indentation direction), but it is maintained in the x and y directions. Using steered molecular dynamics (SMD) at a constant velocity, we slowly lower the tip towards the MoS2. The indentation velocity is set to 1 m/s with an associated force constant of 1 N/m. This indentation process lasts for 3 ns until the tip is fully adsorbed by the MoS2. We then switch from constant-velocity indentation to constant-load indentation and allow the system to equilibrate under this condition for 1 ns. To ensure comparable contact conditions for tips of different sizes, the pressure at the tip-MoS2 interface is kept constant at 1.3 GPa in all cases. Nevertheless, the main results show to be independent of load conditions for the range considered in this work (see Supplementary Note 11). The contact area is estimated using the approach proposed by Mo et al. 58, which explicitly accounts for the discrete atomic nature of the tip-sample interface (see Supplementary Note 17). This treatment is essential to avoid overestimating the contact area and, consequently, the applied load for sharp apex tips such as those employed in our simulations. The resulting load forces, which are the controlled parameters in the simulations, range from 5 to 10 nN depending on the tip size. This procedure lasts 1 ns.

Then, we begin the scan process. This is performed in the x-direction, which is parallel to the zigzag direction. For this, we remove the harmonic constraint on the tip in the x-direction and maintain it only in the y-direction. We perform the scan considering the constant normal pressure of ~ 1.3 GPa and use SMD at a constant velocity of 0.5 m/s, with an associated constant force between 1–5 N/m to pull the tip over the MoS2.

During the scanning process, the instantaneous lateral force, defined as the force experienced by the SMD as the tip moves along the x-direction, is recorded every 50 fs over a 1 ps interval. These values are then averaged over the 1 ps window, and the resulting averages are used to construct the lateral force traces presented for the simulation results.

Prandtl-Tomlinson model

To solve Eq. (2), we use finite difference methods, specifically the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method. The parameters we use are the following: m = 1.0 × 10−10 kg, γ = 8.0 × 105 s−1, v = 1.0 μm/s, dt = 1.0 × 10−10 s, and a = 0.316 nm corresponding to the MoS2 lattice constant in the zigzag direction. The initial conditions we consider are the following: x(0) = 0 and x°(0)=0.

Within this framework, each parameter of the PT model is directly related to the physical properties of the experimental system as follows. The spring constant k represents the effective lateral stiffness of the tip-cantilever assembly. This quantity includes both the torsional stiffness of the cantilever and the elastic compliance of the tip–sample contact. Experimentally, it is obtained from the lateral calibration of the cantilever and corresponds to the proportionality between the measured lateral force and the relative displacement between the cantilever base and the tip–sample junction. The damping coefficient γ accounts for energy dissipation during sliding. Dissipation can arise from multiple sources, including internal losses in the cantilever, phononic and electronic dissipation within the contact, and interfacial relaxation processes. In the PT model, these mechanisms are incorporated phenomenologically into a single viscous-like term that controls the relaxation dynamics. The mass m represents the effective inertial mass associated with the torsional motion of the cantilever and the tip apex. However, under low scanning velocities, the condition mx¨≪γx° is satisfied. Consequently, the results are insensitive to the precise value of m.

This procedure is applied separately to the pristine region (V0) and to the defect region (Vd,i), since the maximum lateral force differs in each case. Hence, within the PT framework, the defect contrast is therefore represented as a local modification of the corrugation potential energy landscape experienced by the tip. The lateral periodicity of the PT potential is fixed by the crystallographic structure of the MoS2 surface along the scan direction (a = 0.316 nm for the zigzag direction).

The effect of the normal load is incorporated through its influence on the MD-derived lateral forces. Increasing the load strengthens the tip-surface interaction and modifies the effective corrugation amplitude. In Fig. 5, this effect is modeled by scaling the corrugation amplitude (0.92Vd and 1.08Vd), where Vd corresponds to the value obtained from MD at a normal pressure of 1.3 GPa (normal load of 5 nN), while all other PT parameters are kept unchanged.

Because each PT parameter is linked to measurable experimental quantities or to observables obtained from MD simulations, this procedure provides a consistent mapping between experiment, atomistic modeling, and the PT description, and can be readily extended to other materials and experimental conditions.
