Machine learning-driven estimation of microplastic percentage yield for rapid and accurate quantification
Authors: Yun-Gu Kang, Jiwon Choi, Jun-Yeong Lee, Taek-Keun Oh, Jwakyung Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate microplastic (MP) quantification in agricultural soils is critical for environmental risk assessment, yet variability in extraction efficiency remains a significant barrier. This study This study investigated machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict MP percentage yield and develop interval-wise correction factors for improved MP quantification. Soils were spiked with four MP types and subjected to density separation using seven brine solutions (e.g., zinc chloride and sodium iodide; 1.00–1.58 g/cm3). Pearson correlation and feature importance scores identified brine density as the dominant driver (score = 0.52), while leave-one-condition-out (LOCO) analysis further validated the robust non-linear superiority of ensemble ML models. Among the six ML model, the random-forest (RF) algorithm exhibited the highest coefficient of determination (R2) at 0.991 with the lowest error metrics. Furthermore, RF-driven interval-wise correction factors demonstrated superior adjustment efficiency, effectively aligning predicted yields with target values across diverse recovery scenarios. The proposed integrated framework offers a scalable approach for supporting standardized extraction protocols. The findings of this study can help for an understanding of the complex interaction between experimental parameters and recovery MP yields, ultimately facilitating more precise laboratory-scale MP monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63367-5.

Introduction

Microplastic (MP) particles have recently gained significant attention as emerging environmental pollutants in both terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. They primarily originate from the degradation of plastic products or the direct disposal of MP-containing materials, and can infiltrate various biological and environmental compartments (e.g., cellular structures, fish-gills, and plant roots) due to their small particle size1. Although MP contamination has been documented in various marine environments2, agricultural soils are among the most vulnerable environments, as it is continuously exposed to the inputs of plastic products (e.g., mulching films and pesticide containers). Once introduced, the MP particles in agricultural soils can fragment into even smaller particles, exacerbating MP-induced soil contamination and accelerating its spread3. Furthermore, MP particles exhibit strong resistance to microbial degradation, allowing them to persist in agricultural soils and plant tissues for hundreds to thousands of years. These environmental threats have gradually raised awareness and intensified research efforts on MP contamination in agricultural environments.

With growing concern over MP-driven contamination, various extraction methods have been proposed, such as density separation, visual sorting, and electrostatic separation. Among these methods, the density separation method has been widely adopted due to its advantages, including simplicity, high recovery rates, and low cost2–5. However, the reported MP percentage yields, which are critical for accurate quantification, vary significantly depending on experimental conditions (Table 1)6–10. These conditions include the type of the MP particles, the number of extraction repetitions, the specific density of the brine solutions, and soil characteristics4,9,11. Because the density separation method relies on differences between the specific densities of the MP particles and brine solutions, it is particularly sensitive to these features. For instance, a previous study found that the MP recovery yields were often below 60%, regardless of the intrinsic density of brine solutions3. Similarly, another study reported wide variations in separation efficiency, ranging from 5% to 100%, and emphasized that increasing MP percentage yields typically requires time-consuming and labor-intensive procedures11. To address this challenge, machine learning (ML) can be employed to predict MP percentage yields under diverse experimental conditions, facilitating more accurate MP quantification9.

This study hypothesized that (1) the MP percentage yield is primarily influenced by the experimental parameters of the density separation method, and can be predicted using various ML algorithms; and (2) correction factors derived from these predicted values can enable rapid and accurate adjustment of low MP percentage yields. To assess these hypotheses, we proposed a systematic framework consisting of the following steps. First, an experimental database was prepared by spiking four types of MP particles into soil matrices and performing density separation under varying brine solution conditions. Second, the fundamental relationships between experimental parameters and MP yields were characterized using Pearson correlation and feature importance analyses to identify key mechanistic drivers. Third, six ML algorithms were trained and validated to identify the optimal predictive model based on their regression performance and error metrics. Finally, an adaptive optimization strategy was implemented by developing interval-wise correction factors to refine the predicted MP yields, ensuring high quantification precision across diverse recovery scenarios. Through its integrated approach, we propose a practical and efficient strategy for optimizing MP quantification across different types of MP particles and experimental setups.

Results

Correlation and feature importance in forecasting the MP percentage yield

The correlation between experimental conditions (e.g., MP and brine solution densities) and MP percentage yield were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r) and feature importance scores (Fig. 1). The MP percentage yield exhibited a positive (+) correlation with most experimental conditions associated with the density separation method, with the exception of MP density (MP den.). Specifically, brine solution density (Solution den.) showed a strong positive correlation (Pearson’s r = + 0.60) with the MP percentage yield (MP yield), whereas the MP den. parameter showed a moderate negative (-) correlation at -0.47. The MP concentration in agricultural soil (Conc.) and the number of separation repetitions (Rep.) revealed relatively weak positive correlations of + 0.15 and 0.21, respectively.

Feature importance scores mirrored the correlation analysis, and were ranked as follows: the Solution den. (0.52), MP den. (0.34), Rep. (0.08), and Conc. (0.05) parameters. These findings suggest that increases in brine solution densities, repetition counts, and soil MP concentrations may improve the MP percentage yield during the separation process.

Selection of the optimal ML algorithm for MP yield prediction

Six ML algorithms, including random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), multiple linear regression (MLR), ridge regression (Ridge), and lasso regression (Lasso), were evaluated to compare their regression performance for predicting MP percentage yield (Table 1). The RF algorithm achieved the highest adjusted coefficient of determination (R2) at 0.991, indicating excellent explanatory ability, whereas the Lasso algorithm exhibited the lowest model performance (adjusted R2 value = 0.517). Additionally, the linear-based regression models (i.e., MLR, Ridge, and Lasso; Eq. 1) generally underperformed compared to the ML algorithms that capture non-linear relationships. The adjusted R2 values of the MLR, Ridge, and Lasso algorithms were 0.622, 0.615, and 0.517, respectively, whereas the GB and KNN algorithms yielded higher values at 0.962 and 0.773, respectively. When applied to the test dataset, the linear-based ML models showed slight improvements in the adjusted R2 values, suggesting better generalizability. Specifically, the adjusted R2 value of the MLR algorithm was enhanced from 0.622 (training) to 0.663 (test), and both Ridge and Lasso algorithms showed the increases from 0.615 to 0.641 and 0.517 to 0.538, respectively. In contrast, the regression performance of the RF and GB algorithms saw slightly decreased, and the adjusted R2 value of the KNN algorithm showed a more remarkable decrease to 0.543. These findings suggest that the relationships between experimental setups and MP yield can be accurately explained with the ML algorithms that involve non-linear relationship, and highlighting the need to evaluate multiple ML algorithms due to variability in predictive performance.

The error metrics, namely MSE, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, quantify the difference between the measured and predicted MP percentage yield, and the ML algorithm, involving the lower error metrics, was considered to have the superior regression performance9,11. The RF algorithm consistently yielded the lowest error metrics compared to other ML algorithms, and these metrics were increased as both R2 and adjusted R2 decreased. For example, the MAPE value, indicating the accuracy of predictive model, for the RF algorithm stood a 0.046% in training dataset, while the GB, KNN, MLR, Ridge, and Lasso algorithms yielded the values of 0.091, 0.234, 0.333, 0.359, and 0.451%, respectively. Furthermore, the RF algorithm yielded the value of 6.598% in test dataset, and this was 1.21-fold and 2.77-fold lower than those of the GB and Lasso algorithms, respectively. These findings indicate that rigorous validation is essential for accurately evaluating the regression performance of ML models.

Validation of regression performance between the RF- and MLR-based models

In this study, the RF and MLR algorithms, representing the non-linear and linear relationships-based ML algorithms, respectively, were selected based on their adjusted R2 value, and they were subjected to the K-fold cross-validation (Table 2). The mean R2 value of the RF and MLR algorithms was estimated at 0.96 and 0.62, respectively, and their median R2 values were 0.96 and 0.65. However, both regression models exhibited the negative skewness at -0.67 (RF) and − 0.94 (MLR), indicating that most findings were better than the averaged R2 value, with a few low-performing outliers. The positive kurtosis values further supported the robustness of both regression models. The RF algorithm also outperformed the MLR-based model in terms of error metrics. The mean values of the MAE and RMSE metrics exhibited the lowest values at 3.63% and 5.76% in the RF algorithm, and it had the 3.92-fold and 2.94-fold lower values than the MLR algorithm.

Furthermore, a leave-one-condition-out (LOCO) analysis was performed to validate the predictive models and evaluate the relative contribution of each experimental parameter to the overall model performance (Fig. 2). The ensemble-based RF and GB algorithms demonstrated high robustness, maintaining mean R2 values above 0.80 even when the Rep. parameter was omitted. In contrast, the exclusion of MP den., Solution den., and Conc. resulted in a significant increase in the average RMSE values, indicating their critical role in model accuracy. While the trends in R2 degradation were consistent across all ML algorithms, with linear-based models consistently yielding lower R2 values, the sensitivity of RMSE to variable exclusion varied by ML algorithm types. For the KNN model, the change in averaged RMSE values across different variable combinations was relatively marginal. However, the ensemble-based models maintained consistent levels of uncertainty even in the absence of the Rep. parameter. For the linear-based MLR, Ridge, and Lasso algorithms, the exclusion of MP den. and Solution den. led to a substantial rise in averaged RMSE values, whereas the omissions of the Rep. and Conc. parameters did not induce significant fluctuations. Collectively, the relative influence of each experimental feature on model performance followed the order: Solution den. > MP den. > Conc. > Rep.

Development and verification of the interval-wise correction factors

Correction factors, deriving from the mean value of the predicted MP percentage yield by the RF and MLR algorithms, were grouped into 10% intervals (e.g., 50–60% and 90–100% intervals) and summarized in Table 3. Especially, in this study, the correction factors were developed by selecting 10% intervals, which was strategically intended to maintain a balance between granular sensitivity and statistical robustness. This approach was taken because setting narrower intervals could risk over-fitting due to an insufficient data densities per segment, while broader intervals might fail to capture the non-linear deviations inherent in the ML predictions. Additionally, since all measured MP percentage yield exceeded 50%, the correction factors were only developed for the 50–100% range.

Discussions

The MP percentage yield can be improved by increasing the number of separation repetitions, with the high MP concentration in the environmental matrix. This background is consistent with the extraction processes for other substances (e.g., heavy metals, soluble salts, and nutrients) from agricultural soils. Despite the widespread use of the density separation method for extracting the MP particles from agricultural soils, sediments, and aquatic ecosystems, the previous studies have reported in consistent findings regarding the optimal density of the brine solutions for achieving accurate and efficient MP extraction3–5,12,13. Previous study noted that the NaCl (1.10–1.30 g/cm3) solutions, recommended by the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), were economically favorable and effective for low-density MP samples (e.g., PE and PS)14. However, these solutions were less effective at extracting high-density MP particles. Moreover, in other study, the ZnCl2 (1.50 g/cm3) and NaI (1.60 g/cm3) solutions to extract five MP types (i.e., PP, PE, PS, PVC, and PET) yielded averaged MP percentage yields of 83.9% and 82.2%, respectively4. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that the difference between the intrinsic densities of the MP particles and brine solutions is a crucial factor influencing the MP percentage yield3–5,15,16. This was reflected in the negative correlation of the specific MP density within our regression models. The alignment between previous studies and our findings underscores the importance of selecting brine solutions based on the intrinsic density of the target MP particles in order to achieve accurate MP yield and its prediction3,4.

Additionally, this study found that the specific impact of input features on prediction performance varied significantly between linear and non-linear ML algorithms17. While the Pearson correlation analysis provided a foundational justification for feature selection by quantifying global monotonic relationships, it primarily captures linear dependencies. The unique advantage of Pearson analysis lies in its ability to provide a computationally efficient and statistically grounded diagnostic of the physical data trends before subjecting them complex modeling18. By establishing these foundational baselines, we ensured that the feature selection was rooted in the fundamental physics of density separation. In contrast, the superior performance of the RF algorithm is deeply rooted in how it interprets these features compared to the MLR model14. The RF model captures non-linear interactions by utilizing parameters like Solution den. and MP den. as primary splitting nodes. This allows the model to identify critical buoyancy thresholds that a linear MLR model, which assumes a simple additive relationship, inherently overlooks. This mechanistic importance was further validated by the LOCO analysis, which demonstrated that the exclusion of density-related features led to a more severe degradation in model performance compared to other parameters. The high robustness of the RF and GB algorithms during the LOCO test, maintaining averaged R2 values above 0.80, highlights their capacity to maintain predictive stability by leveraging hierarchical decision structures. Conversely, the significant increase in RMSE for linear models when density features were removed reinforces the necessity of non-linear architectures for capturing the complex buoyancy-driven separation process. These findings suggest a non-linear relationship between the experimental setups and the MP yields, and emphasize the need for ML algorithms capable of interpreting such complexity for accurate prediction of the MP percentage yield. This observation aligns with previous study, demonstrating the correlation between the model performance and error metrics17–20. These studies commonly reported that regression models with higher R2 values typically exhibit lower error metrics (e.g., RMSE and MAPE), reinforcing the inverse relationship between model fit and prediction error. By integrating both statistical baselines (Pearson correlation), algorithmic interpretability (feature importance and SHAP analysis), and robustness validation (LOCO analysis), this study ensures that the model is not only mathematically accurate but also mechanically representative of the complex density separation process21. Therefore, understanding both the mathematical characteristics of ML algorithms and the nature of the input features is essential for building accurate and reliable predictive models.

The interval-wise correction factors developed in this study significantly contributed to the accuracy of the predicted MP yields, particularly when applied using the RF algorithm. This improvement further highlights the importance of not only selecting an appropriate regression model but also applying post-modeling correction to refine outputs. The superior performance of the RF algorithm-driven modification compared to the MLR algorithm again emphasizes that advantage of non-linear approaches in handling variability in environmental data. However, although the implementation of interval-wise correction factors significantly enhanced the alignment between predicted and target yields, the relationship between these specific experimental conditions and the broader generalizability of the corrections factors should be carefully considered. The correction factors derived in this study were developed based on data concentrated in the 50–100% range, reflecting high-efficiency recovery scenarios. While this ensures high predictive accuracy for optimized processes, applying these factors to low-yield environments or systems (particularly below 50%) with significantly different ingredient characteristics may require further calibration. Furthermore, since these factors are closely linked to the specific thermal and chemical profiles of our experimental setup, their universal application to diverse reactor geometries or varying catalyst concentrations remains to be fully validated. Hence, to enhance the robustness of this predictive framework, future studies should remain effective across universal production and recovery environments.

Nevertheless, the findings of this study collectively establish an integrated, adaptive optimization framework for MP quantification. In this framework, the Pearson correlation analysis and feature importance evaluation served as the initial diagnostic stage, identifying the mechanistic drivers and justifying the selection of input features. This was followed by the algorithmic optimization stage, where the non-linear superiority of the RF model was leveraged to handle complex interactions within the dataset21,22. Finally, the development of interval-wise correction factors functioned as an adaptive refinement stage, compensating for residual systematic biases across varying yield segments. By integrating these analytical stages, from initial feature prioritization to post-modeling adjustment, this study provides a comprehensive optimization loop that ensures both statistical rigor and practical reliability. In real-field applications, this framework can be deployed as an automated decision-support tool in environmental laboratories, enabling practitioners to pre-optimize extraction protocols and apply real-field correction to raw MP recovery data for more efficient, large-scale soil monitoring. Such a holistic approach is significant as it demonstrates that maximizing the accuracy of environmental forecasting requires a synergistic transition from fundamental statistical characterization to adaptive algorithmic correction.

Summary and conclusions

This study has demonstrated the potential of machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict the percentage yield of microplastic (MP) particles and to develop interval-based correction factors for improving the accuracy and consistency of MP recovery rates in agricultural soils. Input features included the specific densities of the four MP particles (i.e., PE, PS, PET, and PVC; 0.94–1.40 g/cm3) and six brine solutions (i.e., NaCl, NaH2PO4, CaCl2, NaBr, ZnCl2, and NaI; 1.00–1.58 g/cm3), alongside separation repetitions and soil MP concentrations. Prior to model development, correlation analysis confirmed that brine density (Pearson’s r = + 0.60; importance score = 0.52) was the most influential feature affecting MP recovery, which was consistently reflected in the model outcomes. Among the six evaluated ML algorithms, the random forest (RF) model exhibited superior predictive performance, as confirmed by cross-validation stability and lower error metrics compared to other models.

The proposed interval-wise correction approach offers distinct advantages by providing a statistically supported and practically applicable framework to compensate for systematic biases in MP extraction efficiency without requiring exhaustive manual re-testing. By incorporating cross-validation predictions and accounting for variability across experimental replicates, the framework explicitly reflects uncertainty in both observed and predicted MP yields. the used of non-linear ML models (e.g., RF) enables effective capture of complex interactions among environmental variables, resulting in improved predictive reliability compared to conventional linear correction approaches.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. A primary disadvantage is the computational complexity and the requirement for a robust initial dataset to avoid over-fitting, particularly when defining narrow intervals. Furthermore, the current study is limited by its specific experimental boundary conditions (e.g., particle shape, aging, biofouling, and organic matter interactions); the developed correction factors are optimized for the 50–100% yield range and specific soil matrices used herein. Consequently, their direct applicability to low-yield scenarios (specifically below 50%) or highly heterogeneous organic soils remains to be fully validated. Overall, while the integration of ML algorithms and correction factors significantly reduces experimental efforts and enhances quantification accuracy, future research should focus on expanding the dataset to include diverse environmental media to ensure the universal applicability of this predictive framework.

Materials and methods

Experimental data collection

Soil samples for evaluating the MP yield were randomly collected from 10 locations within public reclaimed agricultural land (Saemangeum, South Korea; 35˚ 49 ‘06.3’’ N, 126˚ 41’ 51.2’’ E), where upland crops cultivated under a farm rental system. The samples were air-dried for three weeks in a glass greenhouse at Chungnam National University (Daejeon, South Korea) until a constant weight was achieved to ensure complete moisture removal and were then passed through a 2-mm sieve. Previous studies have highlighted soil organic matter (OM) content as a key factor hindering the MP separation efficiency5. Accordingly, the reclaimed agricultural soils, known for their low OM content, were selected for evaluating the MP percentage yield (Table 4).

The density separation method-based MP extraction was performed as follows: (1) Twenty grams of experimental soils, spiked with each MP sample at concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 0.5%, was treated with Fenton’s reagent to oxidize soil OM and dried in a dry-oven at 60℃. (2) One hundred milli-liter of each brine solution was added to the dried soil samples and stirred for 30 min. (3) The mixtures were allowed to settle for 30 min, and the MP-containing supernatant was collected. (4) Interfering materials (e.g., plant debris and soil sediment) were removed using a separatory funnel. (5) The isolated MP particles were filtered, dried at 60℃, and weighed. This process was repeated five times, and the MP percentage yield was recorded at each interval2. A summary of the experimental features and descriptive statistics is provided in Tables 5, 6.

Modeling and validation

Predictive model and correlation analysis

To identify the most influential parameter affecting MP recovery, the relationships between experimental variables and MP percentage yield were evaluated using the Pearson correlation method for continuous variables. Correlation coefficients (r) and associated p-values were calculated to assess both the strength and statistical significance of pairwise linear relationships. This preliminary analysis was used to screen variable relevance, detect potential collinearity among predictors, and support the final selection of model input features. All modeling, statistical analysis, and data visualization were conducted using Python software (version 3.10.4).

Selection of machine learning algorithms

Six ML algorithms were employed to predict the MP percentage yield and derive correction factors as follows: random-forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), multiple linear regression (MLR), ridge regression (Ridge), and lasso regression (Lasso). These algorithms were selected based on their reported effectiveness in prior studies and their suitability for regression tasks under limited-data conditions21,22. Given the objectives of this study—to identify the superior-performing predictive model and develop optimal correction factors for MP percentage yield modification—we evaluated the regression performance of each ML algorithm. Particularly, the ensemble-based models (i.e., RF and GB) were included to capture non-linear responses and high-order interactions among variables22–24, whereas the linear models (i.e., MLR, Ridge, and Lasso) were used as interpretable baseline models and to assess the extent to which regularization improved robustness under potential multicollinearity25,26. The KNN model was additionally evaluated as a non-parametric method capable of modeling local patterns without assuming linearity.

Model training and over-fitting

To ensure the robustness of the predictive framework and address potential over-fitting, the experimental dataset was partitioned into training (n = 1,120) and testing (n = 280) sets at an 8:2 random split (Table 6). The test set was held out during model development and used only for final performance evaluation. A K-fold cross-validation (K = 10) procedure was applied to the primary RF and MLR models within the training dataset to assess the stability of the model performance across different subsets. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of the cross-validation scores were recorded to quantify performance uncertainty and to determine whether model behavior was sensitive to data partitioning. For reproducibility, a fixed random seed (20250702) was applied to all procedures involving randomness, including data splitting and tree-based model construction. For ensemble algorithms, this seed ensured reproducible decision-tree structures and corresponding yield predictions. Model hyperparameters were selected using the training data only, and no information from the test data was used during model fitting or parameter adjustment. This strategy was adopted to avoid information leakage and to obtain an unbiased estimate of predictive performance.

Evaluation error metric and statistical validation

Regression performance was evaluated using the adjusted R2 value and for error metrics (i.e., MSE, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE). Adjusted R2 value was prioritized over standard R2 value because it accounts for the number of predictors and therefore provides a more conservative measure of model explanatory power when multiple input variables are included. Both MSE and RMSE values were used to quantify the magnitude of squared prediction errors, MAE was used to summarize absolute deviations in the original response scale, and MAPE was used to assess relative prediction error in percentage terms26.

To improve statistical rigor, model performance was reported not only as point estimates but also in terms of variability across cross-validation folds, thereby providing an explicit measure of predictive uncertainty. In addition, replicate-based descriptive statistic (mean ± SD) were used for the experimentally observed MP percentage yields. Where relevant, uncertainty in predicted values was interpreted together with fold-to-fold variability and residual dispersion27.

The reliability and statistical validity of model residuals were further examined using residual and Q-Q plots. Specifically, residual plots were used to assess homoscedasticity, that is, whether residual variance remained approximately constant across the fitted range, whereas Q-Q plots were applied to assess the normality of residuals. These diagnostics were included to verify whether the assumptions of regression-based inference were reasonably satisfied and to identify possible systematic bias, non-linearity, or outlier effects.

Development of correction factors and practical assessment

Interval-wise correction factors were derived from the mean predicted yields of the RF and MLR models. For each interval, the correction factor was calculated as a model-based adjustment coefficient intended to compensate for incomplete MP recovery under different density separation conditions. To assess their practical applicability, these correction factors were applied to 80 randomly generated validation scenarios representing realistic operational ranges: MP density (0.85–1.75 g/cm3), soil concentration (0.1–0.5%), and separation repetitions (1–10 times). These randomized scenarios were designed to test the generalizability of the correction framework beyond the exact combinations used during model training. The corrected yields were then compared with the original model outputs to evaluate the consistency and practical usefulness of the proposed correction approach across diverse laboratory-scale recovery conditions (Fig. 5).
