Sema3E‐plexinD1 Axis Is a Modulator of Foxp3+ Regulatory T‐Cells in House Dust Mite Model of Asthma
Authors: Mojdeh Matloubi, Chukwunonso Onyilagha, Lianyu Shan, Sujata Basu, Andrew J Halayko, Mohamed Almiski, Abdelilah S Gounni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAirway epithelial cells (AEC) are the primary sources of Semaphorin3E (Sema3E) in human airways and its expression is downregulated in severe asthmatics, underscoring the AEC‐derived Sema3E critical role in lung homeostasis. Here, we investigated the effects of AEC‐derived Sema3E on immune responses in an acute house dust mite (HDM) asthma model. Transgenic Sema3E overexpression in AECs, using tamoxifen‐inducible Scgb1a1Cre, did not alter lung neutrophils, eosinophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells, and CD4 T‐cell number and BALF cytokine levels. However, it significantly increased lung Foxp3+ Treg cell number and proliferation. To assess the role of Sema3E‐plexinD1 signaling in Foxp‐3 cells, cell‐specific Foxp3‐PLXND1 knockout (KO) mice were subjected to HDM exposure. These mice exhibited reduced Foxp3+ Treg cell numbers in the lungs, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes, accompanied by increased lung eosinophil infiltration, elevated IFN‐γ+ CD4+ T cells in the spleen, and higher BALF levels of IL‐17, IL‐5, and CXCL8. Foxp3 PLXND1 deficiency also enhanced the number of T follicular helper cells, germinal center B cells, and IgG1/IgE antibodies in sera. Additionally, Muc5AC gene expression and goblet cell hyperplasia were elevated in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice. These findings highlight the Sema3E‐plexinD1 axis as a key regulator of Treg cell‐mediated immune homeostasis, suggesting its therapeutic potential in asthma.Keywords: airway epithelial cells, airway hyperresponsiveness, airway inflammation, airway remodeling, asthma, PlexinD1, regulatory T cell, Semaphorin3E

Introduction

Asthma is a complex respiratory disease characterized by inflammation in the airways, increased mucus and collagen production, and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), leading to symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, and coughing [1]. Notwithstanding the progress made in pharmacological treatments for asthma, approximately 5%–10% of patients demonstrate a lack of responsiveness to these medications [2]. This underscores the need to identify novel regulatory pathways that govern airway immune tolerance and homeostasis.

Semaphorins were initially identified as neuronal guidance molecules in the neural system [3]. However, they are present in various organs and tissues, participating in diverse signaling pathways [4, 5]. In respiratory diseases, semaphorins play crucial roles in processes like cell–cell contact, migration, proliferation, differentiation, and immune system regulation [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Among them, semaphorin 3E (Sema3E) has gained significant attention due to its immunomodulatory properties, particularly in asthma pathogenesis [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Sema3E binds strongly to plexinD1, serving as its primary receptor [19, 20, 21], and a key regulator of axon guidance, vascular patterning, and thymocyte migration [6, 21]. The loss of plexinD1 function has been linked to various autoimmune diseases and cancers [22], highlighting its importance in immune regulation and cellular signaling.

Previous findings from our research group have revealed the key roles of Sema3E and its corresponding receptor, plexinD1, in modulating airway inflammation, remodeling, and hyperresponsiveness [23]. Specifically, the global deletion of the Sema3E gene in mice resulted in significant lung granulocyte recruitment, accompanied by a pronounced Th2/Th17 immune response [13, 14, 17]. This was characterized by increased AHR, excessive mucus production, and collagen deposition, leading to sub‐epithelial fibrosis. Conversely, the intranasal administration of recombinant Sema3E effectively alleviated these pathological manifestations [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 23], thereby highlighting the crucial homeostatic function of the Sema3E‐plexinD1 axis within the context of allergic asthma.

Based on RNAseq data, airway epithelial cells (AEC) emerge as the primary source of Sema3E in the lungs (www.lungmap.net). In the Severe Asthma Research Program (SARP), a significant association was found between airway epithelial‐derived Sema3E and severe asthma in humans, after accounting for various factors, including age, sex, race, as well as inhaled and oral corticosteroids (ICS, OCS) [24]. Moreover, in agreement with these observations, we showed that primary AEC from severe asthmatics expressed lower levels of Sema3E mRNA and protein in vitro. Ex vivo, Sema3E protein level is reduced in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of severe asthmatics compared to healthy donors and was associated with compromised lung function (reduced forced expiratory volume in 1 s, FEV1) [25]. In line with observations in asthmatic patients, both acute and chronic murine models of asthma showed a substantial reduction in Sema3E immunoreactivity at the airway epithelial barrier following a challenge with house dust mite (HDM) [13, 14], highlighting the potential role of AEC‐derived Sema3E in the pathogenesis of asthma and the maintenance of airway homeostasis.

Immune homeostasis in the context of allergic asthma is maintained by induction of a tolerogenic response to the allergens [26]. Genetic and immunologic studies reinforce the pivotal role of regulatory T (Treg) cells in fine‐tuning immune tolerance, which leads to the prevention of allergic asthma [26]. However, the beneficial Treg cell response could be pathologically reversed in a pro‐allergic milieu [26]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms underlying control of Treg cell functions in allergic asthmatic conditions is of great importance. Given the observed reduction in Sema3E levels in severe asthma [25] and its role in immune modulation, we hypothesize that the Sema3E‐plexinD1 signaling axis is a critical regulator of Treg cell function in allergic airway disease.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the role of AEC‐derived Sema3E in the HDM allergic asthma model. To test this, we developed a transgenic mouse with inducible overexpression of Sema3E in lung airway epithelial cells (AEC‐Sema3E) and assessed how epithelial‐cell specific Sema3E overexpression influences immune cell populations and cytokine profiles in the acute HDM model of asthma. Our findings demonstrate that while Sema3E overexpression in airway epithelial cells does not alter the overall numbers of immune cells or affect cytokines and antibody levels, it significantly enhances the number and proliferation of Foxp3+ Treg cells in response to HDM exposure in the lungs. Mechanistically, in vivo deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3‐expressing cells resulted in marked reduction in Foxp3+ Treg cell numbers across the lungs, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes. Loss of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells led to a shift in immune balance, promoting stronger inflammatory responses both locally and systemically. This was associated with increased lung eosinophils number and enhanced levels of IL‐5, CXCL‐8, and IL‐17 in the BALF. Moreover, we found higher numbers of activated IFN‐γ CD4+ T cells, as well as germinal center activity, including higher germinal center B cells and T follicular helper cells (TFH) in the spleen and lungs, as well as IgG1 and IgE antibodies production. Interestingly, these immune changes occurred without affecting airway hyperresponsiveness, suggesting that inflammation and airway function may be uncoupled in this model. In the lungs, we also observed excessive mucus production, as shown by upregulated Muc5AC expression and goblet cell hyperplasia. Overall, this study offers innovative insights into the role of the Sema3E‐plexinD1 pathway in maintaining immune homeostasis in asthma.

Method and Material

Sex as a Biological Variable

Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes.

Animals

We generated a Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mouse model that expresses a conditional inducible expression of Sema3E transgene using a vector containing a CAG promoter separated from the full‐length cDNA mSema3E sequence by a loxP‐stop‐loxP sequence (Cyagen Biosciences, Santa Clara, CA). This vector was linearized and electroporated into R1 embryonic stem (ES) cells, and clones with single insertion events were identified using Southern blot analysis as previously described [27]. Three clones were selected to assess mSema3E expression before and after tamoxifen treatment, revealing varying expression levels of the mSema3E transgene. These clones were then used to generate chimeras through a standard ES cell aggregation assay [28]. All chimeras produced germline transmission, and two independent lines were chosen and further characterized for phenotypical analysis in this study. Subsequently, to create AEC‐specific Sema3E transgenic mice, hereafter AEC‐Sema3E, Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mice were bred with Scgb1a1Cre mice (Scgb1a1 tm1cre/ERT)Blh/Scgb1a1, (Jackson Laboratory) as previously described [29]. These mice conditionally express tamoxifen‐inducible Cre, specifically in non‐ciliated epithelial cells and basal progenitor cells primarily located in the bronchioles and respiratory tree [29], allowing the inducible expression of the Sema3E transgene in these cells upon tamoxifen administration in AEC‐Sema3E mice.

PLXND1 fl/fl mice (B6;129‐PLXND1 tm1.1Tmj/J ) [9] were kindly provided by Dr. T.M. Jessell (Columbia University/Howard Hughes Medical Institute, New York, NY) and were crossed with Foxp3 EGFP‐cre‐ERT2 mice (Foxp3tm9(EGFP/cre/ERT2)Ayr/J, Jackson Laboratory Strain#:016961) to generate Foxp3 EGFP‐cre‐ERT2 :PLXND1 fl/fl mice. The breeding scheme consists of crossing hemizygous Foxp3‐ creERT2 mice with homozygous PLXND1 fl/fl mice [30].

HDM‐Induced Airway Inflammation Model

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane. To induce allergic airway inflammation using HDM, anesthesia was induced by placing the mice in an induction chamber with the gas flowmeter set to 500–1000 mL/min and the vaporizer at 4% isoflurane. The absence of a pedal reflex confirmed the successful induction of anesthesia.

Six‐ to eight‐week‐old male and female AEC‐Sema3E and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice underwent tamoxifen treatment (Sigma) to specifically induce the expression of the Sema3E transgene in Scgb1a1 airway epithelial cells or delete the PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells. In brief, each mouse received eight milligrams of tamoxifen (80 mg/mL, dissolved in canola oil) per day through oral gavage every other day, for 3 days [31]. Subsequently, AEC‐Sema3E, Sema3E‐STOP fl/fl , PLXND1 fl/fl and Foxp3 PLXND1 mice were intranasally administered 25 μg of HDM extract (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, lot 259 585; Der‐p‐1: 298.36 mcg/vial, protein: 5.34 mg/vial, endotoxin: 615 EU/vial, Greer Laboratories, Lenoir, NC) in 35 μL of sterile saline for 5 days per week over two consecutive weeks (Figure 2A) as we described previously [13, 32]. The control mice were challenged with 35 μL of sterile saline. The mice were then sacrificed 48 h after the final challenge for outcome assessment.

To capture the early effects of AEC‐derived Sema3E overexpression on the Treg cells in the lung, we used a brief HDM exposure. In this approach, AEC‐Sema3E mice initially received tamoxifen, as mentioned earlier. Subsequently, these mice, along with the Sema3E‐STOP fl/fl group, underwent intranasal administration of 50 μg of HDM extract in 40 μL of sterile saline for 2 days. The mice were then sacrificed 48 h after the last challenge (Figure 2F). For tissue collection and experimental endpoint, anesthesia was achieved as mentioned above and maintained using a nose cone, with the gas flowmeter adjusted to 100–200 mL/min and the vaporizer set to 2%–3% isoflurane, ensuring that the mice remained unconscious and experienced no pain. Mice were then euthanized via cardiac perfusion or cervical dislocation, following ethical guidelines.

Methacholine Challenge Test

Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) parameters, including airway resistance (Rn), tissue resistance (G), and tissue elastance (H), were measured using the FlexiVent animal ventilator (Scireq, Montreal, QC, Canada) as previously described [13]. Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium (54.7 mg/mL) at a dose of 90 mg/kg based on their body weight (e.g., 33 μL for a 20 g mouse). Anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex, and a 45 mg/kg maintenance dose was administered if necessary to maintain proper anesthesia. Once anesthetized, a tracheostomy was performed.

Mice treated with either HDM or saline underwent tracheostomy and then received an intratracheal administration of increasing doses of methacholine (Saline, 3, 6, 12, 25, and 50 mg/mL) at 5‐min intervals, and lung physiology parameters were captured, as previously described [13].

Characterization and Immunophenotyping of AEC Sema3E and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO Mice at Steady State

Under steady‐state conditions, immunophenotyping of lungs, spleen, and blood cells was conducted using FACS. Tissues were collected from both Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl , Sgba1Cre, AEC‐Sema3E, PLXND1 fl/fl and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice treated or not with Tam. Single‐cell suspensions were prepared [17]. After washing and blocking with Fc‐blocker, cells were stained with a mixture containing the following anti‐mouse antibodies (0.5 μL of antibodies/20 μL of flow buffer per tube), for AEC‐Sema3E mice: fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐eFluor 450 (clone 30F11; eBioscience), CD3‐APC/Cyanine7 (clone 17A2; BioLegend), CD4‐PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (clone RM4‐5; Biolegend), CD‐8‐FITC (clone 53–6.7; BioLegend), B220‐allophycocyanin (clone RA3‐6B2; BioLegend), Ly6G‐PE (clone 1A8; BioLegend), NK1.1‐PE/Cyanine7 (clone PK136; BioLegend). Moreover, for Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice: The first panel consisted of fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐APC‐Cy7 (clone 30‐F11; BioLegend), MHC class II (I‐A/I‐E)‐PB (clone AF6‐120.1; BioLegend), Siglec F‐PE (clone E50‐2440; BD Biosciences), CD11b‐PE/Cy7 (clone M1/70), CD11c‐PerCP/Cy5.5 (clone N418), Gr‐1 (Ly6G)‐allophycocyanin (clone 1A8). The second panel included fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐APC‐Cy7 (clone 30‐F11; BioLegend), CD3‐PE (clone 145‐2C11; eBioscience), CD4‐allophycocyanin (clone GK1.5; Biolegend), and B220‐FITC (clone RA3‐6B2; BD Biosciences). Subsequently, the samples were acquired using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid Collection and Differential Cell Count

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected by instilling 1 mL of sterile PBS containing 0.05 mM EDTA into the airways twice for Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl (wild‐type) and AEC‐Sema3E mice as well as PLXND1 fl/fl (wild‐type) and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice. The fluid was centrifuged, and the supernatant was stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. Total BALF cells were counted using trypan blue exclusion and a hemocytometer. For differential cell counts, cells were prepared on cytospin slides (100 K cells per slide), fixed, and stained with Wright‐Giemsa (EASY III Rapid Differential Stains, Azer Scientific). Two independent observers, blinded to the experimental groups, performed differential counts on 200 cells per sample.

Moreover, eosinophils and neutrophils in the BALF were characterized by flow cytometry. In brief, BALF cells were incubated with fixable viability dye APC‐Cy7 (eBioscience), CD45‐APC‐Cy7 (clone 30‐F11; BioLegend), Siglec‐F PE (clone E50‐2440; BD Biosciences), Gr‐1 (Ly6G)‐allophycocyanin (clone 1A8) as we previously described [33]. Subsequently, the samples were acquired using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Analyzing Lung Immune Cell Populations

Lungs were collected from Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl (wild‐type) and AEC‐Sema3E mice challenged with either saline or HDM. The whole lung was minced and enzymatically digested in RPMI 1640 medium containing 2 mg/mL collagenase IV (Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ) at 37°C for 30 min. After red blood cell lysis with ACK (ammonium‐chloride‐potassium) buffer, the cells were counted and stained with a mixture of anti‐mouse antibodies using two antibody panels (0.5 μL of antibodies/20 μL of flow buffer per tube) after Fc blocking [17]. The first panel included fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐eFluor 450 (clone 30F11; eBioscience), MerTK‐allophycocyanin (clone 590H11G1E3; BioLegend), CD64‐PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (clone 10.1; BioLegend), CD11b‐PE‐cy7 (clone M1/70; BioLegend), CD11c‐FITC (clone N418; BioLegend), Siglec F‐PE (clone S17007L, BioLegend), MHCII‐APC/Cyanine7 (clone M5/114.15.2; BioLegend) as previously described [30]. The second panel included fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐eFluor 450 (clone 30F11; eBioscience), CD4‐PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (clone RM4‐5; Biolegend), CD25‐PE (clone BC96; Biolegend), Foxp3‐APC (clone FJK‐16 s; eBioscience), Ly6G‐FITC (clone RB6‐8C5; Biolegend), B220‐APC/Cyanine7 (clone RA3‐6B2; BioLegend), and CD11b‐PE‐cy7 (clone M1/70; BioLegend). Subsequently, the samples were acquired as described above.

Phenotyping of Foxp3+ Treg Cells in the Spleen, Lung and Lymph Nodes

Lungs, lymph nodes, and spleen cells were used for phenotyping Foxp3+ Treg cells using FACS flow cytometry [17]. Tissues from PLXND1 fl/fl and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice, both before and after HDM exposure, were collected, and single‐cell suspensions were prepared, as described above. Following washing and blocking with an Fc‐blocker, cells were stained with a mixture (0.5 μL of antibodies per 20 μL of flow buffer per tube) containing the following anti‐mouse antibodies: fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐eFluor 450 (clone 30F11; eBioscience), CD4‐PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (clone RM4‐5; BioLegend), CD25‐PE (clone BC96; BioLegend), and Foxp3‐APC (clone FJK‐16 s; eBioscience). The samples were then acquired and analyzed as described above.

Phenotyping of GC B Cells and TFH Cells in the Spleen and Lungs

Lung and spleen cells from both PLXND1 fl/fl and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice were used for phenotyping germinal center B cells, and T follicular helper (TFH) cells using FACS flow cytometry [17], both before and after HDM challenge. As previously described, after cell suspension preparation, GC B cells were characterized using fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐eFluor 450 (clone 30F11; eBioscience), B220‐APC (clone RA3‐6B2; BD Biosciences), FAS‐PE (clone SA367H8; BioLegend), and GL7‐FITC (clone GL7; BioLegend). TFH cells were characterized using fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience), CD45‐eFluor 450 (clone 30F11; eBioscience), CD4‐FITC (clone GK1.5; BioLegend), PD‐1‐PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (clone 29F.1A12; BioLegend), and ICOS‐APC (clone 15F9; BioLegend). The samples were then acquired and analyzed as described above.

Intracellular Cytokine Detection

Intracellular staining of cytokines was performed as previously described [13]. Briefly, spleen cells were cultured for 4 h at 37°C with 5% CO2 and stimulated with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA), ionomycin, and the protein transport inhibitor brefeldin A (Invitrogen).

After culture, cells were collected and subjected to extracellular staining using anti‐mouse CD3 PE/Cy7 (clone 145‐2C11; eBioscience) and CD4‐allophycocyanin (clone G1.5; eBioscience). This was followed by intracellular staining with specific anti‐mouse antibodies, including IFN‐γ PerCP‐Cy5.5 (clone XMG1.2; eBioscience), IL‐4 PE (clone 11B11; eBioscience), and IL‐17A (clone TC11‐18H10.1; BioLegend). Samples were acquired using the FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Cytokine Measurement

The levels of TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐10, IL‐13, IL‐17A, IL‐33, CXCL‐8, and IFN‐γ were measured using the MesoScale Discovery system (MSD U‐PLEX, Rockville, Maryland, US Cat#K15069M‐1) in BALF supernatants according to the manufacturer's instructions. Plates were read by the MESO QuickPlex SQ 120MM machine (MSD, Rockville, Maryland, US).

Measurement of Immunoglobulins in Serum

Cardiac blood was collected from mice treated with either saline or HDM. After centrifugation, serum samples were obtained to measure total and HDM‐specific IgE, IgG1, and IgA levels using ELISA, following the manufacturer's instructions (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL) [13]. ELISA data were analyzed using SoftMax Pro software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Lung Histology

The lung was dissected, fixed in 10% formalin overnight, and embedded in paraffin. Lung tissue sections were stained with periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) to evaluate mucus production, Sirius red to visualize collagen deposition, and H&E staining to evaluate the histological changes and severity of inflammation [13]. Mucus production and collagen deposition were assessed in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and wild‐type mice after saline or HDM administration.

Goblet cells were quantified using ImageJ software. The basal membrane length was determined by outlining the region of interest with the freehand tool. Using the color difference between PAS‐stained goblet cells (purple) and other basal membrane cells, the software counted the goblet cells within the specified region of interest [34]. For collagen deposition estimation, picrosirius red images were analyzed using ImageJ software. The color channels of the image were split and based on the color difference between collagen fibers (red) and other basal membrane cells, the software estimated the collagen‐covered area and reported it as a percentage. H&E staining sections were quantified using pathological score, as described before [33].

Immunofluorescence Staining

Tissue sections were stained following the previously outlined procedure [14]. In brief, deparaffinized 5‐μm tissue sections were washed and treated with a blocking solution (1% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% cold fish skin gelatin, and 5% second animal serum in Tris‐buffered saline) for 45 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit anti‐mouse Sema3E polyclonal antibody (1:100; Abcam, CA). Afterward, tissue sections underwent incubation with Alexa Fluor‐568 goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Life Technologies, Burlington, ON, Canada) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were extensively washed for 3–5 min with Tris‐buffered saline and counterstained with ProLong Gold Antifade mount with DAPI (Life Technologies). Imaging was conducted using a digital Zeiss Axioskop 2 mot plus microscope with AxioVision Rel software version 4.8 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy LLC, Thornwood, NY).

The signal intensity of Sema3E staining was quantified by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity in 20 randomly selected HPFs per experimental group using ImageJ software (version 1.48v; NIH, Bethesda, MD; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij). The results were expressed as the mean fluorescence intensity for each group.

Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from the middle lobe of the lung using TRIzol (Ambion). MultiScribe reverse transcriptase was performed for 1 μg of RNA to synthesize cDNA according to the manufacturer's instructions (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The gene expression of SEMA3E, collagen (COL3), mucin (MUC5AC), Fibronectin, and alpha‐smooth muscle cell actin (α‐SMA) genes were analyzed by quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) (Table 1). Eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (EEF2) was used as a housekeeping gene. qRT‐PCR was done in a 96‐well optical plate with an initial one‐cycle denaturation step for 10 min at 95°C, 40 cycles of PCR (95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 30s, and 72°C for 30s), one cycle of melting, and one cooling cycle (Bio‐Rad CFX96 real‐time PCR system). Product specificity was assessed by performing a melting curve analysis and examining the quality of amplification curves. The amplification of target genes was calculated by normalizing by the amplification of EEF2 (∆ Ct) and then normalizing by control groups (∆∆ Ct) [35].

Results

Overexpression of Sema3E From Airway Epithelial Cells (AEC) Did Not Affect Immune Cells Composition at the Steady State

First, we generated a well‐controlled transgenic mouse to target the specific overexpression of Sema3E in airway epithelial cells (AEC). Compared to non‐TAM treated control groups, AEC‐Sema3E, Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl , and Scgb1a1‐Cre mice, SEMA3E mRNA expression occurred exclusively in the lung of AEC‐Sema3E mice that received TAM. Conversely, TAM administration did not alter SEMA3E expression from epithelial cells covering other tissues, such as the colon. Specifically, no differences in SEMA3E mRNA expression were observed in colon tissue between TAM‐treated and non‐TAM‐treated AEC‐Sema3E, Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl , and Scgb1a1‐Cre mice (Figure 1B). This data show that AEC‐Sema3E overexpression is specific in lung tissue (Figure 1A).

(C and D) Lung sections were stained for Sema3E using immunofluorescent staining. Moreover, to ascertain the overexpression of Sema3E by AEC at protein levels, we performed immunofluorescence staining to assess Sema3E immunoreactivity in the lung sections (Figure 1C). Sema3E staining was primarily positive in the columnar epithelial cell layers, and mean intensity calculation showed a significant increase in Sema3E levels at the epithelial layer in AEC‐Sema3E compared to Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mice (Figure 1D). This result validates the efficiency of the TAM‐inducible system in driving Sema3E overexpression in AECs in the lungs.

To investigate whether the overexpression of Sema3E from airway epithelial cells has an impact on the composition of immune cells and whether it affects inflammation, we conducted FACS‐based immunophenotyping of various immune cell populations, including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, NK, NKT, B cells, and neutrophils in the lungs, spleen, and blood at the steady state. The number of immune cells in the lung (Figure 1F), spleen (Figure 1G), and blood (Figure 1H) was comparable between TAM‐treated and non‐TAM‐treated AEC‐Sema3E, Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl , and Scgb1a1‐Cre mice. Overall, our data indicate that AEC‐Sema3E expression is specific to lung tissue and does not perturb immune cell composition at the steady state.

Airway Epithelial‐Derived Sema3E Enhances the Number of Foxp3+ Regulatory T Cells in the Lungs Upon HDM Exposure

Inflammation is a key factor in the pathophysiology of asthma, driving its chronic progression and persistence as a respiratory disorder [36]. We investigated whether lung‐specific Sema3E over‐expression affects airway inflammation upon HDM exposure. To do so, we first induced Sema3E expression in AEC‐Sema3E mice through tamoxifen administration 1 week before subjecting them to the HDM allergen challenge for five consecutive days over 2 weeks (Figure 2A). Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl control mice were used.

The numbers of inflammatory cells, including neutrophils, eosinophils, alveolar and interstitial macrophages, CD11C+ CD11B+ dendritic cells, B cells, and CD4 T cells, were assessed using flow cytometry in the lungs [30]. Overexpression of Sema3E from airway epithelial cells did not alter the numbers of these immune cells in the lungs (Figure S1A–G). Additionally, the levels of inflammatory cytokines/chemokines, such as TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐10, IL‐17A, CXCL8, and IFN‐γ, were measured in the BALF, along with the levels of immunoglobulins IgG1, IgE, and IgA in the sera of AEC‐Sema3E and Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mice. Similar to the inflammatory cells, no significant differences were observed in the levels of these inflammatory cytokines and antibodies between AEC‐Sema3E and Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mice (Figure S2A–I & Figure S3A–F). Lastly, H&E staining of lung tissue sections revealed a similar severity of inflammation between the two strains of mice (Figure S1H,I).

Interestingly, in response to Sema3E overexpression from airway epithelial cells, the number of Foxp3+ Treg cells significantly increased from less than 2 × 106 in the lungs of Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl to more than 4 × 106 in AEC‐Sema3E mice upon HDM exposure (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2B–D). These results led us to the early effects of AEC‐derived Sema3E on the Treg cell recruitment in the lungs. As such, we used a short‐time HDM exposure (Figure 2E). We investigated whether these Foxp3+ Treg cells are proliferating by using the Ki67 marker in flow cytometry. We showed that the number of Ki67+ Foxp3+ Treg cells significantly increased from nearly 5 × 104 in the lungs of Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl to more than 105 in AEC‐Sema3E mice upon HDM encounter (p < 0.05) (Figure 2F–H).

Overall, a significant increase in the number of Foxp3+ Treg cells and their proliferation was observed in AEC‐Sema3E mice upon HDM exposure. These findings suggest that epithelial‐derived Sema3E may influence the expansion and activation of regulatory T cells in the lungs.

Cell‐Specific Deletion of PLXD1 in Treg Abolishes Their Number at Several Organs Upon HDM Exposure

Given the observed increase in the number and proliferation of Foxp3+ Treg cells in response to Sema3E overexpression from airway epithelial cells, we investigated the underlying mechanisms by which Sema3E affects Treg in our HDM model of allergic inflammation using cell‐specific deletion of plexinD1, the canonical receptor for Sema3E, in Foxp3+ cells.

Foxp3‐specific PLXND1 knockout (Foxp3 PLXND1 KO ) mice, we aimed to elucidate the direct role of the Sema3E‐plexinD1 signaling axis in the regulation of Treg cells in the context of HDM‐induced allergic asthma.

As observed for AEC Sema3E mice (Figure S1A,B), immunophenotyping of various immune cell populations, including neutrophils, eosinophils, B cells, dendritic cells, CD3, and CD4 T cells, in the spleen of Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice at the steady state showed no significant differences in the number of these immune cell populations between the two strains (Figure S4C). These findings suggest that the absence of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells does not affect the composition of immune cells in the spleen under steady‐state conditions.

Next, Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice were exposed to HDM for five consecutive days over 2 weeks (Figure 3A). The number of Foxp3+ Treg cells was investigated in the lungs, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes of Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl mice using FACS flow cytometry upon HDM challenge in the acute model of asthma. In response to the deletion of PLXND1, the number of Foxp3+ Treg cells was significantly reduced from 1%–1.5% in the lungs of the PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice to 0.5% in the lungs of the Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice (p < 0.05) in the HDM model of asthma (Figure 3B,C). Moreover, the number of Foxp3+ Treg cells significantly decreased in the secondary lymphoid tissues. Specifically, the percentage of Foxp3+ Treg cells decreased in the spleen and mediastinal lymph nodes from 3% and 4% of PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice to 1.5% and 2% in the Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice, respectively (p < 0.01) (Figure 3D–G).

PLXND1 deletion on Foxp3+ cells reduces the number of Foxp3‐Treg cells in different organs in HDM model of asthma. (A) The allergic airway disease model was induced by intranasal exposure to HDM for 2 weeks; control mice received saline. The number of CD4 Foxp3+ Treg cells was investigated in the (B and C) lungs, (D and E) spleen, and (F and G) mediastinal lymph nodes using flow cytometry in both PLXND1 fl/fl and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO . Results are shown as mean ± SEM, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 by 2‐way ANOVA.

Overall, the deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3‐expressing cells leads to a significant reduction in the number of Foxp3+ Treg cells in the lungs, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes upon HDM exposure.

PLXND1 Deficiency on Foxp3+ Cells Increased the Number of Airway Eosinophils With no Impact on Airway Hyperresponsiveness Upon HDM Exposure

To determine whether the deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells affect overall lung inflammation, we analyzed the total and differential immune cell number in the BALF. The recruitment of inflammatory cells in the lungs was markedly induced by allergen challenge, but not significant difference was observed betweenFoxp3 PLXND1 KO and littermate PLXND1 fl/fl controls (i.e., 2 × 106 cells/mL in the BALF of the Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to 1.5 × 106 cells/mL in the BALF of the PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice) (Figure 4A). Interestingly, a greater eosinophil recruitment in the BALF of the Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to the PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice was observed (p < 0.01) (Figure 4B). Although the number of neutrophils increased in response to PLXND1 deletion in Foxp3+ cells, the results did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4C). Moreover, we did not observe significant difference in the number of macrophages and lymphocytes in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to the PLXND1 fl/fl (Figure 4D,F). Lastly, although H&E staining of lung tissue sections revealed an increasing trend in severity of inflammation in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to the PLXND1 fl/fl the result did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4G,H).

The percentage of BALF (D) macrophages and (F) lymphocytes was calculated on cytospin slides for 200 cells per slide. Allergen challenge induced constrictive responses of the airways to methacholine doses. However, we did not find significant differences in airway resistance (Rn), tissue resistance (G), and tissue elastance (H) between the Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and the PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice (Figure S5A–C).

Overall, deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3‐expressing cells leads to an increase in the recruitment of inflammatory cells, particularly eosinophils, in response to HDM allergen exposure in an acute asthma model, without significantly affecting airway hyperresponsiveness.

Deletion of PLXND1 on Foxp3+ Cells Enhanced the Levels of Inflammatory Cytokines

Inflammatory cytokines are essential mediators in the development and progression of asthma [37]. To assess the CD4 T cell subsets and their cytokine production, spleen was harvested from Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl mice. Spleen and lymph node cells were stimulated ex vivo with a cocktail containing PMA, ionomycin, and the protein transport inhibitor brefeldin‐A, then analyzed for surface and intracellular cytokine expression by flow cytometry [30].

The number of IL‐4 producing CD4 T cells in spleen was significantly decreased in Foxp3 PLXND1KOcompared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 5A,B). In contrast, the number of IFN‐γ producing CD4 T cells significantly increased (p < 0.001) in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice (Figure 5C,D). However, no significant difference was observed in the number of CD4 T cells producing IL‐17 and IL‐10 between Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl mice (Figure 5E–H). We did not observe any significant differences in the number of CD4 T cells producing IL‐10, IL‐17, IL‐4, and IFN‐γ in the draining lymph nodes in between Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl mice (Figure S6A–D).

The levels of IL‐17, CXCL‐8, and IL‐5 were significantly increased response to PLXND1 deletion on Foxp3+ cells compared to WT mice (p < 0.0001, p < 0.05, p < 0.01 respectively) (Figure 5J,L,N). Although the levels of IFN‐γ, TNF, and IL‐33 increased in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice, the result did not reach statistical significance (Figure 5I,K,P). Interestingly, despite no statistical significance, the levels of IL‐4 and IL‐13 were enhanced in response to PLXND1 deletion in Foxp3+ cells in the BALF, similar to splenic CD4 T cells (Figure 5M,O).

Overall, these findings suggest that PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells may contribute to the regulation of cytokine production.

The Levels of IgE and IgG1 Significantly Enhanced in Response to the Ablation of PLXND1 on Foxp3+ Cells Upon HDM Exposure

The levels of total and HDM‐specific IgE and IgG1 in the serum of PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice were investigated using ELISA.

In naïve mice, the levels of total IgE (1–1.5 ng/mL) and IgG1 (< 50 μg/mL) were very low (Figure 6A). However, in response to HDM challenge, the total IgE levels increased to 5 ng/mL in PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice compared to less than 40 ng/mL in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A). A similar trend was observed for HDM‐specific IgE (p < 0.05) between PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) and Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice (Figure 6B).

Moreover, the levels of total and HDM‐specific IgG1 were significantly enhanced in response to PLXND1 deletion in Foxp3+ cells. Specifically, serum levels were 100–150 μg/mL and 0.6 (405–690 mm) OD in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to 50–70 μg/mL and 0.3 (405–690 mm) OD in PLXND1 fl/fl (wild type) mice upon HDM exposure (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 6C,D).

Overall, deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells results in significantly increased levels of both total and HDM‐specific IgE and IgG1 in response to HDM challenge, compared to wild‐type mice. This indicates that PLXND1 on Foxp3+ cells plays a role in regulating the germinal center responses and antibody production in response to HDM exposure.

In Response to PLXND1 Deletion in Foxp3+ Cells the Number of Germinal Center B Cells and T Follicular Helper Cells Enhances Significantly Upon HDM Encounter

Since we found significant difference in the levels of antibodies in the sera, and plexinD1 has emerged as an important regulator of germinal center responses and humoral response [38]. We investigated germinal center responses, including the number of germinal center B cells (GC B cells) and T follicular helper cells (TFH), in response to the ablation of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells in the spleen and lung using flow cytometry.

The number of GC B cells significantly increased from > 5% to < 10% in the spleen (Figure 7A,B) and lungs (Figure 7C,D) of Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively).

The percentages of GL7+FAS+ germinal centre B cells were assessed in the (A and B) spleen and (C and D) lung by flow cytometry. Furthermore, in response to PLXND1 deletion in Foxp3+ cells, the number of TFH cells increased from < 15% to < 30% in the spleen (Figure 7E,F) and from > 50% to < 60% in the lungs (Figure 7G,H) of Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice (p < 0.001 and p < 0.05, respectively).

Overall, these results show that the ablation of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ cells leads to a significant increase in both GC B cells and TFH cells in the spleen and lungs, indicating that PLXND1 plays a critical role in regulating germinal center responses in these tissues.

Deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ Cells Increased Mucus Gene Expression and Goblet Cell Hyperplasia in the HDM Model of Asthma

Excessive mucus production and collagen deposition, also known as subepithelial fibrosis, contribute to airway thickening, stiffening, and irreversible narrowing, ultimately impairing lung function [39]. We investigated whether the absence of PLXND1 in Foxp3‐expressing cells affects the expression of genes involved in lung remodeling events in response to HDM, specifically mucin (Muc5AC), collagen (Col3), fibronectin, and α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA). The results were further validated using PAS and Sirius Red staining on lung tissue sections to visualize goblet cell hyperplasia and collagen deposition, respectively.

Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice exposed to HDM exhibited significantly higher Muc5AC expression compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 8A). However, there were no significant differences in Col3, fibronectin, and α‐SMA expression between Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl mice (Figure 8B–D).

(A) Mucus (Muc5AC) (B) Collagen 3 (COL3), (C) Fibronectin (D) α‐SMA expression were analyzed by real‐time PCR in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO and PLXND1 fl/fl mice. (E and F) goblet cell hyperplasia and (G and H) Collagen deposition was visualized using PAS and Sirius red staining, respectively. Scale bars: 50 μm. Results are shown as mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05, by 2‐way ANOVA.

PAS staining revealed increased goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus production in the airways of Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice (p < 0.001) (Figure 8E,F). Although Col3 gene expression (Figure 8B) and sub‐epithelial collagen deposition (Figure 8G,H) tended to be higher in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice compared to PLXND1 fl/fl mice, these results did not reach statistical significance.

Overall, the absence of PLXND1 in Foxp3‐expressing cells leads to increased Muc5AC expression and goblet cell hyperplasia in response to HDM exposure, indicating enhanced mucus production. However, the absence of PLXND1 does not significantly affect the expression of Col3, fibronectin, and α‐SMA, nor does it significantly influence sub‐epithelial collagen deposition.

Discussion

This study explored the impact of Sema3E overexpression in airway epithelial cells (AEC) and immune responses in an acute HDM‐induced asthma model. Transgenic Sema3E expression in AEC did not significantly affect inflammatory cell populations, cytokine and antibody levels in the lungs and serum upon HDM allergen challenge. However, it notably increased both the number and proliferation of Foxp3+ Treg cells in the lungs.

Interestingly, the increased Foxp3+ Treg population was seen specifically in response to HDM exposure, rather than in naïve conditions, suggesting that AEC‐Sema3E does not intrinsically drive Treg proliferation but rather enhances antigen‐dependent recall responses, likely in memory Tregs (mTregs) [40]. Memory Tregs are a subset of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells that develop following antigen exposure and persist in mediating long‐term immune tolerance upon re‐exposure [41]. Their selective expansion in HDM‐exposed lungs, but not in naïve conditions, suggests that in our model Sema3E preferentially supports antigen‐experienced mTregs rather than inducing de novo Treg differentiation. This observation aligns with prior findings indicating that mTregs play a crucial role in maintaining tissue homeostasis [40]. Furthermore, Sema3E‐plexinD1 signaling has been implicated in immune cell trafficking and functional stability, raising the possibility that it enhances mTreg retention or functional fitness in the lungs [6, 23].

Despite the increased number and proliferation of Treg cells in response to AEC‐Sema3E, there was no reduction in airway inflammation, as we observed no significant changes in inflammatory cell infiltration, cytokine levels, or antibody production. One possible explanation is the short duration (2 weeks) of our acute HDM asthma model; a longer exposure, as in a chronic asthma model, may be necessary for Sema3E‐induced Foxp3+ Tregs to exert their suppressive function on inflammation. Alternatively, the inflammatory milieu in HDM‐exposed lungs may override the suppressive effects of expanded Tregs, as has been observed in chronic inflammatory conditions where Treg function is impaired despite increased numbers, a process termed Treg resistance [42].

Sema3E is a key immune‐modulatory molecule known to regulate various immune cells through its receptor, plexinD1. Previous studies have demonstrated that the Sema3E‐plexinD1 axis influences T cell trafficking, activation, and differentiation [6, 9, 23]. However, its role in Treg homeostasis and immune regulation in the HDM airway inflammation model remains poorly understood. Interestingly, Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice exhibited a substantial reduction in Foxp3+ Treg cell numbers across the lungs, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes, along with increased eosinophil recruitment into the lungs and higher levels of IL‐5, IL‐17, and CXCL‐8 in the BALF. Moreover, the number of IFN‐γ‐producing CD4+ T cells in the spleen increased. Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice also demonstrated enhanced germinal center responses, including an increased number of germinal center B cells and T follicular helper (TFH) cells in both the lungs and spleen, elevated serum levels of total and HDM‐specific IgG1 and IgE, and goblet cell hyperplasia. These findings highlight the critical role of the Sema3E‐plexinD1 axis in maintaining Foxp3+ Treg cell‐mediated immune homeostasis and suggest that its disruption leads to heightened inflammatory responses in the acute HDM model of asthma.

Our findings suggest that the Sema3E‐plexinD1 pathway might play a crucial role in Treg homeostasis and immune regulation in asthma, possibly by influencing natural regulatory T cell (nTreg) development in the thymus. In our study, Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice exhibited a significant reduction in Foxp3+ Treg cells across the lungs, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes. This widespread reduction suggests that the defect may stem from an impaired thymic output of nTregs rather than a loss of peripheral Treg maintenance alone. The diminished Foxp3+ Treg population in these mice correlated with enhanced inflammation, including increased eosinophil recruitment, elevated IL‐5, IL‐17, and CXCL‐8 in the BALF, and an expansion of IFN‐γ‐producing CD4+ T cells in the spleen. These findings indicate that plexinD1 signaling is critical not only for maintaining Treg numbers but also for controlling immune activation in allergic inflammation.

PlexinD1 is highly expressed on CD4+ CD8+ double‐positive (DP) thymocytes and regulates their migration by repressing CCR9/CCL25‐mediated chemotaxis and β1 integrin adhesion [6, 9]. In the absence of plexinD1, CD69+ single‐positive (SP) thymocytes fail to migrate into the medullary region properly, leading to disrupted thymic architecture and ectopic thymocyte clustering [6, 9]. Given that nTregs primarily differentiate within the thymic medulla [43], mis‐localization could impair thymic selection and nTreg differentiation, ultimately leading to systemic Treg deficiency.

In this study, we investigated germinal center responses following PLXND1 deletion in Foxp3+ cells and demonstrated that in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice, both cellular and humoral immune homeostasis were disrupted in the HDM model of asthma. These data are in agreement with the role of plexinD1 as an important regulator of germinal center responses and the humoral immune response [38].

Furthermore, the deletion of PLXND1 in Foxp3+ Tregs resulted in increased TFH cell numbers, expanded GC B cell populations, and elevated IgG1 and IgE levels, mirroring TFR‐deficient models [44, 45]. These observations reinforce the role of plexinD1 in TFR‐mediated suppression of TFH and GC B cells, preventing excessive antibody production [38, 45]. PlexinD1, known for its role in axon guidance and immune cell migration, may regulate TFR cell localization within lymphoid tissues, ensuring their optimal interaction with TFH and GC B cells [6, 46]. Disruption of this signaling pathway could lead to unrestrained TFH expansion and enhanced antibody production. Taken together, these findings suggest that plexinD1 might play a key role in modulating TFR function, maintaining immune homeostasis, and preventing excessive allergic responses.

Our findings demonstrate that the reduction in Foxp3+ Tregs in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice was accompanied by fewer IL‐4‐producing CD4+ T cells in the spleen and a decreasing trend of IL‐4 levels in BALF, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between plexinD1 signaling and IL‐4–mediated Treg stability. IL‐4 has been identified as a tolerogenic cytokine that supports Treg‐mediated immune suppression by enhancing Treg survival and granzyme expression [47]. Studies on IL‐4 knockout (IL‐4 KO) mice have shown that while Treg frequencies remain unchanged, their suppressive capacity is significantly impaired, indicating that IL‐4 is essential for maintaining Treg function rather than their development [47]. Therefore, the reduction in IL‐4‐producing CD4+ T cells in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice suggests that plexinD1 may indirectly regulate IL‐4–Treg interactions, which are essential for maintaining immune tolerance in allergic inflammation. The increased inflammation and germinal center responses (elevated TFH, IgE, and IgG1 levels) in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice further indicate that PLXND1 deficiency disrupts both cellular and humoral immune homeostasis, aligning with IL‐4's established role in immune regulation [48, 49, 50]. These results suggest that plexinD1 signaling might be necessary for supporting IL‐4–dependent Treg function, which helps suppress allergen‐induced immune activation. These findings suggest that plexinD1 signaling might support Treg homeostasis by maintaining the thymic selection of nTregs, modulating the function of TFR cells, and sustaining IL‐4‐producing CD4+ T cell populations, which in turn enhances Treg suppressive function. This interplay provides novel insights into how plexinD1 signaling maintains immune tolerance through Foxp3+ Treg cells, with potential implications for therapeutic strategies targeting asthma and allergic diseases.

Despite these immune alterations, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) remained unchanged in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice. Consistently, we did not find significant changes in COL3, Fibronectin, and α‐SMCA expression, collagen deposition, or subepithelial fibrosis. This suggests that plexinD1 in Treg cells, while crucial for immune regulation, has a limited or indirect impact on AHR.

Tregs regulate allergic airway inflammation by suppressing inflammatory cell activation and cytokine production [51], but their role in airway smooth muscle (ASM) contractility remains unclear. Given that AHR results from both immune and nonimmune factors [52], the absence of changes in airway smooth muscle and collagen deposition suggests that plexinD1 Treg‐dependent immune modulation alone may not be sufficient to alter AHR. While Tregs promote pulmonary fibrosis via platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF) and transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) [53], we did not observe significant airway fibrosis in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice, suggesting that this pathway is not predominant in our model.

Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice exhibited increased goblet cell hyperplasia, mucus production, and Muc5ac expression. This suggests that Tregs regulate mucus secretion through pathways distinct from those controlling ASM contractility and AHR. Given that Tregs limit inflammation [51], their dysfunction in Foxp3 PLXND1 KO mice resulted in elevated IL‐17, IFN‐γ, IL‐5, or TNF, leading to excessive mucus secretion [54], despite unchanged AHR. This aligns with studies showing that mucus hypersecretion and AHR can be dissociated, as excessive mucus does not necessarily correlate with bronchoconstriction [55].

This study highlights the critical role of the Sema3E‐plexinD1 signaling axis in maintaining immune homeostasis during allergic airway inflammation. Our findings suggest that airway epithelial cell (AEC)–derived Sema3E may enhance the stability and function of Treg cells, thereby promoting long‐term immune tolerance. Additionally, plexinD1 signaling within Tregs appears essential for sustaining systemic regulatory networks. Disruption of this pathway impairs both cellular and humoral immune regulation, leading to unchecked inflammatory responses. Overall, these findings position the Sema3E‐plexinD1 axis as a key modulator of Treg function and immune tolerance, offering novel insights that could inform therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring regulatory immune pathways in asthma and allergic diseases.

Figure S1: Over expression of transgenic Sema3E from airway epithelial cells did not change the immune cell composition in the lungs of HDM model of asthma. Lung tissue was harvested from AEC‐Sema3E mice, Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mice. Following enzymatic digestion, single‐cell suspensions were analyzed using FACS with specific antibodies to characterize various inflammatory cell populations. The differential number of immune cell populations, including (A) neutrophils, (B) eosinophils (C) conventional DC, (D) alveolar macrophages, (E) interstitial macrophages, (F) CD4 T cells, and (G) B cells were compared between AEC‐Sema3E and Sema3E‐Stop fl/fl mice in HDM acute model of asthma. (H) The severity of inflammation and lung histological changes was visualized using H&E staining in the paraffin‐embedded lung sections. (I) The results were reported as pathological score. Then, the levels of (A) total and (B) HDM‐specific IgE, (C) total and (D) HDM‐specific IgG1, as well as (E) total and (F) HDM‐specific IgA were measured using ELISA.
