Clinical internship curriculum systems: a comparative analysis on the 4C/ID perspective
Authors: Tian Cheng, Yunfeng Han, Wenqing Yuan, Shixian Gu, Ning Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study compares clinical internship curricula between Chinese and foreign medical schools through the lens of the Four-Component Instructional Design (4C/ID) model. By analyzing curriculum objectives, content structures, and evaluation frameworks, we aim to identify systemic strengths and distinctive features within each educational context.MethodsA qualitative comparative case study design was adopted, selecting ten top-ranked foreign medical schools (Times Higher Education 2024) and ten leading Chinese institutions. Publicly available curricular documents—including syllabi, implementation protocols, and competency assessments—were analyzed through a 4C/ID framework using content analysis, word frequency analysis, and cross-case synthesis.ResultsBoth systems demonstrated structured adherence to 4C/ID principles within their clinical internship programs but revealed divergent emphases. Foreign medical schools focus on “practice,” while Chinese schools emphasize “ability,” suggesting different approaches to integrating authentic tasks. Foreign medical schools build collaborative learning ecosystems, whereas Chinese institutions systematize conceptual knowledge. Extraneous cognitive load is managed through staged, progressive curricula and diverse teaching methods in foreign schools, whereas Chinese schools employ phased skill assessments. Regarding part-task repeated practice, both groups recognize its importance, with foreign schools employing more innovative approaches.ConclusionsApplying the 4C/ID model, this study reveals distinct yet effective strengths in the clinical internship programs of Chinese and foreign medical schools. The sophisticated approaches of top-ranked foreign medical schools in managing students’ cognitive load offer valuable insights for medical education reform, potentially enhancing adaptability to the demands of modern healthcare.Keywords: Clinical internship, Curriculum, 4C/ID, Comparative analysis

Introduction

The ongoing shift from biomedical to biopsychosocial medical models [1, 2] demands that medical education cultivate comprehensive and holistic competencies through authentic clinical training. As a pivotal transition from classroom learning to professional practice, clinical internships require students to integrate multidimensional knowledge, develop clinical reasoning, and master patient-centered communication within cognitively demanding conditions [3]. Cognitive load theory posits that optimal learning occurs when instructional designs align task complexity with learners’ cognitive capacity [4, 5], making effective curricular frameworks critical for facilitating competency development during this transformative phase [6, 7].

The Four-Component Instructional Design (4C/ID) model emerges as a robust framework for structuring workplace learning. Proposed by Dutch scholar Jeroen J. G. van Merriënboer [8, 9], this evidence-based approach systematically addresses cognitive load management through four interrelated components: (1) authentic learning tasks, (2) supportive information, (3) procedural guidance, and (4) part-task practice. In medical education contexts, this framework translates to: (1) Embedding learning in authentic clinical workflows to promote knowledge application. (2) Providing conceptual knowledge and cognitive strategies to scaffold problem-solving. (3) Delivering just-in-time, step-by-step instructions to reduce extraneous cognitive load. (4) Implementing part-task repetition to build diagnostic schemas and procedural fluency [9–12]. Multiple clinical internship studies have confirmed that the 4C/ID model can optimizes curriculum design, enhances medical students’ knowledge integration and reduces their cognitive load during internships [13–15].

China’s medical education has evolved through disciplinary-centered training (1950s), problem-based learning reforms (1990s), toward current competency-based organ system integration [16, 17]. Despite recent progress towards integration [17], the historical legacy of discipline-specific teaching often results in a comparatively later and/or less comprehensive integration of basic and clinical sciences than in systems with longer traditions of organ-system or spiral curricula. Consequently, the structure, level of responsibility, and scaffolding provided during the pivotal clinical internship phase may differ Significantly. Recognizing the centrality of the clinical internship within this evolving landscape, this study aims to deconstruct clinical internship curricula at leading Chinese and foreign medical schools using the complete 4C/ID framework. This comparative analysis enables systematic identification of Similarities and differences in curriculum design and implementation. By elucidating how different cultures operationalize 4C/ID principles, this research contributes to the global discourse on clinical education reform while providing evidence to inform localized curriculum enhancements.

Method

Subjects and data sources

Twenty medical schools were purposively sampled through stratified selection:

Foreign cohort (n = 10): Top-ranked institutions in clinical education from Times Higher Education (THE) [18] World University Rankings 2024 (clinical & health) were selected for their comprehensive indicator systems and focus on teaching reputation (Oxford, Harvard, Cambridge, Imperial College London, Stanford, Johns Hopkins, Yale, UCL, Toronto, and Pennsylvania).

Foreign cohort (n = 10): Top-ranked institutions in clinical education from Times Higher Education (THE) [18] World University Rankings 2024 (clinical & health) were selected for their comprehensive indicator systems and focus on teaching reputation (Oxford, Harvard, Cambridge, Imperial College London, Stanford, Johns Hopkins, Yale, UCL, Toronto, and Pennsylvania).

Chinese cohort (n = 10): Nationally recognized medical schools were selected based on two criteria: official designation as “double first-class” universities by China’s Ministry of Education, and leadership in curriculum reforms such as organ-system integration training (Peking Union Medical College, Peking University, Fudan University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Zhejiang University, Wuhan University, Central South University, Sun Yat-sen University, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and Sichuan University).

Chinese cohort (n = 10): Nationally recognized medical schools were selected based on two criteria: official designation as “double first-class” universities by China’s Ministry of Education, and leadership in curriculum reforms such as organ-system integration training (Peking Union Medical College, Peking University, Fudan University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Zhejiang University, Wuhan University, Central South University, Sun Yat-sen University, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and Sichuan University).

Primary data included clinical internship curricula, syllabi, and competency frameworks from institutional repositories (2020–2024). Regulatory documents and peer-reviewed studies served as secondary sources. To ensure accuracy and timeliness, supplementary data was obtained from relevant educational policy documents, academic journal articles, and educational research reports. These documents were categorized by institution and uploaded to a secure database for systematic analysis.

Study design and data analysis

To ensure a rigorous and systematic comparison, we utilized a combination of methodologies, including content analysis, word frequency analysis and case studies. Initial content analysis involved a detailed review of curriculum documents to identify the educational contents in which key terms were used. Two researchers independently conducted a content analysis of these documents using a coding framework based on the four components of the 4C/ID model. For example, segments of the curriculum describing clinical rotations that involved real patient interactions were coded as “authentic learning tasks.” Sections providing background scientific knowledge or problem-solving strategies were coded as “supportive information.” Descriptions of step-by-step clinical procedures were coded as “procedural guidance.” Finally, details of repetitive skill drills or case simulations were coded as “part-task practice.”

For word frequency analysis, we utilized R software (version 4.4.2) with the tm (English) and jiebaR (Chinese) packages to identify and extract key terms from course documents. The dplyr package was then employed for term frequency calculation. Two researchers manually consolidated semantically Similar terms to refine the dataset. Descriptive statistics were subsequently used to summarize the frequency of these key terms. This process enabled quantification of term frequencies associated with each 4C/ID component.

Next, we selected representative case studies from both cohorts to illustrate the implementation of the 4C/ID components. The selection was based on the availability of detailed curriculum data, ensuring that the analysis was grounded in rich information. Each case study involved a thorough review of the curriculum objectives, focusing on how the 4C/ID framework was integrated. These narratives provided qualitative insights that complemented the quantitative findings from the word frequency analysis. To ensure validity, data from multiple sources were cross-referenced, enhancing the reliability of the findings by corroborating them across different data points. This mixed-methods approach enabled both frequency-based comparisons of educational priorities and narrative explanations of curricular design logic.

Results

Implementation of authentic learning tasks

Task design characteristics

Comparative lexical analysis revealed convergent priorities in cultivating clinical competence across the selected institutions. Both Chinese and foreign medical schools emphasized “cultivating clinically competence to meet the needs of the real society” in their overarching objectives, with “clinical”, “medicine/healthcare”, and “cultivation” ranking among the highest-frequency words in both cohorts (Table 1). Moreover, in operationalization objectives, diverse emphases have emerged: foreign medical schools prioritized experiential learning markers (“clinical practice” ranking 2nd) and patient-centered care (“patient-centered” in 6/10 schools), while Chinese curricula emphasized competency development (“clinical/diagnostic ability” as primary focus) and systemic healthcare integration (“humanities/society” ranking 4th). This slight variation reflects distinct paradigms for integrating authenticity into their curriculum.

Contextual integration

Case studies demonstrated context-specific implementations of authenticity. For example, Stanford’s global-local clinical training rotations exemplify whole-task design by requiring students to simultaneously apply biomedical knowledge, communication skills, and resource management strategies within underserved populations. This approach enhanced experiential authenticity by exposing students to diverse healthcare contexts, which was a common goal among foreign medical schools. In the same vein, Chinese medical schools achieved contextual integration by aligning clinical internship programs with the nation’s healthcare milieu. A notable example was the comprehensive teaching clinic at Peking University Health Science Center. The program cultivates diagnostic proficiency through structured clinical immersion involving supervised patient consultations, systematic case analyses, and immediate faculty feedback. Evaluations indicated dual benefits: enhanced practical skills among trainees and the provision of more considerate, professional medical services to patients. Patient feedback within the teaching clinic reported high satisfaction and anticipation for the program’s continuation.

Delivery of supportive information

Scaffolding approaches

Cross-national comparisons of teaching strategies (Fig. 1) identified distinct scaffolding methodologies. Foreign medical schools emphasize constructing collaborative learning ecosystems. Harvard University’s longitudinal integrated clerkship effectively regulates cognitive load through sustained mentorship and learner-centered curricular design [19–21], while Cambridge University’s three-stage internship (core→specialty→applied practice) employs a scaffolded progression model enabling incremental skill advancement. Both exemplify strategic scaffolding approaches that systematically sequence clinical challenges. In contrast, Chinese institutions focus on refining theory-practice integration through faculty-guided, case-based learning. Here, instructors systematically disclose case information in stages to bridge theoretical knowledge with clinical application. Despite methodological differences, both pedagogical systems pursue a shared objective: cultivating students’ clinical problem-solving competencies.

Supportive information in course contents

The course contents of medical schools reflect their approach to the delivery of supportive information for problem-solving. While both Chinese and foreign medical schools aim to equip students with essential clinical knowledge, differences emerge in their curricular emphasis. Foreign institutions, such as Johns Hopkins University, integrate interdisciplinary themes such as “Gene”, “Translational”, and “Regenerative” medicine into their curricula. This broad exposure provides students with diverse conceptual frameworks for comprehensive problem-solving. On the other hand, Chinese medical schools prioritize alignment with practical healthcare demands. For instance, systematic theoretical and practical training on infectious diseases was offered during internships at 7/10 of Chinese schools surveyed, compared to only 2/10 of foreign schools. Amid globalization and emerging infectious diseases, this equips students to address public health challenges and global threats. Both approaches highlight different priorities in delivering supportive information for effective problem-solving in medical education.

Implementation of procedural guidance

Assessment design for cognitive load management

Assessment in medical education serves as a critical tool for the implementation of procedural guidance in routine skills training. Word frequency analysis (Table 2) identified “examination” as the dominant metric in Chinese evaluation systems (27 occurrences), reflecting reliance on standardized testing for procedural competency verification. Foreign evaluation systems featured “student” as the highest-frequency term, embedding pedagogical frameworks like “cognitive apprenticeship” [22, 23], “scaffolded learning” [24, 25], and “reflective practice” [26, 27]. These deliberately sequence conceptual preparation before complex clinical scenarios—reducing intrinsic cognitive load during skill acquisition and optimizing conditions for procedural guidance implementation.

Feedback modalities as just-in-time guidance

The timely assessment of students’ learning status can provide teachers with timely feedback, which can assist them in adjusting their teaching strategies according to students’ cognitive load levels [28]. Table 2 shows the term “feedback” occurring 13 times in foreign evaluation systems, highlighting its importance in helping students adjust their learning strategies and correct mistakes promptly. This facilitates the effective implementation of procedural guidance by ensuring students receive immediate corrective input.

Chinese medical schools are also transforming their traditional end-of-term examination into phased, multiple assessments to include more timely feedback. For example, Wuhan University Medical College has adopted staged, multiple assessments such as skill tests, Mini-CEX, medical record writing, and routine evaluations. This approach allows students to consolidate knowledge incrementally during their learning process, thereby preventing cognitive overload that may arise from confronting a large volume of complex content at the end of the semester. Additionally, the high frequency of “internship” and “skill/ability” underscores the importance of skills assessment during internships. Regular skills tests provide students with frequent opportunities for feedback, enabling them to address learning issues promptly and ensuring the effective implementation of procedural guidance in clinical settings.

Part-task practice for clinical automation

Knowledge schema construction through iterative review

Top institutions universally leverage repetition to build diagnostic schemas. The University of Cambridge, for instance, incorporated weekly group clinical activities under the supervision of junior doctors, where students engaged with simulated patient cases multiple times to deepen their understanding of disease management and therapeutic decision-making. In China, Peking Union Medical College emphasizes systematic case review cycles with faculty feedback, focusing on key medical knowledge and concepts. This approach allowed students to reinforce learning and apply their skills confidently in clinical settings. This systematic repetition was pivotal in constructing a solid clinical knowledge base for efficacious patient care.

Skill automation via deliberate practice

The emphasis on deliberate practice as a key component of clinical skill development was evident in both Chinese and foreign medical schools. A team of researchers at Johns Hopkins University developed the Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RDCP) [29]. This innovative approach combined the concepts of deliberate practice, mastery learning, and crisis resource management with the aim to facilitate the rapid acquisition of necessary skills through a structured process of short, intense practice sessions interspersed. RDCP ‘s use in training emergency clinical situations has been associated with improved team performance in several different studies. Parallelly, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine has implemented a standardized skills laboratory where students practice life-saving skills on mannequins, leading to a significant improvement in performance during actual clinical encounters.

Discussion

The integration of the 4C/ID model into clinical internships is designed to provide students with a systematic learning environment within an authentic task context, thereby effectively addressing the limitations inherent in traditional clinical internship systems. Our cross-national analysis demonstrates that 4C/ID model [8, 9] provides a robust cross-cultural framework for analyzing clinical internship curricula. Whether in Chinese medical schools or foreign medical education institutions, the principles of the 4C/ID model are effectively embodied through context-specific adaptations in the design of clinical internships.

Core findings and theoretical positioning

The 4C/ID model positions learning tasks as the cornerstone of authentic education—a principle reflected across both systems, yet through culturally distinct lenses. Whether it is Stanford University’s community and international medical practices or Peking University’s comprehensive teaching outpatient services, the authenticity of clinical experiences is enhanced through diverse healthcare settings and meticulous teaching processes. Here, systematic case analyses and faculty feedback align with the 4C/ID principle of integrating knowledge and skills into holistic tasks. Crucially, lexical analysis revealed foreign schools prioritize “practice” (experiential variation), while Chinese counterparts emphasize “ability” (competency systematization). Though distinct, these approaches are complementary and both align with contextual learning theory [30–32].

In the realm of global medical education, both Chinese and foreign medical schools emphasize gradual learning progression, despite differences in scaffolding methods. This aligns with Bruner’s “spiral curriculum” theory, which advocates for presenting knowledge in a spiral manner [33]. This approach allows students to revisit and build upon concepts at different stages of learning, thereby accommodating their cognitive development levels and promoting in-depth understanding of knowledge. Similarly, this method of curriculum design is consistent with Vygotsky’s concept of providing appropriate support within the “Zone of Proximal Development” [34]. Vygotsky’s constructivist theory highlights that learning is a social construction process where students, through interactive scaffolding with teachers and peers, incrementally build knowledge.

The 4C/ID model emphasizes procedural information and phased curricula to regulate cognitive load, a principle reflected in foreign schools’ staged programs and methods like TBL, which provide effective scaffolding. While Chinese medical schools also prioritized cognitive load management, their approach leaned more toward traditional lecture-based and teacher-guided strategies. However, the shift from traditional end-of-term exams to phased assessments in Chinese schools indicated an effort to distribute learning tasks more evenly. In evaluation and feedback, foreign medical schools often employed student-centered systems focusing on individual differences and cognitive apprenticeship. Chinese medical schools, on the other hand, emphasized diverse examination forms. Yet, the implementation of frequent internship assessments in Chinese schools allowed students to address learning issues promptly, alleviating anxiety from accumulated problems.

Part-task practice in the 4C/ID model emphasizes deliberate repetition to reinforce skill automation while avoiding rote learning. This approach is reflected in both foreign and Chinese medical schools. Repeated practice is essential for the consolidation and improvement of clinical skills. Behaviorist learning theory states that learning can be consolidated and improved through repeated practice and reinforcing feedback [35]. It is important to note that repeated practice should focus on complex and challenging learning tasks rather than mundane repetition [36, 37]. Harris et al. showed that longer duty hours were not correlated to higher scores or overall internship grades [38]. Simple repetitive tasks are not conducive to in-depth skill acquisition and creative thinking. Therefore, when designing and implementing clinical internship programs for medical students, medical educators should design more challenging learning tasks for repetitive exercises.

Practical implications for medical education reform

The integration of educational theories into medical training has prompted innovations like simulated teaching, a method to achieve authenticity. It enables learners to solve problems and think like professionals in real-world scenarios, acquiring relevant knowledge, skills, and attitudes [39]. While widely adopted in foreign medical schools, simulation implementation faces significant barriers in developing nations due to resource constraints [40–42]. To bridge this gap, hybrid simulations—combining high-fidelity mannequins and standardized patients—offer a cost-effective pathway to diverse clinical experiences [43–45]. Chinese schools often integrate clinical rotations with simulator training [46], avoiding ethical issues and financial burdens while bridging the gap between simulation and clinical practice [47]. Looking ahead, the emerging integration of AI-powered virtual patients and natural language processing systems offers unprecedented opportunities to enhance clinical authenticity [48]. These technologies align with the 4C/ID principle of holistic task integration by generating dynamic clinical scenarios that adapt to learners’ decision-making patterns in real-time [49].

Building on this technological evolution, medical education reform in China also necessitates a parallel shift in assessment paradigms. Educational philosophies are increasingly emphasizing individual differences and the learning process itself, moving evaluation towards a greater focus on diagnostic feedback and improvement to better manage students’ cognitive load and enhance overall educational quality. Future reforms should actively incorporate supportive information strategies from the 4C/ID model, such as just-in-time procedural guidance. AI’s capacity for personalized learning trajectory optimization presents innovative solutions for cognitive load management. The adaptive learning platform utilizing machine learning algorithms may be able to analyze individual performance data in the future to automatically adjust the complexity of tasks and provide just-in-time cognitive scaffolding.

Limitations and prospects

The qualitative comparative analysis approach used in this study offers a nuanced understanding of the complexities involved in clinical internship programs. However, it is limited by the subjectivity inherent in qualitative analysis and the potential for bias in the selection of case study schools. The use of word frequency analysis provides a quantitative measure of educational focus, but it may not fully capture the depth or context of the educational objectives. The study’s limitations include the reliance on official available data, which may not fully represent the breadth of educational practices. The cross-cultural comparison also presents challenges in interpreting terms and practices across different educational systems.

Future research should aim to address these limitations by expanding the sample size and including a more diverse range of medical schools. Longitudinal studies could provide insights into the long-term impact of different clinical internship models on student performance and patient outcomes. Additionally, mixed-methods research could offer a more comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between educational theory and practice in medical education.

Conclusion

This study used the 4C/ID model to compare in detail the similarities and differences in curriculum objectives, contents, teaching methods, and evaluation systems between medical schools in China and the world. Both curricula emphasize authentic learning to achieve the goal of clinical competency development. The diversity of teaching methods and the evaluation system of internships in foreign medical schools are more student-centered and emphasize the reduction of cognitive load, while Chinese medical schools emphasize the consolidation of basic knowledge and systematic training. Advanced experience of foreign medical schools in relieving the cognitive load of students could be used for reference to better meet the needs of modern medical education development.
