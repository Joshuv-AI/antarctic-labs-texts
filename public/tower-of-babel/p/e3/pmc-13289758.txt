Dehydrative Allylic Amination in Water via a Polymer‐Supported Ruthenium Catalyst
Authors: Shota Mizuno, Yasuhiro Kubota, Toshiyasu Inuzuka, Kazumasa Funabiki, Munenori Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern organic synthesis relies heavily on toxic, nonrenewable organic solvents that often generate large volumes of waste, particularly in pharmaceutical processes, where solvents account for over 80% of chemical waste. Conventional cross‐coupling reactions frequently require activated electrophiles, produce unwanted byproducts, and involve complex purification procedures. Herein, we report a solid‐supported ruthenium catalyst that enables dehydrative allylic amination in water, directly converting allyl alcohols and amines into allylic amines. The 2‐mercaptopyridine–ruthenium complex immobilized on an amphiphilic polymer efficiently catalyzed the reaction under mild aqueous conditions, producing only water as a byproduct. This method avoids halide‐based leaving groups, additives, and organic solvents, while addressing three key sustainability challenges in a single reaction system: solvent toxicity, waste generation, and purification complexity. Moreover, the catalyst was readily removed via filtration, eliminating the need for chromatographic purification. Given the significance of allylamines in medicinal chemistry, this environmentally benign strategy offers a practical step toward scalable green synthesis.Keywords: amines, green chemistry, polymer‐supported catalyst, ruthenium, water

Introduction

Water is a major component of living organisms, where enzymes catalyze the synthesis of various molecules and biotransformations in aqueous media [1] with high selectivity, avoiding the production of undesired byproducts. In contrast, since its inception, synthetic organic chemistry has traditionally relied on non‐aqueous environments, with the development and sophistication of modern reaction principles closely tied to organic solvents [2]. These solvents enable uniform dispersion of organic compounds in solution, thereby increasing reaction efficiency. Reaction rates and selectivities can be controlled via appropriate solvent selection, leading to the development and systematization of reaction principles based on the premise that organic reactions occur in organic solvents [3]. Among these methodologies, transition‐metal‐catalyzed cross‐coupling reactions have emerged as one of the most important tools in organic synthesis, widely applied in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and functional materials [4, 5]. However, such reactions typically require liquid‐phase media and additional post‐reaction operations, such as solvent removal, spent catalyst separation, and target compound purification (Scheme 1a). Many commonly used solvents are toxic, and in the pharmaceutical industry more than 80% of chemical waste originates from organic solvents, creating a major environmental burden [6, 7, 8]. Consequently, there is a strong demand for catalytic reactions that eliminate the need for hazardous solvents and purification operations, although this remains challenging [9, 10].

Aqueous synthesis has gained significant attention since the 2000s as a green alternative to traditional organic solvents [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Water is abundant in nature, safe, and an attractive solvent. Numerous successful examples of transition‐metal‐catalyzed reactions in water have been reported, including cross‐coupling reactions, hydrogenation, and oxidation. These reactions established water as a viable reaction medium for various organic transformations, making aqueous synthesis a well‐established field. However, as this approach has become increasingly common over the past decade, there is a growing need to push the boundaries of aqueous synthesis beyond conventional methodologies.

In this context, the incorporation of dehydrative processes into aqueous synthesis would represent a significant advancement in green chemistry [16, 17, 18]. Dehydrative reactions, in which water is eliminated as the sole byproduct, offer an inherently attractive strategy for minimizing waste. When conducted in water, these transformations create a unique paradigm in which the solvent and the byproduct are identical, thereby maximizing atom economy. Several pioneering studies demonstrated the feasibility of dehydrative reactions in water, including alcohol dehydration, condensation, and substitution. These reports have shown that water can, counterintuitively, function as both the reaction medium and a product of dehydrative transformations, challenging conventional assumptions about solvent–product compatibility. A particularly relevant mechanistic precedent was reported by Yi et al., who demonstrated that cationic ruthenium complexes can activate C—OH bonds via an oxidative addition pathway, enabling dehydrogenative coupling of alcohols with alkenes [19]. Although conducted in organic solvents under homogeneous conditions, this work established a conceptual and mechanistic foundation for Ru‐mediated hydroxyl‐group activation and directly motivated the present study.

Building on this foundation, we envisioned that integrating solid‐supported catalysts into aqueous dehydrative processes could further advance sustainable synthesis. Heterogeneous catalysts based on polymer‐supported metal complexes can be removed by simple filtration after reaction completion, thereby eliminating the need for chromatographic purification [20, 21, 22, 23]. This strategy addresses two persistent challenges in synthetic chemistry: waste generation and purification complexity. Recent advances have demonstrated its utility: a recyclable palladium complex supported on a polymer‐bound Xantphos ligand (POL‐Xantphos) enabled cascade dehydrative cyclization to afford benzofuro[2,3‐b]pyrazines [24]; a ruthenium complex immobilized on a 2D aza‐covalent organic framework (Ru(p‐cymene)@Aza‐COF) catalyzed quinazolinone synthesis from allylic alcohols with sustained activity over six cycles [25]; and a hyper‐cross‐linked polymer‐supported iridium complex (Cp*Ir@HCPs) enabled N‐trideuteromethylation of amines with negligible loss of activity over six consecutive runs [26].

To demonstrate the potential of this integrated approach, the dehydrative allylic amination of allyl alcohols was selected as a model transformation. This reaction is well‐suited because hydroxyl‐group activation and elimination have been extensively investigated, providing a strong foundation for mechanistic analysis [27, 28, 29]. Furthermore, various transition metals, including Fe [30, 31], Pd [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37], Ni [38], Ir [39], and Pt [40], can promote the departure of hydroxyl groups under appropriate conditions (Scheme 1b). However, many reported systems require exogenous Lewis or Brønsted acid activators to sufficiently enable substitution of allyl alcohols [32, 39]. Allylic substitution in aqueous media is well documented, demonstrating the compatibility of π‐allyl chemistry with water as a solvent [41, 42, 43, 44, 45]. In a particularly relevant example, Ma et al. reported a palladium‐catalyzed dehydrative synthesis of allylic amines directly in water without additives [46], demonstrating that activator‐free allyl alcohol substitution is achievable under aqueous conditions. However, this system—and, to the best of our knowledge, all additive‐free aqueous allylic amination methods reported to date—relies on homogeneous catalysis, which necessitates post‐reaction catalyst separation and complicates product isolation. To our knowledge, a heterogeneous catalyst capable of additive‐free dehydrative allylic amination in water has not been reported. The direct conversion of allyl alcohols and amines to allylamines in water using a recoverable solid catalyst simultaneously addresses key sustainability challenges, including solvent toxicity, waste generation, additive use, and purification complexity.

Given the demands of this transformation, we focused on ruthenium as a versatile catalyst for allylic substitution that often exhibits reactivity complementary to those of other transition metals [47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52]. The oxidative addition of Ru(II) into C—OH bonds, as demonstrated by Yi et al. in homogeneous dehydrogenative coupling [19], provides a mechanistic rationale for translating this reactivity to heterogeneous aqueous systems. Consistent with this premise, ruthenium complexes have shown high activity in hydroxyl‐group activation, supporting their potential for direct alcohol functionalization [53, 54, 55, 56, 57]. In this study, we developed a method for the direct synthesis of allylamines from allyl alcohols and amines in water using a solid‐supported ruthenium complex catalyst (Scheme 1c). Our approach employs an amphiphilic polymer‐supported ruthenium complex bearing a 2‐mercaptopyridine ligand, yielding only the desired allylamine and water as the sole byproduct. Unlike homogeneous aqueous systems, the heterogeneous nature of the catalyst enables straightforward product isolation by filtration without the use of organic solvents. This work constitutes the first example of heterogeneous Ru‐catalyzed dehydrative allylic amination in water, advancing the field of aqueous synthesis toward improved sustainability and operational practicality.

Results and Discussion

To address the challenges of direct allylic amination in water, we envisioned a ruthenium complex capable of hydroxyl activation through dual roles: Brønsted acid assistance and strong electron donation to facilitate π‐allyl formation. We selected [Cp*Ru(MeCN)3][PF6] as a suitable precursor, previously reported to promote challenging substitution reactions [49, 51, 52]. As a bifunctional ligand, 2‐mercaptopyridine was chosen for its ability to act both as a proton donor [58] and a chelating [59] electron‐rich N, S‐type ligand [60, 61, 62, 63, 64]. Treatment of allyl alcohol 1a with [Cp*Ru(MeCN)3][PF6] and 2‐mercaptopyridine afforded the corresponding π‐allyl ruthenium complex 2 [65], which was isolated and characterized via NMR and X‐ray crystallography (Scheme 2). Upon addition of amine 3a, the desired allylamine product 4a was obtained in an 87% yield under mild conditions. To evaluate the catalytic ability of complex 2, dehydrative amination of allyl alcohol 1a with amine 3a (1.1 eq.) was performed using 5 mol% of the ruthenium complex 2 (Table 1). To assess reactivity and solvent effects, several representative solvents were examined (more details on the conditions can be found in the Table S1, Supporting Information). Acetonitrile (MeCN), which was also used during complex formation, provided the desired allylamine 4a in an 87% yield (entry 1). The reaction also proceeded in dichloromethane and toluene, affording moderate yields of 68%–88% (entries 2 and 3). Notably, when water was used as the solvent, the reaction exhibited remarkable reactivity with quantitative conversion (entries 4 and 5). This enhanced reactivity likely arises from carbon—oxygen bond activation, facilitated by the hydrogen‐bonding network formed between water and the hydroxyl group of the allyl alcohol [66]. Furthermore, increasing the reaction temperature to 100°C enabled complete conversion within just 1 h, demonstrating that highly efficient transformation is achievable within a practical timeframe (entry 6).

Motivated by the high efficiency of the homogeneous system, we sought to develop a heterogeneous analogue of the ruthenium catalyst to facilitate product purification. Synthesis began with allylation of 2‐mercaptonicotinic acid (5) under basic conditions to afford the corresponding S‐allyl ligand precursor 6 in quantitative yield (Scheme 3). For polymer immobilization, we selected a commercially available amine‐functionalized Tentagel resin, a well‐established amphiphilic support widely used in aqueous‐phase catalysis [20, 21, 22, 23]. EDC‐mediated coupling of the allyl‐functionalized ligand to the Tentagel resin furnished the immobilized ligand 7 in high yield. Completion of the amidation was further confirmed by the TNBS test (Figure S13). Subsequent complexation with [Cp*Ru(MeCN)3][PF6] in MeCN at 25°C rapidly produced the polymer‐supported ruthenium complex 8 (Ru‐NPs 8), with a metal loading of 0.13 mmol Ru per gram of resin. Ligand immobilization and subsequent complex formation were evaluated by FT‐IR spectroscopy. The characteristic carbonyl stretching band at 1678 cm−1, assigned to the carboxylic acid of the free ligand (6, Figure S9), was absent in the spectrum of the resin‐supported ligand (7, Figure S6), consistent with amide bond formation between the ligand and the resin amino groups and thus covalent immobilization. Furthermore, the IR spectra of Ru‐NPs 8 and the non–immobilized complex 2 showed good agreement for the characteristic peaks associated with the metal coordination environment, notably at 849 and 3530 cm−1 (Figures S7 and S8). These results confirm that immobilization did not induce decomposition and that the targeted ruthenium complex was successfully constructed on the resin. Ru‐NPs 8 was further characterized via ICP‐AES (Table S2), SEM (Figures S3 and S4), EDX (Figures S11 and S12), and solid‐state 19F NMR spectroscopy (Figure S10), which collectively confirmed the successful coordination of the ligand to the ruthenium center. Hot‐filtration and leaching tests were conducted to assess catalyst stability; no ruthenium species were detected in the filtrate, indicating that the catalyst remains tightly bound to the solid support under the reaction conditions (Figure S15). Notably, in the homogeneous catalyst studies (Table 1), conventional workup procedures (including EtOAc extraction) were employed to determine NMR yields. Although appropriate for the initial optimization, these operations are not representative of the final methodology. In contrast, reactions employing the heterogeneous catalyst Ru‐NPs 8 require no organic solvent at any stage: the reaction proceeds entirely in water, and the product is isolated by filtration to remove the solid catalyst, followed by washing with hot water, without organic solvent in either the reaction or workup (Table 2, entry 1 and Figure S1).

Isolated yields.

Using the synthesized immobilized ruthenium catalyst, we investigated the substrate scope of the dehydrative amination reaction with various amines and allyl alcohol derivatives (Table 2). With respect to the amine scope, acyclic aliphatic secondary amines were well tolerated. For example, dioctylamine (3b) delivered the desired allylamine 4b in an excellent yield, and the sterically demanding bis(2‐ethylhexyl)amine (3c) also reacted smoothly to afford 4c in >98% yield (entry 3). Moreover, N‐methylaniline (3d), an aryl‐substituted secondary amine, was converted to 4d in an >98% yield (entry 4). Similarly, bis(4‐methoxyphenyl)amine (3e) provided 4e in a high yield, indicating that the reaction proceeds efficiently with aromatic as well as aliphatic amines (entry 5). When a primary amine such as aniline (3f) was employed, the monoallylated product predominated; however, small amounts of the diallylated byproduct 4f’ were also detected as the reaction proceeded (entry 6).

Next, we evaluated the scope of allyl alcohol substrates beyond 1a by examining a range of substituted allyl alcohols (1b–1l). For example, cinnamyl alcohol (1b) afforded 4g in 79% yield (entry 7). High yields were also obtained from regioisomeric alcohols; notably, 1‐phenylprop‐2‐en‐1‐ol (1c) delivered 4h in excellent yield (entry 8). Irrespective of whether branched or linear substrates were used, the reaction consistently afforded linear allylamine products. Although ruthenium‐catalyzed allylic aminations typically favor the branched regioisomer [67], ligands bearing Brønsted acidic functionalities have been reported to promote formation of the linear products [68, 69]. In the present system, 2‐mercaptopyridine, which can also function as a Brønsted acid, likely plays an analogous role. These observations suggest that ligand electronics modulate the nucleophile's approach to the π‐allyl ruthenium intermediate. Accordingly, the regioselectivity appears to be dictated primarily by ligand effects rather than by any substrate memory effect. Regarding electronic effects, substrates bearing electron‐donating substituents (e.g., 1d, methyl) provided moderate yields (e.g., 4i, 65%), whereas substrates containing electron‐withdrawing substituents, such as trifluoromethyl (1e) and chloro (1f), afforded the desired products in high yields (4j, >98%; 4k, 92%) (entries 9–11). Although the substitution position influenced the outcome to some extent, all substrates afforded good yields, as illustrated by 4l and 4m (entries 12 and 13). Substrates bearing extended aromatic systems, such as naphthalene (1i), also proceeded in good yield (entry 14). A 2‐pyridyl‐substituted allylic alcohol (1j) likewise reacted efficiently with morpholine (3h) to give the heteroaryl allylamine product 4o in an 80% yield (entry 15), indicating good tolerance of electron‐deficient heteroaromatic substituents. Allylic alcohols substituted at the allylic position (1k) also showed high conversion (entry 16). The methodology was further extended to cyclic allylic alcohols: 2‐cyclohexen‐1‐ol (1l) reacted smoothly with amine 3a to afford the corresponding product 4q in an 85% yield (entry 17), highlighting compatibility with conformationally restricted substrates. Substrate–nucleophile combinations that did not proceed or afforded yields of ≤5% are summarized in the Supporting Information (Scheme S1). Collectively, these results demonstrate the broad substrate compatibility of this reaction system.

To confirm that the observed catalytic activity originates solely from the ruthenium complex, a series of control experiments was conducted. No product was detected when Ru‐NPs 8 was replaced with the bare Tentagel resin, the resin‐supported ligand 7 (without ruthenium), or the resin combined with free ligand precursors 5 or 6 (Table S4). Furthermore, no reaction occurred in the absence of any catalyst, with free ligand 6 alone, or with [Cp*Ru(MeCN)3][PF6] in combination with free ligand 6 under air (Table S1, entries 34–36). Collectively, these results demonstrate that neither the polymer support nor the ligand alone accounts for the observed activity, and that formation of the well‐defined resin‐immobilized ruthenium complex is essential for catalysis.

Several experiments were conducted to elucidate the reaction mechanism (Scheme 4). To gain insight into the observed regioselectivity, a branched allylic alcohol derivative 9 was used as a substrate. The reaction afforded the corresponding linear product 4f (Scheme 4a), suggesting a reversible equilibrium between the allylamine product and a ruthenium π‐allyl intermediate, ultimately favoring the formation of the sterically more stable linear isomer [69]. To investigate the catalytically active species, a reaction was performed by mixing the catalyst precursor [Cp*Ru(MeCN)3][PF6] with 2‐mercaptopyridine in MeCN at 25°C. After heating the mixture at 80°C for 1 h, a dark green dinuclear ruthenium complex 10 was obtained (Scheme 4b), which remained unchanged upon the addition of amine 3a. When the dinuclear complex 10 was employed as the catalyst for the dehydrative amination of allyl alcohol (1a) with amine 3a, the desired product 4a was obtained in only 22% yield (Scheme 4c). Under standard catalytic conditions using the polymer‐supported system, the initially brown resin turned green after the reaction (Scheme 4d), indicating a catalyst transformation. These observations imply that complex 10 is catalytically inactive or dormant, and that the true active species is likely a mononuclear ruthenium complex.

Based on these findings and the relevant literature, a plausible catalytic cycle was proposed (Figure 1) [70, 71]. The π‐allyl complex 2 first reacts with the nucleophile to generate intermediate A. Allyl alcohol 1 then coordinates to the ruthenium center to form complex B. In B, the Brønsted acidic moiety of the thiolato ligand engages the hydroxyl group of the coordinated allyl alcohol through hydrogen bonding, which concomitantly increases substrate electrophilicity and enhances the nucleophilicity of the ruthenium center. This cooperative activation promotes oxidative addition of the Ru(II) center into the allyl moiety to produce the π‐allyl intermediate C [72]. The cooperative activation of the allylic C—OH bond can be rationalized by two synergistic contributions. First, the thiol proton of the 2‐mercaptopyridine ligand acts as a Brønsted acid and forms a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group in intermediate B. This interaction increases the electrophilicity of the allylic carbon via charge polarization, while the nucleophilic Ru(II) center promotes oxidative addition of the allyl C—O bond to afford the π‐allyl Ru(IV) intermediate C [69]. Second, the aqueous medium provides additional assistance by stabilizing the departing hydroxide through strong hydrogen‐bond solvation; delocalization of the negative charge within a water cluster further facilitates C—O bond cleavage [41, 73]. This dual activation—by the ligand SH proton and by the surrounding water network—accounts for the pronounced rate enhancement observed in water (Table 1, entry 1 vs. entries 4–6) and represents a key advantage of conducting this transformation under aqueous conditions. Nucleophilic attack of the amine at the π‐allyl carbon then furnishes the allylamine product and regenerates the active species A [55]. Although the elementary steps are largely reversible, the product can reversibly revert to the π‐allyl complex, which favors formation of the more thermodynamically stable linear isomer. These findings further suggest that complex 10 is catalytically inactive (or dormant) and that the true active species is most likely a mononuclear ruthenium complex. This inactivity can be rationalized in terms of the metal oxidation state. By analogy with the structurally related dinuclear complex CpRu(µ‐pyS)2RuCp2 reported by Kirchner et al. [62], complex 10 is expected to contain a Ru(III)–Ru(III) core with a metal–metal single bond. Because the proposed catalytic cycle requires oxidative addition of the allyl C—OH bond at a Ru(II) center, the higher oxidation state of the dinuclear species renders this elementary step thermodynamically unfavorable, consistent with the markedly lower yield obtained when 10 is employed as the precatalyst (22%, Scheme 4c). Although the structure of 10 could not be confirmed because single crystals suitable for X‐ray analysis were not obtained, the pronounced ring distortion reported for the analogous Ru(III)–Ru(III) complex [55] suggests that the dinuclear framework may partially dissociate under forcing conditions (heating in water), which could explain the residual low catalytic activity observed with 10. In contrast, immobilization of the ruthenium complex on the polymer support in Ru‐NPs 8 is proposed to suppress dimerization by maintaining spatial separation of the metal centers, thereby preserving the mononuclear Ru(II) active species throughout the catalytic cycle and accounting for the sustained activity of the heterogeneous catalyst.

Reuse and Application Considerations

To assess the practical reusability of Ru‐NPs 8, catalyst recycling experiments were performed (Scheme 5a, Table S3). Under the standard conditions (allyl alcohol 1a, 1.0 mmol; 100°C; 1 h), significant deactivation was observed after the first run: the yield of 4a decreased to 48% in the second cycle and fell to trace levels by the third cycle. To extend catalyst lifetime, modified conditions were examined, including increasing the loading of allyl alcohol 1a to 1.5 equivalents, lowering the reaction temperature to 80°C, and extending the reaction time to 3 h. Under these conditions, the catalyst retained high activity over the first two cycles, affording 4a in 96% and >98% yield, respectively. However, activity dropped sharply in the third cycle (52% yield), and no product was detected in the fourth cycle. Consistent with the mechanistic studies described above, this progressive deactivation is attributed to the accumulation of the catalytically inactive dinuclear ruthenium complex 10 on the polymer support. Although these results demonstrate that the catalyst can be reused under optimized conditions, further optimization of the reaction parameters and catalyst design will be required to improve long‐term recyclability.

(a) Recycling test of the Ru‐NPs 8 catalyst. (b) Direct synthesis of pharmaceuticals and useful intermediates.

To further demonstrate the synthetic utility of this methodology, the heterogeneous catalyst was applied to the synthesis of pharmaceutically relevant targets (Scheme 5). The antifungal drug Naftifine (4r) was obtained in a 92% isolated yield on a gram scale (2.6 g), confirming that the reaction is readily scalable without loss of efficiency. Furthermore, coupling alcohol 1c with N‐Boc‐piperazine (3k) under the standard conditions afforded the protected allylamine intermediate 4s in a >98% yield. This intermediate serves as a versatile building block: straightforward Boc deprotection followed by N‐functionalization enables direct access to the antihistamine and calcium channel blocker cinnarizine, as well as the related antimigraine agent flunarizine [38]. Collectively, these examples demonstrate that the present methodology provides a practical and operationally simple route to piperazine‐containing pharmaceutical scaffolds, which are among the most prevalent structural motifs in medicinal chemistry, without requiring organic solvents, activated electrophiles, or chromatographic purification.

To quantitatively assess the sustainability of this methodology, green metrics were calculated for the reaction of allyl alcohol 1c with amine 3j. The atom economy was 94%, consistent with water being the sole stoichiometric byproduct. The E‐factor, calculated excluding water as the reaction medium, was 0.24, comparing favorably with values typically reported for pharmaceutical syntheses (25–100) [74]. These results quantitatively substantiate the sustainability of the methodology and align with the dual role of water as both solvent and byproduct.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a dehydrative allylic amination reaction catalyzed by a polymer‐supported ruthenium complex bearing a 2‐mercaptopyridine ligand in water. This methodology enables the direct conversion of allyl alcohols and amines to allylamines without the need for halide‐based leaving groups, organic solvents, or chromatographic purification. The catalyst demonstrated a broad substrate scope and could be easily recovered by simple filtration. The key characteristics of our system include quantitative yields in water, exclusive formation of linear allylamine products regardless of the substrate structure, and operational simplicity, requiring only heating and filtration. From a broader perspective, this study challenges the long‐standing paradigm in which efficient organic transformations require organic solvents and activated electrophiles. By combining water as a reaction medium, dehydrative activation, and heterogeneous catalysis, we demonstrate that these elements—traditionally viewed as incompatible—can be integrated to achieve highly efficient synthesis. This breakthrough suggests that many fundamental assumptions regarding reaction design may need reconsideration, potentially triggering a paradigm shift toward biomimetic synthetic approaches that mirror enzymatic processes. If this methodology is widely adopted, pharmaceutical manufacturing can be fundamentally transformed by eliminating solvent waste streams and simplifying purification processes. Scalability and operational simplicity may accelerate the development of continuous‐flow processes for pharmaceutical synthesis, while environmental benefits could drive regulatory changes favoring such green methodologies in industrial settings. Future research should focus on expanding this dehydrative approach to other cross‐coupling reactions and exploring the generality of the bifunctional ligand design for aqueous‐phase catalysis.
