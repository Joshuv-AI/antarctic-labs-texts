A comprehensive survey on graph neural networks for gene regulatory network inference
Authors: Noor Jamal Alkhateeb, Mamoun Awad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gene regulatory network (GRN) represents a complex web of genetic interactions that governs cellular functions and responses to environmental stimuli. Understanding these intricate relationships is crucial for advancing developmental biology, disease modeling, and therapeutic discovery. With the growing interest in graph-based approaches, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for GRN inference, offering the ability to capture high-dimensional dependencies and topological structures within gene networks. This survey presents the first comprehensive review of GNN-based methods for GRN inference, analyzing 16 state-of-the-art approaches. We categorize these methods based on their underlying architectures, inference strategies, and computational frameworks. Additionally, we provide a critical evaluation of their strengths, limitations, and real-world applicability. Unlike prior surveys that focus on either scRNA-seq or deep learning broadly, this work systematically unifies graph architectures, learning paradigms, and data regimes under a common benchmarking framework. By identifying key challenges—such as scalability, interpretability, and dataset limitations—this survey aims to guide both life scientists in selecting appropriate computational models and researchers in developing next-generation GRN inference techniques using graph-based learning.

Introduction

Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) represent intricate interactions between regulatory elements that influence biological processes in living organisms. These networks dynamically control gene expression across various cell types and developmental stages in a spatio-temporal manner. Understanding the role of individual genes and their relationships within GRNs is crucial for interpreting biological processes at the molecular level. The reconstruction and analysis of GRNs have become essential for studying the mechanisms that drive genotypes to phenotypes. GRN inference holds significant potential for a wide range of applications, including precision medicine, disease modeling, biomarker discovery, and drug development [1].

Recent advances in biochemistry and sequencing technologies have enabled the monitoring of biological systems at an unprecedented resolution, leading to the development of GRN inference methods for both bulk sequencing [2–4] and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data [5]. Bulk sequencing-based GRN inference has been widely used to analyze gene interactions across different conditions, offering valuable insights into regulatory mechanisms at the tissue level. However, bulk sequencing averages gene expression across cell populations, making it insufficient for capturing cell-type-specific regulatory interactions and dynamic changes in heterogeneous systems. In contrast, scRNA-seq-based GRN inference enables the dissection of complex tissues into individual cell types, allowing for the study of gene regulation at single-cell resolution. This approach provides deeper insights into cell differentiation, lineage specification, and dynamic regulatory processes. Deep learning approaches in GRN inference typically reformulate network reconstruction as a supervised [3, 6] or semi-supervised learning problem [7] on graphs. Many computational methods have been developed to infer regulatory networks from gene expression data, predominantly using unsupervised techniques. Unsupervised methods rely on expression data only but tend to achieve lower prediction accuracies than supervised methods [8]. By contrast, supervised methods require information about known interactions for training, and this information is typically sparse. Semi-supervised methods reflect a compromise and can be trained with much less interaction data, but usually are not as accurate predictors as supervised methods. Authors in [9] evaluated supervised inference of regulatory networks (SIRENE) in comparison with context likelihood of relatedness, algorithm for the reconstruction of accurate cellular networks (ARACNE), relevance networks, and a Bayesian network on an Escherichia coli benchmark data set by Faith et al. [10], and found that the supervised method considerably outperformed the unsupervised techniques. Cerulo et al. [11] compared supervised and semi-supervised support vector machines with two unsupervised methods and found the former superior.

Despite substantial progress, GRN inference remains challenging due to high dimensionality, class imbalance, technical noise, and incomplete ground-truth networks, which collectively affect scalability, generalization, and the reliability of inferred regulatory interactions. These challenges are exacerbated in scRNA-seq data, where dropout events, sparsity, and heterogeneity across cells introduce additional sources of uncertainty. As a result, traditional correlation-based and information-theoretic methods often struggle to scale or generalize across datasets and experimental conditions [12].

Graph-based deep learning has recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for GRN inference by explicitly modeling gene interactions as structured graphs. Graph neural networks (GNNs) and their variants enable the integration of node features, edge attributes, and prior biological knowledge through message passing, attention mechanisms, and latent embedding learning [13]. This has led to a diverse landscape of models—including graph convolutional networks (GCNs), graph attention networks (GATs), autoencoder-based frameworks, and transformer-inspired architectures—that reformulate GRN reconstruction as link prediction or graph representation learning problems. In addition, recent methodological innovations further emphasized the importance of improving structural representation learning for GRN inference. Emerging graph-based deep learning models demonstrated enhanced capability to capture complex regulatory dependencies, suggesting that future research should focus on improving model generalization across heterogeneous biological datasets [14]. More recently, authors in [15] proved that GRN inference can be formulated as a link-prediction problem on biological interaction graphs, motivating the use of graph neural architectures capable of learning complex molecular dependencies. Their method was able to predict the synergistic interactions between cellular communication and downstream GRNs and construct complete cellular communication networks, which improve biological significance and interpretability. Furthermore, the rapid emergence of multi-omics sequencing technologies has highlighted the importance of integrating complementary regulatory signals beyond gene expression alone, motivating the development of graph learning frameworks capable of modeling complex cross-modal biological dependencies [16]. Similarly, recent work demonstrated that hybrid deep learning strategies, including transfer learning across species, provided a promising direction for improving scalability and generalization of GRN inference models. They proved that transfer learning enables cross-species GRN inference and enhances performance in limited-data scenarios [17].

Despite the advantages of GNN-based models in capturing structured dependencies and complex regulatory interactions [18], however, existing surveys lack a unified and systematic analysis of graph-based GRN inference methods across architectures, data modalities, and learning paradigms. Chen et al. [19] provide a simulation study to assess the performance of some GRN methods. However, they include only three methods that are single-cell specific and do not provide an in-depth discussion of the underlying techniques. Fiers et al. [20] recapitulate the GRN inference methods from the application perspectives without going into details of the analysis techniques. In addition, many of the methods mentioned are not available for download as standalone packages. Other studies provide a literature review from a computational perspective. Hang et al. [21] provide a comprehensive review of GRN reference, providing a simulation study and deep discussion, but their study is limited only to scRNA-seq methods. Recently, authors in [22] have provided a survey of GRN inference using deep learning methods, considering different scenarios for evaluation. Their study is very general and discusses the deep learning models for GRN inference without going into details for each deep learning method and properties.

The comparative analysis presented in this survey leads to several key observations. First, convolution-based GNNs remain the most computationally efficient solutions and are well suited for large-scale GRN reconstruction, although they are often limited by local message passing and oversmoothing. Second, attention-based architectures improve robustness to noisy single-cell data by selectively weighting regulatory interactions, but require higher computational resources. Third, causality-aware approaches provide greater biological interpretability by explicitly modeling regulatory directionality, although they depend on stronger assumptions and additional prior information. Fourth, transformer-enhanced and hybrid architectures offer improved capacity to capture long-range dependencies and integrate heterogeneous biological information, but their increased complexity may reduce reproducibility and scalability. Overall, no single architecture consistently outperforms all others across biological settings; instead, the suitability of a GRN inference framework depends on the characteristics of the dataset, the availability of prior knowledge, and the intended biological application. To facilitate side-by-side comparison of the reviewed approaches, we synthesize their major strengths, limitations, interpretability, scalability, and biological applicability through a qualitative cross-family comparison. The review first introduces the fundamental GNN architectures relevant to GRN inference, followed by implementation considerations and the practical usability of existing methods. It then provides a comparative analysis of state-of-the-art approaches and presents experimental evaluation and benchmarking results. Finally, current challenges, limitations, and future research directions are discussed. This survey aims to support life scientists in selecting methods appropriate for their data and research objectives, while also providing computational researchers with insights into current methodological limitations and opportunities for developing more robust GRN inference methods and supporting infrastructure.

Architectural GNN variants applied in the GRN inference

To provide the necessary methodological background, we first briefly review the main architectural variants of GNNs that form the foundation of GRN inference models. This section focuses on general GNN designs rather than specific GRN applications, which are comparatively analyzed in Section 4.

GNNs have evolved into various architectures to address different challenges and applications in GRNs. Figure 1 illustrates the graph approaches in GRN prediction.

Illustration of GNN architectures used in GRN inference, highlighting local aggregation in GCNs, attention weighting in GATs, and latent reconstruction in GAEs.

GCN is the most popular and also a foundational type of GNNs. The key idea behind GCNs is to update the representation of a node by aggregating and transforming the features of its neighboring nodes and itself. This aggregation mechanism enables GCNs to capture the local graph structure around each node. GCNs are widely used for node classification, graph classification, and other tasks where understanding the local structure is crucial. While graph convolution–based models are computationally efficient and scalable, their reliance on local aggregation can limit their ability to capture long-range regulatory dependencies [3, 23].

GATs introduce attention mechanisms to the aggregation step in GNNs (adding weight to the aggregation step). The importance of each neighbor’s features is dynamically weighted during the aggregation process, allowing the model to focus more on relevant neighbors for each node. Attention mechanisms allow models to differentiate influential regulators from less informative neighbors, which is particularly relevant in noisy and sparse single-cell RNA-seq data. Extensions of GATs introduce multi-head attention, quadratic neurons, or dynamic attention schemes to further improve robustness and expressiveness. However, attention-based models typically incur higher computational costs and increased sensitivity to graph construction strategies [24, 25].

Graph Autoencoder (GAE) approaches treat GRN inference as a latent structure learning problem. GAEs and matrix factorization frameworks embed genes into low-dimensional spaces by reconstructing expression patterns or regulatory graphs. These models are effective in capturing global regulatory structure and reducing dimensionality but often rely on correlation-derived priors and require careful regularization to avoid overfitting. Their performance is influenced by the quality of the initial graph and the choice of reconstruction objectives [4, 26, 27].

Transformer and Hybrid Architectures represent a recent direction in GRN inference. By leveraging global attention mechanisms, these approaches aim to overcome the locality constraints of traditional GNNs and capture long-range gene–gene dependencies. Some models integrate pretrained transcriptomic language models with graph encoders, while others fuse multiple inferred networks through transformer-based representations. These architectures offer enhanced flexibility and representational power but are often computationally intensive and less explored in large-scale benchmarking settings.

While these architectural families share the common objective of learning regulatory relationships from graph-structured biological data, they differ substantially in their information propagation mechanisms, scalability, interpretability, and suitability for different GRN inference scenarios. This comparison serves as a conceptual framework for the detailed methodological analysis presented in Section 4.

Implementation, reproducibility, and practical usability

The practical adoption of graph-based approaches for GRN inference depends not only on methodological innovation but also on the availability, robustness, and usability of their implementations. Although many recent models report promising performance, their real-world applicability is often constrained by reproducibility challenges, complex software dependencies, and limited scalability. We selected 16 GRN inference methods based on GNNs from 2020 to 2025. Eleven methods are publicly available for implementation and presented in Table 2. In Table 1, we present the available hyperlink, programming language, references to their original articles, license, and rating for the overall usability of the tools. We believe that this metadata is essential for users to be aware of before investing time to understand the method and to conduct any analysis. The usability assessment was restricted to methods with publicly available source code or executable implementations. Methods lacking accessible implementations were excluded from this comparative usability analysis to ensure reproducibility and objective evaluation.

Most of these methods are free for noncommercial use and can be modified and redistributed. These methods can be included in other analysis pipelines or can be modified to serve the users’ purpose, given that the users follow the terms given in the license. However, users who want to redistribute the software or embed these methods in their workflow need permission from the authors of the tools.

Regarding programming language, Python is the most common language for most of the authors. It is used to implement GRN inference by the 11 available methods. Among the 11 methods, all 11 public ones are provided in the form of a stand-alone package via GitHub. Although it is convenient to perform an analysis using these tools, combining these with other packages might require additional implementations to make it work smoothly between different environments. We also provide ratings for the usability of the surveyed methods. For each method, we provide a score for each of the following categories: (i) tutorial, (ii) documentation, (iii) code quality, (iv) user-friendliness, and (v) completion rate. First, we check if the authors provide a detailed and easy-to-follow tutorial for their tool. DeepRIG, Q-GAT, and GRINCD are the best in providing high-quality tutorials, whereas GNNLink, [3], and DGCGRN provide only short scripts about the dependencies. Second, we check if each of the functions and parameters is correctly documented with details. Q-GAT provides the most detailed documentation. In contrast, the other methods provide only minimal comments about function parameters. Third, we assess the quality of the code regarding its structure, testability (e.g. unit test), compatibility (with different operating systems, dependencies, compilers), and reliability (how frequently the software crashes). GNNLink, GENELink, and Jereesh et al. appraoch [3] run easily. In contrast, GMFGRN was the worst in the configuration bcz it depends on a specific version of dgl, which is not compatible with many operating systems and requires users to manually resolve conflicts and missing dependencies. We assess how easy it is for the users to perform an analysis using their own data, e.g. preparing the input expression and creating additional required inputs. It is worth noting that the data processing and gene filtering steps are not included in most of the provided source code. Most of the tools do not provide code or instructions for the gene filtering step, while the analysis results reported in the reference articles involve this step. To perform an analysis for these methods, users have to use additional code to infer the input. For GNNLink, GENELink, [3], DeepRIG, and IGEGRN users can easily prepare the expression matrix as input. In contrast, GMFGRN, Q-GAT, and DGCGRN require users to prepare data in specific formats. DeepFGRN has an official GitHub, but it is very sparse—no full training script is exposed; several recent works cite the repo mainly for its benchmark datasets rather than runnable code. Newer papers explicitly say they used DeepFGRN’s benchmark data from that GitHub only, as we have our own datasets, so we were not able to complete the training and testing task for DeepFGRN. Finally, we assess the methods based on completion rate using 105 datasets from our simulation studies with varying numbers of genes (100–3000), samples, and sparsity levels (30%–70%). All the methods except DeepFGRN have 100% completion rate. The overall usability score of each method is shown in the last column of Table 2. GENELink and [3] have the highest overall usability rating (4.8/5), whereas GMFGRN(1.5/5) has the lowest rating as it took the maximum time to install and configure the dependencies. GT-GRN is not available, but recently, there has been a new version of it with a public code and a GitHub repository [28]; however, the version that we analyzed earlier is not publicly available, so we did not include it in the implementation.

We note that several recently proposed GRN inference models do not provide open implementations. While these methods may demonstrate strong predictive performance in the original studies, the absence of reproducible pipelines limits independent validation and practical adoption. Future work in this domain should prioritize open-source dissemination to facilitate transparent benchmarking.

Comparative analysis of state-of-the-art methods

In general, GRN inference methods aim at capturing the network dynamics that explain the underlying regulatory states in different cellular compartments and conditions. Each GRN inference model follows an explicit assumption about the regulatory dynamics that can be observed through the changes in expression data. The overall workflow of GRN inference methods is presented in Fig. 1. The input includes an RNA sequence expression matrix in which rows represent genes and columns represent cells (or vice versa). Due to computational limitations, all GRN methods start with a gene filtering step, which narrows the analysis to genes with high variability or genes that are of the user’s interest (predefined genes). Depending on the assumption of the regulatory dynamics and the inference technique used, the filtered data are then transformed into the necessary structure/format, such as binary values, pseudo-time data, or gene correlation. These methods then utilize different techniques to infer the ultimate network. The output graph of these methods can be directed or undirected, and can be represented in different forms, such as a continuous adjacency matrix, a binary adjacency matrix, or a list of ranked edges.

Unlike bulk sequencing, single-cell data provide the expression of individual cells, many of which are from different developmental stages. This enables the arrangement of cells temporally, allowing for the modeling of individual gene expression changes over time [35]. Among the 16 methods surveyed, six used scRNA-seq and time-ordering information, and eight used bulk RNA-seq, and two methods only implemented both scRNA-seq and Bulk RNA-seq.

Regarding validation, numerous techniques have been utilized to assess the performance of the network inference methods, including simulation, enrichment analysis, literature support, expert interpretation, and laboratory validation. In simulated studies, the ground truth (reference networks) is known and is used to assess the quality of the constructed networks. In enrichment analysis, the constructed networks are enriched against existing knowledge bases (STRING, hESC, hHEP, mDC, mESC) to find biological processes and pathways that are relevant to the underlying condition. In literature support, authors often search for evidence in the literature that supports their findings. In expert interpretation, experts in the field (who are often co-authors) interpret the results and provide insights based on their knowledge and expertise. In laboratory experiments, authors usually perform Chip-seq sequencing to detect the binding sites and confirm the interactions between genes.

Rather than reviewing individual models in isolation, this section instantiates the proposed taxonomy by comparatively analyzing representative GNN-based GRN inference methods within each architectural family. For each family, we first outline the shared modeling assumptions and inference strategy, followed by a synthesis of representative approaches, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and suitability across data regimes. This structure enables architectural and methodological trade-offs to be interpreted consistently, and directly informs the performance trends discussed in Section 5. This architectural criterion largely determines how information is propagated across the gene network and how robust the model is to noise, sparsity, and directionality. Based on these axes, we obtain the following major families, which structure the remainder of this section: (i) convolutional GNNs for GRN link prediction, (ii) attention-based GNNs, (iii) causality-aware GNN frameworks, (iv) subgraph-based and semi-supervised frameworks, (v) transformer-enhanced approaches, and (vi) hybrid and multi-component learning frameworks. Convolution-based GNNs for GRN link prediction

Convolution-based GNN architectures represent the earliest and most widely adopted formulation of GNN-driven GRN inference. These methods operate by propagating information through the gene interaction graph via neighborhood aggregation, producing node embeddings that are subsequently used to score potential regulatory links. The underlying graph may originate from time-series gene expression, co-expression similarity, curated transcription factor (TF)–gene priors, or integrated heterogeneous gene–cell relationships. These models typically formulate GRN inference as a binary link prediction task, where a decoder—often a dot product, cosine similarity, or neural scoring function—estimates the likelihood of a regulatory edge. In link prediction–based formulations, the objective is to estimate the probability of a regulatory interaction between a TF and a target gene. While these methods share the common principle of propagating regulatory signals through local graph neighborhoods, they differ substantially in graph construction strategies, supervision mechanisms, and robustness to sparsity. Understanding these differences is essential for interpreting their applicability across biological scenarios.

Representative models in this family differ primarily in how the input graph is constructed and how node representations are decoded. The framework proposed by Jereesh et al. [3] formulated GRN inference as a GAE problem applied to bulk RNA-seq data, systematically exploring convolution operators, aggregation strategies, and decoding functions. The major components of the pipeline included multi-layer convolutional networks, three types of decoders to obtain the link scores (dot product, neural network, and cosine decoder), and negative links were added to make it a binary classification task. The computational module of their approach consists of 3 core modules: the propagation, sampling, and pooling. The propagation operators are divided into a convolution operator and a skip connection. A single-layer feedforward neural network using the weight vector followed by LeakyReLU nonlinearity was used. Skip connections were used as they improved the performance on deeper models, where noise propagation and over-smoothing occurred. They claimed that the convolution operator is a crucial component of GNNs, and the choices made in selecting the appropriate operator can significantly impact the results depending on the specific task at hand. They used two specific convolution layers, namely ChebConv and Hypergraph, which demonstrated outstanding performance. The aggregation methods add and sum showed promise. Skip connections were most effective in deep networks with many layers. Increasing the number of layers had a positive impact on Hypergraph and ChebConv models but negatively affected SSGConv, ClusterGCN, and ChebConv models, likely due to oversmoothing issues and overfitting. Increasing the number of epochs initially improved performance but soon reached saturation, indicating the possibility of overfitting. The ideal choice of activation function for optimal performance depended on the specific GNN layer and the characteristics of the dataset in question. The dot product decoder consistently outperformed other decoders, while the neural network-based decoder warranted further investigation. Incorporating time series information into graph data generally reduced overall GNN performance, suggesting the need for alternative representations. This study did not explore learning configuration parameters, batch normalization, interlayer designs, or various skip connection variants, nor did it show the impact of these dimensions.

Similarly, GNNLink [6] employed a GCN-based encoder operating on cell-type–specific prior interaction graphs derived from scRNA-seq data. By integrating expression-driven node features with a supervised edge prediction objective, GNNLink achieved improved AUROC and AUPRC performance while reducing training cost, particularly when using two-layer architectures and moderate embedding dimensionality. The model further incorporated negative sampling to balance the sparse GRN ground truth, demonstrating the importance of feature variability filtering during preprocessing. Interestingly, using more layers returned diminishing results, potentially due to overfitting and smoothing issues. The best average AUROC and AUPRC scores were achieved when the representation dimension is set to 128, suggesting an optimal balance between representation capacity and complexity. DeepImpute [36] was used to impute zero entries in the raw scRNA-seq dataset. The GNNLink had a short running time, but it was limited in the optimism of the node features employed for reconstructing GRNs, particularly considering the sparsity of known ground truth networks.

Other convolution-based approaches introduced additional architectural innovations. DeepRIG [29] constructed weighted co-expression graphs via Spearman correlation and applied a GAE to reconstruct missing edges, enabling unsupervised or weakly-supervised inference. DeepRIG was also employed to infer the cell-type-specific GRNs from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and identified several hub TFs and marker genes of CD14 + monocytes and B cells, suggesting that DeepRIG can assist in determining cell-type-dependent cell-type-specific GRNs. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between each gene pair in every single cell was calculated to construct a weighted gene co-expression network. They built a prior regulatory graph of genes using the WGCN as prior knowledge. Similarly, GMFGRN [31] extended the convolution paradigm to heterogeneous gene–cell graphs, using GCN-based matrix factorization to generate embeddings for both entities before supervised TF–gene prediction. The model’s superiority lies in the ability to achieve more accurate performance compared with other computationally intensive methods while maintaining lower hardware costs. Self-supervised learning was performed to learn the embeddings of genes and cells by reconstructing the expression values between genes and cells. Specifically, a heterogeneous graph of genes and cells was first constructed, and then a GCN was utilized to perform matrix factorization (MF) and learn the embeddings of genes and cells. At the second stage, the gene embeddings of TF–gene pairs were integrated with the embeddings of cells associated with them. Subsequently, the integrated features were used as the input for the prediction of gene interactions by stacking multiple Fully Connected (FC) layers. Another representation in this direction is DGCGRN [32], which adapted convolution to directed graphs and enhanced low-degree nodes via a conditional variational autoencoder (VAE), while also incorporating sequence-derived features through the Bi-Directional Gated Recurrent Units (Bi-GRU) and physicochemical descriptors. Local augmentation strategy was also adopted for reducing the impact of low-degree nodes and generating additional enhanced features. A generative model, Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE), was used to learn the feature distribution of neighboring nodes on the condition of the central node’s feature distribution [37].

Across these models, convolution-based GNNs demonstrate strong performance in settings where (i) relational biological priors are available, (ii) expression-derived similarity structures are reliable, and (iii) regulatory directionality is either absent or inferred post-hoc. Their limitations emerge primarily in scenarios involving (a) highly sparse ground truth, (b) strong cellular heterogeneity, and (c) long-range regulatory dependencies that exceed local message-passing capacity. Compared within this family, supervised convolutional models generally demonstrate stronger predictive performance when reliable prior regulatory structures are available, while reconstruction-driven approaches exhibit greater flexibility in exploratory settings with limited prior knowledge. Methods incorporating directed graph modeling provide improved biological interpretability but often require additional preprocessing and parameter tuning. Overall, convolution-based approaches offer scalability and architectural simplicity; however, their reliance on local message passing can limit their ability to capture long-range regulatory dependencies in large gene networks. Consequently, convolution-based GNNs are most suitable for large-scale GRN inference tasks where computational efficiency is critical. Still, they may require complementary mechanisms to address sparsity and long-range regulatory effects.

Attention-based GNNs

Attention-based GNNs extend convolutional architectures by enabling adaptive weighting of regulatory interactions. Rather than treating all neighboring genes equally, these models dynamically learn the relative importance of candidate regulators, potentially improving robustness to noisy biological signals. By learning adaptive weighting schemes, attention-based GNNs offer enhanced discrimination of regulatory relationships, improved robustness to noisy edges, and the ability to model heterogeneous regulatory strength.

GENELink [24] represented a prominent example of this family, introducing a supervised link prediction framework based on GATs applied to scRNA-seq expression data combined with incomplete prior TF–gene interaction networks. GENELink consisted of two GAT layers followed by two channels of multilayer perceptron (MLPs); a self-attention mechanism was applied to the gene representations to obtain the attention coefficients. The model employed multi-head attention to generate expressive node embeddings and subsequently applied parallel MLPs to encode pairwise gene representations, followed by dot-product similarity scoring. GENELink demonstrated superior predictive accuracy across multiple cell types, particularly when evaluated against STRING [38], ChIP-seq [39], Loss of Function, and Gain of Function reference networks. The method further incorporated hard negative sampling to mitigate class imbalance and encourage the model to distinguish regulatory targets from nontargets that share the same TF. A random negative sampling strategy was employed to select negative samples, thereby preventing information leakage. GENELink was applied to a real metastatic scRNA-seq dataset of breast cancer to elucidate transcriptional and metabolic heterogeneity between primary tumors and lung metastases in three human patient-derived xenograft models. While some attention-based methods rely on supervised edge prediction guided by prior networks, others integrate attention mechanisms into graph reconstruction frameworks. These design choices influence both predictive accuracy and interpretability. For example, SCGAT [25], which combined fully connected feature extraction layers with multi-head dynamic attention to infer TF–gene regulatory links from scRNA-seq data. Unlike GENELink, SCGAT introduced a dynamic attention mechanism designed to enhance feature sensitivity and improve the search space of regulatory signals. By masking the attention matrix with known TF–target associations, the model constrains learning to biologically plausible regions of the interaction space. The framework additionally incorporated dropout and LeakyReLU activation to stabilize training and prevent probability saturation. Generalized Additive Model (GAM) [40] was utilized to calculate the variance among genes and the P-value of the variance. SCGAT demonstrated competitive performance on datasets with strong differential gene variability and reflected the growing trend of leveraging attention to capture fine-grained regulatory distinctions. More recently, Q-GAT [34] extended attention-based GRN inference by replacing conventional neurons with quadratic neurons, forming a dual-attention mechanism that simultaneously emphasized node importance and feature importance. This architectural modification yields improved robustness to adversarial perturbations in gene expression values and enhanced signal-to-noise (SNR) ratios during information propagation. The SNR analysis indicated that nonlinear aggregation of quadratic neurons can improve SNR. Their innovation was at the neuronal level from the perspective of neuronal diversity rather than the network structure. Systematic experiments showed that under different levels of adversarial noise, Q-GAT was superior to its competitors in terms of stability in constructing GRNs. Because of the nonlinearity, the use of quadratic operations enhanced the robustness of the constructed GRNs. The importance of each output node can be represented by its neighbors with the obtained attention coefficient. On the other hand, the learned quadratic mapping enjoyed the attention mechanism, which can signify the importance of the features of a given node. The graph quadratic pooling layer was designed to highlight the use of quadratic operations and analogize an attention-based pooling layer. Global mean pooling was employed to obtain the global graph representation. Compared with GAT, Q-GAT can additionally assign weights for features of nodes because of the attention mechanism in quadratic neurons, therefore facilitating the use of more feature information. Extensive robustness and interpretability analyses reveal that quadratic attention facilitated the utilization of richer feature subsets and provided greater resilience to synthetic noise injection. Q-GAT therefore addressed a key weakness of earlier attention-based models—sensitivity to noisy or perturbed measurements—making it particularly relevant for GRN reconstruction in low-coverage or batch-affected datasets.

Within this family, the principal distinction lies in how attention is utilized. Models incorporating multi-head attention can better capture heterogeneous regulatory influences, particularly in single-cell datasets with variable gene expression patterns. Compared with convolution-based methods, attention-driven architectures typically achieve improved discrimination of key regulators but may exhibit reduced scalability on extremely large networks. Collectively, attention-based approaches advance GRN inference by allowing models to (i) selectively prioritize regulatory interactions, (ii) capture asymmetric or cell-state–dependent influences, and (iii) improve robustness in noisy single-cell environments. However, the attention mechanisms introduced increased computational cost and may amplify sensitivity to hyperparameter selection. In addition, these methods remain constrained by reliance on prior networks for graph construction, and their performance depends on the availability and quality of curated TF–gene annotations. Attention mechanisms also inherit limitations of localized message passing and may struggle to capture long-range regulatory dependencies unless augmented with higher-order structures or transformer-based designs.

Causality-based GNNs

A growing subset of GRN inference methods integrates explicit causal reasoning into GNN architectures. These approaches are motivated by the recognition that gene regulatory interactions are inherently directional and mechanistic, and that correlation-based or purely structural methods may conflate downstream consequences with true regulatory drivers. Causality-based GNN frameworks therefore seek to embed causal principles—such as information flow, asymmetric dependencies, and intervention-like perturbation modeling—directly into the learning process. Compared with standard GNNs, these methods aim to disentangle correlation from regulation and generate networks that better reflect biological directionality and regulatory hierarchy.

Authors in [26] were the first to propose GCN based on the causal feature reconstruction method for inferring GRN. The method employed Transfer Entropy to quantify the loss of causal information in the GCN during neighbor aggregation. Subsequently, the node features’ representations were obtained through causal feature reconstruction, which provided more comprehensive node feature representation output from the GCN, and enhanced the accuracy of the downstream link prediction task. The proposed model consisted of three main components: a Gaussian-kernel Autoencoder module, which extracted the gene expression features and fed them to the GCN module, a GCN-based causal feature reconstruction module, and a link prediction module to complete the inference task. The convolution layer of a GCN calculated the interactions between each node and its neighboring nodes through neighbor aggregation. It then combined this information with the input features to generate a new representation for each node. Transfer Entropy was utilized to quantify the causality of each order of neighbor information aggregation, which allowed for a more comprehensive and accurate feature representation of the nodes in the causal reconstruction module. As drawbacks, the model was impaired by class imbalance and known regulatory pairs insufficiency. Another representative method in this category was GRINCD [30], which employed GraphSAGE [41] to learn embeddings from potential co-expression graphs and then applied causal discovery over learned representations. An ANM-based method was derived to calculate pairwise causal regulatory relationships for all the TF-TG and TF-TF pairs. GRINCD operated through two parallel pipelines—linear and nonlinear to capture diverse regulatory mechanisms. Beyond outperforming conventional correlation-based methods, GRINCD demonstrated the utility of embedding causal discovery within GNN-mediated workflows: it successfully identified regulatory shifts underlying disease progression, such as monotonic regulatory changes during the transition from inflammation to colorectal cancer. This ability to detect stage-dependent regulatory reprogramming underscored the value of causal frameworks for mechanistic biological insight. GENELink also predicted the causality features while the embeddings of gene pairs were concatenated and transformed by an external dense layer into two or three output probability nodes.

Compared with purely structural graph learning approaches, causality-guided models offer improved mechanistic insights into transcriptional regulation. However, their effectiveness depends strongly on the availability and reliability of prior causal information. Methods emphasizing temporal ordering or intervention-based regularization are generally more robust in dynamic biological systems but may suffer from reduced scalability and increased computational demands. However, they also introduce new challenges, including increased computational complexity and dependence on high-quality expression features or priors to anchor causal estimation. Moreover, causal GNNs may struggle in sparse or high-dropout single-cell datasets, where noise can obscure asymmetric dependencies. Despite these limitations, this emerging class of models broadens the methodological landscape by aligning GNN-based inference more closely with the mechanistic nature of gene regulation. Subgraph-based and semi-supervised methods

While most GNN-based GRN inference methods formulate reconstruction as a link prediction task operating directly on node pairs, an alternative line of work reframes the problem at the subgraph level, where regulatory relationships are inferred by classifying localized graph neighborhoods rather than individual edges. This formulation is motivated by the biological intuition that transcriptional regulation emerges from coordinated patterns of co-activity within modular gene communities, and that a regulatory interaction may only be distinguishable when contextualized by the topology of its surrounding network structure. Subgraph-based approaches therefore differ from convolutional and attention-based models not only in prediction granularity but also in how neighborhood information, negative samples, and unlabeled interactions are handled.

The GRGNN [7] framework is a primary representative of this category; it was the first approach to employ GNNs in GRN Inference. GRGNN began by constructing noisy regulatory skeletons based on Pearson correlation or mutual information, which acted as inductive scaffolds rather than fixed ground truth. From these scaffolds, the method extracted enclosed subgraphs centered on TF–gene pairs, labeling them as positive or negative depending on the presence or absence of a regulatory link. These subgraphs were then used to train graph classification models capable of identifying regulatory patterns embedded within local topological configurations. Unlike pairwise link prediction models that treated each gene pair independently, GRGNN leveraged relational structure and neighborhood co-regulation to strengthen discriminative signals, particularly in sparse or partially annotated networks. A further distinguishing feature of GRGNN was its semi-supervised learning strategy, designed to address the pervasive absence of comprehensive ground-truth regulatory annotations. GRGNN introduced an iterative refinement mechanism that selected increasingly reliable negative samples from unlabeled interactions, improving classifier robustness while reducing label noise. The framework additionally incorporated graph embedding methods to enrich node feature representations, enabling inductive inference across species and experimental conditions. This characteristic was notable, as it allowed GRGNN to generalize more effectively beyond the training distribution than methods tied to cell-type-specific or organism-specific priors.

Subgraph-based and semi-supervised approaches thus contribute a complementary direction in GNN-mediated GRN reconstruction. Their strengths lie in settings where (i) regulatory annotations are incomplete, (ii) co-regulatory structures are informative, and (iii) local graph context is critical for distinguishing true interactions. In comparison with global graph learning approaches, subgraph-based models demonstrate improved sensitivity to local regulatory motifs but often require extensive sampling and feature engineering. Semi-supervised variants attempt to balance predictive supervision with structural exploration, yet their performance remains sensitive to class imbalance and sparsity inherent in GRN datasets.

Transformer-enhanced approaches

Recent advances have explored the integration of transformer architectures with GNNs to capture long-range dependencies and complex regulatory patterns. Unlike conventional GNNs that rely solely on local message passing, transformers employ global self-attention, enabling them to capture complex cross-gene relationships that may not be readily accessible to neighborhood-based aggregation.

scTransNet is an early example of integrating pretrained transformer models with graph-based learning for GRN reconstruction. Authors in [23] combined gene representations learned from scBERT [42] with graph representations derived from the corresponding GRNs, creating a unified context-aware and knowledge-aware representation. This joint learning approach led to surpassing the accuracy of current state-of-the-art methods in supervised GRN inference. scBERT is used as the backbone because it is a successful pretrained model with the advantage of capturing long-distance dependency. They used Gene2vec [43] to produce gene embeddings, which could be viewed as relative embeddings that capture the semantic similarities between any two genes. The attention scores across cells are utilized from the model to select the most representative cells for the pooling of each gene representation. scBERT employed a matrix decomposition variant of the Transformer, known as Performer, to handle longer sequence lengths. GCN-based interaction graph encoders were utilized to learn gene features by leveraging the underlying structure of the gene interaction graph. The input gene representations from the BERT encoding layer, with the graph representation of each gene from GNN, were concatenated to get the final gene embedding. These final embeddings of pairwise genes were added into two channels with the same structure. Each channel was composed of MLPs to further encode representations to low-dimensional vectors, which served for downstream similarity measurement or causal inference between genes. To address the class imbalance, known TF gene pairs were split into training (2/3), and test (1/3) sets. Positive training samples were randomly selected from the known TF-gene pairs. Negative samples were generated using the following strategies: (i) unlabeled interactions: all unobserved TF–gene interactions outside the labeled files were considered negative instances. (ii) Hard negative sampling: to enhance model learning during training, they employed a uniformly random negative sampling strategy within the training set. (iii) Information leakage prevention: negative test samples were randomly selected from the remaining negative instances after generating the training and validation sets. The positive-to-negative sample ratio in each dataset was adjusted to reflect the network density. A more recent development, GT-GRN [44], integrated the strengths of both unsupervised inference methods and supervised learning frameworks. It combined outcomes from the available inference techniques to minimize method-specific biases, ultimately deriving a more realistic and biologically meaningful GRN. A natural language processing–based unsupervised approach was adopted that effectively captured and integrated information across networks. GT-GRN integrated topological features, which captured the structural properties of the network, gene expression values, which are crucial for identifying gene interactions, and the positional importance of genes. GT-GRN was advantageous in multi-networks integration by incorporating multiple networks inferred by different methods, harnessing their complementary strengths and improved the graph transformer frameworks, which offered a more effective encoding strategy by leveraging global attention mechanisms, enabling better capture of complex regulatory relationships in GRNs. A VAE [45] was utilized to encode gene expression data into compact, meaningful feature representations. It could efficiently capture complex expression dynamics and generate compact feature representations for further analysis and downstream tasks.

Compared with conventional GNN architectures, transformer-enhanced models enable global contextual modeling of gene interactions. However, their effectiveness is often constrained by data availability and increased computational complexity. While these models show promise in large-scale regulatory inference, their practical deployment remains limited by training cost and architectural tuning requirements [28, 44]. As single-cell atlases continue to expand, transformer-GNN hybrids are likely to become increasingly prominent, offering a scalable and expressive paradigm for decoding regulatory architectures.

Hybrid and multicomponent learning frameworks

Hybrid GRN inference frameworks combine multiple representation learning strategies, including graph convolution, attention, embedding integration, and probabilistic modeling. These hybrid systems typically combine structural information encoded by graph models with feature learning modules tailored to gene expression, sequence characteristics, or cross-species orthology. As a result, they broaden the methodological landscape of GRN inference by enabling tasks such as expression prediction, regulation-type classification, directed embedding learning, and integration of heterogeneous biological priors.

An illustrative example is provided by [4], who employed the graph models to predict gene expression levels in maize and related species. They aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of structural embedding methods in capturing the complexity of transcriptional regulation across multiple plant species, including Zea mays, Sorghum bicolor, and Oryza sativa. A major innovation of the work lay in the transformation of gene expression data into orthogroup-level representations, using PLAZA gene family-based orthogroups. This approach not only enabled cross-species comparisons but also facilitated dimensionality reduction and standardization across diverse datasets. By collapsing Arabidopsis iGRN nodes into orthogroups and mapping gene expression accordingly, a more generalized and biologically relevant framework was created for expression prediction. Two model architectures were tested: GAEs coupled with random forest regression, and Partial least squares models [46]. Within the GAE framework, several variants were explored, including shallow and deep graph convolutional layers and GraphSAGE encoders, each aiming to capture structural features of the regulatory network in the learned embeddings. These embeddings were then decoded and assessed based on reconstruction loss and predictive performance on unseen gene expression values. Across all scenarios, a key finding emerged: shallow GCNs consistently outperformed more complex models, such as deeper GCNs and GraphSAGE. This suggests that simpler architectures are more robust in generalizing across unseen genes and expression conditions. A more direct integration of hybrid learning components was seen in DeepFGRN [33], which unified convolutional, correlation-based, and generative modeling components to infer directed TF–gene regulatory interactions and to classify regulatory effects as activation, repression, or nonregulation. The correlation analysis module was designed to extract both the gene expression features of regulators and target genes and the correlation embedding between regulatory factors and target genes. The bidirectional node representation module was established based on a GAN with two generators. It could not only embed the FGRN (Fine Gene Regulatory Network) into a low-dimensional space, but also capture directed node embedding, including source neighbor and target neighbor embedding. The model consisted of Submodules: first, since there were no benchmark datasets for FGRN inference, they collected bulk gene expression data and prior regulatory associations with regulation types from databases. Then, the gene expression profiles of gene pairs were input into the correlation analysis module to obtain the correlation embedding vector, which contained both gene expression features of gene pairs and the correlation information between regulators and target genes. After that, the incomplete ground-truth GRN was sent to the node bidirectional representation module to learn the potential low-dimensional bidirectional representations of each gene pair. This module comprised two generators and one discriminator. Two generators were used to learn the source and target neighbor embeddings of each gene node, and one discriminator to distinguish whether the generated neighbor node was true or false. To confirm that calculating the correlation between regulator and target genes promoted FGRN inference, four models were used. expCNN: only a 1D CNN was used to extract the features of the gene expression data. expResNet: only a 1D ResNet was used to extract the features of gene expression data. Cnet: utilized to extract the correlation embedding between the regulators and target genes. expResNet+Cnet: a 1D ResNet and Cnet are jointly used. To demonstrate the significance of FGRN inference in human disease treatment, they identified potential biomarkers and drugs for breast cancer, liver cancer, lung cancer, and COVID-19. A third hybrid approach, DGCGRN [32], integrated directed graph convolution with representation augmentation via CVAEs, sequence-derived features, and iterative graph reweighting. Traditional GCNs struggled with directed regulatory networks, prompting the authors to introduce a directed convolutional operator and a local augmentation strategy that generated enhanced representations for low-degree nodes. Additionally, DGCGRN incorporated Bi-GRU to derive sequence features and physicochemical descriptors to capture regulatory signals beyond expression alone. A dynamic updating mechanism adjusted edge weights based on successive inference cycles, refining the regulatory graph throughout training. This multicomponent design enabled the model to integrate heterogeneous evidence sources, improving directed GRN reconstruction in both microbial and mammalian contexts. An additional hybrid approach is IGEGRNS [27], which embedded genes using GraphSAGE and enhanced regulatory prediction with Top-k pooling and stacked CNNs. The method first used GraphSAGE to learn low-dimensional embeddings that captured local neighborhood structure. Then it selected the most influential nodes via Top-k pooling and concatenated these embeddings with gene pair features. These features were the input to a CNN-based classifier to predict directed regulatory edges. Compared with the competing methods across multiple scRNA-seq datasets, IGEGRNS demonstrated improved AUROC and AUPR, highlighting the benefit of graph embedding and supervised learning for capturing complex regulatory relationships.

Within this category, methodological diversity leads to varied performance characteristics. Models integrating heterogeneous biological features often achieve improved predictive accuracy but may sacrifice interpretability and reproducibility. In contrast, simpler hybrid architectures provide better transparency but may struggle to capture complex regulatory hierarchies. However, their advantages come with increased architectural complexity and training overhead, and performance may depend sensitively on the alignment between module design and biological context. Nonetheless, hybrid frameworks underscore a fundamental trend: the future of GRN inference is likely to rely on multicomponent systems that integrate structural, functional, and generative modeling perspectives within unified graph-based architectures.

A cross-family comparison reveals clear trade-offs among the different architectural paradigms. Convolution-based models provide computational efficiency and scalability but are limited by local message passing. Attention-based methods improve the identification of key regulatory interactions but introduce higher computational cost and sensitivity to hyperparameters. Causality-based frameworks enhance biological interpretability by modeling directionality, although they depend heavily on prior knowledge and data quality. Subgraph-based approaches capture local regulatory patterns effectively but may struggle with scalability. Transformer-enhanced architectures enable modeling of long-range dependencies but require substantial computational resources. Hybrid frameworks offer the most flexibility by integrating multiple components, yet their complexity can hinder reproducibility. These differences highlight that the choice of GRN inference model should be guided by the characteristics of the dataset, including size, sparsity, and availability of prior biological knowledge.

Experimental evaluation and benchmarking of GRN inference methods

In this section, we present a systematic experimental evaluation of selected graph-based GRN inference methods under a unified benchmarking framework. Unlike previous sections that review existing studies, this section focuses on methods with publicly available implementations that were executed and evaluated by the authors. The objective is to provide a fair and reproducible comparison of model performance across both simulated bulk and single-cell RNA-seq datasets. We assess each method based on standard metrics, including AUROC and AUPR, under varying conditions of network size, sparsity, and data complexity. We generated 115 simulated datasets using known reference networks and then assessed the performance of the network inference methods based on the three metrics listed above. We used the curated human network in the TRRUST database [47] as the reference network and the GeneNetWeaver [48] as the software to generate DREAM5 datasets. Then Sergio [49] is used to extract the scRNA-seq datasets from the bulk extracted datasets. GeneNetWeaver has become a widely used tool to synthesize data from a given network for the purpose of evaluation [50, 51]. This software was also used to generate gold-standard data for DREAM4 [52] and DREAM5 [53] international competitions. Briefly, GeneNetWeaver uses ODEs and SDEs to synthesize data measured at different time points. After generating the datasets, we used the surveyed methods to construct the networks and then compared the constructed networks against the reference networks. For each constructed network, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve is generated by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate at different thresholds when comparing the edges of the constructed network with the reference network. The quality of each constructed network is assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). A constructed network has an AUROC of 1 if it is identical to the reference network. We assessed the performance of each method in three different scenarios. In the first scenario, we generated datasets with 200 samples and varying numbers of genes (20, 500, 1000,2000, and 3000). Each setting has 20 replicates. We also simulated dropouts (at 30%, 50%, and 70% sparsity) using weighted random sampling, in which low-expression values will have a higher chance of being dropped out. We used the surveyed methods to analyze the datasets and assess their performance by comparing the constructed networks against the ground truth (reference networks). In the second scenario, we generated 25 datasets with different levels of dropout/sparsity—30%, 50%, and 70%—for each of the different gene numbers and dropout levels. We then used the surveyed methods to analyze the datasets and assess their accuracy. In the third scenario, we increased the number of genes and samples and measured the running time of each method. First, we set the number of samples to 200 and increase the number of genes from 100, 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000. The analysis was performed on High Performance Computing provided by UAEU with 128GB of RAM and an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 2950X 16-Core Processor. This method does not converge at all for datasets with 100 genes or more.

Overall performance trends across scRNA-seq GRN inference methods

In this study, five single-cell GRN inference models were evaluated on simulated scRNA-seq datasets spanning increasing gene dimensionalities (100–3000 genes) and controlled dropout levels (30%, 50%, and 70%). Across all experimental settings, model performance was strongly influenced by both dropout severity and graph dimensionality, highlighting the inherent challenges of GRN reconstruction from sparse single-cell data. At lower gene counts (100–500 genes), most models achieved relatively high AUROC values, indicating effective discrimination between regulatory and nonregulatory interactions. However, AUPR values remained modest, reflecting the extreme sparsity of true regulatory edges even in small networks. The comparative evaluation of five single-cell GRN inference models—GENELINK, IGEGRN, GNNLINK, GMFGRN, and DeepRIG—reveals clear performance stratification driven by robustness to dropout and scalability with increasing gene dimensionality. However, it exhibited markedly different behaviors with respect to scalability and robustness to dropout. GENELINK consistently achieved the strongest performance across all gene sizes (100–3000 genes) and dropout levels, maintaining very high AUROC and AUPR values even under severe sparsity, indicating strong resilience to zero inflation and effective suppression of false positives. IGEGRN also demonstrated stable and competitive performance across all experimental settings; however, its nearly invariant AUROC and AUPR values across gene sizes and dropout levels reflect the use of five-fold cross-validation averages as adopted in the original implementation, which may mask variability induced by increasing network complexity. GNNLINK exhibited moderate but steadily improving performance as the number of genes increased, with AUROC and AUPR values rising notably at 2000 and 3000 genes, suggesting that its GNN architecture benefits from larger regulatory contexts but remains more sensitive to dropout than GENELINK. In contrast, GMFGRN exhibited performance close to random guessing across most settings, with AUROC and AUPR values fluctuating around 0.5, regardless of gene size or dropout level, even when trained for 20 000 iterations, as per the original paper, indicating limited suitability for sparse single-cell data. DeepRIG displayed a distinctive behavior characterized by very high AUROC values at smaller gene sizes, reflecting strong ranking ability, but substantially lower AUPR values across all configurations, particularly as gene dimensionality increased. This divergence highlights DeepRIG’s difficulty in maintaining precision in highly imbalanced regulatory spaces, a critical limitation for single-cell GRN inference. Overall, these results demonstrate that while all five models can capture regulatory signal to varying degrees, GENELINK and IGEGRN provide the most robust and reliable performance, GNNLINK shows scalability benefits at larger networks, and GMFGRN and DeepRIG struggle to balance sensitivity and precision under realistic single-cell sparsity conditions.

Overall performance trends across bulk RNA sequence GRN inference methods

The bulk RNA-seq benchmarking reveals substantial performance heterogeneity across models as network size increases from 100 to 3000 genes, highlighting clear differences in scalability, robustness, and sensitivity to class imbalance. Overall, Q-GAT and [3] Approach demonstrate the most consistent and scalable behavior across all gene sizes, achieving high AUROC and AUPR values even for large networks. In particular, Q-GAT maintains strong discrimination ability and precision at both small and large scales, with performance recovering at 3000 genes after a moderate decline at intermediate sizes, suggesting effective exploitation of global graph structure. Similarly, [3] Approach exhibits stable and exceptionally high performance for large networks (2000–3000 genes), with AUROC and AUPR values approaching 0.97 and very low variance across folds, indicating strong generalization and robustness to increasing dimensionality in the bulk setting.

In contrast, DGCGRN shows competitive AUROC values across gene sizes but exhibits substantial variability in AUPR, reflecting sensitivity to class imbalance and a tendency toward reduced precision as network sparsity increases. While DGCGRN performs well in ranking regulatory edges, its precision fluctuates notably, particularly at intermediate gene sizes, limiting its reliability for high-confidence edge discovery. GRGNN displays an opposite trend, achieving relatively higher AUPR values compared with its AUROC scores, which remain consistently low across all gene sizes. This behavior suggests that GRGNN favors precision over ranking performance, likely capturing a limited subset of high-confidence interactions while failing to globally discriminate regulatory structure. GRINCD performs close to random for large networks, with AUROC values converging toward 0.5 and rapidly diminishing AUPR as gene size increases, indicating poor scalability and limited applicability for large-scale bulk GRN inference. Finally, GMFGRN consistently underperforms across all bulk datasets, with AUROC values near random and extremely low AUPR values that worsen with network size, highlighting severe limitations in both ranking ability and precision under bulk RNA-seq conditions.

Taken together, these results indicate that model design plays a critical role in bulk GRN scalability, with graph-attention-based and robust graph-learning approaches outperforming methods that struggle with extreme class imbalance or dense regulatory search spaces. While several methods achieve reasonable performance on small networks, only Q-GAT and the [3] Approach demonstrate reliable accuracy and precision at genome-scale dimensions, underscoring their suitability for large-scale bulk RNA-seq GRN reconstruction. Reported validation on real biological datasets

To complement the simulation-based benchmarking framework, address its limitations, and provide practical insight, we present real-world case studies and a side-by-side comparison of representative GRN inference methods across experimentally validated datasets. Unlike simulated data, these datasets capture biological variability, measurement noise, and incomplete ground truth, providing a more realistic evaluation scenario. Seven cell types of scRNA-seq datasets provided by BEELINE [54] are mostly used with most of the models: (i) human embryonic stem cells (hESC); (ii) human mature hepatocytes (hHEP); (iii) mouse dendritic cells (mDC); (iv) mouse embryonic stem cells (mESC); (v) mouse hematopoietic stem cells with erythroid-lineage (mHSC-E); (vi) mouse hematopoietic stem cells with granulocyte-monocyte-lineage (mHSC-GM); (vii) mouse hematopoietic stem cells with lymphoid-lineage (mHSC-L). Three ground-truth networks are also mentioned in theory from functional interaction networks documented in the STRING database [38], nonspecific ChIP-seq [47], and cell-type-specific ChIP-seq [55]. We selected the results for the 1000 significantly most varying genes with all TFs. The results summarized in Tables 5 and 6 are extracted from the original publications and are not intended to constitute a unified benchmark comparison between methods. The reviewed studies employ different preprocessing pipelines, gene filtering strategies, gold-standard regulatory networks, train-test splits, and evaluation protocols. Consequently, numerical performance values should be interpreted as evidence of validation on experimentally derived biological datasets rather than direct measures of predictive superiority. The primary objective of this comparison is to assess the extent to which graph-based GRN inference methods have been evaluated beyond simulated data and to identify common trends in real-world applications. As shown in Table 5, clear performance differences emerge across models and datasets. Several graph-based methods, including GENELINK, GNNLINK, and IGEGRNS, reported strong performance across multiple real-world datasets, suggesting that graph representation learning can effectively capture regulatory patterns in diverse biological settings. In contrast, models relying primarily on global reconstruction or correlation-driven priors (e.g. DeepRIG and GMFGRN) exhibit more variable performance, particularly in datasets with limited cell numbers or increased heterogeneity. However, performance degradation is observed in immune-cell datasets characterized by increased regulatory heterogeneity and sparse expression patterns. These variations suggest that performance is strongly dataset-dependent, then model scalability and robustness to biological noise remain critical challenges in real-world GRN inference applications. GENELINK, GNNLINK, and IGEGRNS showed performance somehow consistent with the results we obtained in our in silico simulation datasets. In addition to single-cell benchmarks, several studies have evaluated their GRN inference methods on bulk transcriptomic datasets derived from well-characterized organisms such as E. coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae). These datasets typically involve larger regulatory networks with thousands of genes and provide an alternative validation scenario with reduced sparsity but increased network complexity. Table 6 demonstrates strong predictive performance on large-scale regulatory networks, achieving good performance in bacterial transcriptional bulk datasets. Nevertheless, differences in experimental design and evaluation protocols across studies limit the possibility of direct quantitative comparison. Overall, the comparison highlights that while graph architectures can effectively capture regulatory dependencies in real single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data, their robustness remains dataset-dependent. These findings further underscore the need to integrate both simulated and experimentally validated benchmarks when evaluating GRN inference frameworks.

A key observation from Tables 5 and 6 is that performance differences reported across studies are often smaller than the methodological and experimental differences between them. Dataset characteristics, network sparsity, gold-standard definitions, and preprocessing strategies may influence performance as strongly as architectural choices. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as definitive rankings of GRN inference methods. Instead, they demonstrate that graph learning approaches have been successfully applied across diverse biological systems while highlighting the need for standardized datasets, unified preprocessing pipelines, and common evaluation protocols for future benchmarking studies. It should be emphasized that the reported performances originate from independent studies employing different preprocessing strategies, gold-standard definitions, and evaluation criteria. Therefore, the comparison aims to provide qualitative insight into real-data validation trends rather than a unified benchmarking framework. Overall, evidence from real-data evaluations suggests that deep learning frameworks incorporating graph representation learning are capable of capturing complex regulatory patterns in single-cell systems. However, their performance remains sensitive to data quality, network size, and sparsity levels, indicating that further methodological advances are required to ensure robust deployment in large-scale biological studies.

Although several benchmarking frameworks employ ChIP-seq–derived regulatory interactions as ground-truth networks, it should be noted that TF binding evidence does not always correspond to functional gene regulation. Binding signals may reflect indirect interactions or context-specific regulatory activity that is not captured in transcriptomic measurements. Consequently, the biological validation of inferred GRNs remains challenging, particularly in large-scale single-cell studies where experimentally confirmed causal regulatory maps are scarce. This limitation highlights the need for future integration of perturbation-based datasets, such as CRISPR screening or gene knock-out experiments, to enable more rigorous biological validation of graph learning–based GRN inference models.

Challenges, future directions, and recommendations

Although the field of GRN inference using GNNs is rapidly growing, it is still relatively new. The application of these methods is not yet presented in many real-world-setting studies. This may come from the difficulty in dealing with technical limitations in the scRNA-seq platforms or the heterogeneity of single-cell data. The validation of the output networks and computational challenges also raises questions about their applicability. Here, we discuss such problems that need to be addressed for new GRN inference methods in the future.

Data quality and biological variability

Single-cell RNA sequencing data present unique challenges due to high dropout rates, batch effects, cellular heterogeneity, and stochastic gene expression. Unlike bulk transcriptomic data, scRNA-seq datasets are characterized by sparse expression matrices where >50% of entries are often zero or near zero [56]. These missing values can obscure true regulatory signals and negatively affect graph construction and message-passing operations [29, 56]. The reviewed methods addressed these challenges to varying degrees. DeepFGRN explicitly incorporated batch normalization strategies, whereas most methods assumed that batch effects had already been corrected during preprocessing. Similarly, GMFGRN attempted to model cellular heterogeneity through heterogeneous gene-cell graphs, while GENELINK demonstrated the ability to capture transcriptional heterogeneity in metastatic breast cancer datasets. However, the majority of methods still rely on preprocessing pipelines to mitigate technical variation rather than modeling these effects directly. Future research should focus on developing graph architectures that are inherently robust to dropout events, batch effects, and biological heterogeneity. Integrating uncertainty-aware message passing, probabilistic graph representations, and noise-resistant graph construction strategies may improve robustness in highly sparse single-cell datasets.

Temporal and dynamic regulatory modeling

A major limitation of current GRN inference methods is their dependence on pseudotime estimation to reconstruct dynamic regulatory processes. Because true temporal measurements are rarely available at single-cell resolution, most methods rely on inferred developmental trajectories to approximate temporal ordering [35]. However, most reviewed approaches assume linear developmental trajectories and struggle to model branching or cyclic cellular processes. Methods such as GNNLink and DeepRIG rely heavily on pseudotime information, whereas only a limited number of approaches attempt to capture more complex dynamic behaviors. This restriction limits their applicability to developmental systems characterized by cell fate bifurcations and heterogeneous differentiation pathways. Future work should investigate graph architectures capable of modeling branching trajectories, dynamic graph evolution, and temporal regulatory changes without requiring manually curated pseudotime information.

Ground-truth scarcity, incompleteness, and class imbalance

A central bottleneck across supervised/semi-supervised GRN methods is that ”gold standards” are typically incomplete, context-dependent, or available only for a few organisms/cell types. This creates biased training/evaluation where unknown edges are treated as negatives, inflating false negatives and distorting AUPR/AUROC comparisons. Many studies rely on uniform random negative sampling or ”unobserved = negative,” which can under-train difficult negatives and overestimate performance on easy ones. DeepRIG explicitly notes difficulty when no specific ground truth is available. while GRGNN highlights reliance on well-studied model species (E. coli/yeast) because they have curated regulatory edges. GNNLink, GENELink, and scTransNet treat unobserved TF–gene pairs as negatives (common but bias-prone). GT-GRN is motivated by the lack of true GRNs and reliance on inferred priors. This limitation can make models look worse (or better) depending on how negatives are constructed and how sparse the reference network is. Models can maintain AUROC while AUPR collapses when negatives dominate or are poorly sampled—exactly what we saw for some bulk methods. The reviewed methods address this challenge only partially. Most supervised approaches, including GENELink, GNNLink, scTransNet, and DeepRIG, still depend on curated regulatory interactions for training and evaluation, making their reported performance sensitive to the completeness of the reference network. Consequently, performance differences across studies may reflect variations in gold-standard quality as much as architectural improvements. Future research should focus on positive–unlabeled learning, weak supervision, and self-supervised pretraining strategies that reduce dependence on incomplete reference networks while enabling more robust regulatory discovery.

Sensitivity to network construction

Many methods start with a prior/skeleton graph from correlation, ChIP-seq, STRING, or heuristics. Performance and stability can strongly depend on how that prior is built (thresholds, database coverage, cell-type specificity). GRGNN depends on noisy starting skeletons (Pearson/MI), then ensembles. GRINCD builds a potential co-expression network first, then learns GraphSAGE embeddings, then ANM causality. DeepRIG uses WGCNA/co-expression as a prior graph (robust to noise, but may miss causal direction). GNNLink, GENELink, and scTransNet rely on incomplete prior networks (ChIP-seq, generic GRNs). Authors in [3] found that graph structure choices (static versus time series; augmented features) change performance. These issues limit portability across tissues/conditions and can cause ”prior-dominant” predictions. A key observation across the reviewed studies is that network construction often influences performance as strongly as the learning architecture itself. Methods relying heavily on co-expression or prior biological databases may benefit from existing knowledge but risk propagating biases and missing condition-specific regulation. Future work should investigate adaptive graph construction strategies capable of integrating prior knowledge while remaining responsive to context-specific regulatory signals.

Limited handling of directionality and regulatory

Many GRN models predict the existence of edges but not direction reliably (or not the sign/type activation versus repression). FGRN Inference [33] (typed edges) is still rare. DeepFGRN specifically targets direction + regulation type (activation/repression/nonregulation), acknowledging missing benchmarks for typed GRNs. DGCGRN focuses on directed convolution to better model direction. Many scRNA-seq link predictors often operate on TF→ gene candidate space but still evaluate as binary links. Biological interpretability and downstream applications such as perturbation prioritization, pathway modeling, and therapeutic target discovery require directionality and regulation type rather than simple adjacency prediction. Future graph-learning frameworks should therefore move beyond binary edge prediction toward signed and causal regulatory inference supported by perturbation and multi-omics evidence.

Scalability and computational complexity at large gene sets

Even when models are accurate, scaling to thousands of genes is hard because of neighborhood explosion, subgraph extraction cost, memory pressure, and training instability. [3] discusses sampling/pooling modules and neighbor explosion; restricts layers to avoid oversmoothing. GRGNN, Q-GAT rely on subgraph extraction/graph classification pipelines, which can be heavy depending on candidate edges and skeleton density. scTransNet leans on pretrained transformers and pooling for scalability, but this implies heavy infrastructure. Benchmark results can reflect compute limits as much as modeling quality and reproducibility becomes difficult. Importantly, scalability challenges differ across architectural families. Convolution-based methods generally scale more efficiently to large regulatory networks, whereas attention-based, transformer-enhanced, and subgraph-centric approaches often incur substantially higher memory and computational costs. Future research should focus on sparse attention mechanisms, graph sampling strategies, distributed training, and memory-efficient architectures that maintain biological accuracy while improving scalability.

Over-smoothing and performance trade-offs in GNNs

Deeper GNNs can hurt due to over-smoothing and noise propagation; many methods observe best results at shallow depth (two to three layers). GNNLink shows diminishing returns beyond two layers (overfitting/smoothing). [3] limits layers (two to five) and uses skip connections to mitigate over-smoothing; effects depend on the dataset. [4] observes shallow convolution outperforming deeper GNNs/GraphSAGE. This limitation constrains representational power for long-range regulatory effects and motivates transformer/global attention alternatives. The severity of over-smoothing varies across architectures. Convolution-based models are particularly susceptible when network depth increases, whereas attention-based methods partially mitigate this issue through adaptive neighborhood weighting. Recent transformer-enhanced architectures attempt to capture long-range dependencies without deep message passing; however, their computational requirements remain substantially higher. Balancing expressive power, scalability, and robustness therefore remains an open research challenge.

Preprocessing sensitivity

Results vary with preprocessing choices: highly variable genes, TF filtering, imputation, pseudotime availability, normalization, and dropout. For example, GNNLink uses HVG selection, tests imputation (DeepImpute), and reports impact. DeepRIG uses Scanpy/BEELINE pipelines, TF lists from databases, and strict filtering (genes expressed in ¿90% cells). GMFGRN notes that some datasets lack pseudotime; performance comparisons across static versus time-series depend on this. SCGAT relies heavily on variance tests/GAM-based filtering (no code, hard to reproduce). Without standardized preprocessing, model comparisons can become ”pipeline comparisons.” This observation also complicates benchmarking and reproducibility. Because preprocessing decisions are often dataset-specific and inconsistently reported, performance differences between methods may arise from pipeline variations rather than architectural innovations. Establishing standardized preprocessing protocols and benchmark datasets would therefore improve the reliability of future comparative evaluations.

Reproducibility and implementation availability

Reproducibility remains a significant challenge in graph-based GRN inference research. Although most recent studies provide publicly available source code, several reviewed methods either do not release implementations or require extensive dataset-specific preprocessing and environment configuration. As a result, reproducing published results often demands substantial technical effort beyond simply executing the reported models. The reviewed methods also vary considerably in software quality, documentation, dependency management, and computational requirements. Some approaches provide complete training pipelines and benchmark datasets, whereas others offer only partial implementations or lack sufficient instructions for replication. These inconsistencies complicate fair comparison across studies and hinder independent validation of reported findings. Future work should prioritize open-source development, standardized evaluation protocols, containerized software environments, and publicly accessible benchmark datasets. Improving reproducibility will not only facilitate method comparison but also accelerate the translation of graph-learning approaches into practical biological applications.

Multi-omics integration for GRN inference

Although most existing graph-based GRN inference methods reviewed in this study focus on transcriptomic measurements only, one major limitation is that transcriptomic data cannot distinguish between direct and indirect regulatory interactions, which inevitably introduces false positives into the inferred networks. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have enabled simultaneous profiling of multiple regulatory layers, including chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq), DNA methylation, histone modifications, and TF binding dynamics. Integrating such complementary molecular modalities provides a more comprehensive representation of cellular regulatory mechanisms and can substantially improve the accuracy and biological relevance of inferred regulatory networks. DeepTFni [57] addressed this issue by inferring GRNs using chromatin accessibility data, enabling the identification of direct regulatory interactions. It has been observed that multimodal data within the same cell provided new opportunities to reproduce cell types and gene regulation more comprehensively [58]. Several unsupervised learning methods are used to infer GRNs from multi-omics data. scMTNI [59] and SCENIC+ [60] first identified TF binding sites by performing motif analysis in accessible chromatin (ATAC peak) regions to construct initial TF–gene or TF–cis-regulatory element relationships. MTLRank [61] computed TF activity scores from ChIP-seq and scATAC-seq data to estimate the regulatory effects between each TF and gene in the cell, and combined TF activity with TF expression to predict RNA velocity using a multilayer neural network. However, unsupervised methods are affected by the curse of dimensionality when dealing with a large number of genes. Compared with unsupervised models, supervised models can better capture subtle differences between regulated and nonregulated relationships. In recent years, an increasing number of supervised methods for GRN inference have integrated unpaired scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data to infer trans-regulation. Those methods, including IReNA [62], SOMatic [63], UnpairReg [64], linked TFs to REs by motif matching and linked REs to TGs using the covariation of RE–TG or physical base pair distance. Emerging graph learning frameworks have begun to incorporate multi-omics data by constructing heterogeneous graphs or modality-specific embeddings, which are subsequently integrated via attention or fusion mechanisms. MultiCausGRN [65] is a link prediction framework that incorporates prior guided knowledge to the graph construction to infer GRNs from scRNA-seq and ATAC-seq data. Similarly, MultiGNN [16] is a supervised GNN link-prediction framework that integrates gene expression and chromatin accessibility and was evaluated on seven public multi-omics datasets using BEELINE-derived ground truths. Semi-supervised generative models have been proposed in this area; scMultiomeGRN [66] is specifically designed for the precise inference of GRNs through unique integration of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data. The scMultiomeGRN model initiated by constructing an initial attribute graph of the GRNs, using both scATAC-seq data and TF motif analysis to generate putative TF–gene networks from open chromatin regions.

Despite these promising developments, multi-omics GRN inference remains a relatively underexplored area in the current literature. Challenges include the limited availability of matched multi-modal datasets, increased computational complexity, and the need for robust strategies to reconcile heterogeneous noise distributions across modalities. Future research should therefore focus on designing scalable graph architectures capable of jointly modeling transcriptomic and epigenomic signals, as well as establishing standardized benchmarking frameworks for evaluating multi-omics regulatory network reconstruction. Compared with transcriptomics-only approaches reviewed in Section 4, these frameworks demonstrate the potential advantages of integrating complementary regulatory evidence, although challenges related to data alignment, scalability, and benchmark availability remain unresolved.

Generalization across modalities and data scales

Although GNN-based GRN inference methods have demonstrated strong performance on both bulk and scRNA-seq data, their ability to generalize across emerging data modalities—particularly spatial transcriptomics—remains underexplored. Most existing approaches are explicitly designed for transcriptomic assays, where input data are represented as gene expression matrices and regulatory relationships are inferred from co-expression patterns or prior interaction networks. Consequently, these models implicitly rely on characteristics specific to RNA-seq data, including sparsity, dropout effects, and TF–target assumptions.

Extending these methods to spatial transcriptomics introduces both opportunities and challenges. Spatial data provide additional structural information, such as physical proximity between cells and spatially localized gene expression patterns, which are not explicitly modeled in conventional GRN frameworks. While graph-based architectures are naturally suited to incorporate spatial relationships—by defining edges based on spatial neighborhoods or distance metrics—most current GRN inference models do not explicitly leverage this information. As a result, their direct application to spatial datasets may lead to suboptimal performance unless adapted to incorporate spatial priors or spatially aware graph constructions.

The robustness of GNN-based GRN inference methods is also strongly influenced by dataset size and resolution. In single-cell settings, increasing the number of cells generally improves stability by reducing stochastic noise and enhancing signal detection. However, many models do not scale efficiently to very large cell populations due to computational constraints. Conversely, at low cell counts, performance degrades due to increased sparsity and unreliable estimation of gene–gene relationships. Similarly, as the number of genes increases, the search space for regulatory interactions grows quadratically, leading to reduced precision and increased computational cost, particularly in highly imbalanced settings.

Overall, while graph-based architectures provide a flexible framework for modeling regulatory interactions, current GRN inference methods remain largely specialized for transcriptomic data and show limited robustness across different data scales and modalities. Future research should focus on developing unified frameworks that explicitly incorporate spatial information and maintain stable performance across varying dataset sizes and resolutions. Among the reviewed methodological families, graph-based architectures are particularly well suited for spatial transcriptomics because spatial proximity can be naturally encoded within graph structures. Nevertheless, substantial methodological development is still required before spatially resolved GRN inference becomes routine.

Conclusion

This survey systematically examined recent advances in GRN inference with a particular focus on graph-based deep learning models applied to both single-cell and bulk RNA-sequencing data. Across the reviewed methods, a clear transition is observed from traditional correlation- and information-theoretic approaches toward GNNs and attention-based architectures, which formulate GRN reconstruction as a link prediction or graph representation learning problem. By leveraging message passing, attention mechanisms, and learned node embeddings, these models demonstrate improved capacity to capture complex, nonlinear regulatory dependencies, which are difficult to model with classical techniques.

The comparative analysis highlights that no single modeling paradigm universally dominates across all data regimes. Supervised and semi-supervised GNN-based approaches generally achieve strong discriminative performance when reliable prior networks or curated labels are available, but their effectiveness diminishes when ground-truth information is sparse or incomplete. Methods relying on co-expression–derived priors or heuristic skeletons benefit from reduced search spaces but may inherit bias from these initial constructions. Conversely, models designed to improve robustness—through attention mechanisms, causal feature reconstruction, or bidirectional/directed embeddings—exhibit better stability and scalability, particularly in high-dimensional settings with thousands of genes. In single-cell scenarios, performance is further influenced by dropout, gene filtering, and imputation strategies, underscoring the sensitivity of current methods to preprocessing choices.

Overall, the surveyed works demonstrate that graph-based deep learning has become a powerful and flexible framework for GRN inference, capable of integrating diverse data modalities and advancing beyond purely correlation-driven models. Future progress will likely depend on reducing dependence on incomplete gold standards, improving robustness to sparsity and noise, integrating multi-omics single-cell evidence, and adopting globally attentive architectures to improve causal interpretability and robustness in complex biological systems. Establishing standardized benchmarks, transparent pipelines, and biologically grounded evaluation criteria will be critical for translating methodological advances into reliable regulatory insights and downstream biological discovery.
