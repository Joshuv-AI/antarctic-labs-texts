Abies grandis (Douglas ex. D. Don) Lindl. productivity in central European provenance trial reflects populations’ adaptation to seed source climate
Authors: Daniel J. Chmura, Jacek Banach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ongoing climatic changes and their future projections indicate that native conifer tree species would lose their suitable climatic niches in central Europe by the end of century. Maintaining productivity and carbon sequestration capacity in managed forests may require focusing on alternative tree species, including non-native ones. In this study we investigated productivity traits of seven grand fir (Abies grandis (Douglas ex. D. Don) Lindl.) provenances at two common garden testing sites in Poland to examine the extent at which variation could be attributed to climatic adaptation, and to the response to climatic transfer distances. Productivity per unit land area varied significantly between two sites and among tested provenances. The observed patterns of variation pointed to increasing performance of populations with milder climate and more favorable climatic moisture availability at their origin, indicating adaptation of local populations. Seed sources from Vancouver Island, the Olympic Peninsula, and the Cascades of Washington should be prioritized for planting in conditions of central Europe. The use of grand fir provenances in afforestation and reforestation programs must be closely aligned with climatic matching, especially with respect to moisture availability, for maximizing productivity and enhancing structural diversity of managed forests in central Europe in a changing climate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54972-5.

Introduction

Forests cover roughly a third of landmass in Europe1, thus they constitute an important component of biomass production and carbon cycle. They affect environmental processes at the landscape and cross-regional scales and are commercially important in many European countries. Ongoing climatic changes and projections of future climates indicate that conifer tree species, such as Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris L.) and Norway spruce (Picea abies L. (H.) Karst.), which are planted on a large scale in managed forests of central Europe, would likely lose their suitable climatic niches there by the end of this century2–5. The projections are less alarming for northern Europe, where existing populations may be able to accommodate climate change projected for the end of the century3,6, or where species may migrate to3. However, maintaining productivity and carbon sequestration capacity in managed forests in the longer term may require active management of genetic resources, including assisted migration, to support species adaptability7,8, and a focus on alternative tree species3,8–10. This last approach may include the use of non-native tree species with a documented history of introduction in Europe9, but requires knowledge of the extent of variation in adaptive traits residing within these species.

The interest in introducing North American trees to Europe originated from deforestation and the timber shortages during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The favorable results from those initial introductions, particularly with some conifers, gave the incentive for more detailed studies11. The grand fir (Abies grandis Dougl. ex D. Don Lindl.) was among the tree species introduced at that time, which has raised interest in planting in several countries. Subsequently, one of the concerted efforts to investigate the genetic variation among grand fir populations in terms of adaptability and productivity was the 1974–1976 provenance study coordinated by IUFRO (The International Union of Forest Research Organizations), with seeds collected from stands across the species’ native range in the Northwestern US and British Columbia12.

Here, we analyzed variability in growth (tree diameter at breast height (DBH) and height (H)) and productivity traits (basal area (BA) and volume index (Vi) per unit land area) among seven populations of grand fir from the IUFRO 1974–1976 seed collections, growing at two sites of this provenance experiment, established in 1981 in Poland—Kórnik and Krynica (see Methods and Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). Using the genetic cline analysis, which related the site-standardized Vi data to climatic variables at the origin, we analyzed populations’ climatic adaptation (see Methods and Table S1). Our aim was: i) to investigate growth and productivity differences among the tested grand fir populations, ii) to relate the observed variation to climatic conditions at the populations’ origin sites, and to the response of populations to climatic transfer in the experiment. Interquartile ranges for individual populations were much broader at the Kórnik site than in Krynica, implying that growth and productivity traits were more variable among individual plots in Kórnik (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S2). The overall pattern of variation in Vi among populations was similar at both sites. However, there were some differences in the relative performance of populations between sites, despite the insignificant population × site interaction term. Population no. 12020 from the High Southern Cascades in Oregon had the lowest productivity at both planting sites; yet, the other population from this region (no. 12016) did similarly poor in Kórnik, but relatively better in Krynica (Fig. 1). Population no. 12001 from the West Cascades in Washington was among the poorest in Kórnik, but second best after population no. 12040, in Krynica.

Model diagnostics indicated the good or moderate fits for the linear relationships which were statistically significant, although the high-leverage points were present in most of them (Cook’s distance ≥ 0.29). For variables involving frost-free period this points were population 12020 in Krynica and 12001 in Kórnik. After model diagnostics the relationship for MAT (mean annual temperature) was excluded, because the data were strongly bimodal (Supplementary Fig. S3). Parameter bootstrap validation further lead to the exclusion of linear model for CMD (climatic moisture deficit) with highly skewed slope estimate and 95% confidence intervals including zero (Supplementary Table S2, Fig. S4).

Model diagnostics and validation for non-linear relationships indicated that the quadratic parameter for MAP was not stable and close to zero (Supplementary Table S2, Fig. S5). Thus this relationship was not further investigated (Fig. 2). Although the remaining non-linear models were moderately stable, there was a strong intercorrelation among parameter estimates. It indicates that the exact interpretation of parameters may be problematic; however, the shape of the relationship can be properly interpreted. Because of that, the exact location of the climatic transfer distances for optimum productivity is not certain, but could be approximated as the optimum range based on the confidence intervals.

Among the grand fir populations examined at two planting sites, Vi increased with earlier beginning and later end of frost-free period at population origin (bFFP and eFFP, respectively), with warmer extreme minimum temperatures (EMT), longer frost-free period (FFP), more frost-free days (NFFD), and more degree-days above 5 °C (DD > 5), and lower reference evaporation (Eref) (Supplementary Fig. S3). Productivity at both planting sites peaked for populations originating from climates with MWMT between 15.9°C and 17.2°C , AHM between 10.8 and 15.9, and SHM between 69.6 and 84.7 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

The main aim of our study was to disentangle the extent to which productivity patterns of grand fir provenances could be attributed to climatic adaptation. The genetic cline analysis demonstrated clear relationships between provenance productivity and the climatic variables at their sites of origin, indicating adaptation of local populations. Overall, the observed patterns of variation at both planting sites indicated that populations with milder climate and more favorable climatic moisture availability at their origin performed better. This is perhaps associated with their greater plasticity, allowing them to better utilize site conditions for growth13.

Our study provides evidence that grand fir has favorable growth potential in central European conditions, confirming earlier observations from provenance trials in Germany and Austria14–17, and earlier experimental work throughout the years at the site in Krynica18,19. Accounting for the fact that our area-based stand productivity estimates were overestimated (see Methods), and that between-site differences in productivity could not be ascribed entirely to climate, particularly noteworthy is the performance in Krynica. The basal area and volume index values there highlight the high productivity of this species. In Kórnik, growth performance was more variable within provenances, but nonetheless confirmed that the species is able to grow satisfactorily, even under less favorable conditions. These findings underscore the ecological plasticity of grand fir, which is adapted to a wide range of climatic regimes across its native distribution range, extending from the humid coastal regions of British Columbia and Washington to the more continental sites in the Rocky Mountains20. Altogether, our results corroborate the notion that grand fir is a promising non-native conifer species for central European forestry21. Its capacity for high productivity, indicates that the species, when properly integrated into structurally diverse stands, could play an important role in maintaining or increasing timber yields and enhancing resistance and resilience of managed forests under changing climate conditions22.

Given that climate was not the only factor governing between-site variation in productivity, the results of our study point to the importance of climate—site interrelationships. The Krynica site, located at a higher elevation, represents cooler and moister conditions, with a more balanced hydrological regime compared to the lowland site in Kórnik. The productivity optima, identified for populations originating from the sites with the mean warmest month temperature (MWMT) around 16 °C and intermediate levels of annual climatic aridity (AHM), corresponded more closely with the environmental conditions prevailing in Krynica than in Kórnik. These conditions appear particularly favorable for grand fir growth and productivity, resulting in higher basal area and volume index values as well as greater stand density across most provenances. Although the relative variation between the least and most productive provenance was similar at two sites (62% in Kórnik vs. 65% in Krynica), the warmer and somewhat drier Kórnik site not only showed a limitation to the overall productivity, but also a greater variation of individual provenance performance, thereby reducing the significance of expressed genetic differences.

The observed patterns of variation mirror results from other European provenance experiments. In Germany, for example, Rau et al.16 found much greater differences in growth and productivity among test sites than among the groups of origin of grand fir. The greatest productivity advantage was observed at sites with fresh soils, even with a lower annual precipitation, compared to those prone to summer drought16. Similarly, Austrian trials reported by Liesebach et al.15 highlighted substantial site × provenance interactions and dependence of grand fir height growth performance on the site, and especially soil conditions. Together with our findings, this underscores that the successful use of grand fir provenances in central European forestry must be closely aligned with climatic matching, especially in terms of climatic moisture availability, for maximizing Abies grandis productivity.

Our analysis revealed pronounced differences in productivity among tested seed sources of grand fir, consistent with the broad ecological amplitude of the species across its native distribution range. Populations originating from the coastal and submontane regions of the Pacific Northwest consistently outperformed those from higher-elevation environments. In particular, provenances from Vancouver Island, the Olympic Peninsula, and the Eastern Cascades of Washington State exhibited superior productivity in Poland. In contrast, provenances from the High Eastern Cascades of Oregon were clearly unsuitable, with population no. 12020 showing the lowest productivity at both experimental sites, consistent with the assessment of this provenance in a similar experiment conducted in the Czech Republic23. Although population no. 12016 from the same region performed relatively better in Krynica than in Kórnik, its productivity still fell below that of coastal origins, underlining the limited potential of Oregon High Cascades provenances for central Europe. These findings are largely consistent with earlier reports from the Krynica site19,24,25, and from other European experiments23,26. From among seven provenances tested in our experiment, the four originating from Vancouver Island, Olympic Peninsula and Western Cascades (nos. 12040, 12001, 12003 and 12005) where among the best-performing from 65 populations tested at 18 sites in Germany, based on the weighted index involving survival, diameter, stem straightness and branch thickness16. The others from Eastern Cascades in Washington and Oregon (nos. 12007, 12016, and especially 12020) were poorer- performing16. Similarly, at the Czech sites, populations no. 12040 and 12003 were among the most productive, whereas population no. 12020 was average for tree volume at age 38 years, and population no. 12016 was among the least productive, although not significantly worse than the best, and did not demonstrate any health issues26,27.

Importantly, the performance of West Cascades provenance (no. 12001) in our study illustrated the significance of site matching. While its volume index was among the poorest in Kórnik (although with large variation), it ranked second only to Vancouver Island material in Krynica. This suggests that populations from this zone may be valuable for cooler, high-rainfall sites in central Europe, but less suitable for warmer and drier conditions. This population was among the best across the German trials, including both lowland and low-mountain sites16, and showed the highest tree volume in the Czech trial, which was located in only slightly cooler and moister conditions than in Kórnik26. These observations support the conclusion that no single provenance can be regarded as universally optimal, and that seed selection guidelines must account for local site conditions.

The results of this study may have implications for silvicultural practice and species selection in central Europe. The estimated productivity of grand fir under Polish conditions, underlines its potential as a valuable component of future forest stands, especially in regions with climates comparable to those of the Carpathian foothills, with abundant precipitation and balanced seasonal water availability. It may provide an alternative to Norway spruce, both from the silvicultural and wood-properties point of view28. The high growth efficiency of Abies grandis is attributed to its high water- and nutrient-use efficiency, high leaf biomass and shade tolerance, as well as its low requirement for growing space, and low tapering21. The species is known for its drought resistance21,29. However, an analysis of the suitability of 53 coniferous and deciduous species for reforestation in central Europe classifies it as having average resistance to drought and late frosts. Nevertheless, it is considered a valuable coniferous species30. Grand fir may also be an alternative to the other important non-native conifer in Europe – Douglas-fir; however, as shown from test sites throughout northern and western Germany, its height growth is more sensitive to climatic water balance, as well as soil moisture and fertility conditions than in Douglas-fir31. A comparison of 19 conifer species revealed that Abies grandis exhibits both high growth potential and high tolerance to extreme climatic conditions, as evidenced by its low sensitivity to spring frosts and minimal impact of summer drought on growth32. The presence of this species in a tree stand promotes the formation of easily decomposable litter, creating the higher-quality humus than that produced by other coniferous and deciduous species33. At both sites of our trial, A. grandis maintained a high diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi, comparable to that of Abies alba; therefore, this non-native fir species may also play an important ecological role34. Thus, incorporating grand fir into afforestation and reforestation programs could significantly contribute to timber supply and enhance the structural diversity and resilience of managed forests in a changing climate.

The silvicultural use of grand fir is, however, not without challenges from the forest health perspective. The risk for grand fir is significantly increased due to its susceptibility to fungal pathogens35. It is especially susceptible to Armillaria mellea, and the damages from this fungus were reported from Germany and Austria15,21. Another pathogenic species, Neonectria neomacrospora, was reported from numerous Abies species, including A. grandis, in Christmas tree plantations in Belgium, Denmark, Norway, Sweden and the United Kingdom36. However, we did not observe any of those calamities at our research sites. The results on grand fir resistance to windthrow are inconclusive and likely depend on the rooting depth associated with soil conditions15. The reports have also shown a tendency of grand fir for stem cracks, although without any relationship to the population origin, and likely associated with site dryness16. On the other hand, in habitats with high levels of soil water, the tendency for stem forking strongly increases21.

As indicated by our study, provenance selection is of paramount importance for the successful growth and productivity of grand fir. Based on our findings, seed sources from Vancouver Island, the Olympic Peninsula, and the Cascades of Washington should be prioritized for planting in central Europe, whereas the provenances from the High Eastern Cascades of Oregon were unsuitable. According to Konnert and Ruetz37 the isoenzyme variation among grand fir provenances allows identification of differences between the broadly defined regions of origin (e.g. Washington and Vancouver Island vs. southern Oregon). In light of the current study, this broad differentiation with the aid of these markers, or perhaps others, which would allow a higher resolution, might be sufficient to identify suitable seed sources. On the other hand, the climatic optima for productivity identified in our study for thermal and climatic moisture conditions should be considered when selecting seed origins within the broadly defined zones of suitable grand fir origins. The integration of climate matching tools with empirical trial data, may provide a robust basis for successful seed source selection and buffering central European forestry against climate risks.

While the present study provides robust insights into the performance and climatic adaptation of grand fir provenances, several limitations require attention. The analysis was restricted to two Polish sites, which differed in soil and climatic conditions, and early management history. This certainly constrains the generalizations of results across a wider range of central European environments. Furthermore, due to the absence of stem form factors in Kórnik, the absolute volume estimates were not calculated, and were only approximated with a volume index. The long-term resilience against pests, diseases, and extreme climatic events remains insufficiently tested. Future research should integrate information from other test sites with this species in Europe to encompass broader gradients of site conditions, and also incorporate genomic approaches to disentangle adaptive variation, and monitor long-term stability to refine recommendations for seed origin selection and adaptive forest management.

Methods

Research material

The research material for the current study were seven populations of grand fir, growing at two common-garden sites established in 1981 in two locations in Poland—Kórnik and Krynica (Supplementary Fig. S1). The seven tested provenances (Table 1) come from the IUFRO 1974–1976 A. grandis seed collections that were distributed to participating countries12. The seedlings were raised in the nursery in Rogów, and distributed to the partner institutions as 4-year-old plants. Both sites were established in a randomized complete block design with four replications. The seedlings were planted at 2 × 2 m spacing (2,500 trees ha-1) with 100 trees per provenance plot (except for three plots in Kórnik with 80 trees). At the site in Krynica, Norway spruce seedlings were inter-planted between grand fir seedlings to provide shelter from early and late frosts, and were cut down after five years; no shelter was used in Kórnik. Throughout the course of the study only dead and dying trees were removed at both sites. The four additional research sites with the same provenances were planted at the same time in Poland. Unfortunately, two of them failed due to the pests and pathogen infestation (Heterobasidion annosum; W. Buraczyk personal communication), and two others have failed for unknown reasons, so they were not available for investigation.

Measurements and analysis

At the tree age of 44 years in Kórnik (2020) and 45 years in Krynica (2021), the diameters at 1.3 m above the ground (DBH) and heights (H) of all living trees were measured, except for 16 trees with broken tops or severely leaning in Kórnik. For those trees, the heights were imputed with the two-parameter Naslund’s formula in the ‘lmfor’ R package38. For each tree, we calculated the basal area (BA; m2) and Volume index (Vi = DBH2 × H; m3). The BA and Vi values of individual trees were summed at the population plot level and expressed per ha basis. The Vi could not be used as stand productivity estimate in absolute numbers; however, no DBH stem form factor was available for calculation of individual tree volumes at the research site in Kórnik. Based on the stem form factors available for the Krynica site39, those numbers should be reduced on average by a factor of 0.4. However, since the Vi integrates all other investigated traits, we used it as a proxy for stand productivity in further analyses of the relationship between productivity and climatic variables.

Plot-level data for DBH and H, BA, and Vi (Supplementary Table S4) were subject to analysis using mixed-effect model with the effects of site, provenance, and provenance × site interaction as fixed terms, and the effect of block nested within the site as a random term. The Tukey–Kramer HSD test was performed to test for differences among populations across sites. Analyses were performed in the R statistical software (version 4.3.240) with the ‘R Studio’ development environment41, and JMP (19.0.3) statistical software.

Climatic data and growth-climate analysis

To investigate the interrelationship between productivity traits and climatic adaptation among the examined populations of grand fir, climatic data (20 climatic variables; Supplementary Table S1) were obtained from the ClimateNA_MAP (v7.42) for each sampled population for the period 1961–199042, and for the period 1981–2020 for the planting sites from the ClimateEU v.4.6343; however, in further analysis we did not take into consideration degree-days above and below 18°C (Supplementary Table S3) as they have no direct biological interpretation44.

We used the genetic cline analysis on site-standardized Vi data rather than the response function (reaction norm) or the climatic transfer function45 on absolute values for the following reasons. First, a reaction norm based on the data from only two sites would not provide a reliable function. Second, the climatic transfer distances based on individual population origins differed substantially between sites for most of climatic variables (e.g. see Supplementary Fig. S6), implying that fitting separate functions for each site would be necessary. However, because of the small number of tested populations, such equations would be less reliable. Furthermore, the differences in growth potential between sites are clearly visible (e.g. see Supplementary Fig. S6). Still, the patterns of variation among populations are similar across sites (see results), therefore we used the site-standardized Vi data, and pooled the data together for a common equation. We used a quadratic or linear (in the case of insignificant quadratic equation) function to fit the standardized Vi data against the climatic variables at the population origin.

For all models we assessed the usual diagnostics, such as plots of residuals vs. fitted values, QQ plots, leverage plots, and Cook’s distance. In addition, to assess the model stability and uncertainty, two validation procedures were applied: 1) leave-one-out (LOO) validation, where individual observations were iteratively removed, and the model was refitted to the remaining data to evaluate the influence of single data points on parameter estimates; and 2) parameter bootstrap to quantify the uncertainty of parameter estimates, where 1000 resamples with replacement were generated. Empirical distributions of parameter estimates were used to construct percentile-based confidence intervals. For model stability the parameter estimate range, the sign stability and CV was assessed.

For the models with significant quadratic terms, the climatic origin of optimum productivity was derived as the x-value for the maximum of the quadratic function. However, due to the limited sample, the exact location of this “optimum” may not be certain. Thus, the empirical distributions of parameter estimates were used to assess the uncertainty of the model optimum and to construct its (bootstrapped) percentile-based confidence intervals. Subsequently, the climatic range for optimum productivity corresponds to values of lower and upper 95% confidence intervals of the function’s maximum (Supplementary Table S3).
