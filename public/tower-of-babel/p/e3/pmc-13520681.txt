Using issues of water to CURE your teaching: STEM education is civic education
Authors: Davida S. Smyth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTToday's faculty are under enormous pressure, expected to balance teaching, research, and service as time and resources shrink, public trust in science erodes, and the return on university investment is being challenged. As the 2025 Carski Award recipient, I argue that a single pedagogical approach can simultaneously address and alleviate all these pressures. Drawing on Science Education for New Civic Engagements and Responsibilities (SENCER), Classroom Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs), and Project-Based Learning (PBL), I describe three practical tips that use issues of water to reorganize your microbiology courses that leverage living laboratories in your neighborhood, the civic issues of sanitation access, and student engagement and training in wastewater-based epidemiology. Each approach helps develop disciplinary knowledge, enhances students' communication skills, and hones technical competencies in high demand among public health authorities, environmental agencies, and beyond. Teaching this way generates authentic scientific data and broadens participation in research. Critically, it demonstrates the value and uniqueness of a university education.

EDITORIAL

WHY DOES THIS MATTER NOW?

Many faculty operate under an impossible timetable, 60% teaching, 20% research, and 20% service, which more accurately resembles 60:60:60. Such internal pressure is pitting faculty against external pressures that have grown more urgent. Public trust in science and scientific institutions is declining (1, 2). The value of a university education is under political and economic scrutiny (3). A critical workforce gap is emerging in our public health institutions and environmental science agencies, fields that urgently need personnel trained in cutting-edge environmental sampling, molecular surveillance, data analysis and interpretation, and effective communication of findings (4).

We start with your nearest pond, stream, or sewage treatment plant. The SENCER model can provide the framework, CUREs and PBL can provide the structure, and our local environmental issues and challenges provide the context (5–8). This combination will help us to produce students who deeply understand microbiology, prepare them for careers in industries struggling to fill positions, and provide them with an experience that shows science as something that can do things with and for their communities rather than being separate from them.

THE FRAMEWORK: CURES PLUS PBL PLUS SENCER

The American Association of Colleges and Universities (AAC&U) identifies high-impact practices (HIPs), including undergraduate research, community-based learning, ePortfolios, and collaborative projects and assignments, all of which share a common feature: students can apply their knowledge to real-world problems and take genuine responsibility for outcomes that extend beyond the course (9). A well-designed CURE will incorporate collaborative teamwork, feature authentic inquiry, and ask our students to write and reflect. It will develop community-facing products. A single course that combines such HIPs will engage and involve every student, each participating actively, regardless of access, experience, or prior connections, thereby removing competition for the experience.

Established in 2001, SENCER demonstrates that we can teach through complex, unsolved civic problems to basic science. Over 6,000 faculty have since redesigned their courses and student experiences using this approach, with disciplinary content unchanged but taught through a lens that makes the content relevant and consequential.

Today’s students care deeply about civic issues (10). When asked what concerns them, they identify homelessness, food insecurity, traffic, and education. As faculty, we identify antibiotic resistance, climate change, environmental contamination, and emerging pathogens. We need to consider the alignment of what concerns our students with what concerns us as faculty.

TIP 1: LIVING LABORATORIES IN YOUR NEIGHBORHOOD

Water is an ideal SENCER topic, simultaneously a scientific subject (microbiology, chemistry, and hydrology), a public health challenge (contamination, access, and wastewater treatment), and relatable for every student. One of the most accessible resources, sometimes underutilized in science education, is the environment all around us. Ponds, rivers, drainage channels, urban green spaces, Superfund sites, campus fountains, and water dispensers can all serve as living laboratories. Such dynamic understudied ecosystems are accessible to you with no cost.

You can combine community science projects, such as Riverkeeper, and local news reports, such as one describing sewage discharge into the local river, and have the students culture local river samples in a CURE course: civic data, current events, and laboratory results, all unified in a single research context. CURE projects can be built around characterizing microbial communities across sites with different levels of human impact, undeveloped land, residential neighborhoods, and Greenway trailheads, providing students with genuine unanswered research questions while contributing valuable data to networks such as REMNet and Tiny Earth (11, 12).

TIP 2: BETTER TOILETS AS A CIVIC SCIENCE CONTEXT

Sanitation is probably the most universally relevant and systematically under-examined topic in undergraduate science education. I have long taught sanitation issues in first-year seminars built around designing a better toilet, incorporating project-based design challenges, field trips to wastewater facilities, ePortfolios, and expert visits (13). The science is integrated throughout, including pathogen transmission, water treatment, the microbiome, and pharmaceutical contamination of sewage streams and waterways. Students encounter the content in a context that makes it clear why it matters. I have had my students interpret census data on households with and without flush toilets, and they have reached civic conclusions about structural inequity, depending on where you live. Readings on comparative cultural practices, including traditional sanitation practices such as the use of Lotahs, allow students to reckon with Western-centric assumptions and consider universal design across gender, disability, and religious practices. This type of integrated course design promotes retention, allowing the students to derive personally meaningful learning from the experience.

TIP 3: IT’S ONLY WASTE IF YOU DON’T USE IT

Wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) is institutionalized by the CDC and multiple state health departments as part of the U.S. national and local infectious disease surveillance infrastructure. Wastewater samples represent the cumulative output of an entire sewered population, from a household to a city, providing unbiased community-level data that individual clinical testing cannot match, with no appointment needed and fewer barriers to access, and sampling many more individuals per dollar spent. During the COVID-19 pandemic, we applied WBE to SARS-CoV-2 in New York City, monitoring 14 wastewater treatment plants serving 8.5 million people. Variants of concern and novel mutations absent from clinical databases were identified through targeted deep sequencing, and our team, composed of faculty, undergraduates, and graduate students, co-authored several high-impact papers (14). This is just one example of how students can make genuine intellectual contributions to a research study with real impact and consequences. The workforce implications are clear and direct. Large-scale surveillance efforts such as WBE require personnel capable of collecting samples, extracting nucleic acids, performing sequencing, interpreting the generated data, and communicating their findings in ways that public health officials and liaisons can understand. Through CUREs, students gain skills that make them competitive for positions at water authorities, health departments, and environmental monitoring firms that actively seek this training. The targets for WBE continue to expand to include influenza, norovirus, Giardia, and antibiotic resistance determinants; all of these can be incorporated into CURE-style courses. With well-designed approaches aligned with relevant stakeholders, student-generated data are real, usable surveillance data with genuine public health impact and value.

SCIENCE COMMUNICATION AND BROADER IMPACTS

Teaching through civic issues lends itself naturally to the development of scientific communication skills, not as an add-on but through the work itself. In the “Getting Friendly with Bacteria” assignment, students combine their scientific ePortfolio projects with a creative product, such as a sculpture, poem, song, or fermented food (15). Such creative works can be incorporated into public art exhibits, generating the kind of community-facing impact that the community, our institutions, and funding agencies value and reward. We can also develop activities that help our students evaluate misinformation and improve their ability to distinguish fabricated news from credible science. In one such activity, students evaluate a fabricated news headline, voting anonymously on whether it is true, then discuss in groups. The follow-up in-class discussion, in which students share what changed their minds and why, helps students develop the reasoning habits that promote critical consumption of science news (16). By understanding how social media can amplify false health claims, we build the critical-thinking skills our graduates need to be effective in dealing with the rapidly changing information environment. Our students become both critically thinking scientists and effective science communicators.

CONCLUSION: WASTE NOTHING

SENCER-aligned CUREs operate at three levels. At level 1, faculty teach in ways that generate real data, blurring the boundary between the classroom and research. Students make observations that drive hypotheses, their projects generate preliminary data, and the students become our research collaborators, as our NYC wastewater surveillance work demonstrated. At level 2, graduates who have done real science on real problems are prepared for careers in public health that employers need to fill. At level 3, to build public trust in science, students who have investigated their own water and water bodies can engage with communities whose sanitation needs differ from their own. Students who learn to evaluate misinformation view science differently; they typically see it as a public good, a means of civic engagement, and a responsibility. This way of viewing science cannot be transmitted by lecturing alone: students must live it. Living laboratories are everywhere, all around you. The workforce needs are well-documented. Our students are asking us why science matters, and we as faculty have the answer. STEM education is civic education. It is workforce development and a demonstration of the value and uniqueness of a university education. We have what we need to get it done. Waste nothing.
