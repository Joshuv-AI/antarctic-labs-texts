Data mining approach to predicting of death in patients with COVID-19
Authors: Hojjat Sayyadi, Saiyad Bastaminejad, Alireza Vasiee, Aliashraf Mozafari, Shahab Falahi, Yousef Veisani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Despite the World Health Organization’s announcement that the coronavirus pandemic had ended in 2020, the nature of the disease remains unclear, and there are no proven treatments, including effective drugs, to treat and prevent patient death. Therefore, this study was designed to predict the risk of death in hospitalized COVID-19 patients using modern statistical modeling and machine learning techniques.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This cross-sectional study was conducted on data from the COVID-19 disease registration program in 2024. Various machine learning algorithms were used to analyze the data. The performance of the models used was evaluated by determining accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC). The final model was used to determine the most important predictors.RESULTS:The area under the ROC curve for the three models (LR, NN, and NB) is used to compare their performance. The LR model has the highest accuracy (AUC = 95.7). Accordingly, the most important variables predicting death in patients were WBC, BUN, age, and glucose levels. As a result, the model accurately predicted the risk of death in patients by 77.17%, with an accuracy of 95% and a sensitivity of 88.02. In addition, the results of the model test showed that the model can predict the risk of death by 94.54% and the probability of survival by 93.81%.CONCLUSION:Based on the results, the most important variables predicting death from COVID-19 were WBC, BUN, age, and glucose levels in patients, so that the model was able to predict the risk of death in hospitalized patients with very high accuracy.

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2 causes COVID-19, a contagious and zoonotic infectious disease that causes serious respiratory system damage, pneumonia, and, in some cases, death. Today, the number of cases and deaths is increasing worldwide, and Iran is among the top 15 countries in terms of the prevalence of this disease.[123] According to information published by the World Health Organization (WHO), as of February 1, 2024, the number of cases of corona disease has reached 461,582,702 people, with the number of deaths from this disease being 929,975,6.929; in Iran, there were 7,625,812 cases and 146,767 deaths from this disease during this period.[4] Despite the World Health Organization’s declaration of the end of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the nature of the disease remains unclear, and there is a lack of proven treatments, including effective drugs to treat and prevent patient death. Therefore, identifying risk factors that increase the risk of patient death can help triage and prioritize patients and better manage the treatment process.[5]

Previous studies have used machine learning models in COVID-19 patients, including to predict the risk of intensive care admission.[6] Another study used machine learning to determine disease severity.[7] Cao et al.[8] used a machine learning model with an accuracy of over 92% to predict the risk of mortality in hospitalized patients. A similar study by Booth (2021)[9] used a machine learning model with an accuracy of 90% to predict the risk of death. In previous studies, the variables of age, underlying disease, immunosuppression, lung injury, and respiratory failure have been effective in predicting the risk of death.[10] The Chen study identified body temperature, level of consciousness, and chest tightness/chest pain as the main risk factors for increased risk of death.[11] In the Al Sadig et al.[12] study, the variables of age, blood oxygen saturation, and respiratory intubation played a significant role in patient mortality.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify the variables affecting the mortality of hospitalized patients as a result of COVID-19 through the use of modern statistical modeling and machine learning techniques. In addition, the study aimed to identify the factors affecting the increased risk of death in patients using the most efficient model, which was then evaluated and presented for preventive measures.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted cross-sectionally on data from the COVID-19 Patient Registration Program. The aim of this study was to predict the risk of death in patients using machine learning methods. The study was conducted in 2019–2021 with 2441 patient data.

Study participants and sampling

The “Coronavirus Registry Project Questionnaire” served as the instrument in this study, gathering comprehensive patient data such as demographics, medical and clinical histories, serological measurements from laboratory tests, and radiological findings. The number of variables examined in this study was 92, of which 27 were qualitative data and 65 were quantitative variables. Hospitalized patients with a definitive diagnosis of COVID-19 met the inclusion criteria, while pregnant women and COVID-19 patients with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome met the exclusion criteria and were not eligible for the study.

Data collection tool and technique

After a preliminary examination of the data for duplication, missing values, and high-stability values, 57 variables were transferred to RapidMiner software for main preprocessing and cleaning. The selected data were divided into two groups of 30% (test) and 70% (test) and were used as the training model in the model. In addition, using the SMOTE upsampling technique, data balancing was performed to increase comparability and better learning of the algorithms in the two groups [Figure 1].

The data mining process for prediction death in COVID 2019 patients

Data mining techniques the data mining models in this study were logistic regression (LR), generalized linear models (GLM), deep learning (DL), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), artificial neural network (ANN), naive Bayes (NB), and gradient boosting trees (GBT). The next stage involved selecting a portion of the data and training the model to enhance its robustness.

After the initial preprocessing stage, we fitted machine learning models to the data based on the clinical response and then evaluated and compared them using the model evaluation indices of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Fisher’s F-measure parameter, and area under the curve (AUC) [Figure 2].

Performance plots of different machine learning algorithms

Based on Java programming, RapidMiner serves as a data science platform for educational analysis and research in data mining and artificial intelligence. Statistical tools and a machine learning toolbox containing several predictive artificial intelligence algorithms equip this program for both supervised and unsupervised learning purposes. The ethical considerations in this study involved obtaining the ethics code from Ilam University of Medical Sciences (IR.MEDILAM.REC.1401.163), obtaining written informed consent from the research participants, ensuring confidentiality of participants’ information, and adhering to ethical principles in collecting library, research, and human information as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis method

In this study, dispersion indices (mean ± standard deviation) were used for quantitative variables, and descriptive indices of frequency and relative frequency were used for qualitative variables. STATA 11 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA) software was used for descriptive and exploratory data analysis. RapidMiner Studio 9.10 was also used for machine learning analyses. In this study, the Windows 10 version of this software with an Intel processor (Intel I Core I i7-5500U CPU @ 2.40 GHz, 2 core(s), and 4 logical processor(s)) was used.[13]

Results

Of the 2441 patients referred to the medical center, 1336 (54.7%) were male and the rest were female. The mean and standard deviation of the participants’ ages were 56.5 and 17.3 years, respectively. Performance evaluation of machine learning models to predict the risk of death in patients infected and hospitalized with COVID-19 showed that the NB model had the highest sensitivity among the models. The RF model had the highest specificity among the models. The LR model exhibited the highest accuracy and area under the Rockwell curve. A comparison of the F-measure values revealed that the RF model had the highest value. Considering the performance evaluation values, the LR model has the highest balance between sensitivity and specificity among the models (specificity = 95.5, sensitivity = 83.0). It also had the highest accuracy among the models (accuracy = 93.7), so the LR model is selected as the preferred model [Table 2].

Comparison of the performance evaluation of the models using the area under the ROC curve in the three models, LR, NN, and NB, shows that the LR model has the highest accuracy (AUC = 95.7); therefore, it is the preferred model in predicting the risk of death in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 [Figure 3].

The LR model was chosen as the model in this study, and the 10 variables with the highest weight in predicting the risk of death in hospitalized patients with COVID were plt, albumin, myalgia, BMI, mechanical intubation, phosphorus, calcium, red blood cell count, number of days in ICU, and nausea [Figure 4].

Attribute weights of LR model

In this study, after the LR model was finalized, the simulator was used to predict the risk of death by determining and changing the input variables. By selecting the final variables based on the LR model, the most important variables predicting death were WBC, BUN, age, and glucose levels in patients, so that the model was able to correctly predict the risk of death in patients by 77.17% with an accuracy of 95% and a sensitivity of 88.02 [Figure 5].

Important factors to prediction of probability of death by LR model in patients

As part of the same study, the 10 variables that had the most weight in the LR model—plt, albumin, myalgia, BMI, mechanical intubation, phosphorus, calcium, red blood cell count, number of days in ICU, and nausea—were used to test the model and also figure out how likely it was that the patient would survive. Based on this, the most important supportive variable predicting patient survival was the red blood cell count, so that the model was able to correctly predict the probability of patient survival by 0.94% with an accuracy of 94.54% and a sensitivity of 98.22% [Figure 6].

Discussion

The present study was designed to predict the risk of death in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 using modern statistical modeling and machine learning techniques. This study demonstrated the highest accuracy of the LR model (AUC = 95.7), leading to its selection as the preferred model for predicting the risk of death in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Zhao et al.[6] designed and developed a risk classification model to predict the mortality of patients in the intensive care unit (ICU), which accurately predicted the risk of ICU admission and patient death by 74% and 83%, respectively. Vaid et al.[7] used machine learning capabilities to determine the severity of the disease and the risk of mortality of hospitalized patients (AUC-ROCs = 86%). Cao et al.[8] proposed a model that accurately predicted the mortality risks of hospitalized patients (accuracy = 92%). In another study, researchers designed the model on three groups of patients with COVID-19, pneumonia, and patients at risk of respiratory intubation, with AUC values of 91, 82, and 87 percent.[14] Another study examined the mortality risks of patients hospitalized in intensive care units, estimating sensitivity, specificity, and AUC to be 91, 91, and 93 percent, respectively.[11] The LR model identified mechanical intubation, serum albumin, number of days in the ICU, plt, and anemia as the most significant variables in predicting the risk of death in hospitalized patients with COVID. In the study of Agieb et al.,[15] the variables of dyspnea, recent travel history, diffuse lung lesion, and pulmonary failure had the highest weight in the hospitalization of patients, respectively. In a different study, Al Sadig et al.[12] discovered that factors such as age, blood oxygen saturation, and respiratory intubation significantly contributed to patient mortality. In the study by Ma et al.,[16] factors such as body mass index, age, history of immunosuppression and smoking, and blood oxygen saturation were linked to high weight, digestive symptoms, dizziness and headache, gender, and geographical characteristics (place of residence) associated with lower weight. In Barda’s study, they developed a mortality risk prediction model for COVID-19 patients, which achieved a sensitivity of 88%.[17]

The researcher in this study performed the risk of death prediction using the final LR model, which was based on the results of the most important variables predicting death: WBC, BUN, age, and glucose levels in patients. This model correctly predicted 17.77% of the risk of death in patients, with 95% accuracy and 88.02% sensitivity. Researchers have conducted similar studies using machine learning to investigate the risk of death in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Booth’s study revealed that age and underlying diseases played a high role, while digestive symptoms, occupation, gender, and dizziness and headache played a low role.[9] Parchure et al.[18] finally found that the variables of history of immunosuppressive diseases and age played the most important role in the mortality of hospitalized patients. Other studies, including Zhao et al.,[6] Vaid et al.,[7] and Yadaw et al. (2020),[10] have reported age as an important predictor of death. Several studies introduced underlying disease and oxygen saturation as important predictors of death in hospitalized COVID-19 patients.[671014] Other studies also identified the history of immunosuppressive drugs,[8] the time from exposure to onset of symptoms,[11] and lung injury and respiratory failure.[10]

Strengths and limitations

The current study, using a relatively large data set, offers a time-saving approach to identify the key factors influencing the mortality of COVID-19-infected and hospitalized patients. The dataset’s single-organization nature has posed challenges to the results’ generalizability. The study removed some incomplete information records and missing essential fields from the clinical records, focusing on those records with more than 30% incomplete fields. The retrospective nature of the dataset led to a lack of integrity and low information quality in some of the selected information records.

Conclusion

This study showed that the LR model had the highest accuracy (AUC = 95.7) for predicting the risk of death in hospitalized patients with COVID-19. Based on the results, the most important variables predicting death in COVID-19 patients were WBC, BUN, age, and glucose levels in patients, so that the model was able to predict the risk of death in hospitalized patients with very high accuracy.
