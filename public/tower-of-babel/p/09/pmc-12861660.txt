Rare Case of a Complex Coronary Triad Repaired via Axillary Thoracotomy in an Infant
Authors: Tue Minh Vo, Dung Duc Nguyen, Hung Xuan Nguyen, Phuoc Dang Nguyen, Duy Tran Duy Dinh, Chau Dang, Tam Tran, Vinh Duc An Bui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery aneurysm (CAA) involving both the right coronary artery (RCA) and sinus node artery (SNA), with coronary artery fistulas draining into right atrium, is exceedingly rare, previously reported only in adults.Case SummaryA 4-month-old female infant had abnormal right atrial flow on echocardiography. Computed tomography revealed giant RCA and SNA aneurysms, with dual fistulas into the right atrium. Surgical repair via right axillary thoracotomy included SNA occlusion testing, confirming sinus node function, and allowing ligation. Postoperative sinus node dysfunction resolved spontaneously, and the patient was discharged on day 6. One month later, she remained in sinus rhythm, with no residual fistula and favorable RCA aneurysm regression.DiscussionThis triad is exceptionally rare in infants. Early surgery was indicated owing to aneurysm size and risk of shunt-related complications. Right axillary thoracotomy proved safe and effective.Take-Home MessagesCoronary artery fistula with giant aneurysms justifies early surgery. Axillary thoracotomy safely addresses complex right coronary anomalies in infants.Key words: coronary artery aneurysm, coronary artery fistula, axillary thoracotomy, intraoperative occlusion testing, right coronary artery aneurysm, sinus node artery aneurysm

Case Presentation

A 4-month-old female infant presented to a local hospital with a cough and low-grade fever lasting for 3 days. Fever remitted with antipyretics. She was hemodynamically stable, however bilateral rhonchi and an abnormal cardiac murmur were noted at the pulmonary valve area. Clinical examination showed no respiratory distress or heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed abnormal flow into the right atrium (RA), leading to a provisional diagnosis of atrial septal defect or anomalous pulmonary venous return. She was referred to our center for further evaluation.

Past Medical History

The patient was born full-term via uncomplicated vaginal delivery. No abnormality was noted during the perinatal period. Growth and development were appropriate for her age. All vaccinations were up to date. Family history was negative for cardiac or vascular anomalies.

Differential Diagnosis

Abnormal left-to-right shunt into the RA in this 4-month-old infant prompted consideration of common to rare causes: atrial septal defect, partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, sinus venosus defect, and coronary artery fistula (CAF).

Investigations

Echocardiography showed a markedly dilated right coronary artery (RCA) ostium measuring 6.8 mm in diameter. A saccular aneurysm (8.2 mm) arose from the proximal RCA and communicated with the RA. The RA was not dilated, and the left ventricular ejection fraction was 64%. To clarify the coronary anatomy, a 512-slice contrast-enhanced cardiac computed tomography scan was performed using three-dimensional reconstruction with maximum intensity projection, multiplanar reconstruction, and volume-rendered technique (Figure 1). The RCA ostial diameter was measured at 5.5 mm, a more accurate and objective assessment given the modality's higher spatial resolution. A 7-mm sinus node artery (SNA) aneurysm coursed along the posterior wall of the RA and the interatrial septum. The vessel was confidently identified as the SNA based on its proximal RCA origin and characteristic anatomical trajectory.1 The SNA drained into the RA through 2 distinct fistulas, measuring 2.0 and 2.5 mm. In our case, both RCA and SNA aneurysms were classified as giant coronary artery aneurysms (CAA);2 the 2 fistulas were classified as small and medium-sized.3 Given the finding of the coronary aneurysms, a differential diagnosis of Kawasaki disease (KD) was considered, however no suggestive clinical findings were detected. Blood test revealed hemoglobin of 11.1 g/dL, platelet count of 424,000 platelets/mm3, and white blood cell count of 7,700 cells/mm3. Serum testing revealed C-reactive protein of 0.574 mg/dL and alanine aminotransferase of 22.6 U/L. No white blood cells were found on urinalysis. Therefore, KD was unlikely to be the etiology in this case.

Management

The patient was discussed within the multidisciplinary heart team. Given the complex anatomy and potential progression of the aneurysms, surgical correction via right axillary thoracotomy was chosen to optimize exposure while minimizing surgical trauma in this small infant.

The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position (Figure 2). The anterior and posterior axillary lines and incision were marked, and the fourth intercostal space was identified. A vertical skin incision was made along the midaxillary line. Dissection was performed through the fourth intercostal space.

Two retractors were placed to provide exposure. The lung was retracted posteriorly, and the pericardium was incised approximately 2 cm anterior to the phrenic nerve. Pericardial traction sutures were placed to enhance visualization. The ascending aorta, superior vena cava, RA, and left atrium were clearly visualized (Figure 2). The SNA was identified and carefully dissected at its origin. A double-loop rubber band was encircled around the proximal SNA, and SNA occlusion testing was subsequently performed. Electrocardiogram showed persistent sinus rhythm during SNA occlusion (heart rate: 94 beats/min after 2 minutes, 107 beats/min at 5 minutes). We proceeded with proximal SNA ligation, after which the heart rate stabilized at 112 beats/min. A temporary epicardial pacing lead was placed as a precaution.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was extubated 6 hours after surgery and had an uneventful postoperative course. She experienced transient postoperative sinus node dysfunction on postoperative day 1. Electrocardiogram showed loss of P waves, with a heart rate of 109 beats/min. By postoperative day 2, P waves reappeared, and the heart rate increased to 117 beats/min. She was discharged on postoperative day 6. At the 1-month follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic with sinus rhythm (heart rate: 124 beats/min); echocardiography showed no residual fistula, no chamber dilation, and reduced RCA diameter of 4 mm (Figure 3).

Discussion

This case presents to our knowledge the youngest reported patient with the rare triad of a RCA aneurysm, SNA aneurysm, and dual coronary fistulas draining into the RA. Prior reports are very limited and exclusively involve adult patients.4 CAF is rare, with an estimated prevalence of 0.002% in the general population. Among these, multiple fistulas are reported in approximately 10.7% to 16% of cases.5 The presence of this anomaly in a 4-month-old infant underscores the unique nature of the current case and expands the age spectrum of documented CAFs with associated aneurysmal pathology.

From an etiologic perspective, this case does not align with KD, the most common cause of CAA in children. KD is an acute vasculitis affecting small- and medium-sized arteries, with coronary artery involvement being the most serious complication.6 Outcomes in KD are determined by the extent of coronary involvement.7 In the acute illness, necrotizing arteritis may cause aneurysm, thrombosis, or rarely rupture; chronic change can result in stenosis and arterial dysfunction.8 On computed tomography angiography, CAAs in KD typically appear as multiple aneurysms, most commonly involving the proximal left anterior descending artery and RCA, with possible findings of giant aneurysms containing layered thrombus, focal wall calcification. These features differ from atherosclerosis (diffuse calcification), vasculitides (ostial location, associated stenosis), or congenital anomalies (single-vessel involvement and fistulae).7 The absence of KD-specific clinical and laboratory features, with imaging findings of isolated RCA and SNA involvement, supports a non-KD origin. In a case series by Lin et al,2 the most common non-KD CAA was coronary artery structural malformation, with 3 cases of CAF and 1 case of RCA originating from the left coronary sinus. In our patient, the localization of pathology to the RCA, SNA, and fistula suggests a congenital coronary structural abnormality as the most plausible etiology.

CAF may cause volume overload, leading to heart failure, arrhythmias, and structural complications.5 Without treatment, CAF becomes symptomatic in approximately 19% of individuals younger than 20 years and in up to 63% of older patients.5 Notably, small CAFs in children have the risk of progression with age.5 Chronic high-flow shunting through the fistula can cause progressive enlargement of the upstream coronary branches, eventually resulting in marked dilatation and development of aneurysms.5 Indications for CAF closure have been outlined in detail by Al-Hijji et al.3 However, in our patient, dual fistulas into the RA had a significant consequence, with the formation of 2 giant aneurysms at the RCA and the SNA. With these significant changes, early surgical intervention was indicated to prevent long-term adverse sequelae despite the absence of clinical signs of heart failure or dilation of the cardiac chamber. This highlights the need to consider aneurysm formation itself as a potential factor when evaluating the necessity for early intervention, particularly in infants. Owing to this anatomical consideration, the surgical approach in this case was to ligate the SNA proximally, thus obliterating the blood flow through the aneurysm and the fistula.

Surgically, our decision to ligate the SNA was guided by intraoperative occlusion testing, a strategy first proposed by Kanemitsu et al4, 9 and further applied by Shibagaki et al.4, 9 The patient in the Shibagaki et al report was an adult in whom P waves disappeared immediately upon temporary SNA occlusion and reappeared 2 days postoperatively. In contrast, our infant demonstrated persistent P waves intraoperatively, with sinus node dysfunction emerging on postoperative day 1 and resolving spontaneously by postoperative day 2. This discrepancy highlights potential age-related or anatomical variability in sinus node perfusion and ischemic tolerance. At the 1-month follow-up, our patient remained asymptomatic, with stable sinus rhythm and no residual fistula. Echocardiography demonstrated a reduced RCA diameter, indicating favorable vascular remodeling after elimination of high-flow shunting.

We emphasize that even reassuring occlusion test results should be interpreted cautiously. Ligation was performed only after confirming preserved hemodynamics and rhythm, and epicardial pacing lead must be placed. Furthermore, if intraoperative occlusion testing had induced significant bradycardia or sinoatrial arrest requiring pacing, we would have avoided ligation and instead proceeded with aneurysm resection, SNA reconstruction, and fistula closure—prioritizing sinus node preservation.

Although axillary thoracotomy has been increasingly used for congenital heart defects, its global adoption remains limited, and it is primarily reserved for simple lesions. In an analysis by Dodge-Khatami et al,10 only 1 CAF repair was reported among 3,007 axillary thoracotomy cases. Our case is the first reported use for a complex coronary triad involving RCA and SNA aneurysms with dual RA fistulas. This approach enables safe dissection, intraoperative testing, and ligation of right-sided structures without compromising exposure. Compared with sternotomy, it reduced surgical trauma and improved cosmesis, making it a viable, minimally invasive option, even in anatomically complex cases.

Conclusions

We report to our knowledge the youngest case of a coronary triad involving RCA, SNA aneurysm, and dual fistulas to the RA. This case underscores early recognition and suggests that aneurysm formation alone may warrant intervention. Intraoperative occlusion testing guided safe SNA ligation. Right axillary thoracotomy proved to be a safe, minimally invasive approach for similar pediatric cases.
