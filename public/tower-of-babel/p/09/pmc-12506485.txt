Structure and loop dynamics of a chitooligosaccharide deacetylase from the marine bacterium Vibrio campbellii (harveyi)
Authors: Sirikan Pongnan, Robert C Robinson, Outi Lampela, André Juffer, Tamo Fukamizo, Wipa Suginta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA chitooligosaccharide deacetylase from Vibrio campbellii (formerly Vibrio harveyi) (VhCOD) belonging to the carbohydrate esterase family 4 catalyzes Zn2+-dependent deacetylation of a specific GlcNAc residue in chitooligosaccharides. It deacetylates chitobiose, (GlcNAc)2, to produce GlcNAc–GlcN following Michaelis–Menten kinetics. We elucidated the six crystal structures of wildtype VhCOD in ligand-free and -bound states with (GlcNAc)2 (substrate), GlcNAc–GlcN (product), (GlcN)2 (product analog), GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc (product), or GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 (product). The structures of VhCOD comprise the carbohydrate esterase family 4 catalytic domain and the two CBM12 carbohydrate-binding domains, similar to the COD homologs from Vibrio cholerae and Vibrio parahaemolyticus. The catalytic site, where a Zn2+ ion is coordinated with the His-His-Asp triad and three water molecules, is surrounded by six loops (L1–L6). Comparison between the ligand-free and various bound structures uncovered full catalytic cycle, including the product release in company with a large conformational change in L4. Molecular dynamics simulation based on the crystal structures provided further insights into the loop fluctuations, which are proposed to be involved in the catalytic reaction.Keywords: Vibrio campbellii, chitooligosaccharide deacetylase, crystal structure, molecular dynamics, loop fluctuation

Chitin, a β-1,4-linked polysaccharide of GlcNAc, is the second most-abundant biomass on the earth after cellulose, which is widely utilized as biomaterials. Although a large amount of chitin is found in food wastes, most of it is discarded, not fully utilized. Efforts to improve the use of chitin/chitosan have involved breaking them down into chitooligosaccharides (COSs) through acid or enzymatic hydrolysis (1, 2). COSs containing GlcNAc and GlcN have been studied for their biological activities, such as antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immune-boosting, and antitumor properties (3). However, the studies thus far often lacked detailed information about the degree of polymerization, acetylation degrees, and sequences of these COSs, making it difficult to understand how their structures relate to their biological activities. Recent advances have clarified this, since precise identification of COS sequences has become possible by state-of-the-art techniques combining ultra high–performance liquid chromatography with mass spectrometry (4, 5). Today, several strategies have enabled the synthesis of tailor-made COSs with specified sequences (6). A prominent strategy is to use site-specific chitin deacetylases (CDAs) as catalysts (7).

In nature, conversion of a GlcNAc residue to a GlcN residue in polysaccharide chains is carried out by CDAs or chitooligosaccharide deacetylases (CODs), which are widely distributed in archaea, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and insects. These enzymes are efficient tools to produce tailor-made chitosan polysaccharides or oligosaccharides with a specific sequence (7, 8, 9), since they can not only deacetylate from a GlcNAc unit located at a specific position but also acetylate a GlcN unit at the corresponding position in the presence of excess acetate (8, 10). CDAs and CODs are classified under carbohydrate esterase family 4 (CE4) in the CAZy database (11), and their catalytic domains adopt (β/α)7 barrel fold (12, 13). The first crystal structure of the CE4 enzymes was reported for CDA from the pathogenic fungus, Colletotrichum lindemuthianum, in complex with acetate as a product (14). This revealed that the His-His-Asp triad in combination with zinc forms the catalytic site, and nearby, His and Asp residues act as the catalytic acid–base in the enzymatic reaction. Subsequent structural studies on CE4 CDAs from Aspergillus nidulans and Arthrobactor sp. (15), which have broader substrate specificities, yet display similar active-site architecture within the (β/α)7 barrel structure. Two insect CDAs from Bombyx mori also exhibit (β/α)7 barrel fold with conserved His-His-Asp triad and catalytic acid (His) and base (Asp) but possess a longer substrate-binding groove (16). The structures and functions of CODs from Vibrios were reported from different laboratories (17, 18). Vibrio cholerae COD (referred to as VcCDA (17)) revealed a clear view of the enzyme bound with (GlcNAc)2 or (GlcNAc)3. This led the authors to propose a solid mechanism of enzymatic deacetylation. However, the complex structures reported for VcCDA (17) were obtained using the mutant COD, in which the catalytic aspartic acid (residue 39) was mutated to serine. As such, the resulting structures may not accurately reflect the native catalytic mechanism. Furthermore, the other structures of CDAs and CODs reported so far were the complexes with the product acetate, rather than with COSs. This has limited our understanding of the precise catalytic mechanism of the CE4 CDAs and CODs.

In this study, we produced a recombinant protein of the wildtype COD from marine bacterium Vibrio campbellii, formerly V. harveyi, (VhCOD), which was characterized with respect to its structure and function. Steady-state kinetic analysis was conducted in addition to the enzymatic product analysis using MALDI-TOF-MS. Several crystal structures were successfully obtained in the ligand-free form and in the forms bound with chitobiose, chitotriose, or chitotetraose, using the wildtype enzyme, providing a clear view of the catalytic cycle. Furthermore, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations based on the crystal structures were carried out to understand how the loop fluctuations relate to the catalytic action.

Results

Deacetylation specificity toward (GlcNAc)2 and (GlcNAc)3

Figure 1A shows the mass spectrum of the product obtained from incubation of VhCOD with (GlcNAc)2 as the substrate. In addition to the mass signal corresponding to the substrate (GlcNAc)2 + Na+ (m/z, 447.138), we observed the mass signal corresponding to mono-N-acetylated chitobiose with Na+ (m/z, 405.059), which increased with progress of the reaction time. To identify the deacetylated site, we further conducted TLC analysis of the reaction product. As shown in Figure 1B, the TLC spot of the substrate (GlcNAc)2 gradually became fainter until 60 min of the reaction time, whereas the spot for GlcNAc–GlcN appeared at 5 min of the reaction, then it was gradually intensified with progress of the reaction. However, no spot was observed for GlcN–GlcNAc. Thus, the product from (GlcNAc)2 was only GlcNAc–GlcN. Using TLC, product analysis from the (GlcNAc)3 substrate was unsuccessful, because no standard samples for mono- or di-N-acetylated chitotrioses are available. However, as described in the structural data section later, we obtained the VhCOD structures bound with GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc using the cocrystal obtained by mixing VhCOD with (GlcNAc)3. Thus, it is reasonable to define GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc as a sole reaction product from (GlcNAc)3. We concluded that VhCOD deacetylated the second GlcNAc residue from the nonreducing end of (GlcNAc)n.

Steady-state kinetics monitoring with absorbance at 205 nm

To obtain the steady-state kinetic parameters, the reaction time course of enzymatic deacetylation of (GlcNAc)2 or (GlcNAc)3 was monitored using absorbance at 205 nm, which was selected because of the baseline stability, data reproducibility, and the larger difference in Δε between the substrate, (GlcNAc)2 or (GlcNAc)3, and the product, GlcNAc–GlcN or GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc, respectively (19). Initial velocities at given substrate concentrations were calculated based on the change in absorbance at 205 nm, which was converted into the change in molar concentration using Δε at 205 nm. As shown in Figure 2, the relationship between the initial velocity and the substrate concentration was found to follow a Michaelis–Menten mechanism for both substrates, providing kcat, 1.71 s−1 and Km, 70.7 μM for (GlcNAc)2 and kcat, 0.186 s−1 and Km, 28.0 μM for (GlcNAc)3. We could not obtain the values for (GlcNAc)4 because of the lower susceptibility and the lower Δε205 nm.

Crystallographic and refinement data

All crystals obtained in this study provided diffraction data suitable for obtaining the high-resolution structure. The ligand-free crystal belongs to the space group I213, the crystal soaked with (GlcNAc)2 to the space group P3112, the cocrystals with (GlcNAc)2, (GlcN)2, (GlcNAc)3, and (GlcNAc)4 to the space group P212121, P212121, P3112, and P1211, respectively. The ligand-free crystal contained two molecules in an asymmetric unit. For the crystal soaked with (GlcNAc)2 solution, three protein chains were present in an asymmetric unit. Among these, only one chain showed clear and well-defined electron density for the ligand at the putative active site. For the cocrystals with (GlcNAc)2 or (GlcN)2, the asymmetric unit contained four protein chains, all of which bound to the ligand. The cocrystal with (GlcNAc)3 was found to contain three protein chains in the asymmetric unit, whereas all chains bound to the ligand. The cocrystal with (GlcNAc)4 contained two protein chains in an asymmetric unit, whereas one chain bound to the ligand. The molecular replacement (MR) method was employed to solve the phase problem using the structure of COD from Vibrio parahaemolyticus (VpCOD; Protein Data Bank [PDB] code: 3WX7) as a search model (18). The structures were improved by several rounds of refinement, attaining Rwork/Rfree of 19.9/21.8 for the ligand-free structure. The structure bound with (GlcNAc)2 was obtained from the VhCOD crystals soaked with (GlcNAc)2 solution through the refinement attaining 18.2/21.8 of Rwork/Rfree. Cocrystallization with (GlcNAc)2 provided the cocrystals with the product GlcNAc–GlcN, and the structure was refined to attain 15.7/18.9 of Rwork/Rfree. Cocrystallization with (GlcN)2 was successful to obtain the refined structure of (GlcN)2-bound VhCOD with 15.1/18.3 of Rwork/Rfree. Cocrystallizations with (GlcNAc)3 and (GlcNAc)4 produced the crystals of VhCOD bound with the products, GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc and GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2, respectively. Individual structures were refined to attain 18.2/24.0 of Rwork/Rfree for the structure bound with GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc and 17.6/20.1 for the structure bound with GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2. Resolution of the structures lies between 1.7 to 2.9 Å. The RMSDs of bond length and angle were refined to between 0.003 and 0.009 and between 0.55 and 1.01, respectively. Crystallographic data collection and refinement statistics

Within each of the six VhCOD crystal systems, we observed a preferred dimerization interface (Fig. S1). A single homodimer is present in the asymmetric units of the ligand-free VhCOD structure crystallized in space group I213 and the VhCOD complex (GlcNAc)4 in in space group P1211 (Fig. S2). Two homodimers occur within the asymmetric unit in space group P212121 in the VhCOD complexes with GlcNAc–GlcN and (GlcN)2 (Fig. S3). One homodimer and one monomer are present in the asymmetric unit in the P3112 space group in the VhCOD complexes with (GlcNAc)2 and (GlcNAc)3. These two structures demonstrate a clear example of the monomeric state in the crystal, where the monomer does not form a dimerization interface with neighboring asymmetric units (Fig. S4). Collectively, these observations suggest that while a preferred dimerization interface promotes crystal packing, the protein exists as a monomer in solution.

Crystal structure of ligand-free VhCOD

We determined the crystal structure of ligand-free VhCOD, which displays an extensive intermolecular interface suggestive of dimerization (Fig. 3A). However, small-angle X-ray scattering indicates that VhCOD exists as a monomer in solution (Fig. 3B). Consistently, the crystallographic packing in space group P3112 contains a monomer in the asymmetric unit. Therefore, the structures and the functional data obtained in solution are interpreted in the context of a monomeric protein. Superimposition of the VhCOD structure with that of well-characterized VcCDA is shown in Figure 3C (RMSD, 0.431 Å). RMSD between VhCOD and VpCOD was 0.373 Å. These three CE4 enzymes from Vibrios revealed close similarities to each other.

VhCOD adopts a deformed (β/α)7 barrel fold (cyan), which is linked to two carbohydrate-binding domains belonging to the CBM12 family (Fig. 3B; CBM12-1, pink; CBM12-2, green). Figure 3D shows the view from the top of (β/α)7 barrel. Six loop structures surrounding the substrate-binding site are highlighted referring to the loop nomenclature, L1, L2, L3, L4, L5, and L6, proposed by Andrés et al. (17). In the substrate-binding site (Fig. 3E), a zinc (orange sphere) was found at the center and surrounded by three His residues (His71, His74, and His269), two Asp residues (Asp13 and Asp14), and three bound water molecules. According to the sequence alignment of the five closely related CE4 enzymes (Fig. S5), these amino acids are fully conserved, indicating the significance of the catalytic-residue arrangement of the CE4 enzymes (17). Five of the six loops contribute to the walls of the substrate-binding site. The L2 (violet) loop comprising a major α-helix forms the wall at the left end of the substrate-binding site, prohibiting (GlcNAc)n to bind across this wall. The L3 loop (pink), which has no secondary structures, and the L5 (green) comprising two short helical elements form a big wall along the right-lower side of the binding site, whereas L4 (orange) comprising a minor helix and L6 (gray) form a wall along the upper side of the binding groove.

VhCOD bound with (GlcNAc)2, GlcNAc–GlcN, or (GlcN)2

We successfully obtained the VhCOD structures in complex with (GlcNAc)2, GlcNAc–GlcN, or (GlcN)2. The main-chain structures of these complexes were compared as shown in Figure 4A. Most loops were hardly rearranged in response to the binding of various chitobioses; however, L4 showed a major rearrangement (Fig. 4B). As reported in the study on VcCDA (17), L4 conformation of VhCOD rearranged from “open” (ligand free, magenta) to “closed” (red) upon (GlcNAc)2 binding. The Trp212 side chain horizontally arranged at the tip of L4 in the ligand-free state (magenta) was shifted downward and rearranged diagonally in the (GlcNAc)2-bound state. Here, we also found that a similar arrangement of L4 (“closed” form) was observed in the product GlcNAc–GlcN-bound state (blue) and the pseudo-product (GlcN)2-bound state (aquamarine). The Trp212 side chain also diagonally rearranged upon the binding of all ligands. On the other hand, the L3 main chain was less rearranged (Fig. 4C), and the arrangement of the Trp168 side chain localized to the L3 tip did not change upon the binding of all ligands. Distances between Cβ of Trp212 (L4) and C7 of the indole side chain of Trp168 (L3) were 3.7 Å for both (GlcNAc)2-bound and GlcNAc–GlcN–bound VhCODs, whereas the distance in the ligand-free VhCOD was 8.0 Å (Fig. 4E). The distance was 3.8 Å for (GlcN)2-bound VhCOD.

As shown in Figure 5B, (GlcNAc)2 was found to bind to subsites −1 and 0 in a similar manner to that reported for VcCDA–(GlcNAc)2 complex (17). Here, we used the subsite nomenclature commonly used for CE4 enzymes (19, 20, 21). The −1 GlcNAc is recognized by the guanidyl group of Arg278, where the two nitrogen atoms form three hydrogen bonds toward the carbonyl oxygen of acetamido group and the hydroxyl group of C3. The side-chain carbonyl of Asn93 formed two hydrogen bonds with hydroxyl oxygens of C3 and C4, respectively. The 0 GlcNAc interacts with several amino acids. The side chain nitrogen of His269 formed a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl oxygen of C3. The side chain -NH2 of Gln99 formed a hydrogen bond with the pyranose ring oxygen (Fig. 5B). The main chain -NH- of Tyr143 forms a hydrogen bond with the acetamido CO, which is coordinated with Zn2+ (Fig. 5B). The Asp14 side-chain oxygen forms hydrogen bonds with both −1 and 0 GlcNAc residues through the C6 hydroxyl (−1) and the C3 hydroxyl (0). Zn2+-mediated and water-mediated interaction networks exist between Asp13/Asp14/His71/His75 and the acetamido CO/hydroxyl oxygen of C3 of 0 GlcNAc. Of note, the Trp212 indole side chain localized to the tip of L4 stacks with the pyranose ring at subsite 0 via the hydrophobic interaction.

Here, we first report the structure of VhCOD in complex with the product GlcNAc–GlcN, which was found to bind to the same subsites, −1 and 0 (Fig. 5C). Similar amino acid residues, Arg278, Asn93, His269, Gln99, Tyr143, His71, His75, Asp13, and Asp14, formed similar interactions; however, missing the N-acetyl group of the reducing-end sugar residue resulted in the loss of interactions of the carbonyl oxygen of N-acetyl group of 0 GlcNAc with Zn2+ and the Tyr143 main chain -NH-. In the structure of VhCOD bound with (GlcN)2, further loss of the interactions formed by Arg278 took place, although (GlcN)2 bound to the identical subsites −1 and 0 (Fig. 5D). The hydrogen bonds directly formed with the two pyranose rings, and the CH–π stacking between the Trp212 side chain and 0 GlcNAc appeared to remain even when both N-acetyl groups are missing from the ligand, indicating a predominance of the pyranose–ring interactions in the COS recognition of VhCOD.

VhCOD bound with GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc or GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2

Cocrystallization of VhCOD in the presence of (GlcNAc)3 or (GlcNAc)4 provided the VhCOD structure bound with the corresponding product, in which the N-acetyl group of the second GlcNAc residue from the nonreducing end was removed; GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc or GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2, respectively. Figure 6 shows the superimposition of the main-chain structures of the complexes with that of the ligand-free VhCOD. The main-chain conformations of the loops except L4 (orange) were not significantly rearranged in these complex structures (Fig. 6A). The binding of GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc or GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 to VhCOD resulted in a significant rearrangement in L4 conformation (Fig. 6B). Notably, the Trp212 side chain in the complex with GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc (green, “closed”) was arranged in a similar manner to that with chitobioses (Fig. 4B), whereas that in the complex with GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 almost overlapped with that in the ligand free (magenta, “open”). (Fig. 6C). The L3 conformation was less rearranged in the complexes with the products, mono-de-N-acetylated chito-tri/tetra-oses.

The binding mode of GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc is shown in Figure 7A. The carbonyl oxygen of the 2-acetamido group and the C3-hydroxyl oxygen of −1 GlcNAc interact with the guanidyl nitrogen atom of Arg278, forming three hydrogen bonds. The C3-hydroxyl oxygen of 0 GlcNAc interacts with Zn2+. The Asn93 and Asp14 side chains interact with −1 and 0 GlcNAc residues in a similar manner to that of the chitobiose complexes (Fig. 5, B and C). The side chain of Gln99 interacts with the pyranose ring oxygen of 0 GlcNAc (Fig. 7A). Trp212 similarly stacks with the pyranose ring of 0 GlcNAc. Ser248 forms a hydrogen bond with the pyranose ring oxygen of +1 GlcNAc. The distance between Trp212(Cβ) and Trp168(C7) was 4.6 Å, suggesting that L4 may have been slightly displaced from the “closed” conformation to the “open” by the penetration of an additional sugar residue into subsite +1. This can be regarded as a “half-open” state.

The VhCOD cocrystal structure obtained by incubation with (GlcNAc)4 surprisingly contained the enzymatic product, GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2. Despite the low activity toward (GlcNAc)4, the enzymatic reaction proceeded under the crystallization conditions, possibly because of the combination of high concentrations, long incubation time, and macromolecular crowding. In the structure of VhCOD bound with GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2, however, only a GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc moiety could be resolved (Fig. 7B), and its electron density was observed at the site distant away from the specific binding groove spanning −1, 0, and +1 subsites. VhCOD specifically de-N-acetylates the second GlcNAc residue from the nonreducing end; subsequently, the enzymatic product, GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2, appears to slide away from the catalytic site (subsites −1 and 0) while remaining on the nonspecific binding site before release from the enzyme. Electron density was not observed for the reducing-end GlcNAc residue of chitotetraose, indicating that it may be highly flexible and not in stable contact with the enzyme surface. Only two hydrogen bonds are formed between the hydroxyl oxygen of the middle GlcNAc residue and the amide nitrogens of Gln99/Asn247, indicating a relatively weak interaction. The Asn99 side chain interacting with 0 GlcNAc in VhCOD bound with GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc (Fig. 7A) flips away to the opposite orientation in the complex with GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 (Fig. 7B). Notably, the Trp212 side chain stacks against the pyranose ring of the GlcNAc residue from the upper side, whereas Trp168 side chain also stacks with the neighboring GlcNAc residue from the bottom, sandwiching the product, chitotetraose. Interestingly, electron density of the enzymatic product, acetate, was observed only in the structure bound with the chitotetraose. The displacement of the product, GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2, out of the catalytic site (subsites −1 and 0) may have provided space for the bound acetate.

MD simulation

To further understand the dynamics of VhCOD during the catalytic process, we conducted MD simulation. Here, we employed the three crystal structures, ligand-free VhCOD, VhCOD bound with (GlcNAc)2 (substrate-bound form), and VhCOD bound with GlcNAc–GlcN (product-bound form) for MD simulations, since these structures fully cover the catalytic cycle of the enzymatic deacetylation of (GlcNAc)2. Figure 8 shows the time-dependent RMSD and time-dependent distance between α-carbons of Trp212 (located at the tip of L4) and Trp168 (located at the tip of L3) for individual structures. The ligand-free VhCOD was most intensively fluctuated (Fig. 8A; RMSD, 0.15–0.30 nm), whereas in the substrate-bound state, fluctuation was slightly less (Fig. 8B). In the product-bound state, the fluctuation was significantly suppressed to approximately 0.15 to 0.2 nm of RMSD (Fig. 8C). Similar results were obtained for the distance between L3 and L4. In the ligand-free VhCOD (Fig. 8A, bottom), the distance fluctuation was greatest (0.6–1.8 nm), whereas it was slightly suppressed in the substrate-bound state (Fig. 8B, bottom). Then, the distance was quite stable at the lower level (approximately at 0.5 nm in average) in the product-bound form (Fig. 8C, bottom).

Further analysis of the motion of L1–L6 was conducted through comparison between the root mean square fluctuation profiles of the individual structures. In the ligand-free state (Fig. 9A), fluctuations in L3 and L4 were equally intensive, whereas in L2 and L6, they were less intensive. In L1 and L5, much less intensive. However, in response to the substrate binding, the L4 fluctuation was suppressed to some extent, whereas in L1, L2, L5, and L6, fluctuations were enhanced (Fig. 9B); that is, fluctuations spread over the entire loop regions. After completing the catalytic reaction (Fig. 9C), all loops were stabilized compared with the substrate-bound state. The three loops, L1, L5, and L6, appear to play different roles from those of the L2, L3, and L4.

Discussion

Substrate specificity

As shown in Figure 1, VhCOD deacetylates (GlcNAc)2-producing GlcNAc-GlcN. It deacetylates (GlcNAc)3-producing GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc. A similar mode of deacetylation was reported for VcCDA (17). Steady-state kinetic parameters for VcCDA, which has an amino acid sequence homologous to VhCOD (Fig. S5), were reported by Andrés et al. (17). They used HPLC–MS for discontinuous determination of the products after terminating the enzymatic reaction. The VcCDA reaction toward (GlcNAc)2 exhibited cooperativity with Hill constant of 1.92. kcat and Km were 4.38 s−1 and 690 μM for (GlcNAc)2, 0.58 s−1 and 400 μM for (GlcNAc)3, and 0.18 s−1 and Km 720 μM for (GlcNAc)4, respectively, for VcCDA. Li et al. (22) also reported the kinetic data using the same enzyme. They used 3H-CH3CO-labeled (GlcNAc)n (n = 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6) as the substrates, and the released 3H-acetate was discontinuously determined. kcat and Km were 28.1 s−1 and 10 μM for (GlcNAc)2, 20.4 s−1 and 21.1 μM for (GlcNAc)3, and 7.2 s−1 and 38.9 μM for (GlcNAc)4, respectively, for VcCDA. No cooperativity was reported. The kinetic data reported thus far appear to vary depending on the method of determination. In this study, we obtained the values, kcat, 1.71 s−1 and Km, 70.7 μM for (GlcNAc)2 and kcat, 0.186 s−1 and Km, 28.0 μM for (GlcNAc)3 for VhCOD by continuous monitoring with absorbance at 205 nm, which is based on Δε between GlcNAc and GlcN (Fig. 2). In preliminary experiments on the VhCOD kinetics using isothermal titration calorimetry, we obtained the values, 2.29 s−1 of kcat and 20 μM of Km, for (GlcNAc)2. The isothermal titration calorimetry method is based on the continuous monitoring of the heat release/absorption. Determinations of the kinetic parameters by means of the discontinuous methods reported thus far (17, 20) appeared to provide the data with a greater variability as compared with the kinetic data obtained by the continuous methods in this study. Nevertheless, all kinetic data reported for Vibrio CODs thus far agree that (GlcNAc)2 is the best substrate, with activity decreasing significantly as the substrate-chain length increases. It is likely that the contact of GlcNAc residue(s) with subsite(s) +1/+2 may bring about the partial opening of L4 (“half-open”, Fig. 7A), resulting in reduction of the catalytic efficiency.

Catalytic cycle

In this study, we successfully obtained the crystal structures of (GlcNAc)2-bound and GlcNAc–GlcN-bound forms, in addition to the ligand-free form only using the wildtype enzyme. These structures provide a definitive view of the catalytic cycle of VhCOD as shown in Figure 10. (GlcNAc)2 (“S”) first binds to subsites −1 and 0 of VhCOD (“E”) forming the enzyme–substrate complex (“ES”) in company with the L4 rearrangement from the “open” form to the “closed” form. In the “ES” state of Figure 10, the Asp13 side chain as a catalytic base activates the water molecule coordinated with Zn2+ (Fig. 5B). The activated water molecule attacks the carbonyl carbon of the N-acetyl group of 0 GlcNAc, converting the acetamido group to a tetrahedral oxyanion intermediate, which is stabilized by Zn2+ and the main chain nitrogen of Tyr143 (Fig. 5B) (17). Subsequently, the protonated His269 side chain as a catalytic acid donates the proton to the C–N linkage of the intermediate to release the acetate (“P1”) from 0 GlcNAc (Fig. 5B). This action leads to the “EP2” state in Figure 10, keeping its “closed” form in L4. All amino acid side chains and the water molecule involved in this catalytic reaction are correctly arranged in the crystal structures presented in this study (Fig. 5B). Since we did not observe electron density for the product acetate in the VhCOD structure bound with GlcNAc–GlcN, the acetate may have been released immediately after catalysis and prior to the “P2” (deacetylated product) release from the binding site. The L4 conformation returns from the “closed” state to the “open” state upon the “P2” release from the enzyme to complete a full catalytic cycle. Although the L4 conformation in “ES” (“closed”) is almost identical to that in “EP2” (“closed”), MD simulations based on these structures revealed that “ES” is more fluctuated than “EP2” (Figs. 8 and 9). The substrate binding was found to suppress fluctuation only slightly, but the fluctuation was strongly suppressed in response to the chemical conversion from (GlcNAc)2 to GlcNAc–GlcN. After a large conformational change of L4 upon substrate binding, it appears that fine-structural fluctuations are necessary for the catalytic reaction.

Catalytic cycle of VhCOD. Upon the substrate binding, VhCOD changes its conformation from the “open-unbound” state to the “closed-bound” state. The catalytic reaction takes place by the aid of the acid/base, His269/Asp13 (Fig. 5B), keeping its “closed-bound” conformation, but all fluctuations are suppressed after catalysis. The “open-bound” state in green bracket is formed just before releasing the enzymatic product from the (GlcNAc)4 substrate. The numerals, −1 and 0, are the subsite numbers. VhCOD, Vibrio campbellii (formerly Vibrio harveyi) chito-oligosaccharide deacetylase.

Loop dynamics and catalysis

MD simulations provided important information on the local fluctuations of the six loops in VhCOD (Fig. 9). Loop fluctuations have been shown to be important in enzyme catalysis (23, 24, 25). Here, we found that the six loops played diverse roles in the enzyme function. In the ligand-free VhCOD, fluctuations were intensive in L2, L3, and L4, as shown in Figure 9A, most probably facilitating adaptation to the substrate structure to enhance the specificity. However, in the substrate-bound VhCOD, fluctuations were enhanced in L2, L5, and L6, yet suppressed in L4 to some extent (Fig. 9B). Trp212 located at the tip of the L4 hydrophobically interacts with the pyranose ring of the substrate. This interaction appears to suppress the local fluctuation of L4. The substrate interactions with amino acids localized to the nonloop regions (Asp13, Asp14, His71, His75, Tyr143, and His269) may anchor the substrate molecule, whereas the interactions with amino acids localized to the loop regions with enhanced fluctuations (L2, Asn93 and Asn99; L5, Asn247 and Ser248; and L6, Arg278) may destabilize the bound substrate. This situation may facilitate formation of the transition state; tetrahedral oxyanion intermediate, which is produced by a nucleophilic attack of the water molecule to the carbonyl carbon of the N-acetyl group of 0 GlcNAc, resulting in reduction in the free energy of activation for deacetylation reaction. Similar findings were reported for the glycoside hydrolysis catalyzed by GH5 cellulase from Thermobifida fusca (26). The L2/L3/L4 fluctuation may be involved in substrate binding, whereas the L2/L5/L6 fluctuation may be relevant to catalysis. To confirm this, mutational analysis of the individual loops is now underway.

An additional role of L3, L4, and L5

Andrés et al. (17) proposed that the “subsite-capping model” for explaining the substrate and deacetylation-site specificities of the enzymes belong to the CE4 family. In this model, the six loops (L1–L6) cap the edges of the substrate-binding cavity, defining which GlcNAc residue of the substrate is located at the subsite 0 (catalytic site). L1, L2, and L6 cap the nonreducing end, whereas L3, L4, and L5 cap the reducing end. Due to the capping of L1, L2, and L6, chitobiose fits in the −1 and 0 subsites; however, for the chitotriose and chitotetraose, L3, L4, and L5 move to open the subsites +1 and +2 and to accept additional GlcNAc units. In fact, the structure bound with chitotriose, GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc (Fig. 7A), the distance between Trp212 and Trp168 was 4.6 Å, whereas the distances are 3.7 to 3.8 Å in the chitobiose-bound structures (Fig. 5). Thus, the chitotriose-bound structure can be regarded as a “half-open” state. Thus, the subsite-capping model can be applied to many enzymes in the CE4 family. However, the structure bound with chitotetraose, GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 (Fig. 7B), where the chitotetraose binds to the “nonproductive binding site,” outside the contiguous subsites, −1, 0, and +1, suggesting that the additional sugar residues at the reducing end fully open the subsite capping by L3, L4, and L5 and form a gateway for releasing the oligosaccharide product with a longer chain (“EP2∗,” green bracket in Fig. 10). Comparison in the electron densities between the bound GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc (−1, 0, and +1) and bound GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 provided the experimental evidence for this idea (insets of Fig. 7, A and B). The electron density of the third GlcNAc residue from the nonreducing end of bound GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2 is fainter than those of the other sugar residues, whereas that of the fourth GlcNAc residue is not detected, indicating fluctuation of the fourth GlcNAc. Thus, the structure shown in Figure 7B may be a snapshot before the P2 release from the enzyme. Therefore, L3, L4, and L5 is important not only to control the length of the oligosaccharides to be accepted but also to pave the way for the release of the products after catalytic reaction as proposed in Figure 10 (“EP2∗,” green bracket).

Experimental procedures

Materials

COSs, (GlcNAc)2, (GlcNAc)3, and (GlcNAc)4, and the de-N-acetylated derivatives from (GlcNAc)2, (GlcN)2, GlcNAc–GlcN, and GlcN–GlcNAc, were purchased from Megazyme Ltd. Chromatography resins for the enzyme purification, Ni–NTA affinity resin was product of Genscript Ltd. and Superdex-200, was the product from Cytiva.

Synthesis of the COD gene

The sequence of a gene encoding a potential COD protein (COD) from V. campbellii type strain BAA 1116 was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database (sequence ID: WP_012128958.1). A synthetic cod gene encoding 427 amino acids of the polypeptide chain was obtained from Gene Synthesis Service (Genscript Ltd) and cloned into the pQE60 expression vector. The full-length cod gene was codon-optimized for high expression in the Escherichia coli M15 (pREP) host.

Protein production and purification

The construct pQE60-cod was transformed into E. coli M15 (pREP) host cells, which were then plated onto an LB agar plate containing 100 μg.ml−1 ampicillin and 25 μg.ml−1 kanamycin and grown overnight at 37 °C. A transformant was cultured in LB media supplemented with 100 μg.ml−1 ampicillin and 25 μg.ml−1 kanamycin and grown at 37 °C until an absorbance at 600 nm reached 0.6 to 0.8. Then, the protein was overproduced by adding 50 μM IPTG and further incubated at 18 °C for 16 h. Then the cells were harvested and resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, DNase I, 0.1% (v/v)Triton X-100, and 1 mM PMSF) and lysed with an ultrasonicator. After centrifugation at xx xg and 4 °C for 45 min, the supernatant containing crude proteins was applied to a Ni–NTA affinity column, and the bound protein was eluted with 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4 containing 100 mM NaCl and 100 mM imidazole. The partially purified VhCOD protein was further purified by gel-filtration chromatography on a SuperdexTM-200 (16/120) column equilibrated with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4) containing 100 mM NaCl. The purified VhCOD exhibited a single protein band at 45 kDa on SDS-PAGE (27), whereas the theoretical molecular mass is 44,373.06 Da. Protein concentration was determined spectrophotometrically by reading absorbance at 280 nm using the extinction coefficient of 92,165 M−1 cm−1, which was calculated by ProtParam using the equation proposed by Pace et al. (28).

MALDI-TOF-MS of the enzymatic product

The reaction mixture (total volume, 50 μl) containing 4 μM VhCOD and 0.5 mM (GlcNAc)2 in 5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5 was incubated for 1.5, 3.0, 5.0, 10, and 30 min at 30 °C, then the enzymatic reaction was terminated by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. The resultant solution was centrifuged at xx xg and 4 °C for 20 min, and the supernatant (1 μl) was mixed with 0.5 μl matrix solution consisting of 10 mg 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid in 30% acetone and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid to dry on a plate. Measurements were conducted in positive ion mode using a Bruker autoflex speed MALDI-TOF spectrometer.

TLC analysis of the enzymatic product

The reaction mixture containing 8 μM VhCOD and 0.1 mM (GlcNAc)2 in 5 mM Tris–HCl buffer, pH 7.5 (total volume, 100 μl) was incubated at 30 °C for 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 60 min, 6, 12 h, and overnight. Products from the reaction were separated by TLC on a Silica gel 60 F254 plates (Merck) using n-propanol:H2O:ammonia (70:30:1, by volume) as mobile phase (29). After development, the TLC plate was visualized by spraying reagents, including diphenylamine, aniline, acetone, and orthophosphoric acid, then it was heated at 180 °C for 5 min. To identify the enzymatic products, (GlcNAc)2, GlcNAc–GlcN, and GlcN–GlcNAc were used as the standard.

Steady-state kinetic analysis of the reaction catalyzed by VhCOD

Initial rates of deacetylation catalyzed by VhCOD were determined by the methods reported by Hekmat et al. (19) with minor modifications. Using a Cary spectrophotometer, absorbance at 205 nm was continuously recorded to monitor the enzymatic reaction. The substrate solution (600 μl), (GlcNAc)2 or (GlcNAc)3, was mixed with 100 μ VhCOD solution in 5 mM sodium borate buffer, pH 8.5 and then incubated at 30 °C for 10 min. The final concentrations of VhCOD were 0.1 μM for (GlcNAc)2 substrate and 0.8 μM for (GlcNAc)3. The substrate concentrations of (GlcNAc)2 were 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 250, 375, and 500 μM, whereas those of (GlcNAc)3 were 12.5, 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, and 250 μM. The initial rates of decrease in absorbance at 205 nm were determined in triplicate from the slopes of the steady-state phase, and the averaged values were plotted against substrate concentrations, providing the values of Km and Vmax by nonlinear least-square fitting of the Michaelis–Menten equation to the experimental data points (initial rates). The kcat value for the substrate (GlcNAc)2 was calculated from the Vmax value expressed in decrease in absorbance at 205 nm (ΔA205nm) per second using the difference in extinction coefficients (Δε205nm) between the substrate (GlcNAc)2 and the product GlcNAc–GlcN. The kcat values for the substrate (GlcNAc)3 were similarly calculated from Δε205nm determined by measuring the total change in absorbance at 205 nm upon complete turnover of (GlcNAc)3 by VhCOD at pH 8.5 and 30 °C.

Crystallization of VhCOD

To screen the condition for VhCOD crystallization, the purified VhCOD (5 mg.ml−1) was employed for crystallization trials by a sitting drop method using Morpheus Screen Kit and PACT premierTM Screen Kit (both purchased from Molecular Dimensions Ltd). After 7 days of incubation at 19 °C, single crystals were obtained under the optimized conditions listed in Table S1 and employed for X-ray diffraction experiments. To obtain the crystal structure of VhCOD in complex with various ligands, the purified protein (5 mg.ml−1) was incubated with 15 mM ligands, (GlcNAc)2, (GlcNAc)3, (GlcNAc)4, GlcNAc–GlcN, GlcN–GlcNAc, or (GlcN)2, in ice for 1 h, then the crystallization was conducted as described previously. However, we failed to obtain cocrystals with GlcNAc–GlcN or GlcN–GlcNAc. Furthermore, well-shaped crystals of VhCOD were soaked with 15 mM (GlcNAc)2 or (GlcNAc)3 solution for various duration times before X-ray diffraction experiments, but only the crystals soaked with the (GlcNAc)2 solution for 3 days at 19 °C diffracted sufficiently for structure analysis.

X-ray diffraction data collection and structure refinement

Crystals were harvested, cryoprotected with 20% (w/v) PEG400 or 20% (w/v) PEG3350, and cryocooled in a cryostream. X-ray diffraction experiments were performed at the beamline, TPS-05A, in the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center, Taiwan. Crystals were automatically mounted on the goniometer and irradiated with X-ray at λ = 0.9997 Å, and the diffraction images were collected by MX300-HS (Rayonix, LLC), integral to the beamline, and the images were analyzed and diffraction data scaled by HKL2000 (30). The resulting intensity data were transformed to structural factor data and phased by MR using the coordinates deposited for VpCOD (PDB code: 3WX7) (18) as the template by PHASER MR in CCP4i program suite, version 7.0 (31, 32). The modeled structures were improved by a combination of rebuilding and real space and reciprocal refinement in Coot (York Structural Biology Laboratory, York, UK (33)) and Phenix.Refine (34, 35, 36, 37) in Phenix 1.9 (38). Six structures were successfully solved with resolutions 1.7 to 2.9 Å and deposited in the PDB under the PDB codes 8YFP for the ligand-free VhCOD, 9JT0 for VhCOD/(GlcNAc)2, 9JEO for VhCOD/GlcNAc–GlcN, 8YH4 for VhCOD/(GlcN)2, 9JT8 for VhCOD/GlcNAc–GlcN–GlcNAc, and 9JEN for VhCOD/GlcNAc–GlcN–(GlcNAc)2. The crystallographic data collection and refinement statistics are listed in Table 1. The structures were displayed by the PyMOL software, version 3.0.3 (Schroedinger Inc., NY).

MD simulation based on the crystal structures

MD simulations were conducted using the three different conformations of VhCOD, ligand-free, (GlcNAc)2-bound, and GlcNAc–GlcN-bound VhCOD (PDB codes: 8YFP, 9JT0, and 9JEO, respectively) as initial structures. The states of the His269 side chain (catalytic acid) were assumed to be protonated in the ligand-free and the (GlcNAc)2-bound forms, whereas deprotonated in the product-bound form according to the catalytic mechanism proposed by Andrés et al. (17). MD package Gromacs (39), version 2019 and 2020.7, was used for the simulations and analysis of the simulated data. Protein topologies were generated with Amber99SB force field (40), whereas the ligand topologies were generated with ACPYPE server (41). Amber GAFF force field was used for generation of the parameters and partial charges with AM1-BCC model to correspond to HF/6-31G∗ RESP charges (42). The structures were placed in a rectangular box 1 nm from the protein and solvated with TIP3 water model (43). Counter ions were added to neutralize the system. The structure was energy-minimized with steepest descent algorithm, and 100 ps NVT and NPT simulations constraining the heavy atoms were performed before the actual NPT production runs. A modified Berendsen method for velocity rescaling (44) was used for temperature coupling and the method of Parrinello and Rahman (45) for pressure coupling. Production runs were at least 500 ns with time step of 2 fs, and the conformations and energies were collected every 10 ps. RMSDs of the whole protein from the starting conformation were calculated based on the backbone atoms. Root mean square fluctuations were calculated as the averaged deviations of Cα-atomic positions from their mean positions over time.
