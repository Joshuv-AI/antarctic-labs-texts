Real-world investigation of platelet apoptosis for mechanistic insights into severe immune thrombocytopenia and bleeding
Authors: Tarun Tyagi, Kanika Jain, Martin Matthews, Stephanie Halene, John Hwa, Natalia Neparidze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the low platelet counts in patients with immune thrombocytopenia (< 100 × 109/L) and its pathophysiological complexity, it remains both a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Routine platelet function tests could not be performed due to the extremely low counts in a patient with gastrointestinal bleeding.Key Clinical QuestionCan an unconventional approach to platelet investigation provide important insights into a case of severe thrombocytopenia of unknown origin?Clinical ApproachThe patient experienced repeated episodes of bleeding, including gastrointestinal bleeding, with platelet counts falling to critically low levels (< 10 × 109/L). The use of a Platelet Mitochondrial Depolarization Assay, using flow cytometry, provided clinically useful data before and after bortezomib treatment cycles. Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) can lead to an increased risk of bleeding and/or thrombosis, contributing to significant morbidity and mortality [1]. Given the low platelet counts in ITP patients (< 100 × 109/L) and its pathophysiological complexity, ITP remains both a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Secondary ITP, such as observed in B-cell lymphomas, further complicates clinical management [1]. Clinical assays of platelet function are constrained by low platelet numbers and scarcity of assays to evaluate platelet destruction; thus, clinicians are generally left without the necessary laboratory investigations to identify the underlying factors that may guide clinical management.

In this study, we report the use of unconventional platelet investigations for mechanistic and diagnostic insights, to provide guidance in a case of severe thrombocytopenia of unknown origin, refractory to conventional treatments and with an urgent need to intervene. The patient suffered from intermittent severe bleeding, including gastrointestinal bleeding, in the setting of critically decreased platelet counts (< 10 × 109/L). Clinical platelet function assays could not be performed due to prohibitively low platelet counts. The key clinical question was whether the patient’s life-threatening thrombocytopenia was due to (1) circulating antibody-mediated platelet destruction, or (2) a nonimmune platelet intrinsic cause. Our evaluation of plasma-induced platelet apoptosis (patient plasma combined with donor platelets) and dissection of inducing mechanisms provided mechanistic insights. This study was conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board and the Declaration of Helsinki on human subjects research. Patient and human donors consented to the study and to the use of deidentified data. The use of platelet investigations [2] provided urgent mechanistic information needed for initial clinical management.

Case Description

A 56-year-old woman presented with lower gastrointestinal bleeding (mucocutaneous) and severe thrombocytopenia with platelet counts falling to critically low levels (< 10 × 109/L). Peripheral blood smear revealed abnormal platelet clumping (Supplementary Figure 1). Anticoagulation with citrate was used instead of EDTA, which excluded the possibility of EDTA-mediated platelet clumping. Evaluation of viral infections was negative. A diagnosis of idiopathic ITP was considered, and patient received pulse doses of dexamethasone (dex) without a response. Serum protein electrophoresis revealed a monoclonal protein measuring 0.6 g/dL, identified by immunofixation as immunoglobulin (Ig)M kappa. Bone marrow biopsy showed a small clonal population (5%) of mature B cells that were lambda-restricted, positive for CD19 and CD20, and negative for CD5 and CD10. Circulating M protein may arise from a clone that is below the threshold of detection in marrow. Combination treatment with steroids and rituximab failed to improve platelet counts with persistent bleeding complications. The patient was started on romiplostim, a thrombopoietin mimetic, as second-line therapy with minimal response.

Results and Discussion

Clinical laboratory platelet function tests, including platelet aggregation and PFA-100 testing, were attempted but failed due to very low platelet counts. Initially, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on patient and control platelets isolated from citrated blood, as previously described [3,4]. TEM displayed unusual, distinct platelet morphology [[5], [6], [7]], including significantly increased apoptotic vesicular bodies and membrane blebbing, in the patient (Figure A). The morphologically abnormal platelets demonstrated distinct differences suggestive of apoptotic platelets (Figure A). Apoptosis was further confirmed in patient platelets by increased caspase-3 activity (Figure B) using a kit-based (Abcam) method described previously [4]. Considering the low platelet counts, the clinical urgency, we sought to assess platelet viability and function using a Platelet Mitochondrial Depolarization Assay (PMDA) via flow cytometry (FC). The tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE)-based PMDA can quickly (a few hours turnaround time in the laboratory workflow) detect and quantify mitochondrial dysfunction leading to apoptosis (mitochondria-mediated programmed cell death) in platelets [2,8,9]. TMRE is a cell-permeant, cationic, fluorescent dye that is readily sequestered by active mitochondria. Circulating platelet-targeting antibodies have been previously identified in some of the patients with ITP to cause platelet apoptosis [10]. To assay for humoral factors leading to platelet destruction, we performed “on-bench plasma exchange,” incubating healthy donor platelets with the patient’s plasma. We used platelet-rich plasma (PRP) from a healthy, blood-type-matched donor to perform plasma exchange. PRP was isolated from citrated human blood by low-speed spin (200 × g, 10 minutes). Donor PRP was incubated with patient plasma (1:1 v/v) at 37 °C for 30 minutes, followed by TMRE-based PMDA as per method described previously [8]. TMRE (1 μM, Invitrogen) was added to platelets for 10 minutes at 37 °C, followed by dilution in phosphate buffered saline. The events were acquired on a BD LSR II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo 10. Gating for TMRE negative events was done based on previously optimized carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone control set to ensure mitochondrial depolarization. The patient’s plasma induced a 5-fold increase in platelet apoptosis within 30 minutes in healthy donor platelets compared with control plasma (57.9% compared with 9.8%; Figure C, D, and Supplementary Figure 2), supporting plasma-mediated induction of platelet apoptosis in healthy donor platelets. Guided by the results indicating plasma-induced platelet apoptosis and by the finding of a monoclonal B-cell population and M-spike, the patient was started on bortezomib with dex for refractory ITP. After treatment (8 cycles) with bortezomib (+dex), the PMDA was reperformed with patient plasma and donor platelets. Interestingly, the patient’s plasma-induced platelet apoptosis was reduced to 34.8% (Figure E, F) compared with 57.9% prior to treatment. This corroborated with the improvement of patient’s platelet count in response to bortezomib (+dex) cycles (a timeline of platelet count and major clinical events is presented in Figure G). These results supported plasma-mediated platelet destruction, suggesting an ITP-like pathology secondary to humoral factors such as circulating platelet-targeting antibodies, which were investigated next [10,11].

We employed a customized indirect ELISA assay based on previously described ELISAs for circulating antiplatelet IgM and IgG antibodies specific for glycoprotein (Gp)Ib and GpIIb/IIIa (αIIb/β3), the common target receptors of antiplatelet autoantibodies [[12], [13], [14]]. Briefly, recombinant human GpIb and recombinant GpIIb/IIIa proteins (R&D Systems) were immobilized in the wells of a 96-well plate overnight at 4 °C for indirect ELISA (BioLegend), as per indirect ELISA protocol and reagents (Biolegend). This was followed by a wash step, addition of the plasma sample, a wash step, addition of an antihuman secondary antibody (anti-IgG or anti-IgM) conjugated with horseradish peroxidase, and a 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine or TMB substrate and stop-solution step. We detected 6-fold higher levels of anti-GpIb IgM and 7-fold higher levels of anti-GpIIb/IIIa (αIIb/β3) IgM (compared with control) in our patient’s plasma (Figure H–K), which were reduced after bortezomib treatment (Figure H–K). Levels of anti-GpIb IgG and anti-GpIIb/IIIa IgG were also higher (around 4-fold higher compared with donor control level) in patient prior to bortezomib treatment. In response to cycles of bortezomib treatment, the level of anti-GpIb IgG was reduced while the anti-GpIIb/IIIa IgG remained unchanged (Figure J, K). Antiplatelet antibodies were at minimal detectable levels in healthy control plasma, consistent with previous reports [14]. The higher circulating levels of these platelet receptor-targeting IgG and IgM antibodies in plasma were consistent with the PMDA results. Compared with IgG antiplatelet antibodies, the bigger fold increase in IgM antibodies (Figure H–K) in this case corroborates with IgM gammopathy. The increased antiplatelet autoantibodies could also be responsible for observed platelet clumping in this case. While antiplatelet antibodies were detected, their clonality and light-chain restriction were not assessed; therefore, a direct link between the IgM monoclonal protein and the pathogenic antibody population cannot be definitively established with the available data. The PMDA provided functional evidence of platelet destruction, likely secondary to antiplatelet antibodies.

Later in the course, the patient developed severe pain and nodularity over the lateral aspects of her thighs. A positron emission tomography scan revealed subcutaneous and intramuscular masses with increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in her right thigh, suggesting extranodal lymphoma (Supplementary Figure 3). The biopsy of the soft tissue mass of the right lateral thigh revealed non-Hodgkin’s B-cell lymphoma with plasmacytic differentiation, consistent with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL), and evidence of concurrent amyloid light-chain deposition. Detection of a MYD88 mutation confirmed the diagnosis of Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM)/LPL. A complete response in ITP with counts in normal range was obtained after the definitive treatment with a bendamustine-rituximab regimen. WM is an indolent LPL characterized by clonal B cells producing a monoclonal IgM paraprotein, occasionally accompanied by IgG [15]. Clinically, patients may present with anemia, thrombocytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and, less commonly, symptomatic hyperviscosity. Despite its chronic course, WM has an estimated 10-year survival of approximately 66%; however, other associated factors, such as severe thrombocytopenia and bleeding, can pose serious life-threatening risk in some cases. Auto-ITP associated with WM is rarely reported. Interestingly, the bleeding observed in WM is generally believed to be due to IgM binding to clotting factors [15]. Our study in this case also provides new insights revealing the potential role of autoantibodies against dual Gp targets (both GpIb and GpIIb/IIIa), which are uncommon in the pathogenesis of WM-associated ITP.

Taken together, all these results provided insights into this very interesting, rare case of WM-associated severe thrombocytopenia and demonstrate the effective use of a platelet PMDA, which is rarely used for this purpose in the clinic, to understand the real-world ITP pathology and diagnosis. A targeted workflow involving PMDA not only provided insights into mechanism and diagnosis of auto-ITP associated with WM but also a strategy to monitor effectiveness of ITP treatment. We used TMRE for PMDA, but other fluorogenic dyes, such as rhodamine 123 or JC-1 could also be alternatively used. Additionally, the increased antiplatelet autoantibodies against 2 different platelet receptors, and their selective changes in response to therapy, support this as an etiology of thrombocytopenia and bleeding risk. Further testing of the PMDA in more severe cases of thrombocytopenia could strengthen the rationale to develop PMDA-based diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines. While there are other advanced methods available to assess platelet function [2], they are largely limited by low platelet numbers and specialized equipment availability and cost. Given the urgency and debilitating low platelet counts with active bleeding, the PMDA assay allowed timely assessment. Our unconventional platelet investigation strategy including platelet apoptosis analysis utilizing TMRE-based PMDA followed by ELISA, could thus be considered, in addition to conventional tests, for mechanistic dissection and monitoring treatment effectiveness in thrombocytopenia, including ITP, and, more importantly, in cases where platelet counts are dangerously low and conventional platelet tests are not successful.
