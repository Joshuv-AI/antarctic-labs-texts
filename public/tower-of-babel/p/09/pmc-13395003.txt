Innate Immune Reprogramming Mediated by Endogenous Retroelement Dysregulation Drives Multiple Sclerosis Progression
Authors: Li‐Mei Xiao, Qiu‐Ping Zhao, Run‐Yun Li, Wei Chen, Huan‐Huan Song, Wan‐Jin Chen, Ying Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSkewed myelopoiesis in the bone marrow has been identified as a key driver of multiple sclerosis (MS) progression. Interestingly, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, which has a severe impact on MS patients, can also induce similar skewed myelopoiesis. This shared phenotype raises the question of whether a common mechanism underlies the skewed myelopoiesis in both diseases. Previous studies in mice have demonstrated that the dysregulation of endogenous retroelements (EREs) in HSPCs leads to skewed myelopoiesis. Building on this, we sought to determine whether ERE dysregulation contributes to the skewed myelopoiesis observed in MS and after COVID‐19, which remains challenging.MethodsWe undertook a joint investigation of two public single‐cell/nuclei cohorts respectively representing MS and following COVID‐19. Both cohorts were processed through an identical bioinformatic pipeline to ensure comparable assessment of gene and ERE expression.ResultsWe observed enhanced myelopoiesis in the bone marrow of MS patients compared to healthy controls, along with downregulation of the ERE repressor H3.3 and concomitant EREs overexpression. Notably, a similar epigenetic and transcript feature was found in post‐COVID‐19 individuals.ConclusionThe H3.3low/EREhigh signature may not only explain the common skewed myelopoiesis in MS and post‐COVID‐19 conditions, but also provide a mechanistic link between infection and the innate immune reprogramming that drives MS progression. This offers a novel therapeutic insight for MS.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease characterized by aberrant immune system attacks on the central nervous system (CNS). Previous studies have shown that pathogenic T cell‐mediated expansion of myeloid cells in the bone marrow contributes to MS progression [1]. The etiology of MS is a multifactorial interplay between heredity and environment. Intriguingly, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, which disproportionately affects individuals with MS [2, 3], can also induce similar skewed myelopoiesis [4, 5]. This shared hematopoietic feature raises a fundamental question: what common mechanism underlies the skewed myelopoiesis seen in both MS and post‐COVID‐19 conditions?

Most previous studies in this field, however, have focused primarily on protein‐coding genes—comprising only ~2% of the human genome—while EREs, which account for approximately 40%–50% of the genome [6], have recently attracted increasing attention. EREs are generally expressed only during specific stages of embryonic development and remain transcriptionally silenced thereafter under host surveillance mechanisms [7, 8]. Once their expression escapes this control, it can trigger autoimmune disorders. In particular, a growing body of recent studies in mice has confirmed that the dysregulation of EREs in HSPCs drives innate immune activation and skews differentiation toward the myeloid lineage [9, 10, 11, 12]. Although this mechanistic link is well‐established in mice, capturing such epigenetic and transcriptomic signatures in MS and post‐COVID‐19 individuals remains challenging.

Therefore, our study innovatively collected single‐cell/nucleus RNA sequencing (sc/snRNA‐seq) data from innate immune cells and their progenitors of MS patients [1] and post‐COVID‐19 individuals [4], followed by re‐quantification of gene and ERE expression. Collectively, our analysis identifies a common H3.3low/EREhigh epigenetic state alongside innate immune activation in the monocytes and their progenitors of MS and post‐COVID‐19 individuals. This provides a potential mechanism for the shared skewed myelopoiesis and may serve as a mechanistic link between infection and the innate immune reprogramming that drives MS progression.

Methods

Cohorts

We downloaded bone marrow single‐cell data for multiple sclerosis (Accession: HRA001783; OMIX001112) from the public database Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) [1]. The dataset comprises 14 samples: 7 from the MS group and 7 controls. Severe COVID‐19 recovery patients' BAM data was downloaded from the public database GEO (GSE196988) [4]. This dataset was generated from CD34+ HSPCs and CD14+ monocytes sorted from PBMC, including four sample groups: healthy controls (Healthy, n = 8), individuals recovering from non‐COVID‐19 critical illness (nonCov, n = 6), early convalescent individuals from severe COVID‐19 (2–4 months post‐onset, Severe_early, n = 4), and late convalescent individuals from severe COVID‐19 (4–12 months post‐onset, Severe_late, n = 8). Detailed clinicopathological information for these datasets is available in the Supporting Information S1.

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were maintained in SPF‐grade facilities at Fujian Medical University (SYXK (Min) 2008‐0001) under controlled temperature (22°C–25), humidity (50%–60%), and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice were group‐housed (4–5 per cage) with free access to food and water. All experiments were approved by the local IACUC (FJMU 2022‐0833) and conducted in accordance with U.S. Public Health Service guidelines.

Data Processing

scTE was employed to precisely requantify gene and ERE expression, directly processing BAM files generated by widely used alignment tools including Cell Ranger, STAR, HISAT2, and Kallisto [13]. Cell filtering, clustering, and annotation was performed using the Seurat R package (version 4.3.0) [14]. DoubletFinder was used to detect and remove doublets in the processed data to ensure data quality [15]. The top 15 principal components (PCs) were retained for downstream analysis. Batch effect correction was performed using the Harmony package (version 0.1.1) [16]. Cell type annotation was based on highly expressed marker genes based on previous studies [1, 17]. Following clustering, RNA velocity was applied to infer differentiation trajectories of HSPCs [18]. Cell–cell communication analysis was performed using the CellChat package [19].

HSC stemness score was calculated using Seurat's AddModuleScore function. HSC‐specific marker genes were identified using FindConservedMarkers and intersected with literature‐curated stemness gene sets [20], resulting in the final gene set: CD34, NPR3, HOPX, CYTL1, SPINK2, CRHBP, AVP, RBPMS, MMRN1, ANGPT1, PROM1, BEX2, MYCT1, SMIM24, MDK, BAALC, MLLT3, TFPI, HEMGN, CRYGD, GNAI1, MEIS1, DPPA4, LRBA, LAPTM4A, SELL, BEX4, LIMS1, TAOK3, FXYD5, CD164, BST2, ADAM28, CLU.

The ERE epigenetic inhibitory factor complex is divided into the following four components: H3.3‐related epigenetic regulators (HIRA, MORC3, DAXX, ATRX, H3F3A and H3F3B [21, 22, 23, 24]); KRAB‐ZFP‐TRIM28 complex (TRIM28, DNMT3B, CBX3, DNMT1, DNMT3A, SETDB1, CBX5, CBX1); NuRD complex (RBBP4, RBBP7, MTA1, MTA2, MTA3, CHD3, CHD4, HDAC1, HDAC2, MBD2, MBD3, GATAD2A, GATAD2B); and HuSH complex (MPHOSPH8, TASOR, PPHLN1, MORC2, ATF7IP). These factors form a dynamic interaction network through multidimensional epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, collectively modulating cellular ERE activity [25, 26, 27, 28, 29].

Differential expression analysis was performed using Seurat's FindMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. H3.3low was defined as significant downregulation of H3F3A and/or H3F3B (log2FC < −0.25, adj. p < 0.05); EREhigh was defined as significant upregulation of EREs (log2FC > 0, p < 0.05) in specific cell subsets.

Induction and Evaluation of EAE Mice

Female C57BL/6J mice (8–12 weeks old) were immunized subcutaneously with 200 μg of MOG35‐55 peptide (Genescript, Piscataway, NJ, USA) emulsified in complete Freund's adjuvant containing 500 μg of heat‐killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Difco, Detroit, MI, USA). Pertussis toxin (200 ng) (List Biologic, Campbell, CA, USA) was administered intraperitoneally at the time of immunization and again 48 h later. Clinical signs were assessed daily using a standardized 0–5 scale with 0.5 increments. All evaluations were performed in a blinded manner. Mice were euthanized at the peak of disease severity.

Magnetic‐Activated Cell Sorting of LinN Bone Marrow Cells

Following euthanasia, bone marrow was flushed from femurs and tibiae with cold PBS. Cells were filtered, centrifuged, and treated with ACK buffer to lyse red blood cells. LinN progenitor cells were isolated using a mouse hematopoietic progenitor cell isolation kit as previously reported [1]. After magnetic separation, the LinN (flowthrough) fraction was collected for downstream analysis.

Quantitative PCR With Reverse Transcription

Total RNA was extracted from linN cells using trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Promega). qPCR (ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, Vazyme) was performed using the CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad). Detailed information about the primers used for mouse gene and ERE sequences is supplied in Supporting Information S2.

RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA extracted from LinN cells of wild‐type and EAE mouse bone marrow was used to construct strand‐specific cDNA libraries using poly‐A bead enrichment (VAHTS Universal V8 RNA‐seq Library Prep Kit). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Raw 150‐bp paired‐end reads were processed for quality and adapter trimming using fastp. Filtered reads were aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38/mm10) using HISAT2. Genome annotation was obtained from the GENCODE mm10 GTF file. Reads mapping to the genome were counted using featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the edgeR package [30]. Immune cell abundance was assessed using the mMCP‐counter package.

Endogeneous retroelement annotation was based on the rmsk.gtf file downloaded from the UCSC Table Browser.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

For single‐cell visualization in this study, we primarily utilized R packages. In the relevant results sections and figure legends, we provide detailed information on the statistical tests performed and the number of observations, including the sample sizes compared. When these details are not explicitly mentioned, we employed the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test to compare experimental and control groups.

Results

Enhanced Myelopoiesis and Activation of Innate Immune Response in the Bone Marrow of MS Patients

We obtained scRNA‐seq data from the bone marrow of untreated MS patients (n = 7) and healthy controls (n = 7) from the database of GSA [1, 31]. Subsequently, we processed all BAM files using the scTE tool to quantify gene and ERE expression [13]. After stringent quality control, we retained 98,156 high‐quality cells for downstream analysis. UMAP visualization identified five major cell clusters: HSPCs (AZU1, ELANE, MPO, DNTT), myeloid cells (S100A8, S100A9, FCN1, LST1), natural killer (NK) and T cells (CD3D, PRF1, KLRK1, CD7), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1, FCRLA, IGLL5), and erythroid cells (HBM, HBA2, HBB, HBD), along with other minor subtypes (Figure 1A,B). Cell proportion analysis revealed a significant increase in myeloid cells and a concurrent decrease in lymphoid cells (T/NK/B cells) in the bone marrow of MS patients compared to controls (Figure 1C).

Then we performed further classification of HSPCs and constructed their differentiation trajectory. Based on differentially expressed marker genes, HSPCs were classified into the following subtypes: hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs; CD34, SPINK2, AVP), granulocyte‐monocyte progenitors (GMPs; AZU1, MPO, ELANE), monocyte‐dendritic progenitors (MDPs; LGALS1, LYZ, VCAN), CD79A+ common lymphoid progenitors (CD79A+ CLPs; DNTT, IGLL1, CD79A), and GZMB+ common lymphoid progenitors (GZMB+ CLPs; GZMB, TCF4) (Figure 1D,E) [20, 32]. RNA velocity analysis based on splicing dynamics revealed a clear shift in the differentiation trajectory of HSPCs from MS patients toward the GMP (Figure 1D). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated a significant increase in the proportion of GMPs (p < 0.05) and a decreasing trend in CLPs in MS patients compared to controls (Figure 1F). Furthermore, compared to controls, MS patients exhibited the largest number of DEGs in GMPs, followed by MDPs (|log2FC| > 0, adjusted p < 0.05; Figure 1G). Functional enrichment analysis of upregulated genes in MS patients versus controls revealed significant activation of innate immune‐related pathways, specifically the NOD‐like receptor signaling pathway in GMPs [33] (Figure 1H). And assessment of HSC stemness revealed a significantly lower stemness score in MS patients compared to controls (Figure 1I), suggesting impaired self‐renewal capacity. Notably, we found that the top 5% of cells with the highest stemness scores exhibited significantly upregulated expression of GMP signature genes (AZU1, MPO, CTSG, S100A8) and MDP marker genes (CTSS, LGALS1, S100A9) (Figure 1J,K), indicating premature aging of HSC and increased differentiation into the myeloid lineage in the bone marrow of MS patients.

Then we conducted an in‐depth investigation of 820 HSCs. To distinguish this subset from the previously defined HSPC population, we designated it as CD34+ HSPC and further classified it into four functional subgroups: HSCs, multipotent progenitors (MPPs), GMPs, and CLPs (Figure S1A,B). Differential expression analysis revealed that the GMP subgroup in MS patients exhibited the highest number of DEGs, while the CLP subgroup had the fewest (Figure S1C). Notably, there was a substantial overlap of DEGs among HSCs, MPPs, and GMPs (Figure S1D). GSVA analysis demonstrated that, compared to healthy controls, MS patients showed a stepwise increase in the activity of energy metabolism and immune‐related pathways from the HSC to MPP and then to GMP stages, whereas the CLP stage returned to baseline levels (Figure S1E). The bone marrow microenvironment harbors factors that attract T cells from the circulation and lymphoid organs [34, 35], prompting us to explore whether T cells can traffic to the bone marrow and affect hematopoiesis. Therefore, we employed CellChat to analyze intercellular interactions between T cell subsets and bone marrow cells. The results revealed an enhanced interaction from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as the source to T cells as the target, mediated by the CXCL12‐CXCR4 axis (Figure S1H), and significant pro‐inflammatory interactions exist between T cells and CD34+ GMPs (Figure S1I,J). These findings collectively indicate that a marked bias toward myeloid differentiation is already present at the HSC stage in multiple sclerosis.

H3.3 Downregulation and Concurrent ERE Upregulation in MS Myeloid Progenitors

Although previous studies have demonstrated that skewed myelopoiesis in multiple sclerosis is mediated by extrinsic signals from pathogenic T cells [1], a growing body of evidence indicates that aberrant activation of EREs can trigger innate immune responses and reshape HSPC differentiation trajectories to myeloid‐biased proliferation [36, 37, 38, 39]. To investigate the role of this mechanism in MS, we reanalyzed the bone marrow scRNA‐seq data using the single‐cell transposable element analysis tool scTE [13].

First, we found that HSPCs and myeloid cells exhibited higher ERE expression compared to other cell types (|log2FC| > 0.25, adj. p < 0.01; Figure 2A). Differential expression analysis further revealed that ERE was overexpressed in HSPC and myeloid cells of the MS group compared with the control group (|log2FC| > 0.25; Figure 2B,C). In the HSPC subpopulations, GMPs and MDPs in MS patients displayed the most significant ERE activation, especially GMPs (|log2FC| > 0, p < 0.05; Figure 2D,E). Among the 109 upregulated EREs in MS patient GMPs, most were enriched in LINE, LTR (ERV), and SINE families (Figure 2F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that EREs are upregulated in MS myeloid progenitors.

Previous studies have reported that the chromatin remodeling factor H3.3, encoded by H3F3A and H3F3B, plays a critical role in maintaining heterochromatin stability [36]. We also found that MS patients exhibited significantly reduced H3.3 expression in GMPs and MDPs compared to healthy controls, while no significant differences were observed in HSCs or CLPs (adj. p < 0.05; Figure 2G,H). Notably, RBBP4, a component of the NuRD complex, also showed downregulated expression in GMPs and MDPs of MS patients (Figure 2H). However, further examination of these epigenetic regulators' expression patterns showed that H3F3B had the largest reduction in cellular expression proportion (26.8%) in MS patients, followed by H3F3A (20.5%); in contrast, RBBP4 was already expressed at low levels in GMPs, suggesting it may not be a core regulator of ERE expression (Figure 2I). Overall, we found that the myeloid progenitor cells of MS exhibited epigenetic transcriptional characteristics of H3.3low/EREhigh.

We then examined the relationship between H3.3 expression and ERE activation in CD34+ HSPCs from MS patients (Figure 2J). We found a progressive decline in H3.3 expression during myeloid differentiation in MS patients compared to controls, with a notable exception of upregulated H3.3 expression specifically in CLPs (p < 0.05, Figure 2K,L). Correspondingly, ERE expression demonstrated a progressive upregulation during myeloid differentiation in MS patients relative to controls; notably, although CLPs also exhibited increased ERE expression, the overall trend remained suppressive (|log2FC| > 0, p < 0.05; Figure 2M). Classification analysis of upregulated EREs in MS patient CD34+ GMPs revealed that LTR, LINE, and DNA transposon families dominated (Figure 2N). This expression profile correlated closely with our functional analysis of CD34+ HSPC subclusters, demonstrating a progressive enhancement of innate immune activation from HSCs through MPPs to GMPs. Notably, RBBP4 was significantly downregulated in all CD34+ HSPC subclusters in MS patients, with the most pronounced reduction in CD34+ CLPs (Figure 2L). This pattern, however, was dissociated from ERE expression trends, suggesting RBBP4 downregulation may not be the primary driver of ERE activation. Quantitative analysis revealed that an abnormal MS sample (MS2) neither decreased H3.3 nor increased ERE expression (Figure S2A–D), and ERE overexpression was more pronounced in GMPs (the stage where H3.3 downregulation was most significant) than in CD34+ GMPs (Figure S3A,B). These findings further confirm the robustness of the H3.3low/EREhigh expression pattern in the samples.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that H3.3 expression attenuation in MS myeloid progenitors coincides with aberrant ERE activation. This aligns with prior studies in adult mice that have shown that H3.3 deficiency in hematopoietic stem cells weakens H3.3K9me3 mediated transcriptional suppression of EREs, leading to innate immune activation and skewed myelopoiesis [21, 36].

The Epigenetic and Transcriptomic Profiles of CD14 + Monocytes Closely Resemble HSPCs in MS

To identify downstream myeloid cells with functional similarities to HSPCs, we performed an in‐depth analysis of myeloid subsets. Unsupervised clustering of 24,744 myeloid cells identified three functionally specialized subpopulations: neutrophils, CD14+ monocytes and CD68+ monocytes (Figure 3A,B). CD14+ monocytes exhibited higher expression of innate immune and antigen presentation genes than other myeloid subsets, and in MS patients versus controls, they showed marked upregulation of specific innate immune sensors (e.g., TLR7, TLR8) and their downstream factors (Figure 3C). Further subclustering of CD14+ monocytes revealed two distinct subgroups: M.SC1 and M.SC2 (Figure 3D). M.SC1 specifically overexpressed MHC class II molecules (HLA‐DRB1, HLA‐DPB1, HLA‐DRA, HLA‐DRB5), while M.SC2 was characterized by high expression of proinflammatory cytokines (S100A8, S100A12, RETN, LTF; |log2FC| > 0, adj. p < 0.05; Figure 3E). Strikingly, M.SC1 exhibited the highest number of DEGs among all myeloid subsets (Figure 3F). Functional enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in MS patients revealed that M.SC1 was prominently associated with the most significantly enriched pathways related to energy metabolism, neurodegenerative diseases, and inflammation (p adj < 0.05; Figure 3H). Collectively, these findings indicate that CD14+ monocytes, particularly the M.SC1 subgroup, represent the most significantly activated innate immune population among MS patient‐derived myeloid cells.

We next examined the expression patterns of H3.3 and EREs across myeloid subsets. The analysis showed a most significant reduction in H3.3 expression in M.SC1 subset of MS patients relative to controls (Figure 3I,J). Differential ERE expression analysis confirmed that EREs were most significantly upregulated in M.SC1 derived from MS patients compared to controls (Figure 3K,L). Classification of EREs upregulated in M.SC1 revealed enrichment in LINE, LTR, and DNA transposon families (Figure 3M). The heatmap of the top 20 significantly upregulated EREs in MS patient M.SC1 cells demonstrated highly consistent inter‐sample expression patterns, with the exception of MS2—a deviation similarly observed in progenitor cell populations (Figure 3N).

In summary, our results demonstrate that MS patient‐derived CD14+ monocytes, particularly the M.SC1 subgroup, exhibit epigenetic and transcriptional features strikingly similar to those of HSPCs. This subset likely plays a pivotal role in MS with innate immune activation and antigen presentation, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammatory processes [40, 41, 42].

Skewed Myelopoiesis and Elevated ERE Expression in LinN Cells of EAE Mice

The EAE model recapitulates inflammatory demyelination seen in MS, providing a valuable tool for investigating disease mechanisms [43, 44, 45, 46]. LinN cells are defined as lineage‐negative hematopoietic cells that do not express specific lineage markers (e.g., CD3, CD19, Gr‐1). In this study, we compared the gene expression profiles and ERE expression patterns in LinN bone marrow cells between wild‐type (WT) and EAE mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) performed on gene and ERE expression both revealed clear separation between the two groups (Figure 4A,G). Compared with controls, EAE LinN cells exhibited upregulated expression of myeloid marker genes and downregulated expression of lymphoid‐associated markers (Figure 4B). And we found that LinN cells from EAE mice showed a significant increase in myeloid subsets and a decrease in lymphoid subsets (p < 0.01; Figure 4C). Moreover, multiple innate immune sensors and genes were significantly upregulated in EAE LinN cells compared with controls (Figure 4D,E). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that innate immune pathways were enriched in EAE mice (Figure 4F). We also confirmed the upregulation of ERE mRNA in the linN cells from EAE mice by quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (Figure 4H–J). However, we did not find low expression of H3.3 in the bone marrow precursor cells of EAE mice compared with the control group.

Innate Immune Cells and Their Progenitors in Post‐COVID‐19 Individuals Display Similar Epigenetic and Transcriptomic Signatures to Those Observed in MS

Beyond its profound impact on individuals with MS [2, 3], a bias toward myeloid differentiation in hematopoietic precursors has also been observed following COVID‐19 [4]. Therefore, we performed targeted reanalysis of snRNA‐seq data from convalescent severe COVID‐19 individuals, including healthy controls (Healthy), individuals recovering from non‐COVID‐19 critical illness (nonCov), early convalescent individuals from severe COVID‐19 (2–4 months post‐onset, Severe_early), and late convalescent individuals from severe COVID‐19 (4–12 months post‐onset, Severe_late).

A total of 23, 342 HSPCs were classified into three subpopulations: HSC/MPP, GMP, and LMPP (lymphoid‐multipotent progenitors) (Figure 5A,B). GSVA analysis indicated activation of innate immune pathways in GMPs across patient groups compared to healthy controls (t > 0, Figure 5C). While many inflammatory genes returned to baseline in late recovery, certain inflammatory molecules (e.g., S100A4, S100A6, and S100A10) and antigen presentation‐related genes (e.g., HLA‐DRB5, CD74, HLA‐DRA, and HLA‐C) remained elevated (|log2FC| > 0, adj. p < 0.05; Figure 5E–G), suggesting a more differentiated phenotype of HSC/MPP in the late recovery stage. H3.3 expression was significantly downregulated in HSC/MPP and GMP of non‐COVID and early‐recovery groups, with partial recovery in late recovery, whereas LMPP showed milder downregulation (Figure 5D–J). Other ERE suppressors did not exhibit comparable downregulation (Figure 5H–J). Conversely, EREs were upregulated in HSC/MPP and GMP of non‐COVID and early‐recovery groups, with fewer upregulated EREs in late recovery (|log2FC| > 0.25, p < 0.05; Figure 5K–N).

Monocytes were divided into three subsets: CD14+ monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), and CD16+ monocytes (Figure S4A). Although subset proportions did not differ significantly (Figure S4B), CD14+ monocytes from non‐COVID and early‐recovery groups showed upregulated acute inflammatory and antiviral genes; notably, in late recovery, certain inflammatory (e.g., S100A8 and S100A9) and antigen‐ presentation genes (e.g., HLA‐DRB1 and CD74) remained elevated (Figure S4C). CD14 + monocytes further clustered into M.SC1, M.SC2, and M.SC3, with M.SC3 uniquely co‐expressing antigen‐presentation molecules and inflammatory mediators (Figure S4D,E). GSVA analysis revealed that activated innate immune pathways in M.SC3 of non‐COVID and early‐recovery groups (Figure S4F). H3.3 expression was reduced in all monocyte subsets in non‐COVID and early‐recovery groups, with late recovery showing rebound in DCs and CD16+ monocytes but sustained downregulation in M.SC1 and M.SC3 (|log2FC| > 0, p < 0.05; Figure S4G–J). Correspondingly, EREs were upregulated in these subsets, most prominently and persistent in M.SC3 (|log2FC| > 0.25, p < 0.05; Figure S4K–M).

In summary, post‐COVID‐19 patients exhibit H3.3 downregulation and ERE overexpression in innate immune progenitors and monocytes, paralleling MS‐associated signatures and correlating with sustained immune activation.

Discussion

Previous study has confirmed that pathogenic monocytes in MS originate from the skewed myelopoiesis mediated by T cells [1]. Our re‐analysis also confirms the presence of skewed myelopoiesis in MS. In contrast to previous research, our re‐analysis suggests that this phenomenon may be associated with the H3.3low/EREhigh signature in HSPCs. This signature is shared with innate immune cells and their progenitor cells following COVID‐19. H3.3 is the most common replacement variant for histone H3 [23]. Moreover, prior mouse studies have demonstrated that H3.3 deficiency in HSPCs reduces H3.3K9me3 deposition at EREs. The consequent derepressed EREs trigger an antiviral‐like innate immune response, which in turn promotes aberrant signaling and drives skewed myelopoiesis [21, 36]. Therefore, the H3.3low/EREhigh axis likely constitutes a common upstream mechanism underlying the shared skewed myelopoiesis in both diseases. Our study offers an alternative perspective on the origin of pathogenic monocytes in MS, providing supplementary insights for its treatment.

Prior studies of MS have largely been restricted to protein‐coding genes or to the limited role of MSRV/HERV‐W [47, 48, 49]. However, other transposable elements (e.g., LINEs, DNA transposons, SVAs) also play an important role in innate immunity [8, 9, 11, 12, 50]. By applying gene and ERE re‐quantification to primary datasets, we identified a shared H3.3low/EREhigh epigenetic and transcriptomic signature, thereby extending the role of ERE in MS pathology beyond HERV‐W.

Notably, while H3.3 downregulation in COVID‐19 convalescents was more pronounced than in MS patients, ERE overexpression was less severe. Beyond epigenetic regulation, inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IFN‐γ) can also induce ERE expression [48, 51, 52]. Critically, while the MS samples were obtained during active disease phases with cytokine storms, COVID‐19 samples were collected from convalescent stages (2–12 months post‐onset) lacking significant cytokine activity, this temporal disparity likely underlies the less pronounced ERE overexpression observed following COVID‐19. This indicates that the H3.3low/EREhigh state is likely retained as a durable epigenetic remodeling in monocytes and their progenitors. This state aligns with a phenotype of sustained monocyte activation—including enhanced migration, antigen presentation, and pro‐inflammatory potential. These programs may contribute to ongoing pathology in the brain with inflammation, accelerating nerve damage [4, 40, 41, 42, 53]. While environmental triggers, particularly infections, are implicated in MS, their etiology remains unclear. Our joint analysis suggests that H3.3low/EREhigh mediated hematopoietic reprogramming may contribute to myeloid‐driven MS progression post‐infection. To understand long‐term epigenetic changes in hematopoiesis following infection, and their association with MS, further work is needed with larger human infectious and MS cohorts.

While our study identifies a compelling shared epigenetic signature, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we observed ERE overexpression in bone marrow precursor cells of EAE mice, we didn't observe a corresponding decrease in H3.3 expression in this model. Therefore, we need to identify more suitable models to explore the role of H3.3 deficiency in MS pathology. Second, our findings are based on transcriptional data and would be strengthened by protein‐level and direct chromatin‐state assessments. Third, differences in tissue source (bone marrow vs. peripheral blood) and disease phase (active vs. convalescent) between cohorts may influence the observed molecular profiles. Addressing these limitations in future studies will solidify the mechanistic role of the H3.3‐ERE axis and explore its therapeutic potential.

Collectively, we discovered that the innate immune cells and their progenitors of patients with MS and post‐COVID‐19 share a key epigenetic signature characterized by H3.3low/EREhigh. While the epigenetic signature was identified within the context of MS and following COVID‐19, its potential relevance to other neuroinflammatory disorders remains an open question. Conditions such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) or MOG antibody‐associated disease (MOGAD) share features of CNS inflammation. It is plausible that similar epigenetic dysregulation in hematopoietic precursors could contribute to other neuroinflammatory diseases, but this hypothesis warrants direct investigation. Determining whether this mechanism is specific to MS or represents a broader pathway in neuroinflammation will be crucial for understanding its generalizability and translational potential.
