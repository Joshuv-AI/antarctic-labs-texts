Treatment adherence in chronic uveitis: What does the MARS-5 tell us?
Authors: Mehtap Caglayan, Dilbade Yıldız Ekinci, Betül Dertsiz Kozan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate medication adherence among patients with chronic uveitis using the five-item Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5) and to identify the difficulties and barriers affecting adherence.MethodsA single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted with 99 adult patients diagnosed with chronic uveitis. Participants completed sociodemographic and disease-related questionnaires, the MARS-5 scale, and if scoring below 25 a nine-item adherence barrier questionnaire. Descriptive statistics and appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests were used to analyze relationships between adherence and sociodemographic or clinical variables.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 32.6 ± 8.7 years, and the mean total MARS-5 score was 22.9 ± 2.3. Adherence levels were perfect in 38.4% of patients, high in 48.5%, and moderate in 13.1%; no participants demonstrated poor adherence. Male patients exhibited significantly lower MARS-5 scores than females (p < 0.001). A moderate positive correlation was found between adherence and duration of medication use (r = 0.447, p = 0.012). The item most frequently indicating non-adherence was “forgetting to take medication.” The most commonly reported barriers were fear of side effects (34.4%) and medication-related difficulties such as unpleasant taste, tablet size, or injection-related concerns (26.2%).ConclusionMedication adherence among chronic uveitis patients was generally high but influenced by sociodemographic and behavioral factors. Both intentional and unintentional non-adherence were observed, emphasizing the need for individualized adherence-support strategies, improved patient education, and enhanced communication to optimize long-term disease management.

Introduction

Uveitis is an important group of ocular disorders characterized by inflammation of the uveal tract and has the potential to cause severe visual impairment. With an etiology spanning a broad spectrum, the condition may arise from various pathophysiological mechanisms, ranging from infectious agents to autoimmune processes. It is recognized as one of the leading causes of vision loss particularly among young and middle-aged adults, and its chronic course often accompanied by recurrent episodes necessitates long-term and consistent treatment [1, 2]. Effective management of uveitis primarily aims to control inflammation, prevent recurrences, and minimize the development of complications. In line with these goals, corticosteroids, immunomodulatory therapies, and biologic agents constitute the main therapeutic options [3–6]. However, the success of these treatments depends not only on the appropriate selection and dosing of pharmacological agents but also on the patient’s adherence to the prescribed medication regimen. Poor adherence can lead to increased relapse rates, progression of complications, and a consequent rise in overall treatment costs.

A number of instruments have been employed in the literature to assess medication adherence. Among these, one of the most widely utilized tools is the Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS) [7]. The MARS is a practical self-report measure designed to evaluate patients’ medication-taking behaviors, and its validity has been demonstrated across diverse patient populations. It has been extensively applied in chronic conditions particularly in fields requiring long-term treatment such as psychiatry, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, diabetes, stroke and rheumatologic disorders [8–14].

Studies evaluating medication adherence in chronic ocular diseases have largely focused on glaucoma and retinal disorders, whereas research specifically addressing adherence among patients with uveitis remains limited [15–18]. However, given that uveitis frequently follows a chronic course and often requires prolonged immunosuppressive therapy, adherence to medication constitutes a critical component of disease management. Assessing medication adherence in individuals with uveitis and elucidating its relationship with clinical outcomes are therefore essential for both optimizing clinical care and improving patient education.

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate medication adherence in patients with chronic uveitis using the five-item MARS scale and to investigate the difficulties and barriers they encounter in adhering to their treatment regimens.

Materials and methods

This single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted after obtaining approval from Diyarbakır Gazi Yasargil Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (number 363, date 28.02.2025), and written informed consent was received from all participants. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 114 adult patients (≥ 18 years) with chronic uveitis who were followed at the Uvea Unit of our hospital’s Ophthalmology Clinic and who presented between March 2025 and August 2025 and agreed to participate were included in the study. All eligible patients approached during the study period agreed to participate. All participants completed a sociodemographic and disease-related information form, the MARS-5 scale, and among those scoring below the maximum MARS-5 value a questionnaire designed to identify difficulties and barriers related to treatment adherence. For participants who were illiterate, all items were read aloud individually, and their responses were recorded. Data from 15 patients who provided incomplete scale responses or demonstrated internally inconsistent answer patterns were excluded from the analysis. Internally inconsistent responses were defined as logically contradictory answers across MARS-5 items, such as simultaneously reporting “never forgetting to take medication” while also indicating frequent intentional dose omission.

Initially, all participants completed a sociodemographic information form (age, sex, education level, marital status, income level, employment status) and a disease-related information form (disease duration, duration of medication use, number of medications used, presence of comorbid systemic diseases and use of systemic medications for any concomitant systemic condition and level of knowledge regarding the risk of blindness due to uveitis). We also categorized patients according to the anatomical localization of uveitis, disease duration, medication duration, and number of medications.

Medication adherence was assessed using the self-report Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5). The validated Turkish version of the MARS-5 scale was used in this study [24]. MARS-5 consists of five items assessing patient-reported medication-taking behaviors (forgetting to take medication, altering the dose, discontinuing medication for a period, deciding to skip a dose, and taking less medication than prescribed), each rated on a 5-point Likert-type response scale. Participants were asked to indicate the frequency of each behavior. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale (5 = never, 4 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 2 = often, 1 = very often). Higher scores indicate better adherence (25: perfect adherence; 24–20: high adherence; 19–16: moderate adherence; ≤15: poor adherence). In this study, the total MARS-5 score was used, and sub-item analyses were performed when necessary.

Participants scoring below 25 on the MARS-5 were additionally administered a researcher-developed questionnaire based on the relevant literature consisting of nine yes/no items addressing treatment-related difficulties (1- believing the treatment is not beneficial, 2- lack of motivation, 3- fear of potential side effects, 4- low or absent self-confidence, 5- difficulty accessing the physician for prescription renewal, 6- high medication cost, 7- difficulty establishing sufficient and effective communication with healthcare professionals, 8- treatment’s negative impact on daily life, 9- inability to take medication due to medication-related factors such as unpleasant taste, large tablets, or injection fear). The 9-item medication adherence barrier questionnaire was developed by the researchers based on a review of the relevant literature on medication adherence in chronic diseases and uveitis, as well as routine clinical observations in a tertiary uveitis clinic. The items were designed to capture commonly reported intentional and unintentional barriers, including concerns about side effects, motivation, access to healthcare, treatment burden, and medication-related difficulties. The questionnaire was not intended as a psychometrically validated scale, but rather as an exploratory tool to identify frequently encountered adherence barriers in this patient population.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 24.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, percentage, and frequency values, were reported. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-smirnov test. Depending on the distribution of the data, appropriate parametric (t-test, ANOVA) or non-parametric (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis) tests were used to examine the association between MARS scores and sociodemographic or clinical variables. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated for correlation analyses. In addition, a multivariable linear regression analysis was performed with total MARS-5 score as the dependent variable and age, sex, employment status, duration of medication use, and disease duration as independent variables to adjust for potential confounding effects. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 99 chronic uveitis patients 37 women and 62 men who fully completed the sociodemographic and disease-related information form as well as the MARS-5 scale were included in the study. The mean age of the patients was 32.5 ± 8.7 years. The mean total MARS-5 score of all participants was 22.9 ± 2.3. Among the 99 participants, medication adherence was perfect in 38 patients (38.4%), high in 48 patients (48.5%), and moderate in 13 patients (13.1%). None of the participants demonstrated poor adherence.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants and the comparison of their total MARS-5 scores are presented in Table 1. One of the most notable findings was that the mean total MARS-5 score among male participants was significantly lower than that of female participants (p < 0.001). When average MARS-5 scores were compared across employment status, another statistically significant difference was observed. Mean MARS-5 scores were 22.6 ± 2.3 in the employed group, 24.1 ± 1.6 in the unemployed group, and 23.7 ± 3.2 among those who had stopped working due to illness (p = 0.003). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the significant difference was between the employed and unemployed groups (p = 0.014).

Discussion

In this study, medication adherence among patients with chronic uveitis was evaluated using the MARS-5 scale, and the findings once again underscored that treatment adherence is a critical component of clinical management in uveitis. Our results demonstrated that, in a considerable proportion of patients, adherence may be compromised due to both intentional and unintentional factors.

When a patient does not benefit from a prescribed treatment regimen, clinicians must first determine whether the medication has been taken as instructed before concluding that the therapy is ineffective. Poor adherence may lead to the erroneous assumption that the patient is resistant to medical therapy. This misinterpretation can result in unnecessary medication changes, more frequent follow-up visits, and, in some cases, unwarranted surgical interventions. The World Health Organization (WHO) has reported that the average medication adherence rate among patients with chronic diseases in developed countries is only 50%. WHO categorizes the determinants of medication non-adherence into socioeconomic, patient-related, condition-related, therapy-related, and health system/healthcare provider–related risk factors [19].

Although various methods exist to evaluate medication adherence, each has its own advantages and limitations, and no universally accepted gold-standard approach has been established. Objective methods such as plasma drug level measurement, medication counting (pill count or prescription refill frequency), and electronic monitoring devices are not ideal for routine clinical practice. While self-reported adherence scales provide subjective assessments, they are widely used due to their practicality, low cost, non-invasive nature, minimal patient burden, and ease of administration [20, 21].

Chronic uveitis requires prolonged treatment that must be tailored to the patient’s disease course, severity, and underlying etiology. Therefore, therapeutic success depends not only on selecting the appropriate treatment regimen but also on the patient’s adherence to that regimen. The ophthalmology literature contains limited data on medication adherence in uveitis. Mueller et al. evaluated adherence using the Beliefs about Medicine Questionnaire (BMQ) and found that although uveitis patients held strong beliefs about the necessity of their medications, they also reported notable concerns regarding treatment [16]. However, the BMQ does not assess adherence directly; instead, it measures beliefs and attitudes that may indirectly indicate adherence risk.

Patil et al. reported non-compliance in 22.4% of 201 uveitis patients. The most common type of non-adherence was “self-discontinuation of therapy,” reported in 28.9% of cases. The primary reasons identified for non-compliance were inadequate instructions provided by the physician (26.7%), workload or school responsibilities (22.2%), and insufficient understanding of medication side effects (15.6%). Non-adherence was found to be particularly pronounced among school-aged children and working adults. Notably, however, no validated adherence scale was used in this study; instead, the researchers relied on clinical observation and patient self-report to classify adherence [17].

In another study, Oklar et al. assessed adherence exclusively in patients with Behçet uveitis using the 6-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-6) and an additional 4-item “Self-reported Questionnaire About Treatment Compliance” developed by the authors (items: skipping doses due to side effects, cost or insurance issues affecting medication access, irregular use for other reasons, and missing at least two follow-up visits). They identified low adherence in 52.7% of patients and moderate adherence in 47.3% [18]. The MMAS focuses primarily on behavioral barriers to adherence and is useful for identifying reasons underlying non-adherence; however, its yes/no response format reduces sensitivity, and studies have reported lower internal consistency compared with other adherence scales [22, 23].

MARS-5 is a Likert-type scale that measures the frequency of medication-taking behaviors and focuses on the actual frequency and intensity of non-adherent actions. It is considered a reliable and valid instrument for assessing medication adherence in chronic disease populations. Importantly, MARS-5 is capable of distinguishing more clearly between intentional and unintentional non-adherence. The Turkish validity and reliability of MARS-5 were previously established by Temeloglu Sen et al. [24]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate medication adherence in patients with uveitis using the MARS-5 scale.

According to our findings, medication adherence among uveitis patients was relatively high compared with similar studies. Only 13.1% of participants demonstrated moderate adherence. When total MARS-5 scores were compared across categories, male participants exhibited significantly lower mean adherence scores than females, and this association remained robust in the multivariable model after adjustment for relevant sociodemographic and clinical variables. In the broader adherence literature, male sex has been associated with lower adherence in certain chronic disease populations. Although, employed participants demonstrated lower mean MARS-5 scores in unadjusted analyses, employment status did not retain statistical significance in the multivariable regression model. This suggests that the observed differences in adherence by employment status may be attributable to confounding factors rather than representing an independent effect. While workload and time constraints among employed individuals may plausibly influence medication-taking behaviors and follow-up attendance, our findings indicate that employment status alone is not an independent predictor of adherence in this cohort. In addition to the anatomical localization of uveitis, several indicators of treatment burden including disease duration, duration of medication use, number of medications, and concomitant systemic therapy were each categorized into subgroups, and mean medication adherence scores were examined. No statistically significant differences were observed across these subgroups. A moderate positive correlation was observed between total MARS-5 score and duration of medication use, and this finding was further supported by linear regression analysis, in which duration of medication use emerged as an independent factor associated with medication adherence. According to Burden of Treatment Theory, treatment burden reflects the cumulative work patients must perform to manage their condition alongside everyday life demands [25]. In the present study, treatment burden was not measured using a validated burden-of-treatment instrument but was approximated through clinically relevant indicators such as disease duration, duration of medication use, number of medications, and the presence of concomitant systemic therapy. Although no significant differences in adherence were observed across treatment burden subgroups in our analyses, this does not preclude an effect of treatment burden on adherence. Different components of treatment burden may exert varying and potentially opposing influences on adherence behavior. For instance, increased regimen complexity or additional systemic treatments may heighten cognitive and practical demands, thereby challenging adherence. Conversely, longer duration of medication use may facilitate adaptation, routinization of medication-taking behavior, and stronger patient–treatment engagement, which could support adherence over time. The positive association observed between adherence and duration of medication use in our study is consistent with this interpretation.

Item-level analysis of the MARS-5 revealed that the lowest mean score corresponded to the statement “I forget to take my medication” suggesting that unintentional non-adherence may represent a relevant challenge in this patient population. In addition, exploratory findings from the adherence barrier questionnaire indicated that fear of potential side effects and medication-related difficulties, such as unpleasant taste, large tablet size, or concerns about injections, were among the most frequently reported barriers. Findings derived from the adherence barrier questionnaire should be interpreted as exploratory, as the instrument has not undergone formal psychometric validation; nevertheless, they provide clinically relevant descriptive insights into commonly perceived obstacles to long-term treatment adherence in chronic uveitis.

This study has several limitations. As it was conducted at a single center, the results may not be generalizable to all uveitis populations. As with many self-report adherence measures, the MARS-5 is susceptible to social desirability, recall, and ceiling biases, which may lead to an overestimation of adherence levels, particularly in chronic conditions requiring prolonged immunosuppressive therapy such as uveitis. In addition, the absence of detailed data on medication type, dosing frequency, and disease severity represents an important limitation and may have influenced the observed associations between demographic characteristics and medication adherence. Finally, future studies employing validated treatment burden measures are warranted to better delineate how distinct dimensions of treatment burden influence adherence in chronic uveitis.

Overall, our study demonstrates that although medication adherence among patients with chronic uveitis is generally high, it is closely associated with specific sociodemographic and behavioral factors. These findings provide a basis for the development of targeted and personalized adherence-enhancing interventions. For instance, the lower adherence observed among male patients and working individuals suggests that tailored support strategies, such as time-sensitive dosing schedules and simplified treatment routines, may be particularly beneficial in these subgroups. The prominence of unintentional non-adherence driven by medication forgetfulness further indicates that reminder-based interventions, including SMS notifications, mobile applications, or other digital tools, could play an important role in supporting daily medication use. In parallel, to address intentional non-adherence related to concerns about side effects or medication formulation, adherence interventions should emphasize structured patient education, transparent discussion of potential adverse effects, and strengthened physician–patient communication. When clinically appropriate, individualized treatment modifications such as adjustments in tablet size, taste, route of administration, or consideration of alternative formulations may further reduce treatment-related barriers and support long-term adherence.
