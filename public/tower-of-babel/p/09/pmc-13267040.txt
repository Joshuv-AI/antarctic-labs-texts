P4-ATPase Drs2 regulates virulence, stress adaptation, and autophagy in Candida glabrata
Authors: Geng Lv, Ke-Zhi Chen, Yi-Hui Song, Jun-Tao Zhao, Si-Jia Huang, Lu-Ling Wang, Jin-Yan Liu, Ming-Jie Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCandida glabrata is an opportunistic pathogen with increasing clinical significance due to its innate antifungal resistance. This study reveals the critical role of the phospholipid flippase Drs2 (P4-ATPase) in maintaining fungal pathogenicity and stress adaptation. The DRS2 deletion mutant exhibited impaired growth, heightened drug susceptibility, and defective stress responses, including compromised oxidative stress tolerance and autophagy. Importantly, the mutant showed reduced survival in macrophages while eliciting stronger proinflammatory responses, along with attenuated virulence in a Galleria mellonella infection model. Further analysis demonstrated diminished biofilm formation and impaired metabolic flexibility under nutrient-limited conditions. Through transcriptomic profiling, we identified Drs2 as a key regulator of MAPK signaling pathways, cell wall integrity, and oxidative stress responses. These findings collectively establish Drs2 as a central coordinator of membrane homeostasis that critically links cellular stress adaptation to virulence in C. glabrata. These findings identify Drs2 as a promising target for antifungal intervention and provide new insight into the biological functions of lipid flippases in fungal pathogens.KEYWORDS: Candida glabrata, Drs2, virulence, MAPK signaling, stress response, biofilm

Introduction

Cells and organelles are defined by lipid bilayer membranes and their associated membrane proteins. To support essential eukaryotic processes, including secretion, endocytosis, and signal transduction, the lipid composition of the two bilayer leaflets is maintained in a highly asymmetric arrangement (Doktorova et al. 2020). Type 4 P-type ATPases (P4-ATPases) function as lipid flippases that actively translocate phospholipids from the exoplasmic or luminal leaflet to the cytoplasmic leaflet, thereby preserving this asymmetry (Norris et al. 2024). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, five P4-ATPases (Drs2, Dnf1, Dnf2, Dnf3, and Neo1) have been identified, each contributing to the biogenesis of intracellular transport vesicles involved in biosynthetic and endocytic trafficking pathways (Takar et al. 2019). Among them, Drs2 primarily transports phosphatidylserine, and to a lesser extent phosphatidylethanolamine, from the luminal to the cytoplasmic leaflet, implicating it in late-stage secretory vesicle formation (Xu et al. 2013). Drs2 has also been directly shown to mediate phospholipid translocation, supporting the movement of fluorescent NBD-labeled phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) analogs across the plasma membrane and Golgi apparatus (Natarajan et al. 2004). The evolutionarily conserved Cdc50 protein family acts as an essential chaperone for Drs2, ensuring correct folding, endoplasmic reticulum export, stability, and proper plasma membrane localization of the P4-ATPase (Shin and Takatsu 2025). Similar functions of P4-ATPases have been demonstrated across diverse organisms, including Caenorhabditis elegans (Wehman et al. 2011), Homo sapiens (Dieudonné et al. 2023), Leishmania donovani (Weingärtner et al. 2010), and Arabidopsis thaliana (Wang et al. 2021). In Cryptococcus neoformans, deletion of the DRS2 homolog APT1 increases susceptibility to oxidative and nitrosative stress, disrupts vesicle trafficking, and enhances sensitivity to multiple antifungal agents (Hu et al. 2017). Likewise, in the plant pathogen Verticillium dahliae, VdDrs2 localizes to the plasma membrane, vacuoles, and trans-Golgi network, and its disruption severely impairs toxin secretion (Ren et al. 2022). In Candida albicans, P4-ATPases have also been implicated in regulating virulence and antifungal drug susceptibility (Basante-Bedoya et al. 2022).

Our previous work demonstrated that CDC50, which encodes the chaperone (β-subunit) of the C. glabrata P4-ATPase complex, plays a crucial role in regulating yeast budding, antifungal susceptibility, and virulence (Chen et al. 2023). Building on these findings, the present study uses homologous recombination to generate a DRS2 deletion mutant in a standard C. glabrata strain. By systematically comparing the knockout strain with the wild type across multiple phenotypic dimensions, including drug sensitivity, cell wall integrity, oxidative stress tolerance, biofilm formation, and virulence in the Galleria mellonella infection model, we aim to define the functional contribution of Drs2 to C. glabrata pathogenicity and environmental adaptation.

Material and methods

Strains and media

All strains used in this study were derived from the C. glabrata reference strain ATCC 2001. Depending on experimental requirements, cultures were grown on YPD medium (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) or SD medium prepared with Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB) containing amino acids and ammonium sulfate (Coolaber, Beijing, China) supplemented with 2% glucose. Nitrogen starvation experiments were performed using SD-N medium, composed of YNB without amino acids or ammonium sulfate (Coolaber) and supplemented with 2% glucose.

Frozen glycerol stocks stored at −80 °C were revived by inoculation into YPD liquid medium and incubated overnight at 30 °C with shaking at 200 r/min. For assays requiring logarithmic-phase cultures, 200 μL of the overnight pre-culture was transferred into 5 mL of fresh YPD medium and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 200 r/min for 4 h to obtain log-phase cells.

Growth curve analysis, susceptibility testing, and spot assay

Logarithmic-phase cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 in the same media (either YNB or YPD) and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 200 r/min. Growth curves were generated by recording OD600 measurements at defined time intervals in YNB or YPD liquid medium.

Antifungal susceptibility was assessed using CLSI M27M44S-compliant broth microdilution methodology. The DL-96Fungus colorimetric system (Dier Biotech, Guangzhou) served as the testing platform, with MIC endpoints determined following 24 h incubation at 37 °C. Fungal growth was indicated by colorimetric conversion from blue (negative) to red (positive). Quality assurance included reference strains C. parapsilosis ATCC 22019 and C. krusei ATCC 6258.

For spot assays, log-phase cells were harvested and diluted in PBS to 1 × 106 cells/mL, followed by serial tenfold dilutions to a final concentration of 100 cells/mL. A 10 μL aliquot from each dilution was spotted onto solid media supplemented with the relevant stress-inducing agents or additives. Plates were incubated overnight at 30 °C, and colony growth was documented for comparative analysis.

Yeast intracellular ROS detection assay

C. glabrata strains were grown overnight in YPD medium at 30 °C with shaking and subsequently subcultured into a fresh medium to reach a logarithmic growth phase. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using 2‘,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime). Cells were incubated with 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark and washed three times with PBS to remove the excess probe. Stained cells were then exposed to oxidative stress by treatment with 0.6 mmol/L menadione or 3% H2O2 in SC medium for 60 min at 30 °C. After treatment, cells were washed and resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric analysis using a BriCyte E6 flow cytometer (Mindray, Shenzhen, China). Cell populations were gated based on forward and side scatter parameters, and fluorescence intensity was quantified in the FITC channel to assess ROS accumulation.

Yeast autophagy flux western blot analysis

Log-phase C. glabrata cells were incubated in nitrogen-starvation medium or medium containing 3% H2O2 for 2, 4, and 8 h to induce autophagy. At each time point, cells were harvested, and total protein was extracted using a yeast protein extraction kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). Protein lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany).

β-tubulin mouse monoclonal antibody (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) was used as the loading control. GFP-Tag mouse monoclonal antibody (recombinant; Epizyme) was used to detect GFP-Atg8 and free-GFP. Goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), HRP-conjugated (Epizyme), served as the secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using the Omni-ECL™ Femto Light Chemiluminescence Kit (Epizyme) and a standard chemiluminescence imaging system.

Macrophage infection assay

The macrophage infection assay was performed according to the protocol described by Chen et al. (2023). THP-1 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were revived and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. After confirming cell viability, THP-1 cells were adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL and seeded into 24-well plates. Differentiation into adherent macrophage-like cells was induced by treatment with Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 48 h.

Log-phase C. glabrata cells were added to the differentiated THP-1 macrophages and co-cultured for 2 or 4 h. After incubation, culture supernatants were collected for cytokine quantification using a chemiluminescence immunoassay on the Immulite 1000 system (Siemens, Munich, Germany). Non-phagocytosed yeast cells were removed by washing the wells with PBS. Infected macrophages were then either examined microscopically or lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) to release intracellular yeast cells, which were quantified by serial dilution plating on YPD agar.

In vivo virulence assay in Galleria mellonella larvae

The virulence of C. glabrata was assessed using Galleria mellonella (greater wax moth) larvae (Tianjin Huiyude Biological Technology Co., Tianjin, China) as an in vivo infection model. Healthy final-instar larvae weighing 300–400 mg were selected, surface-disinfected with 75% ethanol, and allowed to air-dry. For each experimental group, 10 larvae were injected with 10 μL of a C. glabrata suspension (1 × 106 CFU/larva in PBS) into the last left proleg using a microinjector (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA). Control groups received either PBS (sham control) or no injection (naive control). Following inoculation, larvae were placed in sterile Petri dishes and maintained at 37 °C. Survival was recorded every 24 h for 7 d. Larvae were considered dead when they displayed melanization and failed to respond to gentle tactile stimulation.

Biofilm formation assay and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

The biofilm-forming ability of C. glabrata was assessed using crystal violet staining to quantify total biomass, XTT reduction assays to measure metabolic activity, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for structural visualization. Log-phase cells were inoculated into RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) in 96-well plates or onto plastic coverslips (for SEM analysis) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm development. Non-adherent cells were removed by gentle washing with PBS.

For the XTT reduction assay, biofilms were grown for 48 h, washed with PBS, and incubated with 100 μL of XTT solution (0.5 mg/mL XTT with 1 μmol/L menadione; Sigma-Aldrich) at 30 °C in the dark for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to quantify metabolic activity.

For SEM imaging, coverslip-grown biofilms were washed twice with PBS and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were then washed three times with PBS and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 1–2 h. After three additional PBS washes, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, and 90% for 15 min each), followed by three 20-min incubations in 100% ethanol at 4 °C. Dehydrated samples were critical-point dried using an EM CPD300 dryer (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), mounted onto aluminum stubs, and sputter-coated with a 10-nm gold layer using a JEE-420 coating system (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Biofilm ultrastructure was visualized using a SU8010 scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Transcriptomic profile analysis

Wild-type (WT) and ΔDrs2 strains were cultured overnight in YPD medium at 30 °C and grown to a logarithmic phase. Cell pellets were harvested and submitted to Majorbio BioPharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for RNA sequencing and downstream bioinformatic analysis. Total RNA was extracted, and high-quality samples were used to construct sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina).

Transcript abundance was quantified as transcripts per kilobase million (TPM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria |log2 FC| ≥ 0.585 and an adjusted p < 0.05. Functional characterization of DEGs was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Kanehisa et al. 2025). Enrichment analyses were conducted relative to the full transcriptome background to identify significantly affected biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, and metabolic pathways.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Evolutionary relationships of the C. glabrata Drs2 protein with fungal orthologs and other P4-ATPases were analyzed using maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic reconstruction in MEGA 11 software (Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA, USA). Protein sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm, and phylogenetic trees were generated with 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support.

Unless otherwise stated, all experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent biological replicates. Statistical differences among groups were evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. Survival analyses for Galleria mellonella infection assays were performed using the log-rank test. Transcriptomic data visualization was conducted in RStudio (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA), while both GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and RStudio were used for figure generation and statistical analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of the DRS2 gene and protein in C. glabrata

Given the close evolutionary relationship between C. glabrata and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the S. cerevisiae Drs2 protein sequence was retrieved from the UniProt database and used as a query for a BLAST search against the C. glabrata genome via FungiDB (Stajich et al. 2012). This analysis identified the C. glabrata Drs2 ortholog (gene: CAGL0G06270g). To further examine evolutionary conservation, Drs2 homologs from clinically relevant pathogenic Candida species, including C. albicans, C. parapsilosis, C. tropicalis, and C. krusei, were collected, along with sequences of other P4-type ATPases (Neo1, Dnf1–Dnf3) from both C. glabrata and S. cerevisiae.

Phylogenetic reconstruction using MEGA and a maximum likelihood (ML) model with 1000 bootstrap replicates revealed strong sequence conservation within the P4-type ATPase family (Figure 1a). Notably, C. glabrata Drs2 clustered most closely with its S. cerevisiae counterpart, forming a well-supported clade distinct from Drs2 homologs in other pathogenic Candida species (Figure 1b). This separation suggests a potential functional divergence of Drs2 among pathogenic yeasts.

Drs2 is essential for normal growth rate, drug susceptibility, and cell wall integrity in C. glabrata

Growth curve analysis over a 24 h period revealed clear differences in proliferation among the ΔDrs2, wild-type, and complemented strains. In both YNB and YPD media, the ΔDrs2 strain exhibited markedly slower growth, indicating a pronounced growth defect. During the exponential growth in YPD, the knockout strain required approximately 4 additional hours to increase from OD600 0.1 to 1.5 compared with the wild-type and complemented strains. A similar delay was observed in YNB, where the knockout required ~2 extra hours to reach OD600 1.0. Despite these early-phase defects, all strains ultimately reached comparable optical densities in the stationary phase, suggesting that Drs2 primarily contributes to efficient rapid growth (Figure 1c).

Broth microdilution assays demonstrated that ΔDrs2 cells were significantly more susceptible to antifungal drugs than wild-type and complemented strains (Table 3). The knockout strain exhibited an increased sensitivity to azoles (inhibitors of ergosterol biosynthesis) and to caspofungin (an echinocandin targeting β-1,3-D-glucan synthase), whereas no significant difference in susceptibility to micafungin was observed, likely due to its MIC being below the detection limit. These findings imply that loss of DRS2 may exacerbate the effects of antifungal agents that impair membrane or cell wall homeostasis. Consistent with this, growth inhibition assays showed that ΔDrs2 cells were severely compromised in the presence of cell wall-perturbing agents such as congo red and calcofluor white, exhibiting substantially reduced viability relative to wild-type controls (Figure 2a). Together, these results indicate that Drs2 is critical for maintaining membrane and cell wall integrity in C. glabrata, and its deletion renders the fungus significantly more vulnerable to multiple antifungal classes targeting these essential cellular structures.

Drs2 is essential for oxidative stress response in C. glabrata

The ability to resist and adapt to oxidative stress is critical for C. glabrata survival in diverse environments and during interactions with host phagocytic cells (Seider et al. 2014). To assess the role of Drs2 in this process, hydrogen peroxide and menadione were used to induce oxidative stress. On the agar plates containing these stressors, the ΔDrs2 strain showed markedly increased sensitivity compared with the wild-type and complemented strains. Consistently, spot assays demonstrated that the ΔDrs2 strain exhibited pronounced growth defects when exposed to either hydrogen peroxide or menadione (Figure 2a).

Flow cytometry analysis using the ROS-sensitive probe DCFH-DA revealed that ΔDrs2 cells accumulated significantly higher basal ROS levels than wild-type cells, indicating a compromised oxidative stress response (Figure 2b). Complementation of Drs2 fully restored wild-type ROS homeostasis and stress resistance, confirming that the observed phenotype is specifically attributable to loss of DRS2. Together, these findings demonstrate that Drs2 plays a key role in the oxidative stress defense of C. glabrata, enabling the fungus to maintain redox balance under hostile conditions.

Deletion of DRS2 in C. glabrata influences autophagy in response to nitrogen deprivation and oxidative stress

Autophagy is a key physiological process that enables yeast cells to adapt to environmental stress, maintain intracellular homeostasis, and preserve viability under adverse conditions (Farré and Subramani 2016). Disruption of this pathway compromises cellular balance and impairs the ability of yeast to respond effectively to stress (Awan and Deng 2014). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, loss of DRS2 has been shown to impair autophagy by disrupting ATG9 trafficking and limiting autophagic flux (Puig-Tintó et al. 2023). Because autophagy remains poorly characterized in C. glabrata, we used a GFP-Atg8 fusion protein assay to evaluate the role of Drs2 in this process. Following the approach used in studies of ATG1-mediated autophagy (Shimamura et al. 2019), a GFP-Atg8 construct was introduced into both the wild-type and ΔDrs2 strains. Cells were exposed to nitrogen starvation or 3% H2O2 for 2, 4, and 8 h, and autophagic activity was assessed by monitoring GFP-Atg8 degradation and accumulation of free GFP, using β-tubulin as a loading control.

In the wild-type strain, GFP-Atg8 was efficiently processed over time, consistent with normal autophagic flux. In contrast, the ΔDrs2 strain exhibited markedly reduced GFP-Atg8 degradation and accumulation of free GFP, indicating a substantial defect in autophagy under both nitrogen deprivation and oxidative stress (Figure 2c,d). These findings demonstrate that Drs2 is required for functional autophagy in C. glabrata, and its loss likely contributes to the diminished oxidative stress tolerance observed in the ΔDrs2 mutant.

The ΔDrs2 strain enhances macrophage phagocytosis of C. glabrata and increases secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines

Compared with other pathogenic Candida species, C. glabrata exhibits a remarkable ability to persist within macrophages, contributing to its pathogenicity and drug tolerance (Arastehfar et al. 2023). Because macrophages rely heavily on oxidative mechanisms to eliminate fungal pathogens, and given the role of Drs2 in oxidative stress adaptation, we investigated how Drs2 influences the interaction between C. glabrata and macrophages. In intracellular survival assays, ΔDrs2, wild-type, and complemented strains were co-cultured with differentiated THP-1 macrophages for 2 and 4 h. At both time points, the ΔDrs2 strain exhibited significantly reduced intracellular survival compared with wild-type and complemented strains, indicating that Drs2 is required for efficient survival of C. glabrata inside macrophages (Figure 3a).

Cytokine measurements further revealed functional consequences of DRS2 loss. Following co-culture, levels of TNF-α and IL-1β were quantified by chemiluminescence. Macrophages infected with the ΔDrs2 strain produced higher levels of both cytokines than those infected with wild-type or complemented strains, showing a modest but nonsignificant increase at 2 h (p > 0.05) and a significant elevation at 4 h (p < 0.05) (Figure 3b,c). These findings demonstrate that loss of DRS2 enhances macrophage-mediated clearance of C. glabrata and triggers a stronger pro-inflammatory response, highlighting a key role for Drs2 in immune evasion and intracellular survival.

Deletion of the DRS2 gene attenuates the virulence of C. glabrata in the Galleria mellonella larvae model

Because Drs2 plays key roles in oxidative stress adaptation and autophagy in C. glabrata, we hypothesized that its deletion would also impair fungal virulence. To test this, we employed the Galleria mellonella infection model. Healthy larvae were randomly assigned to four groups and injected with sterile PBS, DRS2, wild-type, and complemented strains.

Larvae were monitored daily, and individuals displaying melanization and loss of response to gentle touch were recorded as dead. Survival analysis revealed a pronounced reduction in virulence for the ΔDrs2 strain compared with both the wild-type and complemented strains (p < 0.05) (Figure 4). By day 3 post-infection, only 30% of larvae infected with wild-type or complemented strain remained alive, whereas 60% of larvae infected with the ΔDrs2 strain survived. These findings demonstrate that deletion of Drs2 markedly attenuates C. glabrata virulence in vivo, consistent with the observed defects in stress adaptation and intracellular survival.

Transcriptome sequencing reveals that DRS2 deletion induces critical alterations in the MAPK signaling pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the functions of Drs2 in C. glabrata, we performed transcriptome sequencing on ATCC 2001 and ΔDrs2 strains. The quality control metrics of transcriptome sequencing are presented in Table S1, demonstrating satisfactory sequencing quality. A total of 228 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was identified, including 148 upregulated and 80 downregulated transcripts in the ΔDrs2 mutant (Figure 5a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant changes in pathways associated with the extracellular matrix, biofilm matrix, plasma membrane components, and sulfate assimilation. KEGG analysis further indicated substantial alterations in the MAPK signaling pathway (yeast), sulfur metabolism, and histidine metabolism.

The MAPK pathway is essential for maintaining cell wall integrity, regulating the cell cycle, and facilitating sexual reproduction in yeast, and it plays key roles in virulence and antifungal susceptibility of C. glabrata (Román et al. 2020). Given our experimental observations demonstrating impaired growth and compromised cell wall integrity in the ΔDrs2 strain, the transcriptomic disruption of MAPK signaling provides a plausible mechanistic explanation. These findings support the hypothesis that Drs2 may exert its effects on virulence, stress adaptation, and drug sensitivity in C. glabrata through modulation of the MAPK pathway.

Deletion of DRS2 affects biofilm formation and nutrient utilization in C. glabrata

Transcriptomic analysis of the ΔDrs2 strain revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in pathways related to biofilm formation and sulfur metabolism. Biofilm development is a major virulence determinant of C. glabrata, contributing to adhesion, environmental persistence, and antifungal resistance (Hassan et al. 2021). To evaluate whether Drs2 influences this phenotype, we performed XTT assays and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Both metabolic activity measurements and structural imaging demonstrated that ΔDrs2 cells exhibited markedly reduced biofilm-forming capacity compared with the wild-type and complemented strains (Figure 6a,b).

Sulfur plays an essential role in numerous metabolic processes, including protein synthesis, coenzyme production, and redox regulation (Hébert et al. 2011). Its functions are tightly linked to iron homeostasis, as both elements are required for iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster biogenesis (Gupta and Outten 2020). Disruption of sulfur metabolism can therefore impair iron utilization and diminish fungal fitness. To test whether Drs2 influences these pathways, we assessed growth under sulfur- and iron-limiting conditions. The ΔDrs2 strain displayed reduced growth on sulfur-free YNB medium and on YPD supplemented with the iron chelator deferoxamine. Moreover, growth defects persisted even under iron-excess conditions (6 mmol/L FeCl3), indicating impaired iron handling in the absence of DRS2 (Figure 6c).

Because C. glabrata pathogenicity is closely linked to its metabolic flexibility and ability to use diverse carbon sources (Chew et al. 2019), we also examined growth on different carbon substrates. The ΔDrs2 strain showed substantial growth impairment on mannitol, sorbitol, lactate, and glycerol, whereas growth on glucose and maltose remained unaffected (Figure 6c). These findings indicate that Drs2 is required for optimal nutrient utilization and ecological adaptability across multiple host-relevant niches.

Discussion

Lipids are fundamental structural constituents of eukaryotic cell and organelle membranes, providing not only a physical barrier but also a dynamic platform for regulating membrane protein localization and activity. A defining feature of these membranes is lipid asymmetry, which is essential for normal cellular function and viability. This asymmetry is maintained through the coordinated actions of flippases, floppases, and scramblases, each contributing to the selective distribution of phospholipids between the two leaflets of the bilayer (Sakuragi and Nagata 2023). Among these, flippases, most commonly P4-ATPases, play a central role by hydrolyzing ATP to transport phospholipids from the outer to the cytoplasmic leaflet. Through this activity, P4-ATPases help preserve membrane composition and curvature, which are critical for intracellular processes such as vesicular trafficking, signal transduction, and the maintenance of organelle identity. Disruption of lipid asymmetry can therefore have profound effects on membrane physiology and downstream cellular pathways, underscoring the importance of flippase-mediated regulation in fungal biology and pathogenicity. To elucidate the functional role of Drs2 in transmembrane phospholipid translocation, we performed annexin V binding assays to detect phosphatidylserine (PS) externalization on the plasma membrane. The experimental data demonstrated that Drs2 knockout significantly increased PS exposure on the cell membrane surface (Method S1 and Figure S2). However, our study did not systematically characterize Drs2‘s regulatory effects on PS distribution in other membrane compartments, including the Golgi apparatus. Notably, evidence indicates that in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Drs2 deficiency impairs the vesicular trafficking of chitin synthase III (Chs3) to the plasma membrane, consequently causing cell wall biosynthesis defects (Xu et al. 2013). In Candida albicans, other P-type ATPases, such as the P-type calcium pump Spf1, have been demonstrated to regulate pathogenicity during systemic infection by maintaining ER calcium homeostasis and modulating ER-PM contact sites (Ji et al. 2025). By analogy to calcium homeostasis, the membrane phospholipid asymmetry maintained by P4-ATPases may also influence the synthesis and secretion of cell wall-associated proteins, thereby affecting cell wall biogenesis, signal transduction, and ultimately the virulence and drug susceptibility of Candida glabrata. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying phospholipid homeostasis remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

In our previous work, we demonstrated that Cdc50, the β-subunit of a lipid flippase complex in C. glabrata, plays an essential role in cell wall architecture, yeast budding, and susceptibility to clinically relevant antifungal agents. Building on these findings, the present study extends this line of investigation by characterizing the functions of another lipid flippase, Drs2. Consistent with the phenotypes observed in the cdc50 mutant, deletion of DRS2 impaired several key traits, including biofilm formation and oxidative stress adaptation. Notably, however, the ΔDrs2 strain also exhibited phenotypes not previously associated with cdc50 deletion, revealing functional distinctions between these related flippase components. These observations suggest that Drs2 and Cdc50, while both integral to membrane homeostasis, contribute uniquely to the physiology and pathogenic potential of C. glabrata.

In model yeast systems, Drs2 has been shown to interact with and stabilize the TRAPPIII tethering complex, thereby regulating ATG9 trafficking and influencing autophagic flux. Autophagy is a highly conserved eukaryotic process that mediates the degradation and recycling of cytoplasmic components and damaged organelles, playing essential roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis, adapting to nutrient stress, and defending against pathogen invasion. Despite its importance, autophagy in C. glabrata remains relatively understudied. Using GFP-Atg8 degradation assays under nitrogen starvation and oxidative stress, we demonstrated that Drs2 is indispensable for functional autophagy in C. glabrata. The ΔDrs2 strain failed to execute normal autophagic processing under both conditions, revealing a previously uncharacterized role for Drs2 in the regulation of autophagy in this pathogen. These findings not only broaden our understanding of autophagy-associated mechanisms in C. glabrata but also provide a mechanistic explanation for the impaired oxidative stress adaptation and reduced environmental fitness observed in the Drs2-deficient strain. Specifically, our results indicate that Drs2 contributes to cellular survival in hostile environments by modulating autophagic responses. However, the molecular basis linking Drs2 to autophagy regulation in C. glabrata, particularly its potential involvement in selective autophagy, remains unclear. In S. cerevisiae, Drs2 participates directly in ATG9 vesicle trafficking (Kriegenburg et al. 2022), underscoring the importance of lipid flippase-mediated membrane asymmetry in autophagic processes. How analogous mechanisms operate in C. glabrata warrants further investigation, especially given the pathogen’s reliance on stress adaptation for survival during infection. Elucidating the autophagy pathway in greater depth will be critical for understanding its role in environmental resilience and antifungal tolerance, ultimately supporting the development of novel targeted therapeutic strategies.

Our transcriptomic comparison between the ΔDrs2 strain and the ATCC 2001 wild type revealed significant disturbances in the MAPK signaling pathway, a conserved regulatory cascade involved in stress responses, cell wall integrity, and fungal virulence. Several key MAPK components exhibited notable expression changes, including the MAP4K gene STE20 (important for cell wall integrity, hyperosmotic stress adaptation, and virulence) (Calcagno et al. 2004), the PAK kinase gene CLA4, the phosphatase regulator PTP2, the MAPK genes KSS1 and FUS3 (crucial for C. glabrata pathogenicity) (Mo et al. 2024), and the cell wall integrity pathway kinase SLT2 (which regulates transcription factors involved in echinocandin tolerance) (Nagayoshi et al. 2014). These alterations likely contribute to the broad phenotypic defects observed in the ΔDrs2 strain. Interestingly, similar perturbations in MAPK signaling were also detected in our previous transcriptomic analysis of cdc50 mutants (Figure S1), suggesting a conserved regulatory link between lipid flippases and MAPK pathway function.

Given that lipid flippases participate in intracellular trafficking and vesicular transport, we propose that disruption of Drs2-mediated lipid asymmetry may impair the trafficking of essential cellular components, including those involved in cell wall maintenance, and disrupt proper signal transmission to MAPK modules. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms connecting lipid flippase activity to MAPK signaling remain unresolved. Future studies dissecting these interactions will be essential for fully understanding how membrane asymmetry governs stress signaling and pathogenicity in C. glabrata.

Additionally, our study revealed that Drs2 is closely linked to iron-sulfur cluster metabolism and carbon source adaptation in C. glabrata. Given the established roles of Drs2 in intracellular transport and membrane lipid remodeling, we propose that its deletion disrupts the trafficking and assimilation of iron- and sulfur-containing metabolites, resulting in impaired growth on nutrient-deficient media. The observed defects under both iron-limited and iron-excess conditions further suggest a broader dysfunction in metal homeostasis. In contrast, the contribution of Drs2 and P4-ATPases more generally, to carbon metabolism remains poorly understood. The growth defects of the ΔDrs2 strain on non-fermentable and alternative carbon sources point to an underexplored connection between lipid flippase activity and metabolic flexibility. Future work dissecting this relationship may uncover new aspects of fungal nutrient sensing and adaptation.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that the lipid flippase gene DRS2 plays a pivotal role in C. glabrata physiology, contributing to cell wall integrity, antifungal drug resistance, oxidative stress adaptation, biofilm formation, and broader environmental fitness. Our findings further suggest that Drs2 regulates these phenotypes, at least in part, through its influence on autophagy and MAPK signaling. These insights expand current understanding of lipid flippase function in pathogenic yeasts and identify Drs2 as a potential target for therapeutic intervention against C. glabrata infections. Future work is needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms linking Drs2-mediated lipid asymmetry to stress signaling and survival pathways, which may uncover new opportunities for antifungal strategy development.
