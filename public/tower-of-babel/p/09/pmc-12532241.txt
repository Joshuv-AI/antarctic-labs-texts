SOLUTE Study: An Obituary for Normal Saline?
Authors: Priyankar K Datta, Prachee Sathe, Riddhi Kundu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the findings of the salt based or BaLanced SolUtion–Trends Existing in Indian Intensive Care Units (SOLUTE) study published by Gupta et al. in the recent issue of IJCCM.1 We must laud the authors for attempting to study the intravenous fluid practices in different ICUs in a country as heterogeneous as ours. The authors reported a better safety profile with balanced salt solutions with better renal safety, and a shorter ICU LOS compared with normal saline. This is not an unexpected finding given recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systemic reviews, and meta-analyses reporting similar findings with balanced salt solutions, though not of the same magnitude as reported in the SOLUTE study.2–4

Hence, we must examine the findings of this study in greater detail.

The authors reported a decrease in the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) from day 1 to day 3 reflected by their creatinine levels in the normal saline group, along with an improvement in urine output. This beckons the question, if normal saline was indeed harmful to their kidneys on day 1, how was a greater cumulative balance of saline infusion on day 2 and day 3 making them better subsequently? The authors have not reported the chloride levels at baseline or on any of the days of the study. The well-known mechanism of higher chloride levels in macula densa causing afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction and contributing to AKI was not explored in this study, which is a missed opportunity.5 Even if there were missing data, we feel readers would have benefitted immensely, if the authors had reported the remaining data that were collected regarding chloride levels at baseline and on the study days 1–3.

This has been described as a described as a “prospective observational study,” but we see “intervention” being mentioned in multiple places. Was this rather a quasi-randomized model? The study was conducted across 44 ICUs over 3 years, who contributed to more than 2,700 patient data. This would amount to ~ 20 patients per ICU, and nearly 7 patients per ICU per year. We wonder the reason for the data collection being restricted to such a small fraction of ICU admissions when there were no obvious exclusion criteria.

The diagnosis of AKI was made on Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss of kidney function, and End-stage kidney disease (RIFLE) or kidney disease: improving global outcomes (KDIGO) criteria in this study. However, the number of patients belonging to each of the stages of AKI in each group was not mentioned. Since there was no difference in the requirement of renal replacement between the three groups, we wonder if it was primarily a milder rise in creatinine that contributed to the outcome difference. This would make the findings of the study less significant clinically, even if it became statistically meaningful.

The timeline when baseline creatinine was measured and the difference in time periods between the baseline creatinine, and day 1 creatinine has not been mentioned. This could be a potential confounder if there were group differences in periodicity. The authors also reported a lower incidence of AKI with balanced crystalloid when compared with Ringer's lactate. We wonder what could be the possible reason for this finding. We also feel a propensity-matched analysis would be of benefit, as it would balance out potential confounders. Since the SOLUTE study had three well-defined patient cohorts, a Bonferroni correction of resultant p-values is important to avoid false-positive results. In addition, the difference between Figures 6A and 6B is not clear to us and has not been mentioned in the manuscript.

The use of isotonic crystalloids is recommended in patients with acute brain injury, with recent data favoring normal saline over balanced crystalloids.6,7 This study cohort does not report the number of patients who were admitted with acute brain injury. It would have been interesting to know if normal saline was as harmful in this subgroup as it was in the rest of the cohort.

We feel that given normal saline is still the most commonly used fluid, especially in resource-poor settings, the missing links in the study leave us with unanswered questions. We are unsure if this study findings are enough to shun the use of normal saline in the ICU, or if we should look for more robust data emanating from the Indian population.
