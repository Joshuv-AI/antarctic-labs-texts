Efficacy and safety of oral 5‐FU‐based adjuvant chemotherapy for geriatric patients with colorectal cancer: Integrated analysis of seven clinical trials conducted at the Japanese Foundation for Multidisciplinary Treatment of Cancer
Authors: Nobuaki Hoshino, Mitsuro Kanda, Koji Oba, Toru Aoyama, Michitaka Honda, Kosuke Kashiwabara, Hiromichi Maeda, Shuhei Mayanagi, Masaru Muto, Kenji Tanaka, Hisakazu Yamagishi, Junichi Sakamoto, Kazutaka Obama, Takaki Yoshikawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdjuvant chemotherapy after radical resection is recommended for patients with high‐risk Stages II and III colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the efficacy and safety of adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients require elucidation.MethodsWe analysed data from seven clinical trials conducted by the Japanese Foundation for the Multidisciplinary Treatment of Cancer. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. Survival analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsThis study included 7756 non‐geriatric (<70 years) and 1706 geriatric patients (≥70 years). Adjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved the 5‐year overall survival in non‐geriatric patients (81.6% vs. 73.6%, p < 0.001) but did not significantly improve the survival rate in geriatric patients (78.9% vs. 76.1%, p = 0.129). However, adjuvant chemotherapy was effective for N+ geriatric patients (hazard ratio, 0.48; 95% confidence interval, 0.35–0.66; p < 0.001). Adverse events associated with adjuvant chemotherapy were significantly more frequent in geriatric than in non‐geriatric patients.ConclusionAdjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients with node‐positive CRC was as effective as in non‐geriatric patients, although no benefit was shown in geriatric patients with node‐negative CRC. More adverse events were observed in geriatric patients than in non‐geriatric patients.

INTRODUCTION

Adjuvant chemotherapy after radical surgery is recommended for patients with high‐risk Stage II and III colorectal cancer (CRC) [1, 2]. Six months of 5‐FU‐based anticancer agent ± oxaliplatin is the current standard chemotherapy regimen, and if the risk of recurrence based on T and N factors is judged to be relatively low, the treatment may be shortened to 3 months [3, 4].

The efficacy and safety of adjuvant chemotherapy were determined based on the results of clinical trials that did not include geriatric patients. Most studies reporting on its efficacy in geriatric patients are based on observational studies. However, the advantages and disadvantages of adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients remain unclear [5, 6]. This study examined the efficacy and safety of adjuvant chemotherapy for geriatric patients with CRC by integrating and analysing data from non‐geriatric (<70 years) and geriatric patients (≥70 years) who participated in clinical trials on adjuvant chemotherapy conducted at the Japanese Foundation for Multidisciplinary Treatment of Cancer (JFMC) to date [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

METHODS

Study design and setting

This study was an integrated analysis of seven clinical trials conducted at the JFMC to examine the efficacy and safety of adjuvant chemotherapy for CRC. A summary of each clinical trial is presented in Table S1. Participants in the trials, excluding those who indicated an intention to drop out, were considered candidates for this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with Stage 0, IV, or unknown; double cancers of the colon and rectum; tumours other than colon or rectal cancer; insufficient lymph node dissection or unknown degree of dissection; T0 or unknown; N stage unknown; and unclassified pathology or unknown. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyoto University (approval number: R4796). The informed consent requirement was waived for this integrated analysis; instead, an opt‐out opportunity was provided.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. In this study, patients aged <70 years were defined as non‐geriatric, and those aged ≥70 years were defined as geriatric. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the period from the date of surgery to death. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. Survival analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model. Factors related to OS were examined and subgroup analyses were performed for each factor. All statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS statistical software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Overall, 10,036 patients were enrolled in seven clinical trials. Of these, 9197 were candidates, excluding 265 patients who dropped out and 309 patients who did not satisfy the inclusion criteria (Figure S1). Finally, this study included 7756 non‐geriatric and 1706 geriatric patients. Patient characteristics are listed in Table 1. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to 72.4% of non‐geriatric and 84.5% of geriatric patients. The proportion of patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy was similar between non‐geriatric and geriatric patients in terms of sex, site of tumour occupancy, pathological findings and T stage. The proportion of patients with lymph node metastases who received adjuvant chemotherapy was high, irrespective of age. Older trials before 2000 (JFMC 7,15) compared the adjuvant chemotherapy group with the surgery‐alone group, whereas most recent trials (after 2000; JFMC 33, 35, 37, 38, 41) did not include the surgery‐alone group.

Efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy

In non‐geriatric patients, the 5‐year OS was 81.6% (95% confidence interval [CI], 80.6–82.6%) in the adjuvant chemotherapy group and 73.6% (95% CI, 71.7–75.4%) in the surgery alone group, indicating a significant OS improvement with adjuvant chemotherapy (p < 0.001; Figure 1). In geriatric patients, the 5‐year OS was 78.9% (95% CI, 76.6–81.0%) in the adjuvant chemotherapy group and 76.1% (95% CI, 70.4–80.9%) in the surgery alone group, showing no significant OS improvement with adjuvant chemotherapy (p = 0.129; Figure 1). However, no significant interaction was observed between adjuvant chemotherapy and age (p for interaction = 0.239; Figure 2).

Association between clinicopathological factors and OS

Sex, tumour location, pathological findings, T stage, N stage and study category were significantly associated with OS in both non‐geriatric and geriatric patients. After adjusting for confounders in multivariable analyses, all factors were significantly associated with OS (Table 2). Univariable and multivariable analyses verified that adjuvant chemotherapy was effective in non‐geriatric (unadjusted hazard ratio [HR], 0.68; 95% CI, 0.62–0.75; p < 0.001 and adjusted HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.75–0.94; p = 0.002). Conversely, it was marginal in geriatric patients (unadjusted HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.63–1.06; p = 0.130 and adjusted HR, 1.08; 95% CI, 0.80–1.47; p = 0.602; Table 2).

Adjuvant chemotherapy discontinuation rate for each study

The discontinuation rates of adjuvant chemotherapy are shown in Table S2. In each study, there were slight differences in the discontinuation rates between the geriatric and non‐geriatric groups; however, none of the studies found a significant difference between the two groups.

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis of the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy was performed according to sex, tumour location, pathological findings, T stage, N stage and study category, demonstrating a significant association with OS. In non‐geriatric patients, adjuvant chemotherapy was effective in almost all subgroups, whereas almost all subgroups of geriatric patients did not show apparent benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, excluding the N+ subgroup (HR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.35–0.66; p < 0.001; Figure 3; Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The results of the integrated analysis in this study showed that adjuvant chemotherapy was effective in non‐geriatric patients with CRC, regardless of stage. In contrast, the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy was not apparent in geriatric patients with CRC. However, adjuvant chemotherapy was effective for geriatric patients with node‐positive disease (Stage III), but not in Stage II CRC, with a significant interaction observed between adjuvant chemotherapy and stage. The risk of adverse events was higher in geriatric patients than in non‐geriatric patients.

The efficacy of 5‐FU‐based adjuvant chemotherapy for non‐geriatric patients with Stage III CRC is well established; thus, it is considered effective in geriatric patients [16, 17, 18]. Although there are no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) exclusively involving geriatric patients, many observational studies have reported the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in such patients [5, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. A meta‐analysis integrating 20,987 patients enrolled in nine observational studies reported a significant improvement in OS (HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.53–0.66; p < 0.001) [28].

In 2001, Sargent et al. reported the results of an integrated analysis of seven RCTs initiated in the 1970s and 1980s for Stages II and III colon cancer, which included 3351 patients, of whom, 506 were geriatric [29]. Compared to non‐geriatric patients, the study found no increase in adverse events in geriatric patients who received 5‐FU‐based adjuvant chemotherapy; thus, therapy could be administered safely. Improvements were detected in OS and freedom from recurrence following treatment with adjuvant chemotherapy in both non‐geriatric and geriatric patients because no significant interactions were determined between adjuvant chemotherapy and age (p = 0.61 and 0.33, respectively). Although, no significant difference was discovered in the age subgroup analysis, adjuvant chemotherapy was concluded to be a reasonable treatment for patients with Stages II and III colon cancer, regardless of age. Meanwhile, the sub‐analysis of the meta‐analysis of observational studies did not show any benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy for geriatric patients with Stage II (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.51–1.06; p = 0.09). Furthermore, geriatric patients benefit less from adjuvant chemotherapy than non‐geriatric patients [28]. Many cases are needed to confirm the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients. Conversely, large‐scale data from clinical trials involving geriatric patients are difficult to obtain because they are less likely to participate in clinical trials. In contrast, observational studies are easier to conduct with geriatric patients, though concerns have been raised regarding data reliability. In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis of data from clinical trials involving geriatric patients with Stages II and III CRC and noted the non‐significant efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients. However, when the analysis was restricted to Stage III, adjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved OS. An interaction was also observed between the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy and stage (N stage), indicating differences in its efficacy according to the CRC stage in geriatric patients. Additionally, in non‐geriatric patients, an interaction between adjuvant chemotherapy and stage was observed, with differences in the efficacy being detected between Stages II and III. However, adjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved OS in both groups. The degree of efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in Stage III was similar between the geriatric (HR, 0.48) and non‐geriatric patients (HR, 0.52). This result differs from a previous integrative analysis that found adjuvant chemotherapy to be effective regardless of Stage II or III and from previous reports that found less benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients [22, 29].

Thus, the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy for Stage II CRC is controversial. Current guidelines generally do not recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for Stage II CRC due to the diversity of patients with Stage II CRC, except those with at least one of the following recurrence risks: pT4 bowel obstruction or perforation, lymphovascular or perineural invasion, inadequate lymph node sampling (<12 nodes), or a poorly differentiated pathology [2, 30, 31]. In this study, no data on risk factors were determined for recurrence in Stage II, and adjuvant chemotherapy was found to be effective even in Stage II CRC for non‐geriatric patients, whereas it was non‐effective in geriatric patients. Therefore, it is unreasonable to recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for geriatric patients with Stage II disease. In addition, in the subgroup analysis, adjuvant chemotherapy was effective in geriatric patients with Stage III disease, but its efficacy was not demonstrated in all geriatric patients in the main analysis. Based on the results of this study, the indications for adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with Stage III disease should be carefully considered.

The safety of adjuvant chemotherapy for CRC in geriatric patients with CRC has also been investigated [29, 32], and the interim analysis of RCT involving only geriatric patients reported that it was safe for administration [33]. If the patient (geriatric or otherwise) is in a generally acceptable condition, age alone does not appear to increase the incidence of adverse events associated with adjuvant chemotherapy [34]. Meanwhile, geriatric patients tend to receive less adjuvant chemotherapy due to comorbidities, decreased physical fitness, and lower survival benefits [35]. In addition, the risk of adverse events was higher in geriatric patients than in non‐geriatric patients in this study. In geriatric patients, the occurrence of adverse events is more likely to worsen the quality of life and activities of daily living than in non‐geriatric patients. When considering the administration of adjuvant chemotherapy, it is important to consider comorbidities other than colorectal cancer, general health status, living environment, and the family support system. Therefore, identifying a group of geriatric patients who would benefit more is important. To identify factors that affect the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients, we performed subgroup analyses of factors related to OS, finding a significant interaction between the N stage and efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy. This indicates that the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy varies by stage. However, no differences in the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy were determined in terms of sex, tumour location, pathological findings, T stage, or study period.

In this study, the results of various clinical trials conducted over a long period were integrated and analyzed. Therefore, there may be heterogeneity among the investigators included in this study, and caution is needed when interpreting the results. Some of the chemotherapy regimens in the clinical trials included in this study contained anticancer drugs that are not commonly used today. Supportive therapies for chemotherapy have evolved over time, so it is possible that newer clinical trials have demonstrated higher efficacy and safety compared to older ones. However, because data collected from clinical trials were utilized, follow‐up periods were set for each trial, and appropriate follow‐up was carried out in all of them.

The strengths of this study include the inclusion of a large number of geriatric patients and integrated analysis of data from clinical trials. As data from clinical trials are considered more reliable than those from observational studies, relatively few geriatric patients participate in such trials. Thus, the large amount of data we accessed highlights the value of this study. However, this study had some limitations. Not all the data used in this study were from RCTs. Therefore, although multivariable and subgroup analyses were performed, the influence of confounders cannot be eliminated. Furthermore, adjuvant chemotherapy regimens vary widely, and the used data are from clinical trials on 5‐FU‐based anticancer agents, not uniform anticancer agents. However, it is ethically difficult to conduct an RCT with a surgery alone arm when many reports indicate that 5‐FU‐based adjuvant chemotherapy is effective, even in geriatric patients. Most clinical trials included in this study did not specify detailed inclusion criteria for stage II patients. Therefore, it is possible that the study population included low‐risk stage II patients for recurrence, who are not currently considered candidates for adjuvant chemotherapy. Although performing integrated analyses of anticancer drug dosage and persistence was challenging, geriatric patients tended to have higher rates of dose reduction or cessation of anticancer drugs in clinical trials. This may have influenced the results of integrated analyses. This study is an integrated analysis of clinical trials that targeted geriatric patients whose overall physical condition was relatively well preserved. Geriatric assessments, such as comorbidities, frailty and functional status, were not considered. When determining the indication for adjuvant chemotherapy in geriatric patients in clinical practice, their general condition significantly impacts the decision. Future clinical trials should consider these age‐specific factors. Furthermore, no evidence was found regarding the adverse effects of oxaliplatin or newer anticancer agents on the geriatric population. Currently, FOLFOX, which adds oxaliplatin to 5‐FU‐based chemotherapy, is the standard adjuvant chemotherapy for colorectal cancer. However, because this study was an integrated analysis of somewhat earlier clinical trials, it was not possible to conduct an analysis of oxaliplatin.

CONCLUSION

Adjuvant chemotherapy was determined to be as effective in geriatric patients with Stage III CRC as in non‐geriatric patients; however, no benefit was demonstrated for geriatric patients with Stage II CRC. More adverse events were observed in geriatric patients than in non‐geriatric patients.
