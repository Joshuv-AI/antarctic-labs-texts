Protocol for reconstructing 3D human skin equivalents on endogenous dermal matrix with optional pigmentation
Authors: Khek-Chian Tham, Zhi Heng Lau, Joycelyn X.Y. Lee, Carine Bonnard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrevious in vitro 3D skin models are limited by their reliance on animal-derived collagen, a poor representation of the human dermis, and low throughput. Here, we present a protocol for reconstructing 3D human skin equivalents on an endogenous dermal matrix, in 12- and 24-well format, with optional pigmentation. We describe the expansion and seeding of fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and melanocytes in non-contractile polystyrene scaffolds. We then detail air-lifting and epidermal maturation, resulting in a fibroblast-derived dermal matrix with a fully stratified epidermis.For complete details on the execution of this protocol, please refer to Tham et al.1

Innovation

In vitro 3D full-thickness human skin models provide greater biological relevance than 2D cell cultures. They recapitulate epidermal differentiation and dermal-epidermal interaction. We previously described a full-thickness skin model composed of keratinocytes and fibroblasts embedded in rat-tail collagen I.1 However, this approach has key limitations: xenogeneic collagen compromises dermal fidelity, and frequent tissue contraction reduces experimental robustness and throughput.

To overcome these challenges, we developed a protocol for generating a contraction-free 3D pigmented human skin model. The primary innovation lies in the dermal compartment. Human skin-derived fibroblasts seeded in polystyrene scaffold inserts synthesize and assemble their own extracellular matrix (ECM) and basement membrane. This native human-derived dermal equivalent does not contract and consistently supports the formation of a highly stratified epidermis.

Optional incorporation of primary melanocytes enables the generation of pigmented skin equivalents. Co-culture of a single melanocyte line with different donor-derived fibroblasts and keratinocytes produced pigmented full-thickness skin model, although pigmentation levels may vary while using melanocytes from different donors.

This protocol provides a versatile platform for studying skin barrier function and dermal-epidermal interactions. Using different donor cells enables investigation of skin diseases, aging, pigmentation disorders, and matrix biology. The system is compatible with 12- and 24-well formats, increasing throughput while reducing costs by eliminating rat-tail collagen I and specialized deep-well plates. Together, these features establish a biologically relevant human skin model that bridges the gap between simple 2D cell culture and complex in vivo systems.

Limitations

This protocol incorporates up to three key cutaneous cell types—fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and melanocytes—into the 3D skin model. However, to fully recapitulate human skin complexity, the model currently lacks immune cells, neural components, appendages (hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands), and a hypodermal layer. Consequently, while suitable for studying epidermal-dermal interactions and pigmentation, this system is not ideal for investigating neuro-cutaneous signaling, immune-mediated regulation, adipocyte biology, or skin appendage development.

While four donor-matched fibroblast and keratinocyte pairs were used, only a single primary melanocyte line derived from a distinct donor was incorporated into the skin models, as melanocytes from the four donors were not available. Because melanocyte biology varies substantially between donors,2 additional mixed-donor combinations should be evaluated to assess inter-donor reproducibility and pigmentation outcomes.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Lack of donor-matched fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and melanocytes.

Potential solution

Five different donor-derived cells are used in this protocol. Skin models are reconstructed using donor-matched fibroblasts and keratinocytes, whereas melanocytes originate from an unmatched donor. This approach demonstrates flexible generation of full-thickness skin models without requiring fully donor-matched cell populations.

Problem 2

The 3D epidermal equivalent layer fails to develop visible pigmentation despite melanocyte incorporation (Steps 4-10 at the “step-by-step method details” section).
