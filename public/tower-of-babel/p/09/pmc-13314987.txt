Simultaneous measurement of linear and angular displacement sensor using POF based on macro-bend loss
Authors: Liu Liu, Ma Rui, Abdul Ghaffar, Muhammad Saleh Urf Kumail Haider, Kamran Ali, Muhammad Asghar Khan, Md. Rajibul Islam, Md Shohel Sayeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a plastic optical fiber-based sensor for simultaneous linear and angular displacement measurement through intensity modulation of macro-bend loss. The sensing mechanism relies on generating bend-induced losses in twisted fiber probes and coupling the attenuated light into receiving fibers. Two independent probes are integrated along a single illuminating fiber: the first measures linear displacement, while the second detects angular displacement. Each probe is actuated by a dedicated stepper motor, which varies the bend radius through controlled dragging, converting mechanical displacement into measurable optical power variations. The system is characterized for both individual and synchronized operation, demonstrating a wide dynamic range (0–150 mm linear, 0°–360° angular) with high repeatability and consistent performance across rotational speeds of 5–20°/s. The sensor exhibited limit of detection values of approximately 0.98° for angular measurement and 0.27 mm for linear displacement measurement, with corresponding hysteresis errors of 0.47% and 0.42% of full-scale deflection, respectively. Environmental robustness is ensured by a protective black tube that eliminates humidity effects and external light interference. The sensor’s compact design, dual-parameter measurement capability, and reliable performance make it ideal for many applications.

Introduction

Accurate measurement of angular or linear displacement is crucial in industrial applications such as in robotics, navigation, machine positioning, and aligners. The essential requirement for linear or angular displacement sensor in these applications is higher accuracy over a larger sensing range1–5. For instance, the current space-based gravitational wave detection mission seeks to achieve a displacement sensitivity about 1pm/Hz and 1 rad/Hz in the low-frequency range over kilometer-scale distances6. Other factors including stability, repeatability, and compactness are also vital in numerous applications utilizing angular/linear displacement sensors.

In the past few decades, a variety of angular and linear displacement sensors have been developed, each with its own set of advantages and disadvantages7–9. These methods include mechanical10, electromagnetic11, and optical methods12–14. Commercially available mechanical sensors for measuring position or angles are limited by their micrometer-level accuracy and resolution, hindering broader application. Electromagnetic incremental encoders convert linear or rotary motion into digital signals based on changes in capacitance, inductance, resistance, or potential. These encoders achieve sub micrometer precision over several millimeters or arcsecond accuracy across a full 360° range known for compact size, high accuracy, and low-power consumption15,16. George et al.10 effectively outlined the pros and cons of magnetic, inductive, and capacitive displacement sensors. Although most of electromagnetic sensors require physical connection to moving or rotating targets during measurement, which can disrupt system movement and introduce errors. Thereby limiting their applications despite excellent performance and versatility. Additionally, there is a scarcity of sensors capable of simultaneously measuring both displacement and rotation.

Optical encoders are widely used for angular displacement measurement, and these devices typically operate by detecting periodic variations in light intensity generated by patterned gratings or coded discs attached to rotating shafts, enabling precise conversion of rotational motion into electrical signals. Optical encoders offer high resolution and fast response. However, their performance can be affected by environmental factors such as dust, vibration, and electromagnetic interference, and they generally require direct mechanical attachment to the rotating component17–22. Other optical methods such as heterodyne interferometry is one of the most precise techniques for measuring linear and angular displacement, as it detects phase changes to achieve high accuracy23–25. A dual-heterodyne laser interferometer enables simultaneous measurement of linear and angular displacements, achieving a sensitivity noise level of 1 pm/Hz and 0.4 rad/Hz at 1 Hz17. Another notable method involves the use of optical gratings, which can act as standards for measuring length and rotation changes20. However, using cooperative mirrors in interferometric sensors and installing gratings on moving targets can introduce inconveniences and potential errors. Other optical methods capable of measuring incremental displacement or rotation include those based on the Faraday effect22, photodetectors26,27, dual-comb interferometry28, coupling29,30, and fiber interferometry31. Each of these methods provides distinct advantages, including high resolution, high precision, and the ability to measure multiple degrees of freedom. Nonetheless, developing a non-contact motion sensor without the need for cooperative mirrors, especially one capable of handling a larger angular sensing range remains a significant challenge and is often impractical.

Recently, laser feedback interferometry has attracted considerable attention for its self-collimation, compact structure, non-contact sensing, and ultra-high sensitivity achieved through frequency-shift modulation32–35. These characteristics make laser feedback interferometry especially useful for applications like vibration, displacement, and velocity measurement, biomedical imaging, and thermal sensing. Xin et al.35 used dual-beam differential approach for linear and displacement measurement based on noncontact approach. But a non-contact approach can be easily influenced by environmental factors such as humidity. However, there have been limited studies applying fiber-based intensity to the simultaneous measurement of both linear and angular displacement.

Compared with existing displacement sensing technologies, optical encoders and optical interferometry methods provide high accuracy and compactness but require direct mechanical coupling and can be sensitive to environmental conditions. Fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors and distributed fiber sensing systems provide high sensitivity and multiplexing capability36. However, they rely on complex and costly wavelength interrogation or backscattering-based measurement units. In contrast, bending-loss–based fiber sensors utilize simpler intensity modulation mechanisms but are typically limited to single-parameter sensing or passive configurations.

In this work, we present a POF-based sensor system capable of simultaneous linear and angular displacement measurement through macro-bend loss intensity modulation. Our approach uniquely integrates two twisted sensing probes along a single illuminating fiber, enabling independent yet synchronized monitoring of both displacement types. The sensor architecture employs stepper motor-actuated probes, a translational stage for linear displacement (0–150 mm) and a rotational mechanism for angular displacement (0°-360°), demonstrating high sensitivity across distinct measurement ranges. The sensor consists of good performance across varying rotational speeds (5–20°/s). This compact, dual-parameter sensing solution addresses critical needs in multi-axis linear or angular displacement monitoring where simultaneous measurements are required. Sensor design and experimental work

Sensor design

The proposed system integrates two sensors: one for angular displacement and another for linear displacement onto a single illuminating fiber. The sensing mechanism relies on a coupling method where two plastic optical fibers are twisted together to form a coupling medium. The sensing probe in this work was carefully fabricated by first twisting the optical fibers and then intentionally bending them to create the desired macro-bend configuration. To ensure environmental robustness, the twisted and bent probe assembly was fully encapsulated within a black protective tubing. This critical design feature serves two important functions37,38: it completely blocks external light interference that could affect measurements, while also providing an effective moisture barrier against humidity effects that might otherwise compromise sensor performance. This approach represents a significant advancement over previous work in the literature, where sensing probes were typically used in their straight, unbent form and without any protective shielding39.

Two separate twisted sections are created to ensure independent operation: the first for the linear displacement sensor (Sensor-1) and the second for the angular displacement sensor (Sensor-2). This separation prevents signal interference, allowing both sensors to function simultaneously without decoupling issues. The choice of POF over silica fiber is driven by several advantages40. First, theI’m i side-coupling sensing principle works more effectively with POF due to its higher numerical aperture and thinner cladding, which improves light coupling efficiency. Second, the flexibility of POF is essential since the twisted bent section must endure continuous movement during displacement measurements. Additionally, POFs are more cost-effective, easier to handle, and simpler to integrate into the system compared to silica fibers, making them a practical choice for this application. This dual-sensor design offers a compact, flexible, and efficient solution for simultaneous angular and linear displacement sensing. The schematic diagram of dual sensor for angular and linear displacement sensor is shown in Fig. 1. Unlike our previous work, which relied on passive deformation of the fiber structure for measuring two-dimensional (X–Y) displacement and did not include angular measurement41, the present study introduces a mechanically actuated setup that enables controlled and dynamic modulation of the macro-bend radius. This advancement allows systematic and simultaneous evaluation of both linear and angular displacement, representing a clear extension beyond the capabilities of the earlier design.

Experimental setup

The proposed sensing system consists of three POFs, including one illuminating fiber connected to a 660 nm LED light source (M660F1, Thorlabs) with a 20-mW output power, and two receiving fibers whose outputs are connected to separate power meters (PM100USB, & S151c photodetector, Thorlabs) for independent displacement measurements. The first receiving fiber is linked to Power Meter-1 to detect linear displacement responses, while the second receiving fiber connects to Power Meter-2 to measure angular displacement responses. Light propagates exclusively through the illuminating fiber, with the two receiving fibers remaining passive and relying on twisted macro-bend coupling to transfer light. The use of separate power meters for each receiving fiber ensures accurate, real-time monitoring of both displacement types. By employing twisting rather than fusion splicing, the system maintains fiber flexibility while achieving efficient coupling42. The combination of side-coupling theory and macro-bend loss, along with the high sensitivity (1 nW resolution) of the power meters, results in a robust, cost-effective displacement sensing solution.

The fiber selected for the experiment was Mitsubishi’s step-index SK-40, chosen for its exceptional elasticity, softness, and flexibility, key properties for the proposed displacement sensing system. Composed of polymethyl-methacrylate (PMMA) resin and fluorinated plastic, this fiber features a core diameter of 980 μm and a cladding diameter of 1 mm, with a core refractive index of 1.49. The large core diameter and minimal cladding thickness enhance two critical optical effects: significant bending loss and strong side-coupling, both essential for sensitive displacement detection. A 16 cm section of the fiber was twisted to form the coupling region, which was then shaped into a circular bend to optimize light interaction. Mechanical displacement was induced by pulling this twisted, bent section using a stepper motor, allowing precise control over movement. The combination of the SK-40 fiber’s material properties and geometric design ensures robust performance, enabling high-resolution measurement of angular and linear displacements through dynamic modulation of coupling efficiency and bending-induced losses.

The experimental setup was designed to enable precise angle and displacement detection. At its core, the system integrates two stepper motor (MF60) with a resolution of 1.8° per step, coupled with a driver unit (KH-01) for controlled motion. A translation stage linked to the motor converts rotational movement into linear displacement, covering a range of 0 mm to 150 mm over a full 0° to 360° rotation. In our setup, the angular motion of the stepper motor shaft was translated into linear displacement of the optical fiber probe through a rotational-to-linear transformation mechanism. Sensing principle

Conventionally, optical power coupling between fibers is achieved using methods such as fusion (permanent bonding of fibers) or tapping (temporary mechanical contact between fibers)43. In contrast, the present work utilizes a twisting-induced coupling structure, where two adjacent fiber segments are helically twisted together to create localized coupling regions along the twisted length (see Fig. 2). The operation of the proposed sensor is based on the combined effects of macro-bend loss and side-coupling between adjacent plastic optical fibers. When light propagates through a straight optical fiber, most of the optical energy remains confined within the core due to total internal reflection at the core–cladding interface. However, when the fiber is bent with a sufficiently small radius, part of the guided optical power leaks from the core into the cladding and surrounding medium. This phenomenon is commonly referred to as macro-bending loss.

In plastic optical fibers, the relatively large core diameter and high numerical aperture enhance the sensitivity to bending-induced radiation loss. When the illuminating fiber is bent, a portion of the radiated optical field extends outside the cladding region. If a second fiber is positioned in close proximity to the bending region, part of this radiated optical power can be coupled into the adjacent receiving fiber through evanescent field interaction and scattering in the coupling region. The magnitude of this coupled optical power depends primarily on the bend radius, the distance between the fibers, and the effective coupling length within the twisted section. Experimental results and discussion

The system integrates two sensors on a single light-transmitting optical fiber, enabling simultaneous measurement of both angular and linear displacement. The sensors were experimentally evaluated for their performance in detecting these two key parameters. All experiments were conducted under controlled conditions at room temperature, maintained at approximately 20 °C to ensure consistent and reliable results. Both receiving fibers maintain measurable optical power even after undergoing twisting. The movement of the sensor can be analyzed in two distinct scenarios. In the first case, the twisted probe initially maintains a larger bend radius before being dragged to a smaller radius, leading to increased bending loss. Conversely, in the second scenario, the probe starts with a smaller bend radius and is then pushed outward, thereby increasing its radius and reducing bending-induced attenuation. Initial coupling power measurements revealed distinct baseline values for each sensor configuration: the linear displacement sensor registered 511 nW, while the angular displacement sensor recorded 331 nW when the initial bend radius was larger. However, when the initial bend radius was smaller, the coupling power significantly increased, with the linear displacement sensor reaching 1952 nW and the angular displacement sensor measuring 1772.1 nW (as illustrated in Fig. 3a and b). These results highlight the critical influence of bend radius on light coupling efficiency, where a tighter initial bend enhances power transmission due to stronger side-coupling effects. In this system, light primarily propagates through the cladding region of the receiving fibers due to side-coupling induced by macro-bend losses. The coupling energy can be modulated by adjusting the macro-bend radius.

Fig. 3Individual response of linear and angular displacement sensor (a) first configuration where pulling-induced expansion of the bend radius (b) Pushing-induced reduction of the bend radius in second configuration.

Individual response of linear and angular displacement sensor (a) first configuration where pulling-induced expansion of the bend radius (b) Pushing-induced reduction of the bend radius in second configuration.

Figure 3a depicts the optical power response of both linear and angular displacement sensors as the sensing probe’s bend radius progressively increases due to pulling. The declining curves indicate that the angle changes from 0° to 360° (equivalent to 0–150 mm linear displacement), with optical power decreasing as the bend radius expands. The curves stabilize when the angle reaches 360° because the movement was stopped, which is due to the bend radius remaining constant, resulting in a flat signal. Conversely, Fig. 3b shows the opposite scenario: the curves exhibit an upward trend as the bend radius decreases due to the probe being pushed inward. Here, the reduction in bend radius enhances side-coupling efficiency, leading to higher detected optical power.

These results demonstrate the bidirectional functionality of the sensors. The system can operate in two complementary modes: (1) Pulling-induced expansion of the bend radius (Fig. 3a), which reduces coupling power. (2) Pushing-induced reduction of the bend radius (Fig. 3b), which increases coupling power. The inverse relationship between bend radius and optical power confirms that the sensors are versatile. The smaller bend radii (requiring pushing) and larger radii (requiring pulling) yield distinct but predictable responses. This behavior underscores the sensors’ adaptability for applications where displacement direction (push/pull) or bend geometry (tight/loose) must be dynamically monitored. To reset the system, the driver unit executed reverse motion, returning the fibers to their original position. Notably, power coupling stabilized whenever the motors halted, regardless of position within the 0°–360° (angular) or 0–150 mm (linear) range. The stepper motor’s driver unit enabled adjustable step changes, which affected the response curve’s slope (faster rise for larger steps, slower for smaller steps) but not the sensor’s fundamental behavior. Onward, we used the second mode where the bend radius reduces gradually in all experiments.

Synchronized dual-sensor response analysis

In the synchronized experiment, both stepper motors operated simultaneously to analyze the combined response of the angular and linear displacement sensors (results in Fig. 4). As the motors began moving through their full range (0° to 360° angular displacement and 0 to 150 mm linear displacement), the sensing probe’s bend radius gradually decreased. This reduction in radius led to increased bending losses, with a portion of this lost light coupling into the receiving fibers. The movement stopped at the maximum displacement point before reversing direction to return the system to its starting position.

The experiment revealed several important characteristics of the dual-sensor system. First, each probe functioned independently, with bending losses generated at the first probe not affecting the second probe’s response. This independence occurred because each probe’s output depended solely on its incoming optical power. The first probe naturally registered higher power levels due to its direct connection to the light source, while the second probe showed reduced power because of losses occurring during light propagation through the first probe section.

Notably, the system required no additional decoupling mechanisms since each probe connected to separate power meters. This design allowed for simultaneous but independent measurement of both displacement types. The reverse motion phase demonstrated symmetrical behavior, with power levels returning to their initial values as the bend radius increased back to its original dimensions. These results confirm the system’s capability for reliable, simultaneous multi-parameter displacement measurement without signal interference between sensors.

The experimental setup successfully maintained signal integrity throughout the entire measurement range, proving particularly valuable for applications requiring coordinated monitoring of both angular and linear movements. The consistent power coupling observed at all positions when movement stopped further validates the system’s stability and measurement accuracy. This configuration offers practical advantages for real-world implementations where space constraints or measurement requirements might prevent the use of separate sensor systems for different displacement types.

Individual measurement response

The measurement results of the linear and angular displacement sensors are presented in Figs. 5 and 6, showing characteristic exponential responses due to macro-bend loss effects. The sensors demonstrate excellent linearity within two specific operational ranges: 0°-220° (0–80 mm linear displacement) and 320°-360° (130–150 mm linear displacement), as confirmed by R² values approaching unity. The second linear region exhibits particularly high sensitivity because of more pronounced bend loss generation in this range. Linear regression analysis yields fitting curves of y = 165.24x − 3422.8 (R²=0.9854) for linear displacement and y = 176.86x − 3763.6 (R²=0.9903) for angular displacement, providing accurate mathematical representations of the sensor responses. While the optical sensing mechanism theoretically offers sub-degree resolution (< 1°), the current system’s practical resolution is limited to 1.8° by the stepper motor’s minimum step size. The forward and backward motion measurements show consistent behavior, with the fitting curves effectively capturing the linear relationships in both displacement directions. These results demonstrate that despite the fundamental exponential nature of bend loss effects, the sensors maintain robust linear performance within well-defined operational windows, making them suitable for precise linear or angular displacement measurement applications.

Dynamic response analysis at various rotation speeds

The dynamic response of the optical fiber displacement sensor was thoroughly investigated at multiple rotation speeds (5°/sec, 10°/sec, and 20°/sec), as illustrated in Fig. 8a and b. Experimental results demonstrated that while the rate of angular displacement varied significantly, the fundamental behavior of the sensor remained consistent across all tested speeds. The optical power coupling exhibited identical initial and final values for each trial, corresponding to the minimum bending radius at 0° (minimum bending loss) and maximum bend radius at 360° (maximum bending loss). This consistency confirms the repeatability of the sensor’s response regardless of operational speed. A key observation from these dynamic tests was the immediate stabilization of power coupling whenever the rotational motion ceased. At any stationary position between 0° and 360°, the system maintained a constant optical power output, demonstrating excellent measurement stability.

The stepper motor’s driver unit enabled precise control over angular step changes, with faster rotation speeds producing more rapid transitions in the power-angular displacement curves (Fig. 5b). However, it is important to note that while the temporal characteristics of the response curves varied with speed, the fundamental relationship between angular displacement and optical power remained unchanged. The experimental data revealed that higher rotation speeds (20°/sec) produced steeper slopes in the power-displacement curves compared to slower speeds (5°/sec), yet all curves followed the same underlying trend. This behavior confirms that the sensor’s sensitivity remains constant across different operational speeds, with the primary difference being the rate at which the system reaches equilibrium. These findings suggest that while the sensor can accommodate a wide range of rotational velocities, the choice of operational speed may be dictated by application-specific requirements for temporal resolution rather than any fundamental limitation of the sensing mechanism itself. This speed-independent response characteristic highlights the sensor’s versatility for applications requiring either slow, precise movements or more rapid displacements.

Advantages and limitations

The proposed plastic optical fiber–based sensor offers several advantages, including a simple and low-cost architecture, the use of intensity-based interrogation without complex wavelength demodulation, and the inherent immunity of optical fibers to electromagnetic interference. The flexibility and large core diameter of POF also make the sensor suitable for applications involving repeated bending. Moreover, the system enables simultaneous measurement of linear and angular displacement using a single sensing device, which may be useful in robotic joints and positioning stages where contact-types sensors are integrated. However, some limitations should be noted. The current configuration requires one detector per sensing probe, limiting multiplexing capability compared with distributed fiber sensing systems. Additionally, the sensing response depends on intensity variations and the mechanical actuation system, which may influence measurement resolution and stability under certain conditions.

Conclusions

This work presents a plastic optical fiber–based sensing system capable of simultaneously measuring linear and angular displacement using a single optical source and intensity-based interrogation scheme. The proposed design employs a twisted macro-bend coupling configuration in which two sensing probes are integrated along a single illuminating fiber, allowing independent detection of linear and rotational motion through controlled modulation of bending-induced optical power loss. This architecture demonstrates how macro-bend coupling in plastic optical fibers can be extended beyond single-parameter sensing to enable multi-parameter mechanical motion monitoring within a compact sensing platform. The main contribution of this study lies in the development of a simple and low-cost optical sensing approach that enables simultaneous monitoring of two mechanical degrees of freedom using a single device without requiring complex wavelength interrogation or interferometric detection systems. Such a sensing configuration may be useful in applications where rotational and translational motions are coupled and must be monitored simultaneously, including robotic joints, electromechanical transmission systems, motion positioning stages, and compact automation platforms. At the same time, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The current configuration requires one detector per sensing probe, which limits multiplexing capability compared with distributed fiber sensing technologies. In addition, the measurement resolution is partially constrained by the stepper motor used in the experimental setup, and further miniaturization of the sensing structure would be beneficial for integration into compact systems. Future work will therefore focus on improving sensor integration, enhancing multiplexing capability, and extending the concept toward multi-axis motion sensing, enabling simultaneous monitoring of roll, pitch, and yaw in more complex mechanical systems.
