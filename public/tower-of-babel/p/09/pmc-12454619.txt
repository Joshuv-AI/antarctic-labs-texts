Arthroscopic T-shaped biceps tenotomy decreased risk of Popeye deformity compared to traditional biceps tenotomy
Authors: Yen-Wei Huang, Pu-Kuang Wang, Shen-Wu Hung, Yu-Pin Chen, Wei-chun Chang, Yi-Jie Kuo, Tai-Yuan Chuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study compared the clinical efficacy of T-shaped biceps tenotomy (release of the long head of the biceps tendon with a portion of the superior labrum) with that of conventional cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy (without the labrum) in patients with reparable rotator cuff tears and concomitant lesions of the long head of the biceps tendon.MethodsAmong patients who underwent arthroscopic repair of rotator cuff tears and concomitant lesions of the long head of the biceps tendon, 89 patients aged ≥65 years who were treated with T-shaped biceps tenotomy (group T; n = 46) or conventional cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy (group C; n = 43) were evaluated. The presence of a Popeye deformity, severity of cramping pain, elbow flexion strength, and forearm supination strength were recorded. The anatomic status of the biceps stump was assessed through ultrasonography. Anatomic failure was considered when the biceps tendon was not identified in the intertubercular groove.ResultsOverall, 86 patients (45 in group T and 41 in group C) were monitored for more than 2 years. Their postoperative functional scores were significantly better than their preoperative scores (P < 0.03) and did not differ significantly between the two groups. Three patients (6.7 %) in group T and nine (22.0 %) in group C had a Popeye deformity (P = 0.036). Ultrasonography revealed anatomic failure in five patients (11.1 %) in group T and 13 (31.7 %) in group C (P = 0.021). Postoperative cramping pain and elbow flexion strength were evaluated, showing no significant differences between groups.ConclusionsFor treating concomitant LHBT lesions in patients with rotator cuff tears, T-shaped tenotomy involving a portion of the superior labrum is an acceptable alternative to conventional cylinder-shaped tenotomy not involving the labrum. T-shaped tenotomy is associated with similar functional outcome and lower occurrence rates of a postoperative Popeye deformity than conventional cylinder-shaped tenotomy.Level of evidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study.

Introduction

Lesions of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) are often associated with rotator cuff tears. When the rotator cuff is torn, the pressure and friction on the biceps tendon increase, thereby increasing the occurrence of LHBT lesions.1,2,3 These lesions can be divided into inflammation, instability, dislocation, and partial or complete tears.4,5,6,1,7,8,3 In addition to rotator cuff tears, concomitant LHBT lesions may cause considerable shoulder pain and dysfunction. Surgical treatment of LHBT lesions is commonly performed during arthroscopic rotator cuff repair.9,10

Although the optimal surgical procedure for LHBT lesions remains controversial, biceps tenotomy or tenodesis is commonly performed during arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Biceps tenodesis is often performed in younger patients, particularly those involved in athletics or manual labor and those wishing to avoid cosmetic deformities. Biceps tenotomy is preferred in older patients as it is a technically simple procedure that can be performed during rotator cuff repair, leads to faster improvement in postoperative pain, and does not necessitate substantial postoperative management and rehabilitation.4,5,11,12,13,14,7,15, 16, 17

Traditionally, in conventional arthroscopic release of the LHBT, the biceps tendon is released without the superior labrum being taken down, leaving a cylinder-shaped stump of the tenotomized biceps tendon. The distal portion of the tendon may subsequently slip distally, possibly causing a Popeye deformity and resulting in muscle belly cramping, residual pain, or weakened elbow flexion and forearm supination.18,19 To avoid these complications, an alternative method of releasing the biceps tendon with a portion of the superior labrum was introduced by Bradbury et al.20 This technique leaves a T-shaped (or funnel-shaped) tendon–labrum structure, which was designed to lodge in the intra-articular entrance to the bicipital groove and potentially result in autotenodesis. Using this technique, the biceps shortened by only a few centimeters, thereby preventing a Popeye deformity and muscle belly cramping.20,21 Although conventional tenotomy is widely used, the persistence of cosmetic deformity and cramping pain prompts investigation into alternative techniques such as T-shaped tenotomy. This study aims to assess whether modifying the release technique can improve cosmetic and functional outcomes.

To the best of our knowledge, no clinical studies have compared the effects of arthroscopic T-shaped tenotomy with those of conventional cylinder-shaped tenotomy during arthroscopic surgery for rotator cuff tears with concomitant LHBT lesions. This study compared the clinical outcomes between two biceps tenotomy methods during the arthroscopic repair of rotator cuff tears with concomitant LHBT lesions: T-shaped tenotomy and cylinder-shaped tenotomy. We hypothesized that T-shaped tenotomy would yield better outcomes because the lodging of the T-shaped stump of the biceps in the intra-articular entrance to the bicipital groove would prevent a Popeye deformity.

Patients and methods

Patient selection

Among patients who underwent arthroscopic surgery for rotator cuff tears with concomitant biceps lesions from September 2014 to May 2017, 89 consecutive patients aged ≥65 years were enrolled in our study. Patients who had partial or massive (≥5 cm) rotator cuff tears, had subscapularis tendon tears, had advanced glenohumeral arthritis, or had undergone revision procedures were excluded from the study. The included patients were divided into two groups; group T comprised 46 patients who underwent T-shaped (or funnel-shaped) biceps tenotomy, and group C comprised 43 patients who underwent conventional cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy. The assignment of patients to group T or C was not based on standard randomization; instead, it was based on the last digit of each patient's hospital chart number (odd numbers were included in group T and even numbers in group C). Before the surgery, the patients were informed of the advantages, disadvantages, and technical difficulties of these arthroscopic procedures. All patients agreed to undergo these procedures. All operative procedures were performed by a single orthopedic surgeon. After institutional review board approval was obtained, the data were retrospectively reviewed.

Preoperative and postoperative evaluations

Subjective pain was measured preoperatively and at the last follow-up by using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), which ranges from 0 to 10. With 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating extremely severe pain. Passive shoulder motions, including abduction, forward flexion, external rotation at the side and at 90° abduction, and internal rotation at 90° abduction, were measured in each patient 1 day preoperatively and 3, 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively. Strength measurements of the rotator cuff and biceps brachii muscle were performed using manual resistance. Active motion and strength were graded throughout the range of motion (ROM) on a scale from 0 to 5, as specified by the Medical Research Council. Abduction strength was assessed with the patient in a seated position and the arm flexed to 90° in the scapular plane. The flexion and extension strengths of the elbow and external rotation and internal rotation strengths of the shoulder were assessed with the shoulder in a neutral position and the elbow flexed to 90°. The Constant score, subjective shoulder value (SSV), modified American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) shoulder score, and modified University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) score were used for clinical assessment.

All patients were observed for the presence of a Popeye deformity. Popeye deformity was defined as a visible and palpable bulging of the distal biceps muscle belly with the patient's elbows flexed and forearms supinated. The assessment also included evaluation for asymmetry in muscle contour compared to the contralateral side. Bicipital groove tenderness was assessed by palpation of the bicipital groove through the subpectoral triangle. Speed's test and Yergason's supination test were also performed. The primary outcome was the presence of a Popeye deformity at 24 months postoperatively. The secondary outcomes were sonographic characteristics, strength measurement using manual resistance, NRS scores, Constant scores, SSVs, and modified ASES and UCLA scores. Serial x-rays were taken 3, 6, 12 months postoperatively. All assessments were performed at follow-up after a minimum period of 2 years by a single member of the research team to maintain a consistency.

Operative techniques

The patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position. Axillary and subscapular nerve blocks were accomplished by infiltrating 10 mL of 0.5 % bupivacaine in divided fractions. A standard posterior viewing portal was created, and a 30° arthroscope was introduced into the glenohumeral joint. A 21-gauge long needle was used to ascertain the precise location for placing the anterior portal. With direction toward to the upper labrum, this portal was created just superior to the lateral half of the subscapularis tendon. After these two portals were established, a thorough arthroscopic examination was performed, and the intra-articular pathology was assessed.

Technique for T-shaped (or funnel-shaped) biceps tenotomy and conventional cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy

In group T, the release of the LHBT was performed with a portion of the superior labrum being taken with the tendon. This left a T-shaped stump of the biceps, which was lodged in the intra-articular entrance to the bicipital groove. Subacromial decompression

An arthroscope was inserted into the subacromial space through the posterior portal. For alleviating bursitis, thorough bursectomy was performed using a shaver. Subacromial decompression was performed using a burr through a lateral portal. A flat undersurface to the acromion was created.

Rotator cuff repair

For assessing rotator cuff integrity, a 70° arthroscope was inserted into the posterior portal. The extent of the tear was intraoperatively determined by direct arthroscopic visualization after debridement of the degenerated tendon edges. Mediolateral as well as anteroposterior tension was assessed using a cuff grasper (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA). To achieve a repair that was as tension free as possible, marginal convergence was performed as needed after the tear pattern was determined. The bone bed of the footprint on the greater tuberosity was prepared using a power shaver and a burr. The suture anchors were placed on the bone bed after the number of anchors needed was determined. The suture was passed 8–12 mm from the cuff margin by using an antegrade suture passer. The sutures for each anchor were tied as they were placed from posterior to anterior. Once the surgery was completed, the glenohumeral joint and subacromial space were infiltrated with 160 mg of triamcinolone acetonide and 10 mL of 0.5 % bupivacaine.

Postoperative management and rehabilitation

All patients were given a sling with a small pillow in the operating room. The sling had to be worn full-time for 6 weeks, except during showers or meals. Forceful elbow flexion was also prohibited during daily activities for 6 weeks postoperatively. At 6 weeks postoperatively, the patients discontinued the use of the sling. Thereafter, they mainly performed home rehabilitation self-exercises. At 6 weeks, the patients started stretching with forward elevation by door sliding and external rotation using a door. At 8 weeks, they began strengthening with pushups using a wall. Progression to using light weights was based on each patient's progress. Complete return to unrestricted activities usually occurred at 3–6 months postoperatively and was based on the initial size of the tear, the strength of the repair, and the patient's rehabilitation progress. Transient medication was only given when a patient occasionally had severe pain.

Musculoskeletal sonographic evaluation

The anatomic status after tenotomy was determined by a musculoskeletal orthopedic doctor at our institution who had 10 more years of experience in ultrasonography (USG). USG was performed using a VFX 13-5 5–13 MHz high-resolution linear transducer on an Acuson Antares System (Siemens, IL, USA). All patients sat on a stool with their shoulder adequately exposed to permit easy access to anterior and posterior regions. The biceps tendon, acromioclavicular joint, subscapularis tendon, supraspinatus tendon, and infraspinatus tendon were examined following the standard protocol. With the forearm in supination, the elbow in 90° flexion, and the shoulder in 10° internal rotation, the USG transducer was perpendicularly oriented between the tuberosities to obtain an image in the axial plane. For eliminating the anisotropic effect, the probe was adjusted parallel to the tendon to obtain an image in the longitudinal plane. Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software package (version 20.0; SPSS Inc.). P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Data were tested to determine the normality of distribution before the appropriate parametric (Student's t-test) or nonparametric (Mann–Whitney U test) evaluation was performed to assess differences in the continuous variables between the groups. The chi-squared test was performed to assess differences in cosmetic deformities and categorical variables between the groups.

Results

In this study, out of 86 patients, 45 were included in group T (20 men and 25 women), and 41 were included in group C (18 men and 23 women). Patients with complete follow-up documentation for at least 2 years postoperatively were included in the outcome analyses. Three patients could not complete regular follow-up evaluations (two because of loss of contact and one because of moving abroad) and were therefore excluded from the outcome analyses.

Complications

Two patients in group T had symptoms of instability. They complained of possible joint dislocation when they performed stretching exercises during the first 2 months (one patient) and 3 months (one patient); however, serial x-rays revealed that their shoulders were not dislocated or degenerated. As the muscles regained strength after 3 months, the sensation of instability diminished. Moreover, two patients in group C had a postoperative superficial wound infection at the surgical incision site, which resolved after treatment with oral antibiotics for 1 week.

Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that the occurrence rates of a Popeye deformity were significantly lower with T-shaped biceps tenotomy than with conventional cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy. In patients with rotator cuff tears with concomitant LHBT lesions, conventional cylinder-shaped tenotomy can introduce a Popeye deformity during arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Therefore, we hypothesized that T-shaped tenotomy would produce better clinical outcomes than would cylinder-shaped tenotomy. Besides the primary result, the patients in both the groups were satisfied with all other clinical outcomes. No significant complications were noted following T-shaped or conventional cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy.

The function of the LHBT in the shoulder joint remains controversial. It has been described as a humeral depressor, a shoulder stabilizer, or an appendix structure.5,23,13,24,25,3 While the function of the LHBT remains unclear, it is widely recognized as a source of anterior shoulder pain.26,18,27,121 LHBT lesions rarely occur as isolated conditions; instead, they are commonly associated with rotator cuff tears.21,18 In a study involving 122 patients, 76 % of patients with complete rotator cuff tears had LHBT lesions.1,3 LHBT lesions vary in degree, presenting with tendinitis, subluxation, dislocations, partial tears, or complete tears.

In rotator cuff tears, LHBT lesions are formed because of impingement; changes in vascular supply, anatomy, or biomechanics; or a concomitant injury. LHBT lesions were originally described by Neer to be secondary to impingement syndrome. Rotator cuff tears could occur at critical zones of vascular supply, not only resulting in rotator cuff pathologies but also disseminating into biceps tendinopathy at critical zones of vascular supply.25,28 Moreover, the proximity of the proximal LHB and its sheath to rotator cuff tendons makes it susceptible to any inflammatory process affecting either structure.5,13,15 The sheath of the LHBT is anatomically continuous with the synovial lining of the glenohumeral joint; thus, the sheath can become inflamed as a result of inflammatory processes that affect the rotator cuff.4,13 Furthermore, LHBT lesions may occur secondary to excessive load and stress on the shoulder after a rotator cuff tear as it traverses the narrow groove and crosses the glenohumeral joint.29,14,30,31 LHBT lesions may also be caused by the same injury that causes rotator cuff tears or other shoulder traumas.32,33

The optimal management of lesions of the LHBT remains a subject of debate. Patients undergoing concomitant management of LHBT lesions have been reported to exhibit a greater improvement than have those undergoing rotator cuff repair alone.12,9,33,10,34 Tenodesis is generally favored in younger or more active individuals, whereas tenotomy is often considered appropriate for older patients.5,35,14,15, 16, 17 While tenotomy is technically simpler and involves less postoperative rehabilitation, it is associated with a higher incidence of complications such as Popeye deformity. 3,10,18,26,27,33,337 For avoiding a Popeye deformity, an alternative method of releasing the LHBT with a portion of the superior labrum was introduced by Kuhn et al.20 This technique produced a T-shaped structure at the proximal end of the tendon, which was entrapped in the junction between the joint and bicipital groove. The excursion of the tenotomized tendon was then limited by the T-shaped stump. This could spontaneously induce an autotenodesis effect. The mean force required to pull the tendon through the bicipital groove was 25.0 N in the cylinder-shaped group and 73.2 N in the T-shaped group in their study. The release of the biceps tendon with a portion of the superior labrum significantly increases the force required to pull the biceps tendon through the bicipital groove.

Cho et al.21 compared the clinical outcomes and cosmetic deformities following T-shaped tenotomy and those following intracuff tenodesis in patients with rotator cuff tears with concomitant LHBT lesions. No significant differences were noted in functional scores between the two groups. A Popeye deformity was detected in 11 patients in the tenotomy group (26.8 %) and 7 patients in the tenodesis group (16.7 %). The reason for the lower occurrence rate of a Popeye deformity in our study is considered to be the older age of our patients and postoperative protection. In Cho et al.’s study, the patients started to perform a passive range of motion exercises on the first postoperative day. The patients' mean age at the time of surgery was 63.8 years in their tenotomy group (range, 44–68 years). In our study, the patients' mean age was 72.5 years in group T and 70.1 years in group C. Moreover, forceful elbow flexion was prohibited during daily activities for 6 weeks postoperatively.

Autotenodesis of the LHBT in the bicipital groove after conventional cylinder-shaped tenotomy has been reported by Osbahr et al.36 The autotenodesis phenomenon of the LHBT has been explained as the LHBT being larger in diameter proximal to the biceps aperture, measuring 8.5 × 7.8 mm2 at the origin versus 4.7 × 2.6 mm2 at the bicipital groove entrance.36 In our study, the thick T-shaped stump became trapped in the narrow bicipital groove. A part of the superior labrum became trapped in the bicipital groove, creating a more favorable environment for the autotenodesis phenomenon.

The fibrocartilaginous rubbery structure of the labrum encircles the glenoid cavity, deepening the socket. Moreover, it provides static stability and shock absorption ability to the glenohumeral joint. Kumar et al. reported the upward migration of the humeral head, osteoarthritis, and instability of the shoulder after LHBT tenotomy.37 In our study, although a portion of the upper labrum was removed in group T, no upper migration or progressive osteoarthritis was noted in comparison with group C. Some patients reported a subjective sense of shoulder instability during the first three months after tenotomy. This sensation may be attributed to the loss of proximal tension in the LHBT, which plays a secondary role in glenohumeral stability—especially during overhead activities or eccentric loading. Tenotomy eliminates this stabilizing effect, potentially altering joint kinematics and proprioception.30,8 Although no objective signs of instability were noted on clinical examination, the subjective feeling may reflect subtle neuromuscular adaptations or the absence of tendon anchorage in the bicipital groove. Importantly, all patients were free of both subjective and objective instability or functional deficits beyond the three-month postoperative period.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the patients were not randomly allocated to the treatment groups. Because no study has compared T-shaped biceps tenotomy and cylinder-shaped biceps tenotomy, a randomized comparison study must be conducted in the future. Second, the timing of performing sonography follow-up was 1 year postoperatively. Although longer follow-up is warranted, the primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of a Popeye deformity, which can be adequately determined by the first postoperative year. Finally, our data may not be applicable to younger patients or patients with massive or partial rotator cuff tears, because younger patients, and massive or partial rotator cuff tears were excluded from this study.

Conclusion

For treating concomitant LHBT lesions in patients with rotator cuff tears, T-shaped tenotomy involving a portion of the superior labrum is an acceptable alternative to conventional 5cylinder-shaped tenotomy without the labrum. T-shaped tenotomy is associated with similar functional outcome and lower occurrence rates of a postoperative Popeye deformity and anatomic failure, as assessed through USG, than conventional cylinder-shaped tenotomy.
