Integrative Dermatology for Longevity: The Synergy of Topical and Internal Approaches
Authors: Diala Haykal, Frederic Flament, Guive Balooch, Pascale Mora, Natalia Kovylkina, Livia Salomao Calixto, Daiane Garcia Mercurio, Mukta Sachdev, Hema Sundaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin aging reflects both intrinsic biological decline and extrinsic influences collectively known as the skin exposome, including ultraviolet (UV) radiation, air pollution, psychosocial stress, fatigue, sleep disruption, and suboptimal lifestyle behaviors. These factors contribute to cumulative molecular and structural damage, positioning the skin as both a visible marker of whole-body aging and a target for longevity strategies. This review examines the validity of an integrative “In and Out” approach, combining topical treatments, such as retinoids, peptides, antioxidants, and exosome-based formulations, with internal nutraceuticals including NAD+ precursors, collagen peptides, polyphenols, and microbiome modulators. By targeting key hallmarks of aging, oxidative stress, inflammation, and epigenetic changes, this dual-modality model has the potential to promote skin regeneration, enhance aesthetic and functional outcomes, and contribute to broader health span optimization. Emerging tools such as skin aging clocks, biomarker-driven personalization, and artificial intelligence (AI)-guided interventions further strengthen this paradigm, establishing a scientifically grounded, preventive, and personalized framework that redefines the role of dermatology in the context of longevity. Personalized strategies that integrate diagnostic tools, lifestyle coaching, and tracking technologies allow for adaptive, dynamic strategies both internally and externally.

Introduction

The quest for longevity has traditionally centered on internal physiological systems, yet the skin, our largest and most visible organ, has emerged as a compelling frontier in this field [1]. Recent advances in molecular dermatology highlight the potential of an integrative “In and Out” strategy, combining targeted topical formulations with internal supplementation to optimize skin regeneration, barrier integrity, and aesthetic vitality [2, 3]. By addressing both intrinsic and extrinsic aging factors, this paradigm aligns with the principles of precision dermatology, where interventions are tailored to individual skin biology and needs [4, 5]. Historically, skincare regimens have focused on surface-level repair; however, emerging research supports a broader role, with the goal of not only superficial improvement but also structural, cellular, and tissue-level correction [6, 7]. The interdependence between internal metabolic processes and the external appearance of the skin underscores the need for a comprehensive, holistic approach [8].

Modern dermatology now explores interventions capable of penetrating deeper skin layers to effect more significant biological changes. Chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and epigenetic alterations are recognized as pivotal contributors to the process of skin aging, and potential therapeutic targets. Notably, age-related changes in DNA methylation patterns can be quantified through epigenetic (biological) “clocks,” offering measurable insights into biological age, distinct from chronological age (Fig. 1). When considered alongside cellular senescence markers, such as p16INK4a, these biological markers highlight the need for interdisciplinary collaboration to guide patient-tailored, evidence-based interventions. The Skin as a Longevity Marker

The skin serves as a visible marker of aging, influenced by oxidative stress, glycation, mitochondrial decline, and epigenetic modifications. As the body’s largest organ, the skin provides an accessible model for studying aging processes and the efficacy of longevity interventions [1, 9, 10]. Cellular senescence in cutaneous tissues is reflective of broader aging processes, emphasizing the need for interventions that go beyond surface-level correction [11, 12]. In vitro studies have demonstrated that aged dermal fibroblasts exhibit decreased collagen synthesis and increased matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity, leading to extracellular matrix degradation and skin laxity [13, 14]. The progressive decline in dermal fibroblast activity was found to be strongly associated with internal metabolic shifts, further reinforcing the link between cutaneous and whole-body aging [15].

Oxidative stress is a primary driver of aging, leading to cumulative DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipid peroxidation, all of which contribute to skin deterioration [9, 16]. Chen et al. demonstrated that excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation accelerates cellular aging, increasing susceptibility to extrinsic factors such as UV radiation and pollution [17]. This damage is cyclical: UV and pollution generate ROS, which in turn heighten the skin’s vulnerability to these and other environmental stressors, perpetuating a cycle of oxidative stress and cellular decline.

Chronic inflammation has also been identified as a key factor in aging skin, where prolonged exposure to inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) contributes to dermal thinning, breakdown of the extracellular matrix, and impaired skin barrier function [18, 19].

Beyond cellular damage, epigenetic modifications play a crucial role in skin longevity. Studies have demonstrated that DNA methylation patterns in keratinocytes and fibroblasts change with age, altering gene expression related to repair mechanisms and collagen production [20]. Haykal et al. showed that epigenetic clocks, such as the Horvath clock, could accurately estimate biological age based on skin DNA methylation signatures, emphasizing the potential for targeted interventions that modulate these pathways [1].

By targeting oxidative stress, inflammation, and epigenetic drift through both topical and internal strategies, dermatology has the potential to delay the visible manifestations of aging while also improving skin function at a cellular level [21]. Accrued evidence supports the concept that modulating oxidative damage and chronic inflammation through combined therapies can lead to improved skin elasticity, hydration, and resilience against environmental stressors [22]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that oral supplementation with antioxidants, including carotenoids, polyphenols, and vitamin C, significantly improves skin photoprotection and reduces the appearance of fine lines [23, 24]. Additionally, emerging evidence from several studies suggests that oral collagen peptide supplementation enhances skin elasticity and dermal hydration while reducing wrinkle depth. These effects may be mediated more by antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways than by direct stimulation of new collagen synthesis [25, 26].

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating dermatological treatments with longevity strategies. By harnessing recent advances in molecular aging research, dermatology is positioned to move beyond traditional symptomatic treatments, toward a more comprehensive, preventive, and regenerative paradigm that supports both skin health and longevity.

Topical Interventions: Beyond Hydration and Repair

Within the “Out” dimension of this model, topical agents act on the epidermis and upper dermis to provide targeted correction and protection. These interventions have significantly evolved from their traditional roles of hydration and barrier repair to include more targeted, science-driven strategies for skin correction rejuvenation [27]. While traditional skincare has primarily focused on maintaining the skin’s protective barrier and preventing dehydration, modern advancements have introduced functional molecules capable of supporting early correction of skin aging signs [22, 28]. These interventions are primarily regulated to act on superficial and epidermal layers, addressing early dysfunctions without reaching the depth or specificity of medical-grade therapies [17, 29]. The incorporation of topical agents such as retinoids, peptides, and antioxidants has transformed skincare into a proactive element in aesthetic and dermatological strategies [30, 31]. Recent innovations have demonstrated that combining topical agents with non-invasive mechanical stimulation, such as nanochip tapping devices, can significantly amplify their efficacy while preserving skin barrier integrity [32]. These compounds not only provide surface-level nutrition but also influence cellular pathways to enhance cutaneous texture, firmness, and resilience. As research continues to progress, the role of topical treatments is expanding, offering corrective outcomes that complement in-clinic procedures.

Retinoids: A Gold Standard for Skin Renewal

Retinoids, derivatives of vitamin A, have long been regarded as one of the most effective topical agents for skin renewal and antiaging [33]. These compounds exert their effects by binding to nuclear receptors, initiating intracellular signaling cascades that influence cellular proliferation, differentiation, and collagen synthesis [34, 35]. Through this mechanism, retinoids stimulate collagen synthesis, accelerate cellular turnover, and enhance the structural integrity of the skin. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated the efficacy of retinoids in improving skin texture, reducing the appearance of fine lines and wrinkles, and promoting a more youthful complexion [36, 37]. Regular use of retinoids has been shown to increase epidermal thickness, refine skin tone, and enhance overall skin quality [38]. However, their application requires careful consideration, as some individuals may experience skin irritation, dryness, or sensitivity [36]. To mitigate these side effects, dermatologists often recommend gradual introduction, combined with appropriate hydration and sun protection strategies [33, 39]. Patient-tailored selection of retinoids can also improve tolerability. Notably, the preventive age of retinoids is increasingly advocated from approximately the age of 30 in aesthetic dermatology and cosmetology, reflecting a paradigm shift from purely corrective to preventive antiaging strategies. At this stage, low-concentration retinoids can prime the skin against the onset of photoaging changes before structural damage becomes clinically apparent, representing one of the most evidence-supported preventive topical interventions available [40, 41].

Peptides: Targeting Structural Integrity and Skin Elasticity

Peptides have emerged as essential components of modern antiaging skincare, primarily due to their potential to modulate key signaling pathways involved in skin structure and function [42, 43]. These short chains of amino acids can mimic natural peptides that regulate collagen synthesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and intercellular communication [44, 45]. Peptides have demonstrated antioxidant properties that contribute to improved skin health. While they are proposed to act as biological messengers supporting elasticity, connective tissue strength, and dermal density, in vivo evidence for these signaling effects remains limited [46]. Specific peptides, such as palmitoyl pentapeptide and copper peptides, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing wrinkle depth and improving skin firmness. A broader range of biomimetic and signaling peptide classes warrants consideration when evaluating the evidence base. Signal peptides (e.g., palmitoyl tripeptide-1/Matrixyl) stimulate collagen and elastin production; carrier peptides (e.g., copper peptide GHK-Cu) facilitate trace element delivery and wound repair; neurotransmitter-inhibiting peptides (e.g., acetyl hexapeptide-3/Argireline) reduce expression-related wrinkling; and enzyme-inhibiting peptides modulate matrix metalloproteinase activity to limit extracellular matrix degradation. The level of clinical evidence varies considerably across these classes: while in vitro data are robust for many peptide families, well-designed randomized controlled trials confirming in vivo dermal efficacy remain comparatively limited, and claims of efficacy should be interpreted in relation to study design, concentration, and formulation vehicle [31, 47, 48]. The growing use of peptide-enriched formulations underscores their importance in non-invasive skin rejuvenation, offering a viable alternative to injectable or surgical procedures for individuals seeking gradual and natural-appearing skin improvement.

Antioxidants: Combating Oxidative Stress and Environmental Damage

Antioxidants play a crucial role in protecting the skin from oxidative stress, which is a key contributor to premature aging. Free radicals generated by environmental factors such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation, pollution, and lifestyle-related stressors can accelerate skin aging by damaging collagen, elastin, and cellular DNA. To counteract these effects, topical antioxidants, including vitamins C and E, polyphenols, and flavonoids, have been widely incorporated into cosmeceutical and dermocosmetic formulations, products positioned between conventional cosmetics and prescription topicals on the basis of their functional biological activity [49]. Vitamin C, in particular, has been extensively studied for its ability to neutralize free radicals (ROS), stimulate collagen production, and reduce hyperpigmentation [50, 51]. Similarly, vitamin E provides lipid barrier protection, enhancing the skin’s ability to retain moisture and resist external stressors [52, 53]. Polyphenols derived from natural sources, such as green tea and grape seed extract, offer additional anti-inflammatory and photoprotective benefits. Together, these antioxidants contribute to skin longevity by preserving cellular integrity and minimizing the impact of environmental aging factors. While vitamin C (l-ascorbic acid) remains among the most comprehensively studied topical antioxidants, its in vivo efficacy is highly dependent on formulation stability, pH, and delivery vehicle; oxidized ascorbic acid is biologically inactive. Emerging evidence also highlights a broader antioxidant landscape, including niacinamide, ferulic acid, bakuchiol, and astaxanthin, which demonstrate relevant antioxidant and antiaging activity and may offer advantages in terms of formulation stability or tolerability [54].

Exosome-Based Therapies: A New Frontier in Regenerative Skincare

Recent advances in dermatology have introduced exosome-based therapies as an emerging approach to skin regeneration. Exosomes are nanosized extracellular vesicles that facilitate intercellular communication and play a role in tissue support and cellular repair [55]. In topical applications, exosomes have demonstrated the capacity to enhance fibroblast activity, stimulate collagen, and improve skin hydration and firmness [56–58]. Preclinical studies have shown promising effects on dermal function and structural integrity, reinforcing their relevance in advanced skincare [59–61]. However, current regulatory frameworks lack clarity regarding the safety and classification of exosome-based cosmetic products. As clinical evidence continues to emerge, the establishment of standardized formulation guidelines will be essential to support the safe and effective integration of exosome therapies into aesthetic practice.

Harnessing the Power of Regenerative Medicine in Dermatology

Regenerative medicine represents a transformative approach in dermatology, leveraging the body’s endogenous healing mechanisms to restore and maintain youthful skin [3, 62]. The incorporation of regenerative elements into topical formulations enables dermatologists to deliver innovative solutions for skin rejuvenation, repair, and long-term maintenance [3]. The inclusion of growth factors, stem cell-derived extracts, and extracellular matrix components in skincare formulations marks a significant advancement in dermatological science [63–67]. Stem cell-derived therapies, particularly those utilizing mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), have demonstrated substantial potential in promoting skin regeneration [68, 69]. MSCs secrete bioactive molecules with the potential to modulate inflammation, stimulate fibroblast activity, and enhance collagen production [70]. Topical formulations containing MSC-derived conditioned media have been shown to improve skin texture, hydration, and elasticity. These formulations offer a non-invasive approach to skin renewal, as an alternative or adjunct to in-office regenerative treatments such as microneedling with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) [71–73]. The ability of stem cell-derived ingredients to support cellular function and repair damaged tissues highlights their growing significance in both therapeutic and aesthetic dermatology [74].

Bioengineered Skin Grafts and 3D Bioprinting in Dermatology

The future of regenerative dermatology is being shaped by advances in bioengineered skin grafts and three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting technologies [75–77]. Bioengineered skin grafts, constructed from autologous or allogeneic cells, are under development to enhance wound healing and support aesthetic restoration [78]. These grafts provide both structural support and biological cues to promote cellular regeneration and tissue remodeling [79, 80]. Moreover, 3D bioprinting techniques enables the precise layering of dermal and epidermal cells, facilitating the creation of personalized dermatological constructs. Such 3D-printed skin models hold significant promise for treating burns, scars, and age-associated skin deterioration [81, 82]. These innovations bridge the gap between topical applications and surgical interventions, opening new avenues for individualized regenerative procedures [83–86].

Nutraceuticals and Oral Supplementation: Reinforcing Cellular Resilience in Dermatological Health

The “In” component of regenerative dermatology targets internal mechanisms, addressing inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial decline through specific nutritional interventions. Oral supplementation has emerged as a valuable adjunct in promoting skin health and longevity. Advances in nutraceutical science have underscored the importance of bioactive compounds in supporting cellular repair, enhancing mitochondrial function, and modulating inflammatory balance. These systemic interventions complement topical treatments and aesthetic procedures by addressing the fundamental biological drivers of skin aging and degeneration [86]. When strategically integrated into dermatological protocols, oral supplementation may address the fundamental biological drivers of skin aging and degeneration, and significantly improve the durability and outcomes of skin rejuvenation protocols.

NAD+ Precursors and Cellular Longevity

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is a pivotal coenzyme involved in cellular energy metabolism, DNA repair, and redox homeostasis [87], making it a focal point in aging research. Physiological NAD+ levels decline with chronological aging, contributing to cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired tissue repair [88, 89]. This decline is associated with decreased activity of sirtuins, a key family of NAD+-dependent enzymes that regulate gene expression and mitochondrial biogenesis [90, 91]. Supplementation with NAD+ precursors such as nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) has shown potential to enhance mitochondrial performance, activate repair pathways repair mechanisms, and support skin health by improving cellular resilience against oxidative stress [92–94]. Notably, NMN has been found to upregulate NAD+ biosynthesis, improve insulin sensitivity, and enhance mitochondrial energy metabolism, positioning it as a potential therapeutic agent in mitigating age-associated metabolic decline and skin degeneration [95–97]. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these benefits and to define optimal dosing regimens for dermatological applications.

Collagen Peptides and Skin Structural Integrity

Collagen peptides represent another class of nutraceuticals gaining attention for their role in preserving skin structural integrity. Upon ingestion, hydrolyzed collagen peptides are broken down into di- and tripeptides that are absorbed and distributed to dermal tissues [98, 99]. In vitro, these bioactive peptides act as signaling molecules that stimulate fibroblast activity and extracellular matrix production [48, 100]. Clinical trials have reported significant improvements in skin elasticity, hydration, and dermal density following oral collagen supplementation, alongside reductions in wrinkle depth and trans-epidermal water loss [26, 101, 102]. These clinical findings support the potential of oral collagen supplementation to improve skin parameters; however, the mechanistic basis remains incompletely understood. It is important to distinguish between observed improvements in skin hydration, elasticity, and wrinkle depth, which are reasonably well supported by randomized controlled trial data, and the direct in vivo stimulation of dermal fibroblast collagen synthesis, for which robust mechanistic evidence is lacking. The beneficial effects of ingested collagen peptides may be mediated as much by systemic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways as by direct dermal remodeling, and this distinction should be borne in mind when interpreting the literature [103].

Polyphenols and Antioxidative Skin Protection

Polyphenols and advanced antioxidants comprise a versatile class of bioactive compounds that play a central role in protecting skin cells from oxidative stress, inflammation, and premature aging [104, 105] Resveratrol is one of the most studied molecules for its ability to modulate mitochondrial function and sirtuin activity. However, other compounds such as quercetin, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), and astaxanthin have demonstrated comparable or superior efficacy in supporting dermal health, often with reduced market volatility [106–109]. These antioxidants act by neutralizing ROS, modulating inflammatory signaling cascades, and preserving mitochondrial and DNA integrity. Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is vital for mitochondrial energy production and has shown clinical promise in enhancing skin firmness and mitigating oxidative cellular damage in keratinocytes. Astaxanthin, a carotenoid, has also emerged as a potent topical and oral antioxidant with tangible benefits on elasticity and wrinkle depth [110]. Glutathione, recognized for its detoxification properties and influence on melanogenesis, is increasingly used as a supplement to promote skin radiance and even skin tone [111, 112]. Together, these compounds can contribute to skin resilience through multifaceted pathways including epigenetic regulation, mitochondrial support, and anti-inflammatory effects. Their integration into oral skin regimens offers corrective outcomes that complement topical interventions, particularly in patients exposed to oxidative environmental stressors or displaying early signs of cellular senescence.

Microbiome Modulation and the Gut–Skin Axis

Emerging research underscores the significant influence of the gut microbiome on skin health, a connection known as the gut–skin axis [113–116]. This bidirectional communication pathway can be stimulated by specific nutraceutical interventions including prebiotics, probiotics, and postbiotics that modulate systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune responses, thereby impacting skin conditions such as acne, rosacea, atopic dermatitis, and eczema [117, 118].

Modulating the gut microbiome through dietary interventions presents a promising avenue to enhance skin or scalp health, while potentially mitigating age-related skin changes. Some probiotic strains, including selected species of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, have demonstrated potential benefit in modulating skin barrier function, reducing systemic inflammation, improving hydration, and attenuating UV-induced immunosuppression in certain study populations. However, results are highly strain-specific, and clinical literature is characterized by significant heterogeneity in probiotic species, dosages, study durations, and outcome measures. Generalization across strains or products is not supported by current evidence, and findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution [119–121].

For example, daily oral intake of a specific Lactobacillus strain powder for 12 weeks significantly improved skin hydration, texture, mechanical properties, and reduced wrinkles [122]. Similarly, consumption of fermented milk containing Bifidobacterium has been shown to enhance skin hydration and reduce oxidative stress markers [123]. Prebiotics, non-digestible food substrates that selectively promote the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, also show promise in modulating skin health parameters. An 8-week oral intake of lactulose and galacto oligosaccharides (GOS) resulted in improved skin hydration and reduced wrinkle appearance [124]. Beyond direct supplementation, skin conditions can also be influenced through the gut–skin axis by dietary habits. Some preliminary studies suggest that consuming a diet rich in fruits and vegetables promotes gut microbiome diversity and may influence skin condition, though further research is needed to fully elucidate these complex interactions [125]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of personalized, microbiome-targeted supplementation strategies to promote skin health and counteract age-related skin changes.

Essential Fatty Acids and Lipid Metabolism

Omega-3 fatty acids, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are well documented for their anti-inflammatory effects and their role in maintaining skin barrier function, elasticity, and hydration [126]. While fish oil remains the primary dietary source, alternative omega-3 sources derived from marine algae and plants are gaining attention for their sustainability and suitability for vegan or environmentally conscious individuals [127, 128]. These alternatives activate similar metabolic and anti-inflammatory pathways and offer additional flexibility for vegan and environmentally conscious individuals [127, 128].

These therapeutic benefits of omega-3 fatty acids are largely mediated through the modulation of eicosanoids such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes—key inflammatory mediators implicated in various dermatological conditions [129]. Additionally, EPA and DHA have been shown to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNFα, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby conferring systemic immunomodulatory effects that extend beyond dermatological health [130].

Emerging evidence also suggests a protective role of essential fatty acids against UV-induced photoaging. For example, studies have shown the association of omega-6 supplementation with reduced UVB-induced erythema and mitigation of DNA damage [131, 132]. Importantly, while omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids contribute to the reduction of UV-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, they are supportive rather than substitutive of traditional photoprotection mechanisms such as physical or chemical sun filters [7, 133].

Long-Term Safety and Future Directions

While nutraceuticals hold considerable promise in dermatological health and regenerative skincare, standardization, regulatory oversight, and long-term safety data remain key challenges [134, 135]. Certain compounds, when consumed in high doses, may pose toxicity risks; for example, excessive polyphenol intake has been associated with thyroid dysfunction, while prolonged use of NAD+ precursors may disrupt metabolic homeostasis [136–138]. Furthermore, potential interactions with medications, particularly in patients with chronic conditions, must be carefully evaluated [139, 140].

A significant limitation in the current landscape is bioavailability. Many bioactive compounds exhibit low absorption, reducing their clinical efficacy. This has spurred the development of advanced delivery technologies such as liposomal encapsulation and nano formulations, which aim to enhance absorption and target efficacy [141]. The absence of large-scale randomized controlled trials poses another barrier to evidence-based integration. Robust clinical data are needed to define therapeutic efficacy, optimal dosing, and long-term safety profiles of these nutraceuticals in dermatological practice. Moreover, regulatory challenges persist: nutraceuticals are often categorized as dietary supplements rather than therapeutic agents, resulting in variability in quality control, labeling, and manufacturing standards across regions.

Looking forward, personalized supplementation strategies that integrate nutrigenomics and metabolomics may offer optimized outcomes by tailoring interventions to individual genetic and metabolic profiles [142, 143]. Future research should prioritize the establishment of standardized clinical protocols, refinement of delivery systems, and investigation into the synergistic effects of nutraceutical combinations. Although the field of dermatological nutraceuticals is evolving rapidly, a collaborative effort between researchers, clinicians, and regulatory bodies will be essential to ensure evidence-based application and consumer safety.

Bridging Aesthetic and Longevity Medicine

Skin, as a longevity organ, serves as both a visible readout of systemic biological aging and a primary target for longevity-oriented interventions. The hallmarks of skin aging—oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, epigenetic drift, inflammaging, and cellular senescence—mirror the core processes driving organismal aging, making dermatology a uniquely positioned discipline within the broader longevity medicine paradigm.

The “In and Out” approach, combining internal and external interventions, extends beyond general dermatology into aesthetic medicine, where it offers a practical framework for enhancing clinical outcomes. Importantly, this holistic methodology is not intended as a one-size-fits-all approach. Rather, it serves as a foundation for flexible, personalized combinations based on individual skin physiology, age, and treatment goals. Many of these strategies can be applied either independently or in combination, allowing dermatologists to tailor interventions for maximum benefit and patient compliance.

Recent advances in diagnostic tools, such as biological skin clocks and molecular profiling, now allow practitioners to assess skin age with greater precision. These methods support the development of personalized strategies tailored to an individual’s skin health based on biological rather than chronological age. As skin biology evolves throughout the lifespan, different stages require distinct strategies for prevention, correction, or regeneration.

For younger individuals in their mid-20s and early 30s, the focus is on prevention and the establishment of foundational care. Topical antioxidants, barrier-strengthening ingredients, and microbiome-supportive formulations can help to mitigate early signs of aging. Concurrently, internal supplementation with NAD+ precursors, probiotics, and adaptogens reinforces cellular vitality and stress resilience.

During midlife, typically around the 40s, visible signs of aging begin more prominent. This period calls for correction and preservation. Topical agents such as retinoids, peptides, and exosome-enriched formulations can target collagen degradation, dermal thinning, pigmentation, and skin laxity. These exosomes play a paracrine role in enhancing skin regeneration and extracellular matrix remodeling. Meanwhile, oral supplementation with collagen peptides, polyphenols, and mitochondrial boosters helps support structural integrity and skin resilience from within [144].

For mature skin, generally beyond age 55, the approach shifts toward regeneration and repair. Advanced topicals containing stem cell-derived actives, exosomes and growth factors support deep tissue renewal by modulating cellular communication, stimulating fibroblast activity, and promoting extracellular matrix restoration. Internal strategies, including NAD+ boosters, collagen peptides, and omega-3 fatty acids, contribute to the restoration of skin density, function, and metabolic balance. At this stage, diagnostic tools, such as biological skin clocks and transcriptomic profiling, can further refine personalized, integrative care strategies.

Integrative Strategy

This integrative strategy can be envisioned as structured interventions across four interrelated domains:

Pre-treatment Protocols: Enhancing Cellular Resilience

Incorporation of antioxidants and NAD+ precursors into pre-treatment regimens has been shown to bolster cellular resilience, thereby optimizing recovery following aesthetic procedures such as laser resurfacing [145, 146]. Antioxidants such as vitamins C and E neutralize ROS generated during such treatments, reducing oxidative stress and facilitating faster healing [147]. Several studies have shown that combined use of these vitamins provides cumulative protection against erythema and sunburned cells, underscoring their efficacy in mitigating treatment-induced oxidative damage [147, 148].

NAD+ precursors, such as nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), further support mitochondrial function and DNA repair mechanisms. These compounds enhance cellular energy metabolism, which is crucial for tissue regeneration and recovery post-procedure [88, 149]. While direct studies on the applications of NR and NMN in aesthetic medicine are currently limited, their documented role in cellular health suggests potential benefits in pre-treatment protocols.

Oral Supplementation: Supporting Structural Integrity

Oral supplementation with collagen peptides and hyaluronic acid has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing the outcomes of biostimulating injectables. A 12-week, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial by Hsu et al. demonstrated that oral hyaluronan supplementation significantly reduced wrinkle depth and improved skin dryness [150]. These findings concur with additional studies showing that oral hydrolyzed collagen and hyaluronic acid are well tolerated and can mitigate signs of skin aging, supporting their integration into aesthetic practice [151].

Anti-inflammatory Agents: Modulating Post-procedural Responses

Adaptogens and omega-3 fatty acids have been explored for their anti-inflammatory properties, which may aid in reducing post-procedural inflammation and promoting a balanced skin response [152, 153]. Omega-3 fatty acids, found in fish oil and certain plant oils, have been shown to modulate inflammatory processes, through the regulation of prostaglandin synthesis and cytokine pathways, potentially reducing erythema and swelling following aesthetic procedures. A narrative review highlighted the beneficial effects of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids on inflammatory skin diseases, suggesting their utility in managing post-treatment inflammation [154].

Adaptogens, such as ashwagandha, Rhodiola rosea, and Panax ginseng, are botanicals known to enhance the body’s resilience to physical, emotional, and environmental stressors, effects increasingly explored for their potential to support skin health and counteract stress-induced aging [155, 156]. Although direct data supporting their use in aesthetic procedures remains limited, their well-documented anti-inflammatory and stress-buffering properties suggest a promising role in post-procedural care, potentially aiding in skin recovery and resilience.

Comprehensive Treatment Plans: A Personalized Approach

By leveraging these combined strategies, dermatologists can offer more comprehensive and personalized treatment plans that align with the principles of longevity medicine. Integrating topical applications with targeted oral supplements addresses both intrinsic and extrinsic factors affecting skin health, leading to enhanced aesthetic outcomes and prolonged benefits [157, 158]. The synergy between topical and internal interventions represents a promising frontier in aesthetic medicine, aligning with the broader goals of promoting healthspan. Continued research and clinical application of this integrated approach are likely to yield further advancements in patient outcomes and satisfaction.

Translational Outlook: Skin Clocks and Predictive Biomarkers

A key area of ongoing and future exploration in integrative dermatology lies in translational science, with a growing focus on the development of biological skin clocks informed by causal and mechanistic biomarkers of aging [1, 159]. These clocks provide a more accurate assessment of biological skin age by integrating epigenetic modifications, mitochondrial function metrics, inflammatory markers, and cellular senescence indicators [1, 160, 161]. In this context, the skin occupies a unique position: it is not only the most outwardly visible indicator of systemic aging but also a highly accessible tissue through which interventions can be implemented and monitored in real time. This dual function elevates the skin from a surface-level concern to a central platform in the broader pursuit of longevity. As a diagnostic interface, the skin reflects underlying physiological shifts; as a therapeutic interface, it offers a tangible entry point for correcting, slowing, or even reversing some of these processes [162, 163]. For instance, a patient exhibiting early signs of mitochondrial decline and low NAD+ levels may benefit from targeted supplementation with NAD+ precursors such as NR or NMN. Conversely, individuals presenting with early signs of fibroblast senescence could benefit more from topical peptides and exosome-based formulations [164].

By incorporating advanced diagnostics, including methylation profiling, transcriptomic analysis, and proteomic screening, into aesthetic and therapeutic practice, we can evolve toward a data-driven, preventive dermatology model of care [165]. This evolving paradigm does not merely seek to treat or delay visible signs of skin aging but aspires to actively reprogram the skin’s biological trajectory, ultimately contributing to both aesthetic outcomes and longevity. As this field advances, collaboration between dermatologists, molecular biologists, longevity researchers, and the cosmetic industry will be essential to translate these biological insights into clinically actionable, scalable solutions that redefine the future of skin health.

Challenges and Future Perspectives

Despite the growing interest in integrative dermatology, several challenges continue to shape its future trajectory [166]. Among the most pressing is the need to better understand the influence of genetic predispositions on skin aging and responsiveness to both topical and internal interventions [108, 167]. Understanding of how inherited factors modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, or collagen degradation may help refine personalization strategies. Another critical aspect is the personalization of treatment protocols, as individual responses to both topical and internal therapies vary based on genetic predisposition, lifestyle, and environmental exposures [1]. The integration of artificial intelligence and biomarker-driven skin diagnostics could facilitate more precise treatment recommendations tailored to patient-specific needs [168]. Furthermore, while current integrative approaches show great clinical potential, regulatory and standardization issues pose substantial barriers to widespread adoption. Many nutraceuticals, cosmeceuticals, and regenerative or biomimetic therapies fall into gray zones between cosmetics and medical therapeutics. Addressing these regulatory challenges is essential not only to ensure consumer safety but also to support scientific legitimacy and clinician confidence. As the field evolves, greater cross-disciplinary collaboration between dermatologists, molecular biologists, longevity scientists, and regulatory bodies will be essential to scale innovations while preserving clinical rigor.

Conclusion

The “In and Out” approach, reviewed in this publication, illustrates how dermatology is evolving into a more integrative and preventive discipline, with the potential to address skin aging across multiple biological levels. In this emerging paradigm, skin health is no longer viewed as merely superficial, but as a dynamic interface between external environmental exposures and internal physiological processes. As such, dermatology is positioning itself at the forefront of longevity medicine, bridging aesthetic outcomes with resilience and systemic well-being. The convergence of topical dermatology and internal interventions is reshaping patient care by integrating scientifically validated nutraceuticals, targeted skincare formulations, and principles of regenerative medicine. Advances in antioxidants, NAD+ precursors, collagen peptides, and other functional molecules are optimizing skin function at the cellular level, enhancing the efficacy and sustainability of both medical and aesthetic treatments. This integrative model marks a shift from reactive to proactive care, where pre-treatment and post-procedure regimens are designed as synergistic strategies that support healing, amplify outcomes, and extend skin vitality. With growing insights into mitochondrial health, epigenetic programming, and inflammation control, dermatologists are now contributing to longevity science that aims to preserve tissue function and delay the biological processes associated with aging.

By embracing this holistic framework, dermatology can bridge the gap between aesthetic goals and integrative wellness. Skin aging becomes not just a cosmetic concern but a critical marker of biological health and resilience. As research continues to evolve, the incorporation of topical and systemic interventions will play a pivotal role in longevity-oriented care which will position dermatology as a key contributor to preventive and personalized medicine to promote healthy ageing.
