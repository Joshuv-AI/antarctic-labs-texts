Eye‐Targeted A20 Gene Therapy Alleviates Ischemic Retinopathy by Reducing Pathologic Neovascularization, Gliosis, and Neuronal Apoptosis
Authors: Sanah Essayagh, Tarek Aridi, Tytteli Turunen, Lynn Choi, Hugo Adam, Nyah Patel, Salvatore T Scali, Mark D Fisher, Karl Skaf, Eva Csizmadia, Phillip Brennan, Anthony Sadek, Jona Skaf, Michelle Zhan, Emily Hansen, Maryam Shamloo, Nicholas R DeStefino, Allen C Clermont, Cleide Angolano, Christiane Ferran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntravitreal administration of anti‐VEGF therapies has been highly beneficial in treating ischemic proliferative retinopathies (PR). However, a quarter of patients do not benefit from these therapies due to primary or secondary non‐response and adverse side effects. The limited success of anti‐VEGF therapies underscores the complex interplay between the various pathogenic contributors to this disease. Mechanistic studies have emphasized the importance of pathologic angiogenesis, reactive inflammatory gliosis, and heightened neuronal apoptosis as key determinants of disease outcomes. We surmise that therapeutic strategies targeting all three components would be highly valuable. Through gain and loss‐of‐function studies of A20/TNFAIP3, a ubiquitous NF‐κB inhibitor with established cytoprotective functions in endothelial and neuronal cells, we uncovered a novel function for this gene as a physiologic regulator of angiogenesis in human retinal endothelial cell cultures. In vivo, intravitreal delivery of an A20 gene therapy, which yields transgene expression throughout retinal layers, significantly reduces central vaso‐obliteration and pathological peripheral neovascular tuft formation in a mouse model of oxygen‐induced PR. In contrast, mice with total or partial A20 knockdown exhibited worse lesions. Remarkably, A20‐related benefits were associated with a significant reduction in inflammatory gliosis and improved glial function, notably in Müller cells, as evaluated by glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP) and glutamine synthetase (GS) immunostaining, and with decreased apoptotic cell death across the retina, including in neuronal cells. Conversely, A20 knockdown aggravated these features. By concurrently reducing pathologic neovascularization, reactive gliosis, and neuronal apoptosis in the retina, A20 emerges as a unique therapeutic target with promising translational potential.Keywords: A20/TNFAIP3, angiogenesis, gene therapy, gliosis, ischemic retinopathy, neuronal death

Introduction

Proliferative retinopathies (PR), such as retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) and diabetic proliferative retinopathy (DPR), are the leading causes of blindness worldwide [1, 2]. Despite differences in their etiologies, these conditions share common features, including the loss of retinal endothelial cells (EC) and hypoxia‐triggered reactive retinal gliosis, leading to the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and angiogenic factors, primarily VEGF‐A, referred to hereafter as VEGF [3]. In turn, these factors trigger the development of aberrant compensatory retinal neovascularization consisting of immature and leaky vessels [2, 3], causing hemorrhages and retinal scarring that exacerbate retinal ischemia and culminate in photoreceptor and neuronal death [4].

VEGF, primarily produced by glial cells and retinal EC in response to hypoxia, binds to VEGFR‐2/Fetal liver kinase‐1 (VEGFR‐2/Flk‐1) receptors, driving downstream activation of extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK1/2) and protein kinase C (PKC), mainly the βII isoform, which respectively promote EC proliferation and heightened permeability and drive PR [5, 6]. Strategies to treat PR by inhibiting PKCβ or directly blocking VEGF were previously developed. While the PKCβ inhibitor Ruboxistaurin failed to prevent pathologic angiogenesis and was discontinued [7], anti‐VEGF therapies fare much better, even if their therapeutic effectiveness remains limited by primary and secondary non‐responsiveness and adverse side effects [8, 9]. Failure of anti‐VEGF therapies may be in part attributed to the compensatory effect of other angiogenic contributors to PR, such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF) [10]. Concerns about side effects of prolonged inhibition of VEGF in the retina stem from its functions as a key survival factor for EC and neurons through an AKT‐dependent mechanism [11, 12, 13] and as important support for the integrity and function of retinal glial cells, that is, astrocytes, Müller cells, and microglia [14, 15]. We postulate that a successful treatment to overcome the limitations of anti‐VEGF must target, beyond pathologic angiogenesis, other pathogenic determinants of PR, namely, reactive gliosis and its associated cytokine production [16, 17]. In support of this argument, blockade/modulation of pro‐inflammatory TNF signaling or reduction of microglia/Müller cell activation in the oxygen‐induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model significantly protected from PR [18, 19, 20]. We hypothesize that therapies that comprehensively target the spiraling interplay between ischemic and inflammatory insults, and reduce pathologic angiogenesis, while protecting the neuronal retinae from death, would ideally address this unmet clinical need.

In this study, we investigated whether A20, AKA Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha Inducible Protein‐3 (TNFAIP3), a protein that we and others have identified as a potent and ubiquitous inhibitor of NF‐κB activation [21, 22, 23], can preclude PR development and limit its severity. Our choice of A20 was informed by its previously established vasoprotective and broad anti‐inflammatory functions, albeit in other vascular beds and anatomical contexts. Indeed, A20 blocks NF‐κB activation in EC in response to inflammatory stimuli relevant to PR, such as TNF, oxidative stress, and PKC activation [21, 24, 25]. A20 also reduces microglial activation in the brain [26, 27] and exerts potent anti‐apoptotic functions in EC and neuronal cells [28, 29, 30]. However, concerns about A20's possible pro‐angiogenic effect needed prior resolution [31, 32]. Our gain and loss‐of‐function studies in primary human retinal EC cultures and in vivo in the OIR mouse model of ROP [33] eased this concern by uncovering a novel function for A20 as a negative modulator of pathologic angiogenesis. Intravitreal A20 gene therapy protects against OIR by comprehensively reducing pathologic retinal neovascularization, decreasing reactive gliosis, and protecting EC and the neuronal retina from apoptosis. Conversely, lesions of OIR were significantly aggravated in mice with partial (Heterozygous/Het) or total (knockout/KO) A20 knockdown [34]. Together, these results underscore the pivotal role of A20 as a physiologic modulator of ischemic PR severity and validate its promise as a therapeutic target.

Material and Methods

Cell Culture and Reagents

Primary human retinal endothelial cells (HREC) from 6 different donors were purchased from Cell Systems (Kirkland, WA), cultured according to the manufacturer's instructions, and used between passages 4 and 8. In select experiments, full medium was removed and replaced with basal serum‐free and growth factor‐free medium for 2 h prior to stimulation with VEGF. Recombinant human VEGF‐165/VEGF‐A was purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN). ARPE‐19, a human retinal pigment epithelial cell line CRL‐2302 (RPTEC), was purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA) and cultured according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Recombinant Adenoviruses and Silencing RNA

Recombinant A20 adenovirus (rAd.A20) expressing human A20/TNFAIP3, and control β‐galactosidase adenovirus (rAd.βgal) were generated and titrated in our laboratory [24, 29]. In vitro, tube formation loss‐of‐function experiments were conducted using a rAd. expressing GFP‐tagged short hairpin (sh) strand‐specific human A20 siRNA (Ad‐GFP‐U6‐h‐TNFAIP3‐shRNA), referred to as rAd.shA20, and control scrambled shRNA Ad‐GFP‐U6‐shRNA (rAd.shScr) purchased from Vector Biolabs (Malvern, PA). HREC and RPEC were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, resulting in 95%–100% of cells expressing the transgene 48 h post‐transduction with negligible toxicity [24, 29]. For scratch assays, A20 knockdown was achieved by transfecting HREC with human A20 siRNA (Hs_TNFAIP3_1 FlexiTube) at a final concentration of 40 nM, using the Hiperfect transfection reagent, both from Qiagen (Germantown, MD). AllStars fluorescent siRNA served as a control. Transduction/transfection efficiency and transgene expression (A20 and βgal) or knockdown (A20) were confirmed by Western blot (WB) analysis, immunohistochemistry (IHC, A20), Xgal staining (βgal), and fluorescence microscopy (GFP, Alexa Fluor), as described [24, 29, 35].

Endothelial Cell Angiogenesis and Migration Assays

HREC angiogenesis was assessed by the endothelial tube formation assay (TF), as described [36]. Corning full (fGF, CB‐40234A) and reduced growth factor (rGF, CB‐40234A) Matrigel membrane matrices were used in this assay (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). HREC (2 × 105 cells/well) were plated in basal medium (Cell Applications Inc. San Diego, CA) in a 96‐well plate, cultured for 48 h, and then imaged at 40× magnification with a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 inverted microscope. Quantitative analysis of TF parameters, including total mesh area (MA), total segment length (MS), number of nodes, and total branch length, was performed using the Fiji software and a plugin extension of the “Angiogenesis Analyzer” written in the ImageJ macro language [37].

EC migration was evaluated by the T‐scratch assay. At confluence, HREC transduced with rAd.A20 or rAd.βgal, or transfected with A20siRNA or AllStars siRNA, underwent wounding by clearing a cross‐shaped area within the cell monolayer using a pipette tip. Bright‐field micrographs centered on the intersection of the vertical and horizontal lines were captured at 40× magnification immediately after wounding and 9 h later. The percentage of residual wounded area 9 h post‐scratching was calculated using the TScratch software [38].

Ex Vivo Aortic Ring Assay

A20‐null (KO), A20 heterozygous (Het), and wild type (WT) littermates (kind gift of Dr. Averil Ma, UCSF) were bred in‐house [34]. Thoracic aortae from 3‐ to 4‐week‐old mice were transversely sliced into 0.5 mm‐thick rings and cultured in Matrigel supplemented with VEGF (50 ng/mL) and 1.5% horse serum. After 5 days, phase‐contrast micrographs were captured, and capillary sprouting was quantified using Image J‐based calculation of the area covered by branching microvessels [39].

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein cell lysates were extracted from HREC or mouse eyes and subjected to Western blot (WB) analysis to assess target protein expression. Primary antibodies used included rabbit anti‐VEGF‐R2, total‐ERK1/2 (t‐ERK1/2), phospho‐VEGF‐R2 Tyr‐1175 (P‐VEGFR2), phospho‐ERK1/2 Thr‐202/Tyr‐204 (ERK1) and Thr‐185/Tyr‐187 (ERK2), and phospho‐AKT Ser‐473 (P‐AKT) from Cell Signaling (Beverley, MA); rabbit anti‐human IκBα and mouse anti‐human β‐actin from Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc. (Santa Cruz, CA); rabbit anti‐t‐AKT and chicken anti‐human A20 from Abcam (Waltham, MA); and mouse anti‐GAPDH from EMD Chemicals (Bedford, MA). Secondary donkey anti‐rabbit and goat anti‐mouse HRP‐conjugated antibodies were purchased from Thermo Scientific (Rockford, IL). Protein bands were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence kit (ECL) from PerkinElmer Life Science (Waltham, MA) and quantified via ImageJ‐based densitometry. Alternatively, membranes were probed with IRDye infrared secondary antibodies, compatible with the LI‐COR Odyssey CLx imaging system (LI‐COR Inc., Lincoln, NE), and bands were quantified using Image StudioTM software (LI‐COR).

Quantitative RT‐PCR

RNA from HREC, fresh mouse retinae, or alternatively from three to four 25 μm retinal tissue sections, cut from Zinc‐fixed paraffin‐embedded samples, was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit and RNeasy FFPE kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD), followed by reverse transcription with iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted to analyze gene expression of human VEGF, VEGF‐R2, TNFAIP3, vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1/VCAM1, and intercellular adhesion molecule‐1/ICAM1, mouse VEGFA, ERK1, p65/RelA, Interleukin‐1β/IL1b, ICAM‐1, GFAP, and GS, and E.Coli LacZ primers and iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad), or TaqMan Mm00627280_m1 for A20 (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) on a 7500 Fast Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), or Stratagene Mx3005P (Agilent Technologies, Lexington, MA). Gene expression levels were determined using the Pfaffl method [40] and normalized to human and mouse 28S housekeeping genes (HKG). Primers used are listed in Table S1.

MTT and LDH Assays

The viability of RPEC after oxidative stress, modeled by treatment with 250 μM of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 16 and 24 h, was evaluated by the 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Sigma‐Aldrich) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release (G‐Biosciences, St Louis, MO) assays, as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Oxygen‐Induced Retinopathy (OIR)

Mouse litters from C57BL/6 (WT × WT, gain‐of‐function) and A20 Heterozygous (Het × Het, loss‐of‐function) breeding pairs [34] were housed with their nursing mothers in sealed hyperoxia chambers under 75% O2 from postnatal day 7 (P7) to P12 [33]. This induces vaso‐obliteration (VO) of developing retinal vessels within 2 days of hyperoxia exposure [11, 41]. At P12, mice were returned to room air, triggering the central hypoxic retina to produce pro‐angiogenic VEGF, mainly by glial cells and EC. Elevated intra‐retinal VEGF levels cause pathological neovascular tufts (NV) at the junction of the avascular and vascularized retina between P17 and P21, with NV tufts being most severe at P17 [42]. In gain‐of‐function experiments, mice exiting the hyperoxia chamber were anesthetized, and their left eye was intravitreally injected with 1 μL of saline, rAd.A20, or rAd.βgal at 1 × 108 viral genome (vg). Non‐treated contralateral right eyes, along with the eyes of mouse pups raised in normoxia, served as controls. Transgene delivery to the retina was confirmed by immunostaining using rabbit polyclonal anti‐βgal (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO) and rabbit monoclonal anti‐TNFAIP3 (Abcam) antibodies, and at the mRNA level by RT‐PCR. Lesions of OIR were assessed at P17, and all animals were sacrificed at this time. In loss‐of‐function experiments, a piece of tail was obtained at the time of sacrifice for DNA extraction and genotyping, as described [43, 44]. All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Guide for Use and Care of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Institutional Committee for Use and Care of Laboratory Animals.

Retinal Angiograms

Retinal angiograms were conducted following established procedures [45]. At P17, the pups' eyes were dilated, and a contact lens was applied to the rAd‐injected left eye. Sodium fluorescein solution (5 mg/kg) was perfused through the jugular vein, and a laser ophthalmoscope was used to acquire 4‐quadrant fluorescence real‐time angiograms. Pertinent digitized images from each quadrant were used to calculate the percentage of avascular areas.

Retinal Whole Mounts

High‐molecular‐mass FITC Dextran (mol wt 2 × 106) from Sigma‐Aldrich was injected into the inferior vena cava of anesthetized mouse pups and allowed to circulate for 2 min prior to sacrificing the animals and collecting their eyes. Retinae were isolated and mounted on slides, and images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The percentage of VO and NV areas per retina was quantified using the Image J‐based SWIFT NV module software, as described [46].

Immunohistochemistry, Immunofluorescence, and TUNEL Staining

Six‐μm zinc‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded eye sections were stained with various antibodies, including the proliferation marker Ki67 (Agilent/DAKO, Santa Clara, CA), P‐AKT (Cell Signaling), the endothelial cell‐specific marker CD31 (BD Pharmingen), VEGF (Santa Cruz), VEGF‐R2 (Abcam), Iba‐1(Abcam), human TNFAIP3 (Abcam), P‐PKCβII Serine‐660 (Santa Cruz), P‐p65/RelA Serine‐536 (Santa Cruz) and βgalactosidase (Novus Biologicals). HRP‐conjugated avidin‐biotin secondary antibodies and DAB ImmPact substrate were purchased from Vector Labs (Burlingame, CA). Counterstaining was done with Hematoxylin/Eosin (H&E) (Newcomer Supply Inc. Middleton, WI) or methyl green (Vector Labs). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining for glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP) and vimentin was done using a rabbit polyclonal anti‐GFAP antibody (Agilent/DAKO, red) and a rabbit anti‐vimentin monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling, green), followed by VectaFluor dye‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Vector Labs). IF co‐labeling for GFAP and GS was done using a chicken anti‐GFAP antibody (NBP1‐0519, Novus Biologicals), and a rabbit anti‐glutamine synthetase polyclonal antibody (# PA5‐28940, Invitrogen), followed by Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure donkey anti‐chicken IgY (Jackson ImmunoResearch Lab Inc., West Grove, PA), and donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen) secondary antibodies. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using 4′, 6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, 1 μg/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich) or Höechst 33 258, pentahydrate (bis‐benzimide) from Invitrogen. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase‐mediated dUTP nick‐end labeling (TUNEL, Millipore, Billerica, MA) and Fluoro‐Jade C (FJC) labeling (Biosensis, Thebarton, South Australia) were used to detect apoptosis and degenerating neurons, respectively, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Positive FJC staining was visualized by fluorescence microscopy (475–650 nm). Unless indicated, for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and IF staining, four to six 200× magnification images were captured per retinal section using the Olympus DP72 microscope camera and CellSens software (Tokyo, Japan), or the MICA Microhub imaging system with LAS X software (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Quantification included the number of Ki67+ and TUNEL+ nuclei per high‐power field (HPF), Iba‐1+ cells per square millimeter, and area of P‐p65+ nuclei/area of total nuclei per retinal section. CD31 IHC was reported in gain‐of‐function studies as the number of CD31+ vessels in the superficial vascular plexus per retinal section, while it was reported in loss‐of‐function studies as the sum of vascular CD31+ pixels per HPF because of excessive pathologic angiogenesis. Integrated staining density/retinal section for VEGF, VEGF‐R2, P‐PKCβII, and P‐AKT was determined using the ImageJ Fiji 2.9.0 software (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Integrated density of grayscale‐transformed images was calculated based on the average pixel product value (0–255) multiplied by retinal area, and reported as arbitrary units (AU). Integrated fluorescence intensity for GS and GFAP was quantified using Fiji 2.9.0. Images were first background‐corrected, and the region of interest was identified. Particle detection was then acquired by the ComDet plugin (v.0.5.5). Detection parameters, including channel, approximate particle size in pixels, and signal‐to‐noise threshold, were optimized and then kept constant at a 5.00‐pixel particle size and an intensity threshold of 20.00. Values (mean and SD) were exported and further analyzed in GraphPad. For both integrated density and integrated intensity, at least 3 fields of view and 3–7 biological replicates were quantified per group.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as mean with standard error of means (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted using Prism 9 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's or Fisher's post hoc test was primarily utilized in most experiments. Unpaired t‐tests were used in selected experiments with two experimental groups. A probability (p) value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

In Vitro Gain‐ and Loss‐Of‐Function Studies Identify A20 as a Novel Regulator of Angiogenic Responses

The effect of A20 on angiogenic responses is still unresolved. To address this, we assessed the impact of A20 overexpression in human retinal EC (HREC) on angiogenic responses using tube formation (TF) and EC migration assays [47]. HREC transduced with rAd.A20 for 30 h, then plated into reduced growth factor Matrigel (rGF) for 48 h, developed significantly fewer tube‐like structures than control rAd.βgal‐transduced cells, as quantified by the Angiogenesis Analyzer software, which measures several TF vectorial elements [37]. Total mesh area (MA), representing areas delineated by segment‐forming tubes, and total master segment length (MS) were significantly lower in rAd.A20 vs. rAd.βgal‐transduced HREC, with mean ± SEM respectively reaching 1.42 ± 0.34 vs. 3.39 ± 0.23 × 106 pixels/high power field (HPF), p < 0.01, n = 4; and 1.95 ± 0.18 vs. 2.98 ± 0.14 × 104 pixels/HPF, p < 0.01, n = 3–4 (Figure 1A). Notably, A20 overexpression in HREC did not affect the number of nodes or total branch length, suggesting that A20 rather disrupts the later stages of the TF (Figure 1A). This A20 effect was not reversed in the full GF Matrigel (fGF) supplemented with optimal concentrations of VEGF. Both total MA (1.73 ± 0.49 vs. 3.05 ± 0.13 × 106 pixels/HPF, p < 0.05, n = 4–5) and total MS (1.97 ± 0.28 vs. 2.87 ± 0.18 × 104 pixels/HPF, p < 0.05, n = 4–5) remained significantly lower in rAd.A20 vs. rAd.βgal‐transduced HREC (Figure 1A). Overexpression of A20 in HREC also hindered cell migration. The percentage of residual wound area 9 h post‐scratch was significantly larger in rAd.A20 (90.6 ± 1.8) vs. non‐transduced (NT) (42.8 ± 5.9) and rAd.βgal‐transduced (47.8 ± 2.5) HREC (p < 0.001, n = 3, Figure 1B). Together, these results provide the first direct evidence that overexpression of A20 in HREC impedes their angiogenic responses in vitro. This novel A20 function did not stem from decreased bioavailability or expression level of VEGF, nor the expression level or membrane localization of its receptor VEGF‐R2/Flk1. Indeed, A20 overexpression in HREC did not affect basal or hypoxia‐induced upregulation of VEGF and VEGF‐R2 mRNA levels in HREC (Figure S1A,B). Basal VEGF‐R2 protein levels were even higher in rAd.A20‐transduced compared to rAd.βgal‐transduced or non‐transduced (NT) HREC, both at baseline (Figure 1C, n = 3, p < 0.01) and after hypoxia (Figure S1C). Downstream phosphorylation of VEGF‐R2 Tyr‐1175 was comparable in all groups, confirming VEGF‐R2 functionality and cell surface expression (Figure S1D), which suggests that A20 modulates angiogenic responses downstream or independently from receptor activation.

Next, we evaluated the effect of A20 knockdown in HREC on TF and EC migration. HREC transduction with rAd.shA20 or transfection with A20siRNA resulted in a significant 65%–75% A20 protein knockdown (Figure S2). HREC transduced with rAd.shA20 exhibit significantly greater total branch length compared to rAd.shScr‐transduced controls, under both rGF (1.82 ± 0.13 vs. 0.98 ± 0.31 × 104 pixels/HPF, p < 0.05, n = 4) and fGF (2.14 ± 0.08 vs. 1.34 ± 0.20, p < 0.05, n = 4) conditions. They also demonstrate a trend towards a higher number of nodes/HPF in rAd.shA20 vs. rAd.shScr‐transduced HREC cultured in rGF (1371 ± 131.5 vs. 887 ± 163.5/HPF, p = 0.06, n = 4). However, A20 knockdown had no impact on total MA or total MS, indicating that increased branching and node numbers in these cells did not translate into a full‐fledged pro‐angiogenic effect (Figure 2A). Additional experiments are planned to explore the mechanisms behind this observation. A20 knockdown also did not affect EC migration in the scratch assay (Figure 2B, n = 5). To address concerns that greater susceptibility of A20‐deficient EC to apoptosis might affect the outcome of these in vitro assays [28], we checked how A20 knockdown impacts angiogenesis in the mouse aortic ring assay, which reduces the susceptibility of A20‐deficient EC to apoptosis by maintaining them in their tissue microenvironment. Ex vivo aortic rings from WT and A20‐null (KO) or heterozygous (Het) mice were isolated, and the endothelium was allowed to sprout in Matrigel. The vascular sprouting area was significantly larger in KO vs. WT aortic rings, measuring 53.5 ± 6.9 vs. 9.0 ± 1.2 mm2/ring, p < 0.001, n = 4, 6 replicates (Figure 2C). Aortic rings from Het mice showed an intermediate phenotype with significantly less sprouting compared to KO (16.4 ± 5.5 mm2/ring, p < 0.01, n = 3, 6 replicates) and a trend towards greater sprouting compared to WT. These results demonstrate that full or partial A20 knockout enhances angiogenic responses, identifying A20 as a physiologic regulator of angiogenesis.

Eye‐Directed A20 Gene Therapy Protects From OIR While A20 Knockdown Aggravates Disease Severity

Having identified A20 as a regulator of angiogenesis in HREC, we investigated the relevance of this finding in the OIR mouse model of ROP [48]. In this model, newborn mice from postnatal days 7 to 12 (P7‐P12) are housed in sealed hyperoxic chambers, which halts the development and maturation of the retinal vasculature. Return to room air at P12 triggers retinal hypoxia and subsequent pathologic angiogenesis. In gain‐of‐function studies, the left eye of C57BL/6 P12 mouse pups, which do not carry any gene for retinal degeneration, received intravitreal injections of saline, rAd.A20, or rAd.βgal upon exiting the hyperoxic chamber. Analysis at P17 of mouse retinae by real‐time 4‐quadrant retinal fluorescence angiography or by fluorescence microscopy following the perfusion of a FITC‐dextran solution shows that the percentage of central avascular area per retina, quantified on nasal and temporal retinal field images of the fluorescence angiography, significantly increased in saline and rAd.βgal‐treated eyes compared to control age‐matched retinae of pups kept in normoxia (p < 0.01, n = 3, Figure 3A). Remarkably, the percent of the avascular retinal area in rAd.A20‐treated eyes was comparable to normoxia controls and significantly smaller than in saline or rAd.βgal‐treated eyes, reaching 19.2% ± 2.2% vs. 50.0% ± 6.5% and 44.0% ± 4.0%, respectively (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05; n = 3–4, Figure 3A). Similar benefits were evident in retinal whole mounts with rAd.A20‐treated eyes exhibiting significantly smaller areas of central retinal vaso‐obliteration (VO), expressed as a percentage of whole retinal area, than saline and rAd.βgal‐treated eyes, reaching 2.9% ± 0.7% vs. 17.2% ± 0.9% and 20.0% ± 1.2%, respectively (p < 0.0001; n = 3–4; Figure 3B). Peripheral retinal areas occupied by abnormal neovascular tufts (NV) were also significantly smaller in rAd.A20 vs. saline and rAd.βgal‐treated eyes, reaching 4.4% ± 0.3% vs. 8.9% ± 0.5% and 11.4% ± 0.6%, respectively (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001; n = 3–4, Figure 3B). VO and NV areas in untreated contralateral retinae were comparable in all groups. These findings demonstrate, for the first time, that delivery of A20 gene therapy to the ischemic retina prevents lesions of ROP.

In loss‐of‐function studies, we used A20 Het and KO mice along with their WT littermates [34]. We confirmed by IHC the absence of A20 expression in retinae of P17 KO mice, while A20 was still detected in the retinae of WT and Het mice, albeit staining intensity was stronger in WT (Figure S3). Due to the vulnerability of KO pups to anesthesia during live angiography, OIR lesions were only assessed on retinal whole mounts following FITC‐Dextran perfusion. P17 retinae of KO pups exposed to hyperoxia exhibited significantly larger retinal VO areas compared to WT (24.1 ± 0.7 vs. 13.7% ± 1.9%, p < 0.001, n = 3), with Het pups fairing in between (18.9% ± 0.5%, p < 0.05 vs. WT and KO, n = 3; Figure 3C). Retinae of KO pups also showed a markedly higher percentage of NV areas than WT (24.9% ± 0.8% vs. 8.2% ± 1.0%, p < 0.0001, n = 3), with Het pups in between (13.1% ± 1.5%, p < 0.05 vs. WT and p < 0.001 vs. KO, n = 3). In normoxia, the retinal NV area remained below 3%. These in vivo findings confirm that A20 is a physiologic regulator of ischemia‐driven pathologic angiogenesis.

Eye‐Directed A20 Gene Therapy Reduces Pathologic Retinal Neovascularization and Associates With Decreased Proliferative Signals Downstream of VEGF‐R2, While A20 Knockdown Aggravates These Features

To elucidate the mechanisms of A20's protective effect in OIR, we conducted IHC, IF, Western blot (WB) analysis, and qPCR on P17 mouse pup retinae to assess markers of angiogenesis and EC proliferation. A20 overexpression significantly decreased the number of CD31‐positive vascular structures (32.8 ± 4.2 per retinal section, n = 5) in the nerve fiber and ganglion cell layers (NFL/GCL), primary sites of pathologic neovascularization in ROP, compared to saline (53.6 ± 3.1 per retinal section; p < 0.01, n = 5) and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae (50.7 ± 3.3 per retinal section, p < 0.05, n = 4, Figure 4A). Additionally, rAd.A20‐treated retinae showed normal vascular plexus morphology, while saline and rAd.βgal‐treated ones displayed typical OIR lesions, including enlarged vessel lumen and intravitreal invasion (Figure 4A). These results underscore the ability of A20 to prevent pathologic NV formation, all the while facilitating normal revascularization of the hypoxic retina. We verified by IHC that intravitreal injection of 1 × 108 vg/eye of rAd.A20 or rAd.βgal, expressed under the control of the universal CMV promoter, resulted in transgene expression throughout all retinal layers and cell types, as consistent with previous gene therapy reports [49]. A20 or βgal expression was robust in the GCL, which encompasses the retinal superficial vascular plexus and astrocytes. It remained substantial in the inner nuclear layer (INL), which harbors the inner retinal vascular plexus as well as amacrine, bipolar, and horizontal cells, in addition to Müller cell bodies. It was also detected in photoreceptors (rods and cones) within the outer nuclear layer (ONL) (n = 4–5, Figure 4B). Expression of A20 and βgal in treated retinae was also confirmed by qPCR (n = 3–4, Figure 4C).

We next assessed cell proliferation using Ki67 immunostaining. P17 mouse retinae treated with rAd.A20 showed very few Ki67+ nuclei, whereas rAd.βgal‐treated retinae exhibited a marked increase, predominantly lining the GCL vascular structures (2.1 ± 0.9 vs. 82.7 ± 7.5 per HPF; p < 0.0001, n = 4–6, Figure 5C). Next, we assessed activating phosphorylation levels of PKCβII (P‐PKCβII) and ERK1/2 (P‐ERK1/2), which drive VEGF and FGF proliferative and migratory signals in EC [6, 52]. Immunostaining for P‐PKCβII Ser‐660, predominating in the GCL and INL, was significantly lower in rAd.A20 vs. rAd.βgal‐treated retinae, as measured by integrated density of staining and expressed in arbitrary units (AU) (0.12 ± 0.05 × 106 vs. 5.77 ± 1.49 × 106, p < 0.01, n = 5, Figure 5C). P‐PKCβII immunostaining in rAd.A20 was comparable to retinae of age‐matched mice kept in normoxia. WB analysis of phospho (P) and total (t) ERK1/2 expression also showed significantly lower levels of both phosphorylated and total ERK1/2 in P17 rAd.A20 vs. saline and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae (p < 0.05, n = 3, Figure 5D). A20‐mediated downregulation of total ERK1/2, and consequently of P‐ERK1/2, in ischemic retinae is a novel finding. It was associated with a marked attenuation of ischemia‐induced increase of ERK1 mRNA levels in P17 rAd.A20 compared to saline/rAd.βgal‐treated retinae, relative to baseline levels in retinae of age‐matched mice kept in normoxia (651 ± 123 vs. 2353 ± 213‐fold; p < 0.0001; n = 4–6; Figure 5E).

In loss‐of‐function studies, we observed a trend, albeit not reaching significance, towards increased CD31+ staining in the GCL and INL of P17 Het and KO mouse retinae compared to WT, suggesting exacerbated neovascularization (Figure 6A). Retinal vessels in P17 Het and KO mice displayed larger lumens and greater tortuosity than in WT retinae, which corresponded with a significantly higher number of Ki67+ cells lining abnormal vascular structures in KO and Het compared to WT retinae (30.3 ± 3.0 and 31.5 ± 3.8 vs. 15.3 ± 1.3 Ki67+ cells/HPF, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively, n = 4–8; Figure 6B). Heightened vascular proliferative index in KO and Het retinae was associated with a trend for higher VEGF‐R2 expression levels compared to WT (Figure 6A), and notably matched with significantly higher P‐PKCβII immunostaining in GCL and INL of KO retinae compared to WT, with Het retinae fairing in between (Figure 6B). Integrated density of P‐PKCβII staining reached 2.77 ± 0.94 × 106 and 1.88 ± 0.49 × 106 AU per retinal section in KO and Het, respectively, compared to 1.15 ± 0.34 × 106 in WT (p < 0.05 KO vs. WT, n = 3–7). It is also associated with a > 9‐fold increase in P‐ERK1/2, but not total ERK1/2, protein levels in KO and Het vs. WT retinae (p < 0.01, n = 2–4, Figure 6C). These findings further ascertain the key physiologic role of A20 in modulating angiogenic responses in the ischemic retina.

Intravitreal A20 Gene Therapy Reduces Hypoxia‐Induced Microglial and Müller Cell Activation and Degeneration, While A20 Knockdown Exacerbates These Features

Reactive retinal gliosis, involving activation of Müller cells, astrocytes, and microglia, and the consequential degeneration of these cells that are vital for endothelial and neuronal cell survival and function, significantly contributes to OIR pathogenesis and severity [1, 19, 20].

Because activation of microglia, astrocytes, and Müller cells in ROP is NF‐κB‐dependent [53, 54], we examined whether overexpression or knockdown of A20, a negative regulator of NF‐κB activation, impacts this response. NF‐κB activation, first assessed by phosho‐p65/RelA Ser‐536 (P‐p65) immunostaining [55], showed that the ratio of P‐p65+ nuclei over the total nucleic area was significantly increased in the GCL, INL, and ONL of saline and rAd.βgal‐treated P17 retinae compared to normoxia controls (22.3 ± 2.6 and 27.5 ± 5.5 vs. 3.0 ± 2.1, p < 0.01, n = 5–7; Figure 7A), In contrast, this increase was remarkably blunted in rAd.A20‐treated retinae (7.6 ± 3.5, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 vs. saline and rAd.βgal, respectively, n = 5–7, Figure 7A). Reduced NF‐κB activation in P17 rAd.A20‐treated retinae resulted in significantly lower induction of downstream NF‐κB targets, and key pathogenic contributors to ROP severity, as reflected by significantly lower mRNA levels of p65/RelA itself, the pro‐inflammatory cytokine interleukin‐1 β, and the adhesion molecule ICAM‐1 in rAd.A20 compared to saline‐ and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae (n = 3–5, Figure 7B).

Next, we evaluated reactive gliosis by immunostaining with the microglial activation marker, Iba‐1 [56]. Our results show a significant increase in the number of Iba‐1+ cells, primarily in the GCL, inner (IPL), and outer plexiform (OPL) layers, in P17 mouse retinae treated with saline or rAd.βgal compared to normoxia controls (405 ± 48.8 and 350 ± 63.2 vs. 153 ± 33.4 cells/mm2, p < 0.01 and p < 0.05; n = 3–5; Figure 7C). Iba‐1+ cells were hypertrophic with distorted morphology, indicative of activation [57]. Conversely, the number and morphology of Iba1+ cells in rAd.A20‐treated retinae were comparable to normoxia controls and significantly lower (196 ± 37.8 cells/mm2, n = 6) than in saline and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively; Figure 7C). Reactive retinal gliosis was also manifested by greater expression of GFAP in Müller cells [58], which was markedly increased in the IPL of saline and rAd.βgal‐treated P17 retinae compared to normoxia and co‐stained with fragmented vimentin, indicating structural damage (Figure 7D). The acquisition of GFAP expression by Müller cells, the predominant glial cells in the retina, along with the stiffening and fragmentation of their filaments, as observed by Vimentin, co‐immunolabeling, is a pathognomonic marker of these cells' activation in response to retinal hypoxia or injury [58, 59]. Remarkably, rAd.A20‐treated retinae displayed a GFAP and vimentin co‐IF staining pattern similar to retinae of age‐matched mice kept in normoxia. Another key pathological feature of Müller cell activation and dysfunction is the loss of GS, a Müller cell‐specific enzyme critical for detoxifying excess glutamate, thereby maintaining neurotransmitter homeostasis and protecting neurons from excitotoxicity [60, 61, 62]. In the OIR model, it is well established that the rise of glutamate levels as a result of reduced levels of GS in Müller cells contributes to disease severity [63]. Accordingly, we assessed GS expression in mouse retinae using both IF and qPCR. In P17 retinae of mice kept in normoxia, GS IF showed strong staining of Müller cell bodies and processes, spanning all retinal layers (NFL/GCL, INL, and ONL, Figure 7E). GS IF, as measured as integrated intensity and expressed as AU/retinal section, was significantly reduced in P17 saline and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae, reaching 2357 ± 314 and 2173 ± 376, respectively, compared to 463 ± 433 AU/retinal section in normoxia (p < 0.01, n = 4, Figure 7E). Remarkably, GS IF in rAd.A20‐treated retinae remained comparable to levels in normoxia (3973 ± 375, n = 7), and hence significantly higher than in saline and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae (p < 0.01, Figure 7E). By co‐IF, we further confirmed that the loss of GS expression in Müller cells was accompanied by a parallel induction of GFAP. Specifically, the GS/GFAP IF integrated intensity ratio that was 3.2 ± 0.6 in rAd.A20‐treated retinae, totally dropped to 0.8 ± 0.15 and 0.9 ± 014 in saline and rAd.βgal‐treated retinae, respectively (p < 0.01, n = 4–6; Figure 7E), indicating a significant molecular switch in Müller cell phenotype. Retinal RNA analysis further supports these findings, showing significantly lower GS and higher GFAP mRNA levels in P17 retinae of saline and rAd.βgal‐treated compared with rAd.A20‐treated retinae (n = 3–5/group, Figure 7F).

In contrast to the gain‐of‐function studies, A20 deficiency increased the ratio of P‐p65+/total nuclei in P17 mouse retinae. KO mice retinae exhibited a significantly higher ratio of P‐p65+/total nuclei area per retinal section compared to WT (39.0 ± 7.6 vs. 22.7 ± 2.4, p < 0.05, n = 3 and 7, respectively, Figure 8A), while Het mice had an intermediate ratio (27.0 ± 3.7, n = 5, Figure 8A). A20 deficiency also exacerbated reactive retinal gliosis. P17 KO mice retinae displayed a significantly higher number of Iba‐1+ cells/mm2 compared to WT (759 ± 169.9 vs. 383 ± 34.9 cells/mm2, p < 0.05, n = 3 and 6, respectively), with Het mice again showing an intermediate phenotype (489 ± 120.7 cells/mm2, n = 5; Figure 8B). Both integrated IF intensity of GS and the GS/GFAP integrated IF intensity ratio were significantly reduced in retinae of P17 WT, HET and KO compared to retinae of age‐matched mice in normoxia (p < 0.001, n = 3–6, Figure 8C). However, no significant differences were observed between genotypes, likely reflecting the already maximal severity of these alterations in WT mice (Figure 8C).

Eye‐Directed A20 Gene Therapy Protects the Neuronal Retina, in Part by Activating the AKT Survival Pathway, While A20 Knockdown Aggravates Neuronal Demise

The severity of OIR, which can result in vision loss, correlates with the extent of retinal damage across all cell types, particularly neuronal cells. A20‐mediated protection from OIR was associated with significantly lower numbers of TUNEL+ apoptotic nuclei in rAd.A20 compared to rAd.βgal‐treated P17 retinae (7.8 ± 2.0 vs. 48.5 ± 13.8 TUNEL+ nuclei/HPF, p < 0.01, n = 6–7; Figure 8A). Next, we stained P17 retinal sections with an antibody against phosphorylated AKT Ser‐473, a surrogate marker for the activation of the PI3K/AKT survival pathway which is crucial for neuronal, Müller/glial cells, and EC health [11, 14, 15, 64, 65]. Data show that rAd.A20‐treated retinae exhibit significantly higher P‐AKT immunostaining compared to rAd.βgal‐treated eyes (9.0 ± 2.3 vs. 2.0 ± 0.6 × 106 AU/retinal section, p < 0.01, n = 6–7, Figure 9A). Interestingly, P‐AKT expression in rAd.A20‐treated retinae was also significantly higher than in normoxic retinae (3.5 ± 0.8 × 106 AU/retinal section, p < 0.05, n = 5), which suggests that overexpression of A20 may advantageously promote the AKT survival pathway, either directly or by amplifying VEGF‐mediated AKT activation. To specifically gauge the effect of A20 on neuronal demise, we stained the retinae with Fluoro‐Jade C (FJC), which only detects neuronal apoptosis [66]. Our results show significantly lower FJC fluorescence in rAd.A20 vs. rAd.βgal‐treated retinae (13.4 ± 1.8 vs. 21.4 ± 2.3 × 106 AU/retinal section, p < 0.01, n = 6), including at the level of the optic nerve (OpN) (Figure 9B). Loss‐of‐function studies showed a significant but comparable increase in TUNEL+ cells in retinae of KO, Het, and WT P17 mice, suggesting that A20 knockdown does not substantially alter the overall rate of apoptosis in hypoxic retinae (Figure 9C). However, P‐AKT immunostaining was significantly lower in KO vs. WT retinae (4.4 ± 1.6 vs. 10.2 ± 1.8 × 106 AU/retinal section, p < 0.05, n = 3 and 10 respectively; Figure 9C), with Het pups displaying intermediate expression (6.5 ± 0.8 × 106, n = 10). Moreover, FJC fluorescence was significantly higher in P17 KO retinae compared to Het and WT (20.9 ± 2.1 vs. 15.1 ± 2.2 and 13.9 ± 0.17 × 106 AU/retinal section, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively, n = 3–9), including at the level of the OpN (Figure 9D). Collectively, these results underscore the neuroprotective function of A20 in the retina, potentially mediated, at least in part, through an AKT‐dependent mechanism.

Discussion

Since their introduction in 2002 for treating wet age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), five intravitreal anti‐VEGF therapies have received FDA approval for AMD, PDR, diabetic macular edema (DME), retinal vein occlusion, and ROP [67, 68]. Despite their tremendous benefits, the success of these therapies remains impeded by ocular and systemic side effects, as well as primary and secondary resistance [68, 69, 70]. Resistance to anti‐VEGF therapies underscores the complex pathophysiology of PR, involving other inflammatory and angiogenic culprits besides VEGF [71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76]. Various therapies targeting these additional factors, such as combined inhibition of VEGF‐A and angiopoietin‐2 and the use of anti‐inflammatory steroids, have only resulted in marginal improvements with significant side‐effects [77, 78, 79]. This underscores the need for safer, more effective alternatives informed by pathophysiology. In this study, we investigated the impact of the potent NF‐κB inhibitory protein, A20, on PR. We chose A20 for its comprehensive anti‐inflammatory and anti‐apoptotic effects in EC, protective functions in the neuronal retina, and unique ability to limit reactive gliosis [21, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. We had to first address the controversy regarding its role in angiogenesis [31, 32] by revisiting the impact of its overexpression or knockdown on EC migration and TF, using primary HREC as a relevant cell type to PR. Using the Angiogenesis Analyzer software, we found that A20 overexpression in HREC significantly reduced MA and MS but not node number, indicating negative modulation of angiogenic responses without complete suppression. A20 overexpression also hindered EC migration. These findings are the first gain‐of‐function studies linking A20 overexpression to reduced angiogenic responses in vitro. A20's effect on TF was resistant to VEGF, despite higher VEGF‐R2 protein, but with unchanged mRNA levels. Since VEGF‐R2 is regulated by trafficking and ubiquitin–proteasome degradation [80] A20, a lysosomal deubiquitinase enzyme [81], may have disrupted its lysosomal delivery or degradation, causing its levels to increase. Notably, A20 overexpression did not alter VEGF‐induced activating Tyr‐1175 phosphorylation of VEGF‐R2, placing its action downstream of receptor activation.

Conversely, A20 knockdown in HREC significantly enhanced early TF steps (node number and total branch length), without raising total MA or MS. Because this result may simply reflect EC dysfunction and the greater susceptibility of A20‐deficient EC to apoptosis, especially when cultured in isolation due to reduced eNOS expression [25, 28, 29, 82], we used the aortic ring assay which preserves the native environment and increases the resilience of these cells [83]. Our results reveal a net pro‐angiogenic effect of A20 knockdown, with greater vascular sprouting in aortic rings from A20 KO and Het vs. WT mice. These results establish A20 as a physiologic regulator of angiogenesis and clarify prior reports that suggested anti‐angiogenic effects based only on reduced TF in A20‐silenced EC, without accounting for compromised survival that can confound data interpretation [31].

Next, we investigated A20 gain and loss in the OIR mouse model of PR. Our data indicate that A20 modulates angiogenic signals in the ischemic retina through mechanisms distinct from conventional anti‐VEGF therapies. Intra‐vitreal administration of rAd.A20 significantly reduced peripheral pathologic neovascularization and central avascularity, restoring near‐normal retinal vasculature by P17, without altering hypoxia‐driven upregulation of VEGF and VEGF‐R2, which was comparable across groups. Although NF‐κB mediates VEGF transcription via HIF‐1α, VEGF was still upregulated in rAd.A20‐treated retinae, despite NF‐κB inhibition [84]. This underscores VEGF complex regulation and suggests that other transcription factors, such as signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), previously shown to be activated by A20 in hepatocytes [85], might be involved.

A20 overexpression decreased EC proliferative indices in the ischemic retinae by significantly reducing VEGF/VEGF‐R2 proliferative and migratory signaling, as gauged by lower levels of P‐PKCβII and P‐ERK1/2 [5, 6, 52], and decreasing protein and mRNA levels of ERK1, uncovering novel anti‐angiogenic targets for this protein. A20 KO and Het retinae showed the opposite, with more pathological angiogenesis, higher proliferative indices, and greater expression of P‐PKCβII and P‐ERK1/2, despite comparable VEGF/VEGF‐R2 levels to those in WT. Taken together, A20's ability to inhibit pathological angiogenesis while preserving VEGF/VEGF‐R2 levels in the ischemic retina ascertains its promise as a safer alternative to anti‐VEGF therapies, which indiscriminately block all VEGF signaling, including essential survival pathways for endothelial, neuronal, and glial cells [14, 86, 87].

Since our rationale for exploring A20 in PR was informed by its anti‐inflammatory properties, we confirmed that its overexpression reduced, while its knockdown enhanced NF‐κB activation, with corresponding effects on downstream targets and pathogenic mediators of ROP, including ICAM‐1, and IL‐1β. In the ischemic retina (ROP, DR), inflammation is largely driven by glial activation, where cytokine‐producing Iba‐1+ microglia accumulate and promote pathologic neovascularization and neuronal loss [88, 89, 90]. We identified A20 as a novel regulator of microglial activation: its overexpression decreased and normalized Iba‐1+ microglial morphology, while its deficiency increased their number and activation. Unlike microglia depletion strategies that worsen pathologic NV, A20 favorably tempered gliosis while preserving microglial integrity [19, 20, 26, 91]. Notably, activation of the matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9, together with abnormal complement activation leading to increased C3 secretion in the vitreous and tears of infants with ROP secondary to gliosis, has also been identified as pathogenic drivers of pathological angiogenesis and disease severity [92, 93, 94, 95, 96]. Future studies are planned to evaluate how A20 gain or loss impacts the expression levels and activity of these additional culprits. A20 also reduced Müller cell activation, a major contributor to pathologic NV and impaired revascularization in OIR [19]. Eye‐directed A20 gene therapy preserved glutamine synthase and suppressed GFAP expression in Müller cells, likely by mitigating IL‐1‐driven GS downregulation in these cells [63], thereby sustaining Müller cells' ability to support photoreceptor health and function [19]. Collectively, these findings highlight A20's broad ability to mitigate gliosis while upholding Müller cell homeostasis, extending its therapeutic indications beyond PR to other retinopathies in which gliosis drives pathology, such as DME and retinitis pigmentosa [73, 97].

Apoptosis, triggered by hypoxia and inflammation, is a critical determinant of outcomes in PR. Early EC loss worsens hypoxia, accelerating glial cell death, depriving EC and neuronal cells of essential survival cues, and consequently amplifying neuronal demise and blindness in PR. A therapy that prevents apoptosis in EC, glial and neuronal cells could therefore confer substantial benefit. A20's documented anti‐apoptotic effects in all three cell types uniquely fit this need [28, 30, 98]. In our model, rAd.A20‐treated retinae showed significantly fewer TUNEL+ apoptotic nuclei than rAd.βgal‐treated controls, particularly in the neuron and glia‐rich INL and ONL. Apoptotic cells were scarce in the GCL of rAd.βgal‐treated retinae, where the superficial vascular plexus is located, suggesting that peak EC apoptosis likely peaks earlier in the OIR model [99]. Neuron‐specific apoptosis staining (FJC) confirmed significantly reduced neuronal cell death in rAd.A20‐treated retinae, whereas A20 deficiency aggravated it. Notably, the partial mismatch between TUNEL and FJC labeling likely reflects TUNEL's limited ability to detect necrotic neuronal death, implying that most TUNEL+ cells in the INL/ONL were glial cells. Mechanistically, A20 appears to support neuronal survival through both direct and indirect effects. Directly, it confers cytoprotection against hypoxia and inflammation, preserving neuronal ability to support reparative revascularization, which is otherwise hindered by vaso‐repellents produced by ischemic neurons, which restore oxygenation [100]. Indirectly, A20 tempers gliosis and preserves glial neurotrophic and EC‐protective functions, which in turn facilitate reparative vascularization, breaking the cycle of ischemia and neuronal degeneration.

At the molecular level, A20 decreases apoptosis largely through activation of the AKT survival pathway, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of its Ser‐473 site [101, 102]. P‐AKT, broadly expressed across retinal layers and cell types, including GCL vascular structures, Müller cells, and the glial and photoreceptor‐rich ONL [64], likely provides survival advantages to EC, neuronal cells, and glial cells alike. Mere protection of EC, as in bax/bak KO models, being already sufficient to limit ROP, underscores the importance of apoptosis control in this pathology, and reinforces A20's therapeutic potential [99]. Notably, A20 overexpression consistently increased P‐AKT while inhibiting VEGF‐driven P‐ERK1/2 and P‐PKCβII pro‐angiogenic signaling, indicating selective reinforcement of the AKT survival pathway, whether VEGF‐dependent or not, while limiting pathologic proliferation. Conversely, loss of A20 reduces P‐AKT and increases P‐ERK1/2, underscoring its regulatory role.

AKT activation may also restrain pathological angiogenesis via inhibitory phosphorylation of c‐Raf (Ser‐259), upstream of ERK1/2 activation [103]. The opposing effects of A20 gain and loss of function on P‐AKT and P‐ERK1/2 levels suggest that such crosstalk may well be implicated in A20's ability to limit pathologic angiogenesis. Unlike prior reports that overlooked AKT's temporal and contextual regulation [12, 104, 105, 106, 107], A20‐mediated increase of P‐AKT in our study remains constrained by physiological AKT levels, providing a clearer framework for assessing the role of AKT signaling in angiogenesis.

Conclusion

In summary, our study provides the first evidence that ocular A20 gene therapy prevents lesions of OIR and promotes reparative neovascularization in the ischemic retina. Mechanistically, A20 suppresses hypoxia‐driven glial inflammation while preserving these cells' neurotrophic and vasculoprotective functions, protects EC and neuronal cells from hypoxic death, and supports molecular cross‐talks to accelerate reparative vascularization while limiting pathologic neoangiogenesis. Loss‐of‐function studies confirm the critical role of physiologic A20 in modulating angiogenic responses and determining PR severity, supporting its therapeutic promise, especially in patients with inadequate A20 expression due to genetic variants or increased protein degradation, such as in diabetes [34, 108, 109]. Indeed, we noted a 30% reduction in TNF‐mediated A20 upregulation in HREC cultured under high D‐glucose, mimicking diabetes (Figure S4), consistent with reports of similarly reduced A20 in microglial cells exposed to high glucose [110], reinforcing its link to PDR severity.

The feasibility of eye‐directed A20 gene therapy for ischemic PR is supported by the availability and safety of adeno‐associated virus (AAV)‐based platforms, yielding sustained transgene expression across multiple cell types [49, 111, 112, 113, 114]. Although intravitreal AAV delivery may not reach the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) [115], we confirmed that A20 overexpression in a human RPE cell line was not only safe but also protective against inflammation and oxidative stress (Figure S5), hinting at even broader indications for A20 therapy in RPE‐centric ocular diseases [115, 116, 117, 118].
