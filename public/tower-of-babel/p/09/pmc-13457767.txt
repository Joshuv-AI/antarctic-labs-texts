Biometric Parameters and Ganglion Cell Measurements in Children of Patients with Primary Angle-closure Glaucoma
Authors: Aylin Huryol, Yasemin Un, Ece Turan Vural
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the morphometric eye characteristics of the children of patients with primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) aged 19–40 years, compared to healthy controls.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 44 eyes of 22 prepresbyopic children of patients with PACG and 44 eyes of 22 sex- and age-matched healthy subjects with no parental history of glaucoma. For both groups, biometric measurements for the anterior segment were performed using the Sirius topographer. These measurements are central corneal thickness (CCT), iridocorneal angle (ICA), anterior chamber volume (ACV), anterior chamber depth (ACD), axial length (AL), horizontal anterior chamber diameter (HACD), macular ganglion cell layer (GCL), peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), and topographic disc measurements.ResultsThe ACD, ACV, ICA, and HACD values of the study group were significantly lower than the control group (p < 0.05 for all). The study group had significantly lower AL values (22.99 ± 0.17 mm) than the control group (23.64 ± 0.17 mm) (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were found between the study and control groups regarding the CCT values, the global mean of macular GCL, and peripapillary RNFL measurements, or optic disc topographic measurements (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe results showed no statistical differences in GCL, RNFL, and disc topographic measurements between the children of patients with PACG and sex- and age-matched healthy subjects without a positive family history. However, AL, ICA, ACD, ACV, and HACD were significantly lower in the prepresbyopic children of patients with PACG.Keywords: Axial length, Children, Ganglion cell layer thickness, Primary angle-closure glaucoma, Sirius topographer, Swept-source optical coherence tomography

Introduction

Glaucoma is one of the most important causes of irreversible vision loss.1,2 Current estimates predict that around 111.8 million individuals worldwide could be living with glaucoma by 2040.1 Among the different types of glaucoma, primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) accounts for the second most common glaucoma type across the world, and the number of patients affected by PACG is estimated to exceed 30 million by 2040.3–6 PACG more frequently leads to severe visual loss than primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG).6 Eyes with PACG typically exhibit the following ocular biometric findings: shorter axial length (AL), narrower anterior chamber depth (ACD), more anteriorly positioned and thicker lens, and hypermetropic refractive error (RE).7–10 Female gender, Asian ethnicity, and family history are among the risk factors for developing PACG.2,4,11 The developing primary angle-closure (PAC) has been found to be 13.6 times higher in affected patients' siblings.12 The inheritance pattern of PACG is thought to be polygenic.9,13,14 Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have led to the determination of several important loci and genes that may be related to the etiopathogenesis of PACG.3,15 Among the highly heritable traits determined for PACG are ACD,9,16 anterior chamber angle (ACA),17 iris thickness,18 and anterior chamber width,19 with ACD having the highest heritability.14 The specific cellular and molecular mechanisms of these identified loci/genes in PACG development remain unclear and require further investigation. Until these mechanisms are fully understood, precise clinical phenotyping remains critical, and the detection of a narrow iridotrabecular angle continues to be a key diagnostic feature of PACG. Since PACG is mostly diagnosed in individuals aged over 50s or 60s2 and most of the anterior segment parameters, like ACA and ACD, undergo alterations with the aging process,20 lens sclerosis, and the thickening and changing position of the lens, may influence the results of studies evaluating these patient populations.

Due to the high heritability of PACG,21 we consider that the identification of biometric differences in the prepresbyopic children of individuals with PACG may have value. Therefore, we conducted this study to evaluate the biometric, ganglion cell layer (GCL), retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), and topographic optic disc measurements in the healthy, prepresbyopic children of patients with PACG in comparison with sex- and age-matched controls to determine whether there were any changes that predisposed eyes to PAC.

Methods

Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Haydarpaşa Numune Training and Research Hospital (Approval No. HNEAH-KAEK 2023/207/4328, dated 20 November 2023-20 December 2024). All procedures complied with the ethical standards established by the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all subjects or their legal guardians after full disclosure of study procedures and potential risks.

This cross-sectional study included 44 eyes of 22 individuals who had one parent with PACG, and 44 eyes of 22 age- and sex-matched healthy controls without any parental glaucoma history. PACG diagnosis was established following the diagnostic criteria outlined in the European Glaucoma Society Guidelines.22 Patients with the diagnosis of PACG who presented to the outpatient glaucoma clinic of Haydarpasa Numune Training and Research Hospital with or without an acute angle-closure attack were provided information about the study and were asked whether they had any eligible children under 40 years. If multiple children were eligible, only one eligible child of each patient with PACG was selected randomly, and they were scheduled for an ophthalmological examination and scans. The control group was formed with age- and sex-matched healthy hospital staff with no history of parental ophthalmological disease, including any type of glaucoma, who volunteered to participate in the study.

All study procedures, including ophthalmological examinations and scans, were conducted by the same ophthalmologist (AH). All patients underwent a detailed ophthalmologic examination, including autorefractometry (model KR-8900; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), biomicroscopic anterior segment examination, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessment using the Snellen chart, dilated fundus examination, and intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement with the Goldmann applanation tonometer. Gonioscopic examination was omitted because of its irritating nature for young healthy subjects. In addition, all patients underwent central corneal thickness (CCT), keratometry, and anterior segment biometric measurements, including ACD, anterior chamber volume (ACV), horizontal anterior chamber diameter (HACD), and iridocorneal angle (ICA) measurements in nonmydriatic state using the Sirius topographer (SCHWIND eye-tech-solutions, Germany), AL measurements with optical biometry (Nidek AL-Scan, Nidek CO, Aichi, Japan), and macular GCL thickness and peripapillary RNFL measurements by swept-source optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Topcon 3D OCT, Tokyo, Japan). Measurements were collected from both eyes of the entire study population.

Study Subjects

The inclusion criteria for the study group were as follows: age between 19 and 40 years, the presence of a parental history of PACG with or without an acute attack history, a spherical RE less than 3 diopters (D), a cylindrical RE less than 2 D, a BCVA value of 20/20, an IOP of less than 21 mm Hg, and the normal appearance of the anterior and posterior segments during the biomicroscopic examination. The exclusion criteria for the study group were a BCVA value less than 20/20, the presence of any ocular disease, amblyopia, strabismus, a history of any ocular surgery, contact-lens use, any inflammatory eye pathology or trauma, any retinopathy, and optic neuropathy. We applied the same exclusion and inclusion criteria for the age- and sex-matched individuals of the control group, except for the parental history of PACG and any type of parental glaucoma or eye disease. Power analysis, incorporating findings from both the present investigation and prior research involving siblings of PACG patients, indicated that a minimum of 19 participants in each group would be necessary to achieve 95% statistical power. This calculation assumed a type I error rate of 5% and an anticipated effect size of 1.21 for ACD measurements.9

Sirius Measurements

Anterior segment evaluation with the combined Scheimpflug–Placido disk corneal topography device, Sirius topographer (SCHWIND eye-tech-solutions, Germany) system was performed in an undilated eye in dim light. Anterior segment parameters, including flat meridian (K1), steep meridian (K2), CCT, ICA, ACD, ACV, and HACD, in both eyes were recorded.

Optical Coherence Tomography

Swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) (DRI-OCT, Triton, Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) was employed for all scans. Scans with a signal strength index of 60 or less were excluded.

Macular GCL measurements

For macular GCL thickness measurements, the fovea-centered 7.0 × 7.0-mm 3D (V) protocol was used. The 3D macula scan had a 512 × 128 scan resolution.23 Using 3D macula scans, one of the grand means of GCL+ and GCL++ thickness measurements detected from the macular glaucoma report, in which GCL+ measurement represents the thickness of the GCL and inner plexiform layer, and GCL++ measurement represents the thickness including RNFL, GCL, and inner plexiform layer.

Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer and Disc Topographic Measurements

For peripapillary RNFL thickness measurements, the 6.0 × 6.0-mm 3D disc scan mode, which has a 512 × 128 resolution,23 was used. The RNFL was measured on a 3.4 mm diameter around the optic nerve head, and the RNFL thickness was obtained as a one grand mean. Topographic disc measurements, including the rim area, disc area, vertical, and horizontal cup-to-disc ratio, and cup volume, were automatically performed by Topcon 3D OCT through RNFL scans.

Outcome Measures

The study group was compared with the age- and sex-matched healthy control group in terms of IOP, AL, spherical and cylindrical REs, anterior segment biometric measurements, and RNFL, GCL, and topographic disc measurements.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were done using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics included frequency distributions (n, %), measures of central tendency (mean), and dispersion metrics (standard deviation, standard error). Between-group comparisons of quantitative variables employed linear mixed modeling with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests, and only one eye (the right eye when both eyes were available) from each participant was included in the analyses. The threshold for statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

The age range of the participants was 19–40 years, with a mean age of 32.1 ± 7.1 years. The gender distribution was equal, with each group containing 11 male and 11 female participants. The study and control groups were also matched in terms of age.

Discussion

In the current study, we found that the healthy children of patients with PACG aged 19–40 years had shorter AL and lower ACD, ICA, ACV, and HACD measurements than the sex- and age-matched healthy subjects without a parental history of PACG. In addition, we found that the macular global GCL+ and GCL++ measurements, and global RNFL measurements were not statistically different between the two groups. The CCT, refractive status of the eyes, and IOP measurements also did not differ statistically significantly between the two groups. Although we did not evaluate presbyopic individuals, the findings we obtained from prepresbyopic individuals can provide some clues concerning the pathogenic process of PACG. While the children of patients with PACG had smaller anterior-chamber biometric measurements and shorter AL, the optic disc topographic measurements and the peripapillary RNFL and macular GCL measurements did not differ statistically significantly when compared to the controls. We may argue that the effect of the lens factor on both the pupillary block mechanism and PAC. In prepresbyopia eyes, without sclerosed and thickened lenses, intermittent angle closure may not develop, despite the presence of a narrow anterior chamber.

The best-known risk factors for PACG are older age, female gender, narrow ACD, short AL of the eye, hypermetropia, steep corneal curvature, a small corneal diameter, and thick, anteriorly positioned lenses.2,24 Epidemiological research in European cohorts has demonstrated an age-dependent increase in PACG prevalence, from 0.02% (40–49 years) to 0.95% (>70 years).4 ACD and ACV gradually decrease throughout life, and this decrease contributes to angle narrowing and increases the probability of PAC among the elderly.2,7,25 Lens thickness (LT) is another factor that contributes to anterior chamber narrowing.26 PACG occurs infrequently in younger populations and typically presents concurrently with additional ocular pathologies or plateau iris syndrome.2 The frequency of PACG is three times higher in females than in males.27 Hypermetropic eyes have the highest chance of developing PACG, while this condition is rare among myopic individuals.2,10,25 In our study group, the mean age was 32.1 ± 7.1 years, and the distribution of male and female individuals was equal. The comparison of eye measurements between the study and control groups showed the only statistically significant difference in the AL value, which was significantly lower in the former (p< 0.001). Since we included only the healthy and young individuals of the children of individuals with PACG, we did not detect a steeper cornea or higher IOP in this group, and there was also no significant difference in RE values. PACG is very rare in young individuals unless there are ocular abnormalities such as nanophthalmos.

We made our anterior chamber measurements using the Sirius topographer. Many studies are using the Pentacam system, which is also equipped with a Scheimpflug camera. Using the Pentacam, several researchers have examined different parameters of the anterior segment of the eye in a short time and with good reliability.28,29 Nasser et al. compared the Sirius and Pentacam findings and reported comparable results, but they noted that Sirius probably provided more accurate findings than Pentacam in the detailed assessment of the anterior segment.30 The authors also stated that all parameters measured with Sirius had high repeatability and concluded clinically significant differences between the measurements with Sirius and Pentacam, thus recommending that these devices not be used interchangeably.

In our study group, the mean CCT was 553 ± 8 µm, the mean ACD was 2.638 ± 0.069 mm, the mean ACV was 134.7 ± 5.7 mm3, the mean ICA was 36.47 ± 1.39°, and the mean HACD was 11.87 ± 0.16 mm, with all these values, except CCT, being statistically significantly lower in comparison to the control group. The highest level of significant difference was detected for ACD.

Nongpiur et al.8 compared biometric parameters, involving ACD, LT, AL, and lens vault (LV), between healthy control groups and PAC, and they used anterior-segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) for anterior chamber measurements. They reported that the mean ages of the PAC and healthy control groups were 65.3 ± 9.1 years and 54.2 ± 7.9 years, respectively, indicating an older patient population than our sample. The authors also found the mean ACD, LT, LV, and AL values to be 2.66 ± 0.37 mm, 4.20 ± 0.92 mm, 901 ± 265 µm, and 22.86 ± 0.93 mm, respectively, in the PAC group, and 2.95 ± 0.37 mm, 3.90 ± 0.73 mm, 316 ± 272 µm, and 23.93 ± 1.37 mm, respectively, in the control group. In the same study, the highest area under the curve value for the differentiation of eyes with PAC for screening belonged to LV (0.94 mm), followed by ACD (0.75 mm) and AL (0.74 mm).

In a study evaluating the siblings of individuals with PACG,11 among 95 evaluated siblings from 47 affected families, 55 participants (57.9%) exhibited angle-closure spectrum findings, with 14 (14.7%) cases already having PACG. The mean age of the affected siblings was 60 ± 10 years. In a study reporting the frequency of family history of glaucoma, 43.8% of patients with PACG had a positive family history. The percentage of positive family history was slightly higher in the PACG group than in the POAG group (p > 0.05).31 The mean age was 52.1 ± 13.4 years for the patients with a positive family history and 59.26 ± 11.4 years for those without a family history (p < 0.0001).

Ciociola et al.16 assessed the heritability of PACG anatomic traits and factors predicting PAC in South Indian siblings using AS-OCT and reported that the lens, iris, and anterior chamber parameters were heritable, while the angle parameters were not. The authors also suggested that LV and LT might have noteworthy roles in the development of PAC. They concluded that older siblings and those with a narrower ACD might need more precise disease monitoring. They also highlighted the importance of age and ACD in disease risk, recommending the careful monitoring of old individuals with a narrow ACD in the presence of a positive family history.

Most of the existing studies on the heritability of PACG in the literature were conducted with the siblings of index PACG cases. Our study is different from most of these studies because we only evaluated the children of patients with a diagnosis of PACG. One more difference in our study is that we only included individuals under 40 years old. Although we did not measure any lens parameters, our findings in prepresbyopic individuals may offer some explanations for the pathogenesis of PACG. As far as we are aware, this is the first study performing biometric, RNFL, and GCL measurements in the prepresbyopic children of individuals with PACG, and thus, it can offer unique information on the early candidates of PACG. Before the initiation of the disease process, a narrower ACD and smaller HACD, ACV, and AL values are probably among the factors predisposing an eye to PAC. Even if gonioscopy was not among our study procedures, ICA measurements with Sirius topography showed the presence of an open angle, and the degree of the angle was smaller in the study group.

Although we determined some biometric differences in the prepresbyopic children of individuals with PACG, we did not detect any GCL or RNFL loss in this group. This is an important finding supporting the idea that ganglion cell loss and glaucomatous optic neuropathy occur secondary to changes in the anterior segment, resulting in a rise in IOP. In a recent study, Gün et al.32 evaluated the adult, healthy children of patients with pseudoexfoliative glaucoma (PEXG) and found some RNFL and GCL losses in their children, of the PEXG, which may indicate that other pathogenic factors, such as decreased vascular supply or compensatory mechanisms for ganglion cells, may be more important than anterior segment pathologies in PEXG. The mean age reported in that study was similar to that of our sample. However, while Gün et al. showed early ganglion cell complex (GCC) and RNFL changes in the children of individuals with PEXG, we did not detect any changes in the children of those with PACG. Various studies have examined RNFL and GCC changes in subjects with a positive family history of POAG.33,34 While some found no significant difference in the macular GCC or peripapillary RNFL measurements between healthy controls and the young relatives of patients with POAG,33 others reported a thinner RNFL and GCC in the adult children of those with POAG.34 Karti et al.35 also reported that subjects with a positive family history of POAG had a thinner RNFL. As far as we are aware, our study is the first in the literature to analyze GCC and RNFL changes in the children of individuals with PACG.

In the current study, we did not detect any significant differences in topographic disc parameters. As reported by previous studies, disc size and rim area have correlations with optic disc configuration rather than gender, age, or RE.36,37 Since our study and control groups included healthy eyes without glaucomatous changes, our findings are not surprising. However, disc size may show differences in the sustainability of nerve fibers as the IOP rises after PAC.

Our study has some limitations, for example, its cross-sectional design and limited sample size. However, the powerful side of our study is that we determined some biometrical differences in the prepresbyopic children of patients with PACG when compared to healthy controls. With aging, lens sclerosis and lens factors aggravate PAC and decrease ACD, but we have determined some biometric factors that might make the eye more susceptible to PAC. Another important limitation of the current study is that we did not include gonioscopic measurements, which provide indispensable information about the ICA. However, the repeatability, sensitivity, and specificity values of Scheimpflug measurements are high enough to obtain reliable results about the angle status. Lastly, we did not measure LT or LV. These measurements could provide very important and valuable data for explaining the role of the prepresbyopic eyes. However, since we only included prepresbyopic individuals, in which lens sclerosis is rarely seen, we consider that the identification of factors other than the lens factor has value.

Conclusion

Supporting previous studies, we determined that the prepresbyopic children of individuals with PACG have lower AL, ACD, ACV, and HACD values. The CCT, IOP, K1, K2, spherical and cylindrical RE, RNFL, and GCL measurements did not differ statistically significantly between the study group and the age- and sex-matched controls. The ACD and AL may be important phenotypical traits leading to the development of PACG.
