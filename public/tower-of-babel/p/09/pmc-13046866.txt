Investigating synergistic effects in Co-prolysis of groundnut shell and waste tyres on product distribution under different blend ratios
Authors: M. Anusuya, P. Suresh Kumar, D. Ommurugadhasan, Murugesan Palaniappan, J. Megala, Sivakumar Karthikeyan, I. Paul Theophilus Rajakumar, S. Dhivya, Kibrom Menasbo Hadish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amid the projected depletion of petroleum products and the severe environmental challenges, this study addresses the urgent need for sustainable alternatives to fossil fuels by investigating co-pyrolysis as a means to valorize two different wastes. Groundnut shell and scrap tyres were individually pyrolyzed at 350–600 °C, while co-pyrolysis was conducted at an optimal temperature of 500 °C using tyre blend ratios of 20%, 40%, 50%, 60%, and 80%. Product yields, synergistic effects and properties of bio-oil, char, and gas were systematically evaluated. Individual groundnut shell pyrolysis favored high CO2 and oxygenated liquids with moderate heating value, whereas tyre pyrolysis produced hydrogen- and hydrocarbon-rich gases, carbon-dense char, and high-calorific oil. Co-pyrolysis demonstrated clear synergistic interactions, particularly at 40% tyre blending, yielding improved liquid fuel with higher calorific value, reduced oxygen content, and intermediate density and viscosity compared to the individual feedstocks. Co-pyrolysis char exhibited enhanced fixed carbon and reduced sulphur relative to tyre char alone, while gas fractions showed increased H2 and CH4 with lower CO2 than biomass pyrolysis. These improvements arise from complementary radical interactions and synergistic behavior of volatiles between the feedstocks. The results demonstrate that co-pyrolysis effectively optimizes product quality and energy potential, providing a sustainable pathway for simultaneous waste valorization, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, and renewable biofuel production in a circular economy framework.

Introduction

The availability of conventional fossil fuel resources continues to decline due to rapid economic development and increased living standards. Petroleum and natural gas resources are expected to be depleted by 20421, leaving coal as the primary remaining fossil fuel, with reserves projected to last until at least 21122. Due to this impact, researchers working on the energy sector are focusing on developing alternative energy solutions3. The potential of biomass and bioenergy to improve energy security, advance sustainability, and spur economic growth makes them significant. Biomass, a renewable energy source, can lessen the adverse effects of climate change and lessen dependency on fossil fuels4. Biomass contributes approximately 10% to the global primary energy supply, positioning it as a crucial alternative to fossil fuels5. Promoting bioenergy also aids in reducing greenhouse gas emissions by utilizing waste materials, thus promoting a circular economy6. The integration of biomass into energy portfolios supports energy diversification, enhancing resilience against energy crises7.

Various biomass conversion techniques, such as thermochemical methods and biochemical processes play a crucial role in the production of biofuels. These methods efficiently convert a wide range of feedstocks into valuable biofuels and chemicals8. Compared to biochemical conversion processes, converting biomass via thermochemical processes has higher conversion efficiency and faster reaction rates. In addition, these methods can produce useful byproducts and emit fewer greenhouse gases than fossil fuels. Pyrolysis is regarded as one of the most effective thermal conversion techniques for transforming biomass into a variety of products, including liquid bio-oil, solid char, and non-condensable gases. During pyrolysis, organic material is converted into useful products which can be utilized for fuel, chemicals, or soil amendment. All the output streams of the pyrolysis process have practical applications and very little residual solid waste is generated. Therefore, this process is often considered a near zero-waste environmentally friendly process, although trace impurities and inert ash may still remain depending on the feedstock9. Furthermore, under a medium temperature, the method has garnered increased attention since it may produce oil yields of up to 75% wt%10. The researchers are presently striving to find the optimum method for the production of high-grade oil and to examine further distinctive kinds of biomass that may be utilized as feedstock for the process11. However, the oil produced by pyrolyzing biomass contains significant amounts of oxygen, which can cause a number of problems, such as corrosion and lower heating value. Recent research findings indicate that oxygen removal techniques remain costly, often exceeding the value of the oil itself12. To reduce costs and enhance the quality of the produced oil, the co-pyrolysis technique is recommended as a competitive alternative to fossil-based liquid fuels.

Tyre pyrolysis is generally considered more important or impactful than the pyrolysis of plant-based biomass for several practical, environmental, and economic reasons. Waste tyres are a major environmental hazard; they are non-biodegradable, accumulate in landfills and cause fire risks. Unlike plant-based biomass, scrap tyres persist for decades, so converting them through pyrolysis directly addresses a serious solid-waste problem. The handling and disposal of discarded tyres provide serious environmental problems globally, as there are over 1.5 billion tyres produced each year. Due to their extraordinary durability and flammability, scrap tyres have a high chance of igniting a fire and causing a catastrophic event that would unleash large amounts of smoke, hydrocarbons, and hazardous metals, as well as contaminate soil and groundwater13. Pyrolysis of scrap tyres at medium to higher temperatures yield oil, char, and gaseous products, which can be utilized as a chemical substance for different industries and as medium- to high-grade fuel for heating applications. Tyre pyrolysis gases are usually made up of hydrogen and C1–C4 hydrocarbons, which have higher energy content and enough energy to serve as a combustible medium. The char can be utilized as a solid fuel, used as carbon black, or further processed into activated carbon.

Individual pyrolysis involves the thermal decomposition of a single feedstock, where the product yields and characteristics depend solely on the intrinsic properties of the feedstock. In contrast, co-pyrolysis refers the use of two or more different feedstocks under identical operating conditions. In co-pyrolysis, synergistic interactions arise due to complementary chemical characteristics of the materials, including hydrogen transfer from plastics, catalytic effects of biomass ash, and improved heat and mass transfer. These interactions lead to higher yields and improved quality of liquid and gaseous biofuels compared to individual pyrolysis. Co-pyrolyzing biomass with used tyres improves the thermal degradation process, leading to better bio-oil qualities and higher heating values. This synergistic relationship between tyres and biomass expedites the production of high-grade fuels14. The synergistic interaction is referring to the non-additive interactions between the two feedstocks that result in product yields (oil, char, and gas) and qualities differing from what would be expected based on the weighted average of their individual pyrolysis. These effects also extend to improved product qualities, such as higher calorific value, reduced oxygen content, intermediate viscosity in bio-oil, enhanced fixed carbon, and reduced sulphur in char, and increased H2/CH4 with lower CO2 in gaseous components. The co-pyrolysis of waste paper and tyre shows that at 400 °C, volatile compounds primarily generate more gaseous products, whereas at 600 °C, the presence of heavier organics results in higher oil yields. This temperature-dependent behavior significantly affects product distribution and char properties15. According to Rajput et al.16 co-pyrolysis of sawdust and scrap tyres enhances thermal degradation at lower temperatures, leading to higher liquid yields. An optimal hydrogen-to-carbon ratio of 0.42 for scrap tyre–sawdust blends at 500 °C was identified as favorable for maximizing valuable hydrocarbons while minimizing contaminants. Abnisa and Daud17 investigated the co-pyrolysis of palm shell and tyre waste at different blend ratios to evaluate its effect on liquid fuel yield and energy content. The authors recommended a 50% biomass and 50% scrap tyre blend as the optimal ratio for achieving enhanced synergistic effects in terms of oil yield and heating value. When palm shell and scrap tyres were pyrolyzed individually at 500 °C, liquid yields of 43.82 wt% and 48.12 wt% were obtained, with the corresponding oils exhibiting higher calorific values of 11.94 MJ/kg and 47.3 MJ/kg, respectively. However, under the same temperature conditions, co-pyrolysis of the palm shell–tyre mixture resulted in a significantly higher liquid yield of 46.22 wt%, which was higher than the aggregated oil yield predicted from the individual pyrolysis processes. The co-pyrolysis process also showed synergy on the improved calorific value of 38.01 MJ/kg. These findings highlight the synergistic effects on quantity and quality of the resulting bio-oil. Uçar and Karagöz18 conducted a study on the co-pyrolysis of waste tyre and pine nutshell at varying blends under controlled conditions (500 °C, heating rate of 5 °C/min, 60-minute residence time, and 50 mL/min inert gas flow). The resultant oil products exhibited a higher carbon content and lower oxygen content compared to the liquid obtained from the pine nutshell oil. Farooq et al.19 investigated the co-pyrolysis of waste tyre and wheat straw under various blend ratios (1/0, 4/1, 3/2, 2/3, and 0/1) to assess the influence of feedstock composition. The experiments were conducted at 500 °C for 30 min with a heating rate of 20 °C/min and an inert gas flow of 50 mL/min. The results demonstrated that the co-pyrolysis liquid products exhibited higher yields and improved quality compared to the pyrolysis of wheat straw alone. These findings also underscore the synergistic benefits of co-pyrolysis in product quality. Ahmed et al.20 studied the co-pyrolysis of scrap tyres and sugarcane bagasse at various blend ratios at 500 °C and observed that a 1:3 bagasse-to-tyre ratio yielded the highest oil production (49.7%), surpassing bagasse alone (42.1%). The co-pyrolysis oil also exhibited a higher calorific value (41 MJ/kg) and lower viscosity, indicating clear synergistic improvements in yield and fuel quality.

It was clear from the earlier research that pyrolyzing waste tyres along with other biomass materials offers a promising approach to addressing waste minimization, energy safety, and environmental issues. Notably, the co-pyrolysis technique, designed for liquid fuel production, holds potential for widespread application. Currently, limited research has focused specifically on the pyrolysis of waste tyre and biomass mixtures. Therefore, when applying different pyrolysis parameters to such combinations, it is important to explore the potential synergistic effects. This study aims to utilize co-pyrolysis technology to produce renewable fuels from a blend of groundnut shell and tyre waste. This study experimentally examines the synergistic co-pyrolysis characteristics of biomass and scrap tyres, focusing on how different blend ratios influence the yields and characteristics of liquid, char, and gaseous products. Further the results demonstrate that this approach effectively optimizes product quality and energy potential, providing a sustainable pathway for waste valorization.

Experimental

Feedstock

The pyrolysis tests were conducted using scrap tyres and groundnut shells. The groundnut shell is the hard, lignocellulosic outer husk remaining after removal of groundnut kernels. The groundnut shells used in this study were raw, untreated agricultural residues collected from a local marketplace in Tiruchirappalli, Tamil Nadu, India, where they are commonly generated as post-harvest waste. No chemical or biological pretreatment was applied to the shells prior to use, ensuring that the material represents a typical biomass residue available for thermochemical conversion. In order to prepare the feedstock for the pyrolysis process, the materials were dried in an open atmosphere during post-monsoon season for more than 7 days. During that period, the average temperatures recorded around 27 °C with moderate high relative humidity around 70–75%. The atmospheric pressure during drying process was 1 atm. Further, the moisture in the material was removed by oven drying at ± 105 °C for 2 h. To enhance the heat transfer characteristics, the material was pulverized and sieved to be less than 0.5 mm diameter. The scrap tyre rubber used in this study was obtained from a local waste tyre recycling facility and processed by milling and sieving to achieve a particle size of less than 2.0 mm. which is suitable for efficient heat penetration and volatile release during pyrolysis. The analyses were conducted in accordance with ASTM standard protocols, with moisture content determined following ASTM D3173, ash content according to ASTM D3174, volatile matter measured using ASTM D3175, and fixed carbon calculated by difference. Additionally, both the samples were analyzed for their moisture content, volatile matter, ash, and fixed carbon in accordance with ASTM standard reported in the corresponding tables.

Pyrolysis plant

The design of the lab-scale pyrolysis unit used in this investigation is depicted in Fig. 1. The system has already been used for the pyrolysis of agricultural-based biomass wastes, waste tyres, plastics, and sewage sludge. The fixed-bed unit was chosen to be effective in achieving the synergistic effects of co-pyrolysis21, and it was also simple in design, relatively inexpensive, and suitable for various types of feedstocks17. The reactor had an inner diameter of 50 mm and a length of 100 mm, and it was heated using an electric heater surrounded the reactor core at a controlled heating rate of 15 °C/min. The reactor was heated using a 2000 W electrical resistance heater, with the power input monitored through an ammeter and voltmeter. An autotransformer was employed to regulate and vary the rate of heat input. The temperature of the reactor was measured using K-type thermocouples located at two different points within the reactor. The reactor was designed to pyrolyze 100 g of feedstock for one run. The outer section of the reactor bed is connected to the condenser through a plastic hose, with a short stainless steel tube installed immediately at the reactor outlet. This metallic section serves to dissipate heat and acts as a thermal buffer before the pyrolysis vapors enter the plastic hose. Although the pyrolysis reactions occur at elevated temperatures within the reactor, the volatile products undergo rapid expansion and cooling upon leaving the heated zone. Consequently, the vapor temperature at the outlet is significantly reduced, ensuring that the plastic hose connecting the reactor to the condenser is not adversely affected. The condenser was used to condense the volatile evolved during the pyrolysis process. Adequate water at the temperature of 0 °C was supplied to the condenser. In order to avoid unwanted heat energy from the reactor, the unit was completely insulated using mineral wool and Chromel Alumel.

Pyrolysis process

The study involved both individual pyrolysis and co-pyrolysis processes under inert atmosphere in a controlled environment. Before heating, the reactor is purged with nitrogen gas for 5 min to remove residual air and oxygen from the reactor chamber. The reactor was further tightly sealed using high-temperature gaskets to prevent air ingress during operation. The system handled up to 75 g of raw materials for each experimental run. The experiments were conducted by changing the bed temperature from 450 °C to 650 °C, and the co-pyrolysis experiments were specifically carried out at a final temperature of 500 °C by loading 20%, 40%, 50%, 60%, and 80% scrap tyres under the same pyrolysis conditions. Each run was extended to a residence time of 60 min to ensure the complete progression. After each run, the reactor was cooled to room temperature to ensure safe handling and effective termination of the pyrolysis process. The condensable vapours were quenched and converted into liquid form. The non-condensable gases were collected into a sample bag for the analysis. After the system reached atmospheric temperature, the char product was removed from the reactor by opening the top flange. The collected char was weighed and stored in a collection bag. The liquid product was also weighed, and the yield of non-condensable gases was determined by subtracting the combined weights of the char and liquid from the initial feedstock weight. Each experiment was repeated at least three times under identical conditions to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results.

Analysis of synergistic effects

A positive synergy means the blends produced more targeted products than the weighted sum from the individual components; a negative synergy means fewer products than expected. Thermal behaviour of groundnut shells and scrap tyres was conducted using a TGA701 analyzer. For the analysis, approximately 2 mg of the samples were separately placed in an alumina crucible and heated from atmospheric temperature to 700 °C at a constant heating rate of 20 °C/min under a N2 atmosphere. The N2 gas was supplied for the analysis at the rate of 50 mL/min. The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve was integrated over time to determine the reaction heat, as described by22. The characterization of the produced pyrolysis oil, char, and gaseous components was conducted using different analytical methods, as reported in Table 1. The oil samples for FT-IR analysis were prepared for analysis using the potassium bromide (KBr) pellet method. The detailed chemical components present in the pyrolysis oil was analyzed using a GC-MS armed with a detector and a capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The GC oven was set to start at 40 °C, increase at a rate of 5 °C/min to 280 °C, and hold at that temperature for 20 min. The injection conditions were set at 250 °C with an injection volume of 1 µL and a split ratio of 50:1. Helium was employed as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Chromatographic peaks were recognized by matching them with standard compound spectra from the NIST library.

The pyrolytic gas composition was analyzed using gas chromatography (GC-2014, Shimadzu Corporation). After removing condensable vapors, the non-condensable gas was collected and injected into the GC system. A suitable column with a thermal conductivity detector was used to separate and quantify major gas components and light hydrocarbons. Identification and quantification were carried out by comparing retention times and peak areas with those of standard calibration gases, and the results were reported in volume percentage.

Results and discussion

Characterization of groundnut shell and scrap tyres

Table 2 presents the physicochemical properties, compositional analysis, and calorific value of the collected groundnut shell and scrap tyres. The proximate analysis of the groundnut shell showed that the material contains 7.9 wt% moisture, 8.1 wt% ash, and a higher volatile content of 67.3 wt%. The higher volatile matter of the material shows its suitability for the pyrolysis experiments. The calorific value of the biomass was identified as 20.1 MJ/kg which is more than the value identified by Hai et al.23 for groundnut shell. A higher calorific value of biomass has a positive influence on the process which promotes more efficient thermal decomposition and the characteristics of the resulting products. The elemental analysis indicated that the biomass consists of 47.32 wt% carbon, 7.84 wt% hydrogen, 1.214 wt% nitrogen, and about 43.7 wt% oxygen. The higher carbon content of the biomass underscores its suitability for valorization through conventional pyrolysis. The basic characteristics of the scrap tyres exhibited a higher volatile matter content (68.6 wt%) and higher fixed carbon (28.2 wt%) compared to groundnut shell. Likewise, the results of the ultimate analysis showed that scrap tyres contain a higher amount of 83.12 wt% carbon and a very low amount of oxygen (5.82 wt%) compared to groundnut shells. Additionally, tyre waste demonstrated a higher calorific value of 38.7 MJ/kg, compared to biomass. The higher energy content of scrap tyres can be attributed to their lower ash and oxygen levels24. The effective (H/C)eff​ is a key parameter for evaluating pyrolysis-derived liquid fuels. The pyrolysis oil obtained from biomass typically exhibits drawbacks such as higher oxygenated elements and low (H/C)eff. The groundnut shell used for this work has an exceptionally lower (H/C)eff of 0.53. Co-pyrolysis of low-grade groundnut shell with scrap tyres having a high effective H/C ratio helps overcome the inherent limitations of biomass pyrolysis oil, as scrap tyres act as hydrogen donors that enhance volatile formation and promote reaction efficiency25.

Thermal analysis of the feedstocks

Figures 2 and 3 display the TGA and DTG curves of the feedstocks. Within the decomposition temperature range, groundnut shell exhibited greater mass loss compared to scrap tyres. The TGA profile provides valuable insights into their thermal degradation behavior and helps assess their suitability for pyrolysis and other thermal conversion processes. Figure 3 shows residual weight vs. temperature for the feedstocks, which describes the rate of weight loss with respect to temperature. Groundnut shell decomposes earlier, whereas the scrap tyres decompose later and leave a higher char fraction due to fillers and carbon black26. For groundnut shell, the small weight loss up to ~ 150 °C represents moisture evaporation and light volatiles. The major weight loss between 200 and 400 °C is attributed to decomposition of hemicellulose (200–300 °C) and cellulose (300–400 °C)27. The broader weight loss around 250–600 °C represents the degradation of lignin28. Unlike cellulose and hemicellulose, lignin contains highly complex cross-linked structure and heterogeneous aromatic structure leading to slow and continuous decomposition rather than a sharp mass-loss event. For scrap tyres the initial weight loss below 200 °C is negligible due to the minimum water molecules. In this case, the primary degradation occurs in the temperature range of 350–500 °C, where polymer chain scission is the dominant reaction mechanism29. Thermal degradation begins at approximately 230 °C and proceeds in two stages, with the first stage occurring between 240 and 315 °C, mainly due to the volatilization of oils, plasticizers, and additives used in tyre manufacturing. The second stage, occurring between 320 °C and 522 °C, corresponds to the main polymer degradation phase, during which rubber constituents such as natural rubber, polybutadiene, and styrene–butadiene rubber undergo thermal decomposition30. Supporting this interpretation, Senneca et al.31 reported that the second degradation peak can be attributed not only to the decomposition of rubbers but also to the degradation of residual compounds that persist after the initial weight loss. This highlights the complexity of tyre pyrolysis, where additives decompose at lower temperatures, while the bulk elastomeric polymers and remaining carbonaceous structures degrade over a broader and higher temperature range32. Individual pyrolysis

Figure 4 represents the yield of pyrolysis products with respect to reactor temperature during pyrolysis of groundnut shell. The pyrolysis experiments reveal a clear dependence of product distribution (gas, oil, and char) on temperature. At the lower temperature of 350 °C, char is the dominant product, accounting for 45.8 wt%33, while oil yield remains at 28.2% and gas yield at 26 wt%. As the temperature increases to 400–450 °C, char yield decreases steadily to 39.8–35.6 wt%, accompanied by moderate increases in both oil (31.1–32.4 wt%) and gas yields (29.1–32 wt%). The maximum oil yield of 38.6% is achieved at 500 °C, suggesting this as the optimal condition for bio-oil production. Beyond this temperature, oil yield slightly decreases (37.3 wt% at 550 °C and 35 wt% at 600 °C), while gas yield continues to rise significantly, reaching 45.2 wt% at 600 °C. In parallel, char yield decreases sharply, reducing to only 19.8 wt% at 600 °C. This trend indicates that increasing pyrolysis temperature enhances gas formation at the expense of char, while oil yield increases up to a peak at 500 °C before declining34. According to Ramya et al.27, lower temperatures favor incomplete decomposition, resulting in higher char formation, whereas higher temperatures promote secondary decomposition of char residues, leading to increased gas production. Therefore, for groundnut shell, 500 °C is the most favorable temperature for maximizing liquid yield, while higher temperatures favor gas production over oil and char. The distribution of pyrolysis products from scrap tyres shown in Fig. 5 also exhibited a strong dependence on reaction temperature. At the lowest temperature of 350 °C, char yield was predominant (56.9 wt%), reflecting incomplete devolatilization and the retention of carbonaceous residue35. As temperature increased to 400–450 °C, char yield gradually decreased (47.1–43 wt%), while oil yield increased significantly, reaching 42.5 wt% at 450 °C. This behavior reflects intensified thermal cracking and increased volatilization of organic constituents27. The maximum oil yield (47.5 wt%) was observed at 500 °C, suggesting this temperature as optimal for bio-oil production from scrap tyres. At higher temperatures (550–600 °C), oil yield decreased slightly (46.9–41.2 wt%), while gas yield increased sharply from 24.3 wt% to 37.5 wt%, due to secondary cracking reactions that converted condensable vapors into permanent gases. Meanwhile, char yield further decreased to 21.3 wt% at 600 °C, confirming extensive decomposition of solid biomass. The results of this section highlight the trade-off between char, oil, and gas yields, with intermediate temperatures favoring maximum liquid production, while elevated temperatures shift the product distribution toward gaseous fractions.

Effect of additive ratio during co-pyrolysis process

The effect of scrap tyre addition on the product distribution during the co-pyrolysis was investigated, and the results are presented in Fig. 6. A clear shift in yield patterns was observed with increasing proportions of scrap tyre in the feed. The gas yield exhibited a consistent decline as the tyre fraction increased, decreasing from 35.2% at 20% tyre addition to 23.9% at 80% addition. This trend was well captured by the predictive model, which showed only marginal deviations (within 1–2%), indicating the reliability of the prediction for gaseous products. The reduction in gas yield with higher tyre content may be attributed to the lower volatile fraction and higher fixed carbon content of tyres compared to biomass, thereby suppressing secondary cracking reactions that normally enhance gas formation. These results are alone aligned with the findings of the authors Ephraim et al.36. In contrast, the oil yield demonstrated a gradual increase with higher tyre loading, ranging from 40.5% at 20% addition to 44.3% at 80% addition. The predictive model followed the same trend, with the maximum deviation occurring at 40% tyre addition, where the experimental yield (45.3%) exceeded the predicted value (42.16%). The higher oil yield can be attributed to the increased formation of condensable hydrocarbons from the rubber matrix of tyres, which preferentially promote liquid production over gaseous products37. The char yield exhibited an opposite trend, increasing from 24.3% at 20% tyre content to 31.8% at 80%, while the model accurately predicted char formation at lower tyre ratios but underestimated it at higher loadings, indicating limitations in representing tyre-derived solid residues. The increase in char with tyre composition is consistent with the higher fixed carbon and ash composition of tyres, which leads to greater solid residue formation during pyrolysis38. Overall, the predictive model demonstrated strong agreement with experimental observations for gas and oil yields, while showing slight underestimation for char yield at higher tyre fractions. These findings indicate that the co-pyrolysis behavior of groundnut shell and scrap tyre is strongly influenced by the tyre content, with higher proportions favoring liquid and char yields at the expense of gas production.

Synergistic evaluation

The synergistic interactions during co-pyrolysis were evaluated by comparing the experimental yields with the theoretical values estimated from individual component pyrolysis. The calculated synergistic effects are presented in Fig. 7. For gas yield, positive synergistic effects were observed at most blending ratios, with the highest enhancement of 0.85% at 50% tyre addition. This indicates that co-pyrolysis at balanced feed ratios promotes secondary cracking reactions, thereby slightly enhancing gaseous products. However, at 80% tyre addition, the effect turned negative (–0.7%), suggesting that excessive tyre content suppresses gas production due to the dominance of heavier hydrocarbon fragments from tyres that favor liquid and char formation39. Oil yield displayed the strongest synergistic response at 40% tyre addition, where a significant positive deviation of 3.14% was recorded. This enhancement is consistent with the findings of Rajput et al.16, indicating that interactions between volatile-rich biomass materials and hydrocarbon-rich tyre material promote the formation of condensable vapors. At other mixing ratios, however, the effect was negligible or even negative, particularly at 60% and 80% tyre additions, indicating that an optimal biomass-to-tyre ratio is necessary to maximize liquid yield. For char yield, negative synergistic effects were evident at lower tyre additions (20–50%), with the maximum reduction (–3.34%) occurring at 40%. This suggests that co-pyrolysis promoted the breakdown of solid residue, possibly due to the interaction of biomass-derived volatiles with the solid matrix40. Conversely, at higher tyre fractions (60% and 80%), positive synergistic effects were observed, with increases of 1.54% and 2.12%, respectively. The results reveal that co-pyrolysis of groundnut shell and scrap tyre exhibits strong synergistic behavior, with the most notable enhancement in oil yield at 40% tyre addition. The findings emphasize the importance of feedstock ratio optimization to balance gas, oil, and char production for targeted applications41.

Characterization of the pyrolysis oil

Physical analysis

The density of the co-pyrolysis oil lies between groundnut shell oil and scrap tyre pyrolysis oil. Compared to conventional diesel, co-pyrolysis oil is significantly denser, due to the presence of heavier oxygenated compounds. Interestingly, its density is lower than coffee shell oil and sewage sludge oil, suggesting partial dilution of heavy oxygenates by lighter tyre-derived hydrocarbons. Viscosity is a critical property for fuel handling and injection. The viscosity, flash point of co-pyrolysis oil and fire point are intermediate between groundnut shell oil and scrap tyre oils. Compared to diesel, the co-pyrolysis oil exhibits higher thermal stability, which improves storage safety. The moderate reduction in flash and fire point relative to biomass oils reflects the contribution of lighter hydrocarbons from tyres42. The acidity of pyrolysis oils is an indicator of corrosiveness and oxygenated content. Co-pyrolysis oil has a pH of 2.9, falling between groundnut shell oil and scrap tyre oil. The acidic nature of the oil requires upgrading or corrosion-resistant handling systems. Compared with coffee shell oil and bambara shell oils, the co-pyrolysis oil remains more acidic, suggesting biomass feedstock strongly controls acidity despite tyre addition43. The most significant improvement from co-pyrolysis is in energy content. The HHV of co-pyrolysis oil is 26.50 MJ/kg, considerably higher than groundnut shell-derived oil. The increase represents a 64% enhancement over groundnut shell oil. This confirms a strong synergistic effect where hydrogen-rich tyre volatiles upgrade oxygen-rich biomass vapors, leading to higher-calorific liquid fuels. The elemental composition presented in the table highlights the influence of feedstock type on the carbon and hydrogen content of the produced liquid oil. The oil derived from groundnut shell exhibits relatively lower carbon (47.2 wt%) and hydrogen (5.98 wt%) contents, which is characteristic of biomass-based oils due to their higher oxygenated compounds. In contrast, waste tyre–derived oil shows higher carbon (84.11 wt%) and hydrogen (10.03 wt%) contents, reflecting the hydrocarbon-rich and aromatic nature of tyre rubber. The co-pyrolysis oil demonstrates intermediate carbon content of 63.24 wt% and hydrogen content of 8.07 wt%, indicating a clear synergistic interaction between the biomass and tyre feedstocks. FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectrum of groundnut shell-derived pyrolysis oil (Fig. 8) provides insight into the major functional groups present in the liquid product. The analysis confirms that the oil is rich in oxygenated functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and ether linkages. This high oxygen content arises from the decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose into acids, aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols, as well as lignin-derived phenolics49. The broad and intense absorption band observed between 3400 and 3200 cm⁻¹ corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, which indicates the presence of alcohols and phenolic compounds, typical of lignocellulosic biomass decomposition products. This is consistent with the high oxygenated fraction derived from cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin in groundnut shells. The strong peaks in the region 2925–2850 cm−1 are attributed to C–H stretching of aliphatic –CH2 and –CH3 groups, confirming the presence of saturated hydrocarbons and long-chain aliphatic compounds50. The sharp band near 1740 cm−1 corresponds to C = O stretching vibrations, characteristic of carbonyl-containing compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, esters, and carboxylic acids. This strong signal suggests that oxygenated species form a substantial fraction of the biomass-derived pyrolysis oil. The absorption peak between 1600 and 1500 cm−1 is associated with C = C stretching in aromatic rings, reflecting the decomposition of lignin-derived aromatic structures. The bands at 1450–1375 cm−1 represent C–H bending vibrations of alkanes, while the prominent absorptions in the 1200–1000 cm−1 range correspond to C–O stretching vibrations, which further confirm the presence of alcohols, ethers, and phenolic compounds. Multiple peaks observed in the 900–650 cm−1 region correspond to aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending, supporting the presence of mono- and polysubstituted aromatic compounds.

The FT-IR spectrum of the pyrolysis oil derived from scrap tyres (Fig. 9) shows a complex pattern of absorption bands, reflecting the diverse mixture of hydrocarbons and functionalized compounds present in the oil. Broadband near 3400–3200 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations of alcohols or phenolic compounds. This indicates the presence of minor oxygenated species, likely formed from additives or oxidative side reactions during pyrolysis51. Strong peaks between 3000 and 2850 cm−1 are characteristic of C–H stretching vibrations in aliphatic hydrocarbons. The peaks around 2920 cm−1 and 2850 cm−1 correspond to asymmetric and symmetric –CH2 stretching, while signals near 2955 cm−1 can be attributed to –CH3 groups. This confirms that aliphatic hydrocarbons are a major component of tyre pyrolysis oil. The weak peaks between 3050 and 3010 cm−1 are attributable to aromatic C–H stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of aromatic compounds derived from styrene–butadiene decomposition. A sharp peak near 1740–1700 cm−1 assigned to C = O stretching in carbonyl groups. This indicates the existence of oxygenated compounds, though at lower intensity compared to biomass pyrolysis oils. The strong peaks in the range 1600–1450 cm−1 are characteristic of C = C stretching vibrations in aromatic rings. The bands around 1600 cm−1 are especially indicative of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which are abundant in tyre pyrolysis oil due to the breakdown of aromatic polymers and additives52. The multiple peaks between 1250 and 1000 cm−1 are associated with C–O stretching of ethers, phenols, and possibly sulfoxide groups in the oil. The peaks below 900–650 cm−1 correspond to aromatic C–H bending vibrations, confirming the existence of aromatic hydrocarbons in the oil.

The FT-IR spectra of the co-pyrolysis oil derived from groundnut shell and scrap tyres (Fig. 10) loaded at a 40 wt% ratio clearly displays both strong O–H and C = O absorptions and intense aliphatic/aromatic C–H and aromatic skeletal bands. This confirms that the co-pyrolysis oil contains a mixture of oxygenated biomass fragments (phenols, alcohols, carbonyls) and hydrocarbon-rich fractions (alkanes, substituted aromatics). Compared with typical groundnut-shell oil, the O–H (3400 cm−1) and C–O (1250–1000 cm−1) bands in the co-pyrolysis oil are present but reduced in relative intensity, while aromatic and aliphatic C–H bands (2950–2850 cm−1, 1600 cm−1, and < 900 cm−1) are comparatively stronger. This pattern suggests hydrogenation/hydrogen-transfer interactions between tyre volatiles and biomass oxygenates during co-pyrolysis53. At the same time, tyre-derived hydrogenated fragments stabilize biomass radicals, producing a higher proportion of condensable hydrocarbons and relatively fewer polar oxygenates. This observation aligns with the increase of calorific value for co-pyrolysis oil relative to biomass pyrolysis oil. C = C band at 1600 cm−1 and plane bending bands that appeared at 900 cm⁻¹ indicate a substantial aromatic fraction due to the existence of styrene–butadiene rubber and carbon-black additives in tyres. The C = O band between 1740 and 1700 cm−1 demonstrated the presence of ketones, aldehydes, and esters in the oil.

GC-MS analysis of the oil

The GC–MS analysis (Table 6) revealed that pyrolysis oils derived from groundnut shell, scrap tyre, and their co-pyrolysis process contained a wide variety of oxygenated compounds, hydrocarbons, aromatics, and heteroatom-containing species. The chemical distribution reflected the nature of the feedstocks. Groundnut shell pyrolysis oil was dominated by oxygenated compounds, including methanol, phenol, furfural, diethylethanolamine, 2-cyclopentenone, and various methoxy-phenols. These compounds are typical products of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin decomposition, and they are the reasons for the reduced calorific value54. Phenols and methoxy-phenols are valuable for the resin, adhesive, and pharmaceutical industries. Furfural is a platform chemical for bio-based solvents and polymers. Cyclopentenones and alcohols are potential intermediates in fine chemicals. Scrap tyre oil showed a higher proportion of aromatic hydrocarbons such as 1,4-dimethylbenzene, 2,6-dimethylnaphthalene, indene derivatives, limonene, azulene, pyrene, and alkylated naphthalenes. It also contained straight-chain alkanes/alkenes like tetradecane and 3-undecene. These compounds arise from the depolymerization of styrene–butadiene rubber and additives during pyrolysis55. Aromatic compounds such as benzene, toluene, xylene, and naphthalene are used as precursors for dyes, plastics, and solvents. Characterization of Char components

Table 8 shows various characteristics of the solid fractions obtained in the pyrolysis of groundnut shell, scrap tyre and their 60/40 mixture. The char produced from scrap tyres exhibited the highest carbon concentration (81.44 wt%), followed by co-pyrolysis char (71.39 wt%) and groundnut shell char (66.35 wt%). The elevated carbon content in scrap tyre char can be attributed to its petroleum-derived polymeric structure, which inherently possesses high carbon density. Co-pyrolysis resulted in intermediate carbon content, demonstrating a synergistic effect of biomass and waste tyres, leading to improved carbon retention compared to biomass char56. Hydrogen content was slightly higher in groundnut shell (2.76 wt%) than in scrap tyres (2.25 wt%), while co-pyrolysis produced 2.62 wt%. Nitrogen content was low in all chars (0.91–1.36 wt%), indicating reduced NOx emission potential during combustion. Scrap tyre char showed high sulphur levels due to additives in tyre formulations. On the other side the shell char contained negligible sulphur; the co-pyrolysis process reduced the sulphur in the char to 1.06 wt%, highlighting the advantage of blending biomass with tyres to mitigate sulphur emissions in energy applications. Generally the char obtained from biobased feedstock has higher oxygen content; likewise, the shell char had the highest oxygen content of 14.85 wt%, while scrap tyres exhibited the lowest amount. Correspondingly, the H/C and O/C ratios were lower for scrap tyre char (0.3292 and 0.1148), indicating a more stable and carbonaceous structure compared to biomass char. The co-pyrolysis chars displayed intermediate values, suggesting partial deoxygenation and aromatic stabilization. This indicates improved fuel quality and thermal stability compared to groundnut shell char. From the proximate analysis it can be noticed that the shell char retained the highest volatile matter of 70.6 wt%, whereas scrap tyre char had very low volatiles of 18.1 wt%. Co-pyrolysis produced 49.6 wt%, which suggests better ignition characteristics and enhanced reactivity compared to scrap tyre char. The tyre pyrolysis char showed the highest fixed carbon of 69.4 wt%, followed by co-pyrolysis char of 41.8 wt% and groundnut shell char of 23.5 wt%. The increased fixed carbon in scrap tyre char makes it suitable for carbon-rich applications such as metallurgical coke alternatives57. The tyre pyrolysis char contained a high ash content of 9.7 wt% due to inorganic fillers in tyres, whereas groundnut shell char had lower ash at 2.8 wt%. Co-pyrolysis reduced the ash concentration to 5.5 wt%, an improvement over scrap tyres, thereby lowering the burden of ash handling and disposal in combustion systems. Moisture content was low in all chars, which enhances storage stability and calorific performance. Scrap tyre char possessed the highest HHV of 29.01 MJ/kg, followed by co-pyrolysis char. This confirms that blending biomass with tyres enhances the energy density of the resulting char, making it more suitable for fuel applications. Characterization of gas fractions

The gas composition showed significant variations depending on the feedstock characteristics. Table 10 displays the component analysis of the pyrolytic gases. In the table, hydrogen (H₂) was found in the highest proportion in scrap tyre pyrolysis gas (23.6 vol%), followed by co-pyrolysis (14.1 vol%) and groundnut shell (7.4 vol%). The higher H2 yield from tyres is attributed to the breakdown of hydrocarbon polymers and additives, while co-pyrolysis enhanced hydrogen formation compared to biomass alone, demonstrating synergistic effects between tyre-derived radicals and biomass volatiles58. This is advantageous for energy applications since hydrogen-rich syngas is valuable for fuel cells and synthetic fuel production. Methane (CH4) was abundant in all gases, with scrap tyres producing the highest fraction (26.5 vol%), followed by groundnut shell (20.6 vol%) and co-pyrolysis (20.3 vol%). The high CH4 yield in tyre pyrolysis is due to the hydrocarbon-rich nature of the feedstock, while biomass pyrolysis generates lighter hydrocarbons. Since CH4 contributes strongly to the calorific value of syngas, tyre and co-pyrolysis gases are attractive for use as combustible fuels. In contrast, carbon dioxide (CO₂) dominated in groundnut shell pyrolysis gas (57.4 vol%), but was significantly lower in co-pyrolysis (39.4 vol%) and lowest in tyre pyrolysis (11.3 vol%). The high CO2 yield from biomass originates from its oxygenated structure (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin), which undergoes decarboxylation and decarbonylation. The low CO2 fraction in tyre pyrolysis reflects its carbon-rich, oxygen-poor structure. Co-pyrolysis reduced CO2 emissions relative to biomass alone, thereby improving syngas quality for energy recovery. Carbon monoxide (CO) was present in small proportions across all cases, being 5.1 vol% for groundnut shell, 3.7 vol% for co-pyrolysis, and 2 vol% for scrap tyres. CO mainly arises from the decomposition of oxygen-containing groups in biomass, which explains its higher presence in groundnut shell gases. Although CO contributes some heating value, it is undesirable in domestic applications due to its toxicity. Heavier hydrocarbons (CnHn) were produced in greater amounts by scrap tyres (19.3 vol%), compared to co-pyrolysis (11.9 vol%) and groundnut shells (7.6 vol%). These compounds include olefins and aromatics that are valuable in petrochemical industries but can increase tar formation in the syngas. Interestingly, co-pyrolysis reduced the fraction of heavier hydrocarbons compared to tyre pyrolysis, producing a cleaner gas stream while maintaining higher hydrocarbon levels than biomass alone. Overall, the gas from groundnut shell pyrolysis was rich in oxygenated compounds, especially CO2, resulting in a lower calorific value but making it suitable for direct combustion and bioenergy conversion. Scrap tyre pyrolysis gas, on the other hand, was hydrogen- and hydrocarbon-rich with minimal CO2, producing a high-calorific syngas useful in power generation and as an industrial fuel, though requiring treatment for tar and sulphur contaminants. The co-pyrolysis process produced a balanced gas composition, with improved H2 and CH4 yields, reduced CO2, and moderate heavier hydrocarbons. This synergy resulted in a cleaner and more energy-dense gas, making it highly suitable for industrial heating, power generation59.

Social benefit, limitations and future perspective of the study

This study carries several societal benefits by addressing two critical challenges simultaneously: waste management and sustainable energy production. The co-pyrolysis of groundnut shell, an agricultural residue, and scrap tyres, a problematic petroleum-derived waste, provides a viable pathway for resource recovery and circular economy practices. By valorizing agricultural residues, the process enhances rural income opportunities and promotes cleaner farming practices, while the safe disposal of waste tyres reduces the risks of landfilling, open burning, and associated environmental pollution. The production of cleaner bio-oil, hydrogen-rich syngas, and carbon-dense char through co-pyrolysis contributes to the transition toward renewable energy and reduced dependence on fossil fuels. This supports national and global goals of lowering greenhouse gas emissions and mitigating climate change. Furthermore, the reduction in sulphur and ash content in co-pyrolysis char compared to tyre char highlights its potential as a cleaner industrial fuel and soil amendment, promoting both energy security and sustainable agriculture. The reduced sulphur content in the char attributed to synergistic effects that enhance fixed carbon and mitigate sulphur transfer to the solid phase. However, the liquid oil from co-pyrolysis still retains significant sulphur originating from the scrap tyres, making the sulphur content prohibitively high for most catalytic upgrading steps such as hydrocracking and hydrotreating, as it reduces efficiency, and leads to SOx emissions. These issues can be addressed by the in-situ desulfurization during pyrolysis by adding sulphur-capturing metal oxide additives such as Fe2O3, CaO, or CuO to the reactor60. These promote reactive adsorption and convert sulphur to stable forms retained in char. Further the sulphur content in the oil can be minimized by the Post-pyrolysis upgrading processes such as oxidative desulfurization, hydrodesulfurization and biological desulfurization. At a broader level, the study demonstrates how combining waste streams from different sectors—agriculture and transportation—can result in improved fuel quality and environmental outcomes. Such integration promotes inter-sectoral collaboration, encourages innovation in waste-to-energy technologies, and enhances public awareness of sustainable waste utilization. Overall, the study not only contributes to scientific advancement but also aligns with societal priorities of environmental protection, energy sustainability, and economic resilience.

This work was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions using a fixed-bed pyrolysis unit with limited feedstock capacity, which may not fully represent the complexities of large-scale systems. The study primarily focused on product yields and basic physicochemical characterization; detailed analysis of contaminants such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), heavy metals, and sulphur species in the oil and char was not performed. Gas characterization was limited to major fractions without detailed calorific value or emission potential assessment. In addition, the economic viability, process scalability, and long-term environmental impacts of co-pyrolysis were not within the scope of this study. Addressing these limitations in future work will be essential to fully establish co-pyrolysis as a viable industrial technology for sustainable energy and waste management. Further research should focus on optimizing co-pyrolysis parameters such as feedstock blending ratio, heating rate, and reactor configuration to maximize synergy and fuel quality. Upgrading techniques like catalytic co-pyrolysis, hydrodeoxygenation, and fractional condensation can be explored to further improve oil stability and reduce acidity. Scaling up the process using pilot and industrial reactors would provide insight into its techno-economic feasibility. Additionally, lifecycle assessment and environmental impact analysis are needed to evaluate the long-term sustainability of large-scale deployment. Investigating the use of co-pyrolysis char for activated carbon production, soil enhancement, and electrode materials could expand its commercial utility. Future studies should also explore the integration of co-pyrolysis with other thermochemical or biochemical processes within a biorefinery framework for maximum resource utilization.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that co-pyrolysis of groundnut shell and scrap tyres represents a promising thermochemical strategy for real-time waste valorization and renewable biofuel production. The experimental results confirm clear synergistic interactions between the two feedstocks, particularly at a 40% tyre blending ratio conducted at 500 °C, where the liquid yield exhibited positive synergy of 3.14%, resulting in liquid characterized by significantly higher calorific value, substantially reduced oxygen content, and intermediate density and viscosity compared to pyrolysis products from individual feedstocks. These improvements were identified by from different degradation pathways such as hydrogen donation, hydrocarbon formation and deoxygenation reactions. The co-pyrolysis char displayed enhanced fixed carbon content and reduced sulphur levels relative to tyre-derived char particles, reflecting synergistic retention of sulphur. Although the gaseous product obtained from the process exhibits an improved combustible species, the presence of CO and hydrogen content are minimum which resist direct syngas utilization. Therefore, further upgrading such as steam reforming or dry reforming, is recommended to enhance hydrogen production and adjust the H2/CO ratio. These findings underscore the effectiveness of co-pyrolysis in mitigating key limitations of individual pyrolysis processes—namely, the high oxygen content of biomass-derived bio-oil, and the sulphur-related challenges in tyre pyrolysis. By leveraging abundant agricultural and industrial wastes, this approach aligns with principles of circular economy, contributing to enhanced energy security, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, and minimized environmental hazards associated with tyre disposal and open biomass burning. To advance toward practical implementation, subsequent research should prioritize pilot-scale demonstrations, techno-economic evaluations, life-cycle assessments, and integration of catalysts to enhance selectivity and scalability. Overall, the co-pyrolysis of groundnut shell and scrap tyres offers a sustainable, dual-benefit pathway that merits further exploration for broader adoption in waste-to-energy frameworks.
