Applying adaptive research methods to explore health literacy in young people with intellectual disability
Authors: Thom Nevill, Jessica Keeley, Zhenmei Yeap, Olivia Lindly, Alice Schippers, Rachel Collins, Katherine Langdon, Jaquie Mills, Aasta Abbott, Sandra Thompson, Jenny Downs, Rachel Skoss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYoung people with intellectual disability experience higher risks for chronic health conditions than their peers. Improving their health literacy could be a strengths-based approach for reducing these risks. However, there is limited research that explores the perspectives of young people with intellectual disability on their health literacy. Given the limited research centring on the voice of young people with intellectual disability, this study examined their health literacy from their own perspectives. We had two aims: (i) explore the health literacy skills and needs of young people with intellectual disability and (ii) apply an adaptive interviewing approach and describe insights about its effectiveness. The adaptive interviewing method included visual materials and communication partners as needed. Sixteen young people with intellectual disability aged between 12 and 22 years who communicated with words or nonverbally were interviewed online. Data was coded using directed content analysis following the framework of the Health Literacy Questionnaire. The findings showed variation in the participants’ health literacy skills regarding relationships with healthcare professionals, managing health, and understanding health information. The results suggest that the health literacy skills of young people with intellectual disability are diverse, with different support needs. Targeted health promotion strategies are needed to meet these diverse needs. The adaptive interviewing was successful in capturing young people’s perspectives. With further refinements, including use of in-person and repeat interviews, this approach can be further utilized to enhance the accessibility of research.

Background

Health promotion supports personal and social development through providing information, education for health, and enhancing life skills. By so doing, it increases the options available to people to exercise more control over their own health and over their environments and to make choices conducive to good health (World Health Organization 2016). Young people with intellectual disability are at increased risk of chronic physical, neurological, and behavioural conditions and have higher rates of healthcare utilization than those without intellectual disability (Bebbington et al. 2013, Liao et al. 2021). One potentially effective avenue for promoting the health of young people with intellectual disability is through enhancing their health literacy skills. Health literacy refers to the ability to access, understand, apply, and use health information to manage one’s health effectively (Sørensen and Brand 2014). Health literacy also includes a broader set of skills and relationships, including healthcare systems navigation, effective engagement with healthcare professionals, and accessing informal and formal support systems (Osborne et al. 2013, Young et al. 2017). Health literacy is a foundational element of current approaches to health promotion (Gugglberger 2019). High health literacy improves individuals’ awareness of health-related issues and enables their engagement in health promotion strategies (Vetter et al. 2023). People with intellectual disability have fewer opportunities to participate in health promotion programmes and activities (Roll 2018) due to structural and contextual barriers such as programme content or formats not being accessible, transportation challenges, and financial costs (St. John et al. 2018).

Health literacy and people living with intellectual disability

Research with adults in the general population suggests that low health literacy is associated with poor health-related outcomes in areas such as hospitalizations (Shahid et al. 2022), management of chronic conditions (Taylor et al. 2018), and use of preventive care (Joshi et al. 2014). Fewer studies have focused on health literacy in children and young people; however, there is evidence that lower child health literacy is associated with poorer health outcomes in the general population (Chari et al. 2014, Park et al. 2017). Higher levels of health literacy potentially increase opportunities for children to adopt healthy behaviours and make informed health choices (Otten et al. 2022). Health literacy interventions targeting young people may be particularly powerful, as fostering the development of healthy behaviours and health knowledge during childhood may enhance health trajectories and reduce future health risks (Velardo and Drummond 2017).

There is limited research on the health literacy of people with intellectual disability, and less that focuses specifically on children and young people (Geukes et al. 2019, Vetter et al. 2023). For example, Lindly et al. (2020) found only four studies in a systematic review that examined health literacy and health outcomes among children with intellectual and developmental disability. Some researchers have examined the health knowledge, a component of health literacy, in young adults with intellectual disability, finding significantly lower levels of knowledge about nutrition and physical activity in young adults with intellectual disability compared to those without (Jobling and Cuskelly 2006, Pownall et al. 2020, Zwack et al. 2022). However, health knowledge is only one element of health literacy. Given these gaps, further research is needed to explore the perspectives of young people with intellectual disability to develop a more comprehensive understanding of their health literacy skill set and, thus, opportunities for targeted health promotion for this group.

Involving people with intellectual disability in research

The scarcity of studies focused on the health literacy of young people with intellectual disability mirrors broader trends in health research, which has traditionally used parents as proxies for children’s experiences (Beighton et al. 2019, Oulton et al. 2018). Reviewing the methodological literature on involving people with profound and multiple disabilities in research, Gjermestad et al. (2023) argue that proxy accounts can be a fruitful way to get close to understanding the perspectives of people with intellectual disability. While proxies provide important information, they may not capture all children’s perspectives (Smith et al. 2025). Children with disability have a right to be involved in research and have their voices heard on matters impacting them (United Nations General Assembly 1989, Lundy 2007, Courchesne et al. 2022, Downs et al. 2024). Thus, it is pivotal that young people are included in research exploring their views and healthcare experiences. However, traditional research methods are often not designed with the communication and cognitive needs of people with intellectual disability in mind. This creates barriers to their participation in research, which can be reduced with adaptation of data collection methods and use of flexible, supportive approaches (McFarland et al. 2024).

Informed by human rights discourses, increasing emphasis is being placed on involving people with intellectual disability in research (McFarland et al. 2024). This shift has contributed to the development of research methods that meaningfully engage people with intellectual disability (Caldwell 2014). These include participatory photo-based methods such as photovoice and the provision of supports during interviews, such as assistive communication devices, communication partners, and adaptive interviewing techniques (McVilly and Dalton 2006, St. John et al. 2018).

Many of these methodological advances have primarily been developed and applied in research involving adults with intellectual disability, with a smaller body of literature exploring their use with young people (Lewis and Porter 2004, Rivero and Moriña 2025). Researchers have used tools such as talking mats and other visual stimuli (e.g. cue cards) to improve accessibility and cater for varied communication needs when collecting data (Lise et al. 2021, Borgström 2023). Arts-based approaches have also been used to explore young people’s perspectives (Gonzalez et al. 2025). Further exploration and utilization of adaptive and accessible methodologies to address the perspectives of children and young people is therefore needed to promote their participation as active contributors to discussions about their own health.

This study

In this qualitative study, we had two aims: (i) to examine the health literacy skills and needs of young people with intellectual disability from their perspectives and (ii) to apply adaptive research methods to explore the health literacy of young people with intellectual disability. Understanding young people’s perspectives is essential for identifying their health literacy support needs and developing effective health promotion strategies.

Methods

The University of Western Australia Human Research Ethics Committee provided ethical approval for this study (2023/ET000948). The research team consists of a parent of a child with intellectual disability, disability and health researchers, and clinicians.

Ethical approval

The study has been approved by the University of Western Australia Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC)—ET000948. All participants provided informed verbal consent that was recorded and transcribed during their interview.

Recruitment and sample

Convenience sampling was used. Participants were recruited from databases housed at The Kids Research Institute Australia, as previously described (Nevill et al. 2026). Parents (all mothers) were contacted by J.D. (PhD, a female professor with approximately 20 years of research experience in intellectual disability) or T.N. (PhD, a male research officer with 6 years of qualitative research experience including 2 years of qualitative research experience in intellectual disability) via phone or email and asked if their child would be interested in participating in an interview.

Young people with intellectual disability were eligible to participate if they were aged 12–25 years. This age range was selected because it was anticipated that adolescents and young adults could potentially play a more active role in managing their health (Lee et al. 2021). Recruitment strategies aimed to capture participants with a variety of primary diagnoses and communication abilities. The final sample size was determined by reaching thematic saturation when additional interviews did not produce novel insights related to the study aims.

Parents provided informed written consent for young people under the age of 18 years, and young people provided verbal assent to participate in the research. For participants older than 18 years, T.N. discussed with the mother to determine if their child could provide informed consent. Discussions with mothers focused on the young person’s ability to understand the consent information. It was determined that all participants over 18 could provide verbal informed consent.

Thirty-one young people were invited to participate. Fourteen mothers declined on behalf of their child, citing that their children were not interested or would not be able to participate due to communication or comprehension limitations, and one was recruited but withdrew before being interviewed. The final sample consisted of 16 young people. The young people ranged from 12 to 22 years of age (median = 16 years), more than half (n = 10, 62.5%) were female and two-thirds had a Down syndrome diagnosis (n = 10, 62.5%). Most parents described their child’s communication as speaking well verbally (n = 12, 75%; see Table 1).

Procedure

The methodology was developed, refined, and implemented iteratively. This involved four key steps: (i) piloting an initial interview schedule, (ii) scanning relevant literature to adjust our research methods, (iii) developing a tool to visualize the interview schedule, and (iv) conducting the interviews using adaptive interview strategies. Piloted the initial interview schedule

This study is part of a larger project that includes a simultaneous qualitative exploration of the health literacy of parents of young people with intellectual disability (Nevill et al. 2026). Similar to the parent interview schedule, the initial young person interview schedule (see Appendix A) was based on the nine domains of the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ) (Osborne et al. 2013) but was shorter and used plain language. The initial interview schedule received a readability score of 7.0 (Grade 7) according to the Health Literacy Sydney Health Literacy Lab (SHeLL) Editor (Ayre et al. 2023).

T.N. piloted the schedule with three participants: P1, a 16-year-old girl; P2, a 12-year-old boy; and P16, a 16-year-old boy. P1 was nonverbal and used a communication device, while P2 and P16 communicated verbally in short sentences. Participants were given the choice to be interviewed with their mother present, and all said yes.

During trial interviews, T.N. observed that the questions were challenging for participants, and responses were limited in their description. For instance, the open-ended questions were difficult for P1, who communicated using one to three-word statements through a communication device. The challenges experienced by P1 suggested that the initial interview schedule was not accessible for people with communication devices. P2 and P16 were able to respond to the questions; however, significant prompting was required from their parents and T.N. These issues indicated that the interview schedule and methods would be unlikely to yield adequate information and needed revision.

Scanning the literature for alternative research methods

We looked for salient literature on accessible qualitative research methods for people with intellectual disability to guide our next steps. Key methods described included extended rapport building to establish trust, providing accommodations to support participation, and the inclusion of a support person in interviews (Caldwell 2014, Corby et al. 2015). Research has described how support persons often act as communication partners, offering encouragement and facilitating participation through strategies such as prompts (Caldwell 2014, Hall et al. 2025). Our initial interview approach already incorporated two of these techniques—the presence of a parent supporter and use of accommodations (e.g. communication devices), to enhance accessibility. McFarland et al. (2024)'s approach described an adaptive interviewing approach they used with adults with intellectual disability, which emphasizes taking a flexible, person-centred approach to interviewing. McFarland et al. (2024) approach incorporated multiple methods to enhance participation: (i) responsive interviewing techniques (e.g. tailoring phrasing of questions and content to individuals’ needs), (ii) the inclusion and support of family carers, (iii) visual tools, and (iv) walking interviews.

While offering valuable insights into the application of adaptive research methods, McFarland’s et al’s approach was specifically developed for adults. Literature on interviewing children, including those with disability, also describes adaptive approaches and tailoring the processes to suit the individual child’s communication needs (Zartler 2014, McNeilly et al. 2021). This includes using visual cues, props, and photovoice to facilitate communication (Steultjens et al. 2023). Informed by the above literature, we adopted two adaptive methods in addition to including parent supporters: the use of a visual aid and responsive interviewing.

Development of materials: visual interview tool

We converted our initial interview schedule into a PowerPoint interview guide using simpler language and including pictures and visual prompts (see Supplementary File S1). The PowerPoint schedule was intended to be used as a form of aided language support during the interviews. We adopted this approach because previous research indicates that visual materials help listeners with communication impairments process information by reducing cognitive load and supporting their memory (Burshnic-Neal et al. 2022, Steultjens et al. 2023).

T.N. worked in collaboration with R.S. (PhD, female, senior researcher, lived experience as primary caregiver) and J.D. to adapt the interview schedule, and drafts were shared with the wider research team for feedback. In revising the initial interview schedule, we removed questions linked to four domains included in the HLQ: finding information, appraising information, having sufficient information to manage health, and navigating the healthcare system. Questions addressing these domains were omitted because participants found them particularly challenging to answer during the trial interviews. Furthermore, since most of the participants were under 18 years old, we felt that these domains were less applicable given parents’ role in healthcare for younger people; thus, we limited the interview to questions considered essential. We divided the remaining domains (social support for health, feeling understood and supported by healthcare professionals, engagement with healthcare professionals, and understanding health information) into single accessible questions (see Supplementary File S1).

The slides included a mix of graphics, photographs, and numbered images, intended to be engaging and depict children and young people of various ages. Some slides include ‘yes’ and ‘no’ buttons to help participants respond. The PowerPoint included two sections: (i) seeing the doctor and (ii) keeping healthy at home. The first section included questions about when the participant last visited a medical professional. Through focusing on a recent doctor’s visit, we aimed to make the questions more tangible and relatable. The second section included questions related to social support and healthy living strategies. The PowerPoint guide received a readability score of 6 (Grade 6) according to the Health Literacy SHeLL Editor, reduced from the score of 7 for the initial interview schedule.

The PowerPoint interview schedule was piloted with one young person who also piloted the first interview schedule (P16, a 16-year-old boy with intellectual disability who communicated verbally using short sentences). Minor revisions were made. For example, the slide ‘What other healthcare professionals do you see?’ was changed from one slide to multiple slides with pictures of different healthcare professionals for more specific prompting.

Interviewing process

Interviews were conducted on Microsoft Teams by T.N. (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) and were audio and video recorded using the record function on Microsoft Teams. Remote interviews were chosen because their flexibility and lack of travel requirements made them more convenient for families, while also enabling the inclusion of participants living regionally or interstate (Thunberg and Arnell 2022). Interviews ranged from 15 to 40 min in duration. The length of the interviews was influenced by how detailed participant responses were to the questions, the duration of participant processing time, and the number of prompts that needed to be provided by the interviewer. Participants had no prior relationship with T.N. and were informed of the study’s purpose and the researcher’s role.

T.N. emailed a copy of the PowerPoint with the participant information sheet and confirmed any accommodation requirements (e.g. mother’s presence during the interview, use of communication device) a week in advance of the interview. Two participants elected to complete the interview without their mother present. A further two participants elected to use a communication device to provide their answers.

T.N. began the interview by confirming the young person’s understanding and verbal agreement to participate. He then shared his screen and asked the clearly phrased questions included on the PowerPoint slides. T.N. utilized responsive interviewing techniques throughout, which included adapting the phrasing of questions, prompts, and depth of follow-up questions for each participant. These responsive interviewing techniques helped tailor the interview process to young people’s strengths, including more detailed prompts to some participants and referencing the visuals more to young people whose parents suggested that they preferred visual over oral information. Participants’ mothers helped facilitate their child’s involvement by acting as a communication partner, rephrasing questions or prompting responses, explaining unclear responses, and maintaining the active participation and contribution of their child. T.N. identified that saturation had occurred during the last three interviews, and after review and discussions with the research team, it was agreed that no further data collection was required. Recorded interviews were transcribed and checked by student interns and T.N.

Analysis

Content analysis was conducted on NVivo (QSR International), using deductive methods of analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005, Assarroudi et al. 2018). A formative categorization matrix was created along with working definitions of categories in each of the nine domains of the HLQ. T.N. coded three interview transcripts and discussed this with the research team. Following coding half of the interviews, it was noted that no data was coded under four of the domains included in the categorization matrix: (i) appraising information, (ii) navigating the healthcare system, (iii) having sufficient information to manage my health, and (iv) ability to find good health information, likely because these domains were not explored specifically in the revised interview schedule. These domains were subsequently removed from the categorization matrix; however, while coding subsequent interviews, T.N. considered whether any new data could be coded under the domains that were removed. T.N. coded the remaining transcripts and selected anchor quotations to illustrate each category. T.N. and R.S. reviewed, refined, and agreed upon the anchor quotations. Throughout this process, underlying assumptions were challenged and discussed with the broader team as needed. T.N. coded the remaining data using this matrix and tabulated working definitions and illustrative quotes. Data was first organized into the HLQ domains and then classified into four overarching groups, where common categories and opportunities for support and learning were evident.

Trustworthiness

Strategies were undertaken throughout to enhance credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Ahmed 2024). Figure 1 illustrates how these elements were addressed in the different study stages. Credibility pertains to the degree the findings can be considered accurately reflective of participants’ experiences (Ahmed 2024). Reflexivity was integrated throughout the research process, enhancing the credibility of our study. This involved recognizing personal biases and preconceptions. We engaged in methodological reflexivity by critically assessing the strengths and limitations of our decisions and clearly communicating the implications of this (Olmos-Vega et al. 2022). Changes to the interview method and study design made the interviews more accessible, ensuring that we more accurately captured their perspectives. Dependability refers to a study’s processes being traceable, logical, and clearly documented (Ahmed 2024). Dependability was enhanced by the detailed and transparent explanation of the steps involved in developing and refining the research methodology. Our transparent, step-by-step account enables others to understand how and why changes to our methodology were implemented. Transferability describes the extent others can extrapolate the findings to their contexts or situations (Ahmed 2024). The description of our recruitment strategy, study context, and participant information enhances the study’s transferability by helping other researchers determine whether the findings apply to similar populations or settings beyond our study’s context. Confirmability describes ensuring analysis is unbiased and grounded in participants’ experiences (Ahmed 2024). Strategies for confirmability included maintaining an audit trail by saving copies of the project in NVivo at each analysis stage. This systematic documentation ensured that our research process could be reviewed and tracked. The research team included a parent of a young person with intellectual disability, and coding was discussed in weekly meetings. This collaboration was beneficial in enhancing the study’s confirmability to increase the accuracy and meaningfulness of the interpretations and conclusions.

Results

Process and procedure

Outcome of applying revised interviewing approach

The revised interviewing methodology was successfully applied. The PowerPoint interview guide was received positively by young people and parents. T.N. referred to components in the initial interview schedule in two interviews, when more complex questions were considered suitable for the participant. Upon reinterviewing participants who had piloted the initial interview schedule, their understanding of and responses to questions were observed to be greater in detail. For example, P2 provided very few answers to questions in the first interview, but during the second, she used her communication device and nonsymbolic communication (e.g. gestures) to respond to each question by selecting options from the slides. In addition to the use of the visual interview schedule, these improvements may have also been linked to the young people having greater familiarity with the researcher. While the revised interviewing methodology was beneficial, potential improvements to the PowerPoint and the format of the interview were nevertheless identified, which are described in the discussion.

Understanding health information

Participants varied widely in their understanding of information, including the reason for visiting a healthcare provider and what was discussed during appointments. Some participants clearly outlined why they visited the doctor and what they needed to do to get better. In contrast, other participants did not know or understand the reason why they went to the doctor. Several participants struggled to recall information, including what was talked about during their last doctor’s appointment, and relied on parents for prompting. Barriers to understanding information were not widely discussed. Two participants said they found it difficult when medical terminology was used and when their providers spoke in long sentences. Some participants’ mothers also noted their children understood questions and explanations of medical problems when phrased simply but did not understand more complex information such as the names of medical conditions.

Relationships and engagement with healthcare professionals

Participants generally felt supported by healthcare professionals. Healthcare professionals made participants feel comfortable by being friendly, asking them questions about themselves, and engaging them in discussions during appointments. Participants described how healthcare professionals usually spoke directly to them, asked them questions, and told them what would happen during the visit. However, some noted that information about treatments was primarily directed to parents. Participants reported that they rarely or never asked questions to their healthcare professional during appointments and instead relied on their parents. Three participants described asking healthcare professionals questions and being actively involved in conversations about treatment options. Some participants described strategies that healthcare professionals adopted to support them, including administering injections at home and providing written, plain language instructions for taking medication.

Actively managing health

Participants explained different ways they stayed healthy, focusing on regular exercise and maintaining a healthy diet. Exercise, such as participating in sports, attending fitness classes, and going to the gym, was often incorporated into participants’ weekly routines by their parents. Participants had varying degrees of understanding about how healthy living practices positively impacted their health. Some described the actions they took to maintain their health, while others elaborated on the specific benefits of activities like exercise (e.g. fitness and building stronger muscles). Some participants acknowledged they did not enjoy healthy living strategies, such as eating healthy food, but generally complied.

While parents predominantly lead their child’s health management protocols, participants often played a role in the day-to-day implementation of healthy living strategies. Several participants took an active role in managing medication, including independently keeping track of medication use and administering medication (e.g. insulin and tablets). Strategies to promote mental health were described by just one participant (P5, teenage male), who explained that he participated in hobbies to keep himself busy and enhance his well-being.

Social support

Participants described primarily receiving support from their parents when they were unwell and less commonly from siblings and grandparents. Forms of support included being provided with medicine, cuddles, and special foods and drinks. Two participants over the age of 18 years said they primarily looked after themselves when sick by taking medication and resting but would ask parents for support with tasks such as booking health appointments if required.

Discussion

This study appears to be the first to use qualitative methods to explore the health literacy of teenagers and young people with intellectual disability. The results indicate that the health literacy skills and needs of young people are diverse. The application of an adaptive interview technique proved useful, and we offer reflections on how it could be utilized in future research.

Reflection on methods

The research methods used in this study offered an accessible approach to gathering the views of young people with intellectual disability. Insights gained from implementing this methodology can inform future research by guiding the further development of components of adaptive interviewing with young people with intellectual disability. The development and use of a versatile online, visual interview aid was one of the study’s central methodological contributions. Previous research examining the perspectives of people with intellectual disability predominantly applied visual methods in face-to-face settings using physical methods such as cue cards (Zartler 2014, Peck et al. 2023). The increased use of online interviews following the COVID-19 pandemic highlights the potential relevance of our approach for enabling remote interviews (Keen et al. 2022). The PowerPoint interview guide was essential in encouraging participant engagement by prompting responses and providing alternatives to verbal communication. The numbered photos and response buttons facilitated the involvement of the two participants using assistive communication devices by giving them the option to select their answers directly from the slides. Visual methods can be effective in engaging individuals who use nonverbal communication in interview studies, who are often excluded from research (Courchesne et al. 2022). Further research utilizing visual PowerPoint guides with young people with intellectual disability is required to better determine the utility of this approach.

Using a responsive interview approach was also critical because of the varied levels of cognitive and communicative impairment among the participants (Caldwell 2014, McFarland et al. 2024). Generally, asking direct and specific questions in plain language enabled successful dialogue. However, the phrasing of questions, the number of prompts provided, and follow-up questions were adapted for each participant. The changes to the interview schedule and adaptations meant it focused on participants’ strengths, providing them with the opportunity to engage in the interviews in ways that worked best for them. For example, questions from the initial interview schedule were used with two participants who demonstrated stronger communication skills which allowed for a deeper understanding of their viewpoints. To improve the accessibility of research for people with intellectual disability, future studies should utilize and develop other responsive and adaptive research methodologies. Third, aside from those who trialled the initial interview schedule, participants were only interviewed once. Other research has highlighted that multiple interviews can be beneficial because they increase young people’s familiarity with the researcher and the interview format leading to richer data (Gjermestad et al. 2023). Future research should consider conducting more than one interview with young people where possible.

The results also endorse the involvement of parents as supporters and offering accommodations as effective strategies to promote participation. These approaches have already been successfully used with adult populations and are now showing positive results with young people. Consistent with Caldwell’s (2014) description, giving young people the option to have a parent present acknowledged participants’ agency. It also ensured that we did not presume the young person felt comfortable being interviewed while unsupported or supported. We acknowledge that the parents’ presence during interviews has the potential to influence young people’s responses to questions and dominate the conversation. It is therefore critical to be clear from the outset with parents regarding their role as a communication partner rather than a proxy in the interviewing process and for the interviewer to be proactive in ensuring the voices of young people are centred. It was also pivotal to incorporate other supports such as communication aids to maximize meaningful participation in interviews (Mactavish et al. 2000).

While the research methods were effective, there were areas for improvement. First, all interviews were conducted online, and one participant was not in an optimal setting to be interviewed (i.e. in a car) and view the slides. While online interviews offer convenience, future research should consider conducting in-person interviews to help build greater rapport between the interviewer and the child. Second, the first segment of the visual interview guide focused on a recent visit to a healthcare professional. The purpose of this was to focus on a concrete example to facilitate responses; however, some participants had not seen a healthcare professional for some time or struggled to recall information. An alternative set of questions could have been developed to account for this or restrict recruitment to those who have attended a healthcare appointment recently.

Young people’s health literacy

Participants’ health literacy skills and needs varied widely. The results also showed a wide range in young people’s comprehension of health information. Some participants reported high levels of understanding of the information provided by healthcare professionals, while others struggled to process and understand information and relied on their parents. The importance of parental support highlights the relational aspects of health literacy and the need to account for the health literacy skills of those in young people’s support networks (Chinn 2014). Differences in participants’ health literacy could also be shaped by other factors including their social environment and age. While outside the scope of this study, future research with larger and more diverse samples could provide greater insights into the factors influencing young people’s health literacy.

Several participants suggested ways their understanding was supported—including healthcare professionals avoiding the use of jargon and providing plain language summaries—which aligns with existing literature on the experiences of adults with intellectual disability in healthcare systems (Howieson 2015). Alongside plain language summaries, visual aids like pictograms and decision tools may also be useful, as supported by existing literature (Bailey et al. 2011, Choi 2011) and the effective use of a visual aid to support communication about health in this study.

Participants valued healthcare professionals getting to know them and developing rapport. Mimmo et al. (2022) reported similar findings when examining nurses’ perceptions of what constitutes good experiences of care for inpatient children and young people with intellectual disability. While participants generally felt comfortable interacting with healthcare professionals, they rarely asked questions and relied on their parents to discuss more complex medical matters. This finding indicates that further support may be needed to develop confidence and skills to engage with healthcare professionals for young people with intellectual disability. It is also important for healthcare professionals to recognize that engaging young people in conversations about their health is a necessary precursor to increased involvement. This emphasizes the need to develop the health literacy skills of both young people and healthcare professionals (Wieczorek et al. 2017).

The participants primarily described strategies their parents implemented to manage their health, including maintaining a healthy diet and regular exercise. Such preventive health measures are essential, considering researchers have consistently reported that fewer young people with intellectual disability meet physical activity and nutrition recommendations in comparison to those without intellectual disability (Einarsson et al. 2015, Koritsas and Iacono 2016). While parents played a central role in overseeing health management, several participants described contributing to the daily implementation of these strategies and, in some cases, took a more independent role, such as managing when they take medication. This highlights the importance of adopting a strengths-based approach, recognizing the contribution and responsibility young people can take in managing their health. However, the degree to which participants in this study understood how these strategies benefited their health varied significantly.

Other studies report that young people with intellectual disability have low levels of knowledge about nutrition and physical activity (Pownall et al. 2020, Zwack et al. 2022). Health promotion programmes for people with intellectual disability have primarily focused on physical activity and nutrition and related interventions (Curtin et al. 2016, Dean et al. 2021, Vetter et al. 2023). Introducing an explicit focus on health literacy in future programmes could strengthen current health promotion efforts by equipping young people with intellectual disability with the skills to understand and act on health information. Greater education, at home, in schools, and from healthcare professionals, about the benefits of exercise and healthy food choices, may be needed (St Leger 2001). The development and sustainability of such initiatives will likely require dedicated investment from governments for research and programmes targeting health promotion for people with intellectual disability. It is also evident that it is important to move beyond individual behaviour change to support health promotion (Dean et al. 2021). This will require organizational and policy change within health systems including professional development for staff and policies explicitly aimed at improving the health outcomes of children with intellectual disability.

Limitations

This study had many strengths, including focusing on young people’s perspectives and employing inclusive research methods. However, there were limitations. First, the sample was relatively small, and there were a high percentage of females and young people with Down syndrome. Further research should include larger and more diverse samples recruiting individuals with more variable communication and adaptive behaviour skills, to obtain a broader understanding of the health literacy of young people with intellectual disability. Second, the HLQ was used to guide the interview questions and analysis. While mapping the interview schedule and results to a known framework is a strength (Ahmed 2024), the HLQ is designed to be used with adults and has not been adapted for people with intellectual disability. While developing our interview schedule, we omitted four domains because they were not considered translatable to the experiences of young people with intellectual disability, our target participants for this study. These domains could also be included in future visual interview aids exploring health literacy. Third, participants were recruited through parents, who assessed their child’s interest and capacity to participate in the study. Relying on parents’ assessments may lead to an underrepresentation of certain groups, such as nonverbal participants or those with negative healthcare experiences. Fourth, young people with intellectual disability were not involved in the development of the interview aid. Greater involvement of young people with intellectual disability in the creation of the interview materials may have better aligned the domains of HLQ with their lived experiences and enhanced the accessibility of our approach. Where possible, young people with intellectual disability should be involved in the co-creation of data collection tools in future research.

Conclusion

It is critically important to recognize the diversity of health literacy skills and support needs of young people with intellectual disability. Health promotion strategies may therefore need to be flexible so they can accommodate children’s varied needs and enable them to meaningfully engage in their own health and well-being. Taking an adaptive approach to research is vital to ensure that the perspectives of young people with intellectual disability are captured. This approach will also enable the development of effective interventions to improve health literacy knowledge and skills in this group.
