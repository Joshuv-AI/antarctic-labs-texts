Molecular mechanisms driving the success of grafting impacted by walnut rootstock
Authors: Xianchao Cen, Shengqun Chen, Lianwen Shen, Youliang Chen, Wen’E Zhang, Xuejun Pan, Na Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe foundation for ensuring walnuts of high yield and quality is excellent rootstock. Numerous studies have shown that the walnut rootstock has a major impact on the grafted varieties’ quality, yield, stress resistance, growth, and development. Elucidating the mechanism of grafting affinity in walnuts is crucial, as it paves the way for selecting premium rootstocks to achieve increased production and efficiency. In this study, a combined analysis of the transcriptome, miRNA, and metabolome was conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying graft compatibility between different walnut rootstocks (Juglans sigillata cv. ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’).ResultsA total of 44, 41, 50, and 28 differential miRNAs were identified between ‘Qianhe 7’ vs ‘Panzhou’ at 1st d, 11th d, 17th d, and 30th d after grafting, respectively. These miRNAs target genes associated with plant hormone signaling pathways, particularly those related to IAA, SA, and ABA, such as ARF, SAUR, and RAS1-like genes. The most dominant metabolites in both J. sigillata rootstocks (cv. ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’) at 1st d after grafting were salicylic acid (SA) and Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). The concentration of SA increased with the extension of time in ‘Qianhe 7’, while IAA decreased significantly on the 30th d. The IAA and abscisic acid (ABA) contents of ‘Qianhe 7’ were markedly higher than those observed in ‘Panzhou’ at 11th d and 30th d post-grafting, respectively. But the concentration of jasmonic acid (JA) in the ‘Panzhou’ was significantly higher than that observed in the ‘Qianhe 7’ at 17th d post-grafting.ConclusionsThese results indicated that SA, ABA, and IAA may play a pivotal role in the adaptability of grafted J. sigillata (‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’), which provides a foundation for understanding walnut graft affinity. A deeper exploration of the synergistic regulatory network between the identified miRNAs and plant hormones is warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms, thereby informing hormone-based breeding strategies for walnut rootstocks and facilitating the sustainable advancement of the walnut industry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12577-2.Keywords: Juglans sigillata, Graft compatibility, Heterogeneous rootstock, Self-rooted rootstock, Transcriptome, Metabolome

Background

Juglans sigillata is a member of the Juglans genus (namely walnut) of the Juglandaceae family, which is of significant economic importance and is widely cultivated in Southwest China. It is known for its richness in bearing oily fruits and nuts [1]. Globally, the walnut is one of the oldest plants distributed, ranking third in terms of nut production [2]. Walnut oil is classified as a strategic species and an essential food source for human nutrition due to its high protein and oil content in its seeds [3–5]. The embryos of walnuts are beneficial for the brain and intelligence, reducing blood lipid levels, oxidative stress levels, and the risk of cancer [6]. However, the walnut industry confronts numerous major challenges, notably the long juvenile phase of young trees.

Grafting is a widely utilized asexual propagation technique in agriculture, enabling the rapid multiplication of desired plant varieties. In this process, two plant segments are joined together: the shoot portion, termed the scion, and the root portion, termed the rootstock. Grafting can shorten the juvenile phase and affect the tree size and fruit quality [7]. In addition, grafting offers benefits such as improved plant vitality, extended harvest duration, higher yield, longer lifespan, and enhanced stress tolerance [8]. For this reason, grafting constitutes an essential means of addressing the issue of excellent germplasm and horticultural cultivation of J. sigillata [9, 10]. Excellent J. sigillata rootstock is the premise to guarantee its high quality and high yield [11]. One of the critical determinants of grafting success is rootstock-scion compatibility [12]. Compatibility is defined as the ability of the scion and rootstock to unite and subsequently maintain normal physiological functions. In contrast, graft incompatibility refers to poor survival after grafting, abnormal growth and development, or the occurrence of physiological disorders [13]. Early graft incompatibility is characterized by short-term survival of the scion or difficulty in establishment, leading to death within a limited period. Cytological assessments often focus on whether plasmodesmata form between the scion and rootstock cells [14]. Late graft incompatibility manifests as swelling or necrosis at the graft union, resulting in progressive growth decline over several years or even decades, eventually leading to poor scion growth or death [15].

Rootstock-scion interaction can not only cause changes in physiological and biochemical levels of the scion, such as changing the hormone and protein content, differential gene expression, and small RNA content, but also exchange the genetic material and small molecules between rootstock and scion, such as non-coding RNA (miRNA and siRNA), peptides, and proteins [16–19]. At present, the mechanism of rootstock-scion interaction has been studied not only in model plants (Arabidopsis, tobacco, tomato) and annual crops (pumpkin, watermelon) [20–22], but also in woody plants [23]. The mechanism of rootstock-scion interaction in fruit trees mainly focuses on the physiological and biochemical levels [24], hormone content level [25], metabolite accumulation [26], and differential gene expression [27]. The most remarkable is the exchange of genetic material between rootstock and scion [28]. Therefore, using grafting technology has the dual role of horticultural cultivation and scientific research. The miRNA is produced in plants mainly by the primary transcription product of a long stem-loop structure after a series of processing under the action of DCL enzyme, and it can regulate many biological pathways, play a crucial role in every stage of plant growth, and is important in the entire regulatory pathway of genes. In plants, it can be seen that miRNA is a principal regulatory factor, playing a significant role in the regulation of gene expression. The target gene of miRNA is a transcription factor that can encode regulatory proteins in several biological processes, including signal transduction, organ morphogenesis, growth and development, and the response to external environmental stress.

Rootstock-scion incompatibility is a common issue in walnut production, which often manifests several years after grafting, leading to premature tree death and reduced fruit yield and quality. It is one of the factors that restricts the healthy development of the walnut industry. Therefore, selection and application of walnut rootstocks with broad compatibility and superior traits have become a critical goal for the walnut industry, making the study of walnut rootstock-scion interactions an urgent scientific priority. Traditionally, researchers primarily focus on plant physiology, histology, cytology, and related aspects to investigate the compatibility of walnut grafted seedlings. With the development of molecular biology technology, the interpretation of the molecular mechanism of walnut grafting compatibility between rootstocks and scions will help identify more suitable rootstocks for high-quality production and enhance their role in economic forestry. Thus, in this study, a combined analysis of the transcriptome, miRNA, and metabolome was conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying graft compatibility between Juglans sigillata rootstocks (‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’), aiming to provide insights and resources for breeding superior rootstocks.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In this study, the grafting seedlings of J. sigillata (cv. ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’) on the 1st day (samples A and B), the 11th day (samples C and D), the 17th day (samples E and F), and the 30th day (samples G and H) after grafting were employed as experimental materials. ‘Panzhou’ was planted at Houzihe Village, Jichangping Town, Panzhou City, Guizhou province (104°38′37.64″ E, 25°56′36.63″ N). The elevation is 1600 m, and the average annual temperature is 13.5℃. The annual precipitation is 1301.7 mm. ‘Qianhe 7’ was planted at Caishen Village, Caishen Town, Hezhang County, Guizhou Province (104°37′37.15″ E, 27°12′44.39″ N). The elevation is 1700 m, and the average annual temperature is 13℃. The annual precipitation is 835 mm. The ‘Yunnan’ walnut cultivar variety was from Shishan Town, Wuding County, Yunnan Province (25°50′13.68” N, 102°37′93.11” E).

RNA extraction, small RNA library construction, and sequencing

The standard extraction method was used to extract RNA from walnut tissues or cells (graft joint region) using the (TIANGEN) kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions, and the RNA samples were then subjected to rigorous quality control using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer to precisely detect RNA integrity. The QIAseq® miRNA Library Kit from Qiagen in Germantown, MD, USA, was utilized to create the compact RNA collection. Initially, 100 nanograms of total RNA were used, with 3′ adapters being attached to the particular 3′OH group of microRNAs before ligating the 5′ adapters. As a result, the fragments ligated with the adapter were subjected to reverse transcription with Unique Molecular Index (UMI) assignment, followed by barcoding and amplification of the cDNA in a single PCR step. Afterwards, the cDNA library was measured using a Qubit fluorometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific in Waltham, MA, USA, and the distribution of fragment sizes was assessed using an Agilent 2200 Tapestation system. The Illumina NextSeq 550 High Output sequencing platform was used to perform sequencing for 75 cycles according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The identification of differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) was conducted using DESeq2 (based on a negative binomial distribution) for all pairwise comparisons between sample groups. DEmiRNAs with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significant and selected for target gene prediction. miRNA-target interactions were determined by taking the intersection of predictions from both miRanda and RNAhybrid.

Transcriptome and data analysis

In this study, transcriptome sequencing was performed on eight samples. The histological sequencing and analysis were done by Wekemo Bioincloud (Shenzhen, China), the transcriptome data were uploaded to the NCBI database ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/?term=PRJNA1162697) [29]. Differential expression genes (DEGs) were screened by |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 & padj < 0.05. Statistical enrichment of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways was analyzed using clusterProfiler (3.4.4) software. Visualization of gene expression levels was performed using TBtools (2.375).

LC-MS/MS analysis of secondary metabolites

(LC-MS/MS) experimental procedure: (1) The obtained raw data were converted into mzXML format (xcms input file format) by Proteowizard software (v3.08789). (2) Peak identification, peak filtration, and peak alignment were performed using the XCMS program package in R (v3.1.3). (3) A data matrix was obtained, including information such as mass to charge ratio (m/z), retention time, and peak area (intensity). Subsequently, precursors in positive and negative ion modes were obtained, and the data were exported to Excel for further analysis.

Secondary metabolites were analyzed using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry and a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. 5 mg of lyophilised sample was combined with 1 mL of 0.1 M HCl (used for analyzing glutathione-ascorbate cycle substances, vitamins, and methionine cycle substances) or 80% methanol (used for analyzing phenolic compounds), along with glass beads measuring 0.5 mm in diameter. The samples were mixed at 6800 rcf, spun at 16,000 rcf, and subsequently passed through Whatman Mini-UniPrep syringeless filters (0.45 μm) before being analyzed using liquid chromatography with mass detection. An Agilent 1200 Rapid Resolution LC system was utilized for the analysis of the samples. An Agilent Technologies 6460 triple-quadrupole MS detector with an Agilent Jet Stream was linked to the system (both from Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). Further elaboration on this system can be found in an earlier document (Cookson et al., 2014).

Statistical analysis

Targeted metabolome analysis utilized principal component analysis (PCA) to uncover the connections between samples using the identified metabolites. OPLS-DA was utilized to distinguish the metabolites with varying accumulation levels (DAMs) among the samples. In the analysis, the standards of log2 (fold change) being greater than 1 and variable importance in the project (VIP) being equal to or greater than 1 were utilized. The data analyses were conducted using the R environment.

Results

Growth status of scions in different graft combinations

This study utilized the high-quality J. sigillata ‘Qianhe 7’—widely cultivated—as the scion, with two distinct J. sigillata cultivars (‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’) serving as rootstocks in rootstock-scion compatibility trials. Post-grafting scion growth was systematically evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the homologous ‘Qianhe 7’/‘Qianhe 7’ graft combination developed robust shoots from the grafted buds, whereas the ‘Panzhou’/‘Qianhe 7’ combination exhibited significantly impaired bud development.

Differentially expressed MiRNAs between two grafting combinations

miRNAs play a crucial role in controlling gene expression. The distinctive miRNA expression profiles observed between ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’ walnut varieties provided an opportunity to identify crucial miRNAs associated with grafting compatibility. To explore the changes in miRNA profiles between the ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’, a total of 24 walnut samples from different rootstocks, collected at various time points, were sequenced. Using the Illumina SE50 platform, we generated 10.11 Gb of small RNA sequencing data. After filtering, the clean reads from the sRNA libraries were mapped to the sRNA database. As illustrated in Fig. 2, a total of 44 differential miRNAs were identified in the A vs. B (‘Qianhe 7’-1st vs. ‘Panzhou’-1st ), comprising 22 up-regulated and 22 down-regulated miRNAs. Among them, miR-Chr14_41322 was the most up-regulated, while miR-Chr11_33643 was the most down-regulated. In addition, the miRNAs miR-Chr09_27748 and miR-Chr12_36983 target the genes OF30124 and OF10490, which encode an auxin-induced protein 22D-like and a protein RESPONSE TO ABA AND SALT 1-like, respectively. Similarly, the C vs. D (‘Qianhe 7’-11th vs ‘Panzhou’-11th ) revealed 41 differential miRNAs, with 26 up-regulated and 15 down-regulated miRNAs. In which miR-Chr09_28669 was the most up-regulated miRNA, while miR-Chr11_33643 was the most down-regulated miRNA. miR-Chr01_2797 targets OF03964 and OF19183, encoding an auxin-responsive protein IAA29-like and a salicylic acid-binding protein 2-like, respectively. Likewise, there were 50 differentially expressed miRNAs in E vs. F (‘Qianhe 7’-17th vs ‘Panzhou’-17th ), which include 23 up-regulated miRNAs and 27 down-regulated miRNAs. miR-Chr13_39298 was the most up-regulated miRNA, and miR-Chr08_26353 was the most down-regulated miRNA. Furthermore, miR-Chr05_17441 targets OF11283, encoding an auxin-responsive protein SAUR21-like. It is noteworthy that miR-Chr04_12703 also targets OF12915 (auxin-responsive protein IAA17-like). Besides, a total of 28 differential miRNAs were obtained in the G vs. H (Qianhe 7 30d vs Panzhou 30d) comparison, including 16 up-regulated miRNAs and 12 down-regulated miRNAs. miR-Chr04_14206 was the most up-regulated miRNA, and miR-Chr14_43172 was the most down-regulated miRNA. miR-Chr12_36983 was identified as a differentially expressed miRNA in the E vs. F comparison group. Analysis of MiRNA target genes based on transcriptomic data

To investigate the expression levels of target genes corresponding to differentially expressed miRNAs in walnut graft combinations, this study employed RNA sequencing to quantify their expression. Specific focus was placed on target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs. Among them, those that were also differentially expressed in the transcriptomic data were further selected for functional analysis using KEGG enrichment. The results revealed that on the 1st and 11th days after grafting, these target genes were significantly enriched in the plant hormone signal transduction pathway. By the 17th day after grafting, significant enrichment was observed in the Cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis pathway, while by the 30th day, enrichment shifted to the Drug metabolism-cytochrome P450 pathway (Fig. 4). These findings suggest a close relationship between graft compatibility in walnut and plant hormone signaling.

The expression levels of the aforementioned hormone-related genes were visualized (Fig. 5). These plant hormone-associated genes exhibited significant differential expression not only across different post-grafting time points but also between different graft combinations. For instance, the expression levels of genes OF06653, OFI8569, OF07626, OF28610, OF24065, OF14509, and OF24091 were differentially expressed between the two graft combinations at 30th after grafting, and they are involved in ETH, GA, and ABA signaling pathways. Additionally, OF08731, a gene encoding a transcription factor in the downstream ethylene signaling pathway, consistently showed higher expression in the incompatible graft combination across all four post-grafting time points.

Identification and functional characterization of MiRNA potential targets

Gene Ontology (GO) terms were used to classify the differentially expressed miRNA targets to better understand the biological processes related to grafting in walnuts (Fig. 6). In the comparison of A vs. B (‘Qianhe 7’-1st vs ‘Panzhou’-1st ), most GO terms assigned under the molecular function category were related to abscisic acid binding, monocarboxylic acid binding, and regulation of protein dephosphorylation. In the C vs. D (‘Qianhe 7’-11th vs ‘Panzhou’-11th ) comparison, the major GO terms were phytoalexin metabolic process, diterpene phytoalexin metabolic process, and nitrate transport. In the E vs. F (‘Qianhe 7’-17th vs ‘Panzhou’-17th ) comparison, the most abundant GO annotations were related to the regulation of the timing of plant organ formation, inorganic anion transmembrane transporter activity, and cellular response to nitrate. The G1/S transition of the mitotic cell cycle, epoxygenase P450 pathway, and cellular response to xenobiotic stimulus were the most abundant GO annotations in the G vs. H (Qianhe 7 30d vs Panzhou 30d).

Differentially accumulated metabolites between two grafting combinations

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to capture the characteristics of multidimensional omics data. As shown in Fig. 5, PC1 and PC2 explained 48.3% and 31.3% of the variation in the A vs. B, respectively. Similarly, in the C vs. D, the PC1 and PC2 explained 65.3% and 19.7% of the variation, respectively. For the E vs. F, the PC1 and PC2 accounted for 40.3% and 25% of the variation, respectively, while in the G vs. H, they explained 56% and 24% of the variation, respectively (Fig. 7). The closely clustered repeated samples suggested a strong agreement among the biological replicates, demonstrating the reproducibility and reliability of the experiment.

To examine the proportional composition of differentially accumulated metabolites across samples, we identified differential metabolites using the criteria of fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and VIP ≥ 1, and visualized their relative distributions by stacked bar charts (Fig. S1). The results showed that salicylic acid exhibited the highest proportion in both graft combinations at the 1st and 11th d after grafting. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) displayed the second-highest relative proportion. On the 17th d after grafting, IAA accounted for the greatest proportion in the ‘Qianhe 7’ rootstock combination, whereas salicylic acid was the most abundant in the ‘Panzhou’ rootstock combination. At the 30th d, the proportional distribution of these two hormones in both combinations was reversed compared to that observed on the 17th d. To investigate the dynamic changes of phytohormones following walnut grafting, we visualized the abundance of various plant hormones across samples using a heatmap (Fig. 8). Particular focus was given to the variation patterns of the most abundant hormones, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and salicylic acid (SA). The results revealed that in the ‘Qianhe 7’ rootstock combination, SA levels initially decreased and subsequently increased, while in the ‘Panzhou’ rootstock combination, they exhibited an opposite trend of initial increase followed by decrease. For IAA, a pattern of decrease-increase-decrease was observed in the ‘Qianhe 7’ combination, whereas the ‘Panzhou’ combination showed a biphasic pattern of decrease followed by increase. These distinct dynamic patterns of SA and IAA in different graft combinations suggest their potentially significant roles in graft compatibility in walnut.

Discussion

Grafting is extensively applied in crop production to improve crop quality, increase yield, optimize plant architecture, and enhance resistance to both abiotic and biotic stresses, thereby significantly boosting their economic value [30, 31]. Examples such as pear [32], peach [33], citrus [34], and grape [9] have shown that good rootstock significantly improves plant growth and the fruit maturation period. Compared to other stone fruits, walnut grafting requires stricter adherence to technical specifications in grafting operations as well as scion and rootstock management, demonstrating greater technical difficulty and demanding more intensive labor input [10, 35]. The success of grafting depends on the physiological compatibility between rootstock and scion, as well as the precise alignment of tissues at the graft union [36, 37]. Graft incompatibility is a complex physiological process influenced by the metabolic interactions between cultivar-rootstock combinations, growth conditions, the presence or absence of viruses, environmental factors, the nutritional status of trees, and various other stresses [38]. The success of walnut grafting is therefore inherently contingent upon these complex physiological interactions. The specific graft incompatibility between different Juglans species has not been reported. While many genes have been identified during plant grafting, the functional role of miRNAs in walnut grafting, particularly across different walnut species, remains largely unexplored [39, 40]. In our study, we established sRNA libraries at 1st, 11th, 17th, and 30th after grafting of two different varieties of walnuts (‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’). We obtained 10.11 Gb of transcriptome data from small RNA sequencing using the Illumina SE50 platform.

A total of 44, 41, 50, and 28 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified in the comparison groups of A vs. B, C vs. D, E vs. F, and G vs. H, respectively. miRNAs can regulate the expression of target genes during plant growth and development [41]. In grafted citrus and hickory, significant alterations in miRNA transcript levels have been observed [42]. Furthermore, miRNA-mediated responses to grafting may also lead to changes in DNA methylation within the scion [43]. A study using M9 rootstock grafted with Fuji apple scion revealed significant differential expression of modules such as Mdm-miR156–MdSPLs, Mdm-miR159–MdMYB, and Mdm-miR171–MdGRAS between rootstock and scion, suggesting their potential involvement in regulating flowering [44]. During rootstock-scion interaction in citrus, miR399 may also undergo long-distance transport, thereby influencing sugar and acid content in fruits [45]. In potato, miR172 and miR156 have been identified as mobile grafting signals that affect tuber formation [46, 47]. Collectively, these studies underscore the importance of miRNAs in regulating growth and developmental processes following plant grafting. In this study, we observed that many miRNAs were initially down-regulated during grafting, but their expression levels increased over time. Substantial differential expression of multiple miRNAs was observed in comparative groups between ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’ walnut cultivars. Notable examples include miR-Chr14_41322 and miR-Chr11_33643 (at 1st d), miR-Chr09_28669 and miR-Chr11_33643 (at 11th d), miR-Chr13_39298 and miR-Chr08_26353 (at 17th d), as well as miR-Chr04_14206 and miR-Chr14_43172 (at 30th d). These miRNAs target genes associated with plant hormone signaling pathways, particularly those related to IAA, SA, and ABA, such as ARF, SAUR, and RAS1-like genes. Collectively, these findings suggest a potential link between miRNA expression and graft affinity in walnut. The differentially expressed miRNAs identified in this study are likely to play important roles in regulating graft compatibility. Nevertheless, the specific roles and mechanisms of these miRNAs in regulating graft compatibility in walnut remain largely unexplored and merit further research.

Plant hormones are essential for tissue healing and grafting, establishing grafting as an effective strategy to mitigate plant stress [48]. Employing rootstocks with high environmental adaptability improves abiotic stress tolerance in grafted plants through rootstock–scion signaling [49]. For example, in cucumber grafted onto heat-tolerant luffa, root-derived ABA serves as a long-distance signal enhancing scion thermotolerance, while in watermelon, melatonin-induced MeJA and H₂O₂ accumulation facilitate rootstock–scion communication under cold stress [50]. Salicylic acid (SA), a hormone-like phenolic compound, is a key regulator of plant defense against biotic and abiotic stresses [51, 52]. Initially studied for its role in systemic disease resistance, SA is now known to also mediate responses to heat, cold, salinity, and drought [53, 54]. It enhances stress adaptation by activating complex signaling networks [55, 56]. Specifically, SA promotes salt tolerance via ABA biosynthesis, interacts with H₂S to alleviate heat damage in maize, and engages H₂S as a downstream signal in cold stress responses in cucumber [57, 58]. These results suggest that SA may play a crucial role in the early post-grafting adaptation of walnut plants, and the differences in its concentration and dynamic changes could be a key factor influencing the grafting healing capacity between the two genotypes. Analysis of miRNA target gene expression further underscores the importance of plant hormones in walnut grafting compatibility. However, the associated regulatory pathways require further experimental validation.

The compatibility between scion and rootstock involves multiple physiological processes, including callus formation and the re-establishment of functional vascular connectivity [59]. Successful grafting depends on a coordinated interplay of hormonal and biochemical traits. Among these, abscisic acid (ABA) plays a well-documented role in mediating plant responses to abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity [60, 61]. In grafted tomato plants, elevated ABA levels in scions have been linked to reduced plant stature, indicating its role in inducing dwarfism. Similarly, in dwarf apple rootstocks (Malus sp.), a high ABA-to-IAA ratio and elevated endogenous ABA content further support their growth-inhibitory function [62]. Despite these insights, the role of ABA in grafting success—particularly in survival and long-term compatibility—remains poorly understood. Most studies focus on early post-grafting responses in herbaceous systems to explain incompatibility [61], while research in woody plants remains limited due to longer evaluation cycles and experimental challenges [63]. Given that graft union formation is strongly influenced by hormonal activity, clarifying the function of ABA could provide critical insights into the physiological basis of graft incompatibility. In this study, we observed dynamic changes in ABA content in both ‘Qianhe 7’ and ‘Panzhou’ walnuts during the grafting process. By the 30th day after grafting, ABA levels were significantly higher in ‘Qianhe 7’ than in ‘Panzhou’, suggesting its important role in post-graft adaptation. Together with IAA and SA, ABA appears to function as a key regulator of graft affinity in walnut. Beyond hormonal changes, we also identified multiple differentially expressed miRNAs targeting phytohormone-related genes, indicating a potential co-regulatory network involving both hormones and miRNAs in grafting responses. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the precise interactions within this phytohormone–miRNA regulatory framework.

Conclusions

Multiple miRNAs and differentially accumulated metabolites were identified across grafting combinations and time courses. Notably, the target genes of these differentially expressed miRNAs were predicted to be predominantly associated with plant hormones. Phytohormone profiling indicated that SA and IAA were the predominant metabolites on the 1st d after grafting in both cultivars, with ‘Qianhe 7’ accumulating higher SA levels. By the 30th d, ABA content was significantly elevated in ‘Qianhe 7’. The grafting process in walnut is accompanied by significant changes in the levels of IAA, SA, and ABA. The identified genes and phytohormone profiles may reflect a “general allograft response.” Collectively, the findings of this study enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying graft compatibility in walnut and offer valuable candidate miRNAs for future investigations into compatible/incompatible grafting.
