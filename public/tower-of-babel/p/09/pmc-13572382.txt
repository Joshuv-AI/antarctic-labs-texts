Seawater salinity and oxygen isotopes along the U.S. East Coast from 24 °N to 44 °N
Authors: Alex A. Quizon, Lucas D. Gomes, Sierra V. Petersen, Justin VanDeVelde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxygen isotope measurements of seawater (δ18Ow) are an important tool in the study of climate, oceanography, and hydrology, both today and in the past. δ18Ow and salinity are controlled by similar hydrologic processes, but δ18Ow is more readily recorded in geologic materials than salinity and is thus more accessible for paleoceanographic reconstructions. Modern instrumental δ18Ow data exist, but these data can be sparse in certain regions and/or sporadically collected through time - some predating modern instrumentation and current standardization practices. In this dataset, we present 364 new paired salinity and δ18Ow measurements from seawater samples collected along the U.S. Eastern Seaboard, nearly tripling the existing data in this region and dramatically improving spatial coverage. By collecting δ18Ow at such a high spatial resolution, this dataset expands the utility of δ18Ow as a tracer for both modern oceanography and paleoceanography, provides valuable insights into nearshore hydrologic processes and basin-scale ocean dynamics, and offers a robust foundation for more integrated studies of ocean circulation and water-mass properties.

Background & Summary

The field of oceanography traces its origin back to roughly the 1800s. Historically, oceanography measurements were collected during individual scientific research cruises. Over time, oceanographic efforts expanded to international collaborative programs such as the International Ocean Discovery Program1 (IODP) and the Global Ocean Ship-based Hydrographic Investigations Program2 (GO-SHIP). Some routine measurements of ocean chemistry conducted by research cruises include seawater temperature, salinity, dissolved inorganic carbon, total alkalinity, and other parameters. Technological advancements such as Argo floats3 and satellite remote sensing techniques4 have further expanded data coverage, substantially increasing our understanding of important oceanographic processes.

One non-routine measurement that is useful for studies of both modern oceanography and paleoceanography is the oxygen isotope composition of seawater (δ18Ow). δ18Ow and salinity are generally governed by similar hydrologic processes5. These include evaporation and precipitation, ice sheet growth and decay, moisture transport, rainout intensity, mixing of riverine and marine inputs, and others. For example, 16O atoms are preferentially brought into the salt-free vapor phase during evaporation of seawater while 18O atoms are preferentially left behind in the progressively saltier liquid phase. As clouds move over the ocean the raindrops that fall first contain preferentially more 18O (but still less than the water body of origin), with consecutive periods of rainout progressively distilling the cloud’s water vapor (i.e., Rayleigh distillation)6,7. When this isotopically depleted vapor eventually rains out over the continental interior, isotopically depleted precipitation fills rivers and lakes and even more depleted precipitation falls as snow and ice at higher latitudes and elevations. Variations in δ18Ow signatures within these terrestrial reservoirs are controlled by other hydrologic factors, both local and regional; for instance, strong δ18O depletions in ice core records have been interpreted as reflecting greater rainout of isotopically enriched precipitation along the transport path and high humidity conditions at the source site8.

δ18Ow in the oceans is comparatively less variable than in terrestrial waters because ocean waters are well-mixed and precipitation-evaporation constantly recycles 18O back into the system. δ18Ow data have previously been used to track modern oceanographic and hydrologic processes such as ocean circulation9,10, melting of ice sheets11,12, and surface fluxes of freshwater13 or submarine groundwater discharge into the oceans14.

While changes in both salinity and δ18Ow reflect the same hydrologic processes, δ18Ow is more easily recorded in geologic archives. Variations in carbonate δ18O signatures (δ18Ocarb) of microscopic calcifiers (e.g., foraminifera) record information about past ocean temperatures and δ18Ow. δ18Ocarb measurements from benthic foraminifera have led to foundational paleoclimate discoveries about global temperature changes in the Cenozoic15 and Plio-Pleistocene16. Alternatively, δ18Ocarb measurements from Miocene marine gastropods from the tropics have been interpreted to reflect dominantly hydrological conditions instead of temperature17.

Reconstruction of deep ocean δ18Ow is possible from seafloor porewaters, but this method is limited to the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM)18,19 and inhibited by uncertainties20,21 in molecular diffusion, sedimentation rate, LGM boundary conditions, and other model parameters. Pairing δ18Ocarb measurements with alternate temperature proxy measurements that are independent of δ18Ow variability (e.g., Mg/Ca, Sr/Ca, Δ47)22–29 has allowed reconstruction of ancient seawater δ18Ow. However, to correctly contextualize and interpret reconstructed δ18Ow, thorough documentation of modern spatial and temporal δ18Ow variability is necessary.

To encourage investigations of modern δ18Ow variability, modern instrumental δ18Ow measurements have been collected and documented over the past few decades in databases such as CISE-LOCEAN30, GEOTRACES31, NASA GISS32, and waterisotopes.org33. Many of these δ18Ow measurements were analyzed via mass spectrometer and could not have been measured shipboard unlike many other geochemical measurements, resulting in dramatically less spatial coverage. Measurements of δ18Ow compiled in the NASA GISS database32 show sporadic point source samples along ship tracks, mostly of surface waters, with some regions containing very few measurements. For example, the southeastern parts of the Pacific and Indian Oceans contain less than 30 measurements over 7 million km2 each. Recent advancements in laser-based systems34 may make semi-continuous shipboard measurements theoretically possible, but it has yet to be widely applied.

Despite spatial coverage that is spotty at times, existing data has revealed global-scale patterns showing that surface seawater δ18Ow varies broadly with latitude. Peak δ18Ow values are observed in extratropical latitudes due to evaporative enrichment driven by contact with dry air in the downwelling arm of the Hadley cell, and minimum δ18Ow values are observed at high latitudes due to proximity to melting glacial ice35. Equatorial δ18Ow values are depleted relative to extratropical values due to rainfall recycling heavier isotopes back into seawater but are not as depleted as high latitude regions reduced by ice sheet melt. As this pattern is driven by global atmospheric circulation patterns that are fundamental to the Earth system, it is likely that this general latitudinal structure of δ18Ow has been present throughout most of Earth’s oceanographic history (i.e., excluding Earth’s past extreme climate states).

While these large-scale patterns have emerged from the existing global compilation and new measurements continue to be added to the above-mentioned databases, persistent data gaps hinder use of δ18Ow to more thoroughly investigate oceanographic processes (e.g., movement/strength of regional surface currents, estuarine-ocean mixing). These data gaps are important, considering that several studies have used existing incomplete datasets to interpolate and model δ18Ow across the globe32,36,37, define region-specific δ18Ow-salinity relationships36–44, and validate Earth system model output45. Where models are based on fewer real measurements, these efforts are more likely to misrepresent δ18Ow variability in the modern ocean.

Given these data gaps, the need is clear for more systematically collected δ18Ow datasets, especially those targeting finer spatial scales than previously measured (i.e., <1° latitude/longitude) or oceanographic regions that have largely been overlooked. There are efforts underway to obtain higher-resolution δ18Ow records in the Pacific46,47, and we encourage more campaigns worldwide to capture as much δ18Ow variability as possible. With better coverage in δ18Ow measurements, the oceanography community can expect its understanding of oceanographic and hydrologic processes to expand as well.

One region in particular worth investigating further is the U.S. Eastern Seaboard, as it is home to many hydrologically complex coastal ecosystems48–51 (e.g. estuaries, tidal flats, lagoons, and salt marshes) and numerous economically important fisheries currently under threat from rapid warming52,53. This region also spans a wide range of latitudes, climates, and moisture regimes, from the warmer and more humid tropics (i.e., southern Florida) to the cooler humid continental climates of mid-latitude regions (i.e., Maine). This regional variability has been previously identified in modelling studies, with Murray et al. (2023) most recently dividing the western Atlantic into 3 subregions (“Atlantic Ocean Primary”; “Delaware Coastal Current”; “Bahamas, Florida, Mid-Atlantic Coast”), each with their own defined δ18Ow-salinity relationship in surface seawater. More data is needed here specifically to populate subregions and properly define variability within each one and the boundaries between them.

In this dataset – which we name the “SeaSOx” dataset (see below) – 364 new paired surface seawater salinity and δ18Ow measurements are presented from localities along the U.S. Eastern Seaboard, where salinity (and δ18Ow) varies over fairly small spatial scales. This study approximately triples the amount of δ18Ow data from along the U.S. Eastern Seaboard, providing high-resolution coverage at an unparalleled spatial scale. By collecting δ18Ow at such a high spatial resolution, this dataset expands the utility of δ18Ow as a tracer for both modern oceanography and paleoceanography, provides valuable insights into nearshore hydrologic processes and basin-scale ocean dynamics, and offers a robust foundation for more integrated studies of ocean circulation and water-mass properties.

Methods

Sample localities

Surface seawater samples (0–1 m) were collected from a total of 139 sites across the Eastern Seaboard, from Maine to southern Florida, with typically less than ~30 km of coastline between sampling locations (Fig. 1). Latitude and longitude coordinates were recorded using a Garmin eTrex 30x handheld GPS system. Samples were collected during several one-to-two week campaigns occurring during 2023–2025 (East Coast Water 1 (ECW1), East Coast Water 2 (ECW2), Florida 1 (FL1), Florida 2 (FL2) – see Table 1 for more campaign details, Fig. 4 for δ18Ow data from marine samples visualized by collection year). These intensive campaigns were supplemented with miscellaneous samples collected at a few additional locations in Maine, Florida, and South Carolina. At some sites, 3–5 samples were taken at a single beach with <5 m between collection points to assess small-scale spatial variability, which was later measured to be <1 parts per thousand (ppt) and <0.3‰ for salinity and δ18Ow respectively. Some locations were revisited (e.g., Virginia), and some measurements were repeatedly collected over the years to assess temporal variability (e.g., Hollywood Beach, Florida). Sampling locations consisted of a variety of coastal and marginal marine environments, including estuaries, creeks, semi-restricted bays, inlets, and back barrier environments, sometimes leading to salinities that were below what would be typically classified as “marine”. For classification purposes we designated each sample as “marine” or “brackish-marine” based on a combination of the measured salinity compared to regional baselines and visual observation of the surrounding environmental setting at the site (e.g., open beach vs. semi-restricted bay). Because fully-marine salinity baselines vary along the East Coast, we developed separate salinity thresholds for three geographic zones, distinguished by the distinct hydrology that dominated each region: (1) Maine to North Carolina, primarily dominated by the Labrador Current; (2) North Carolina to Georgia, primarily dominated by the Gulf Stream Current; (3) Florida, noted for its varied complex hydrologic systems (i.e., salt marshes, barrier islands, everglades). If it was unclear whether a sample should be designated “marine” or “intermediate” based on the thresholds, we deferred to visual observation of the sampling location environment (e.g., open beach vs. semi-restricted bay) to determine a designation. Samples measured to have salinities less than the lower end of the brackish threshold were not included in this dataset.

Sample collection procedures

Samples were collected and stored in 30-mL ThermoScientific Nalgene Wide-Mouth high-density polyethylene (HDPE) vials. Prior to collection, each sample vial was rinsed three times with in-situ seawater. To collect each sample, the vial was completely submerged and capped underwater (<1 m) to minimize air exposure and thus avoid air-water fractionation during sample transportation. Vials were transported to the University of Michigan Stable Isotope Lab, where they were analyzed within two to three weeks of their return.

Salinity measurements

For each sample, salinity was measured using an EXTECH EC170 Salinity Meter either at the site during sample collection (e.g., ECW1) or in the lab using water from the sample bottle prior to isotopic analysis (e.g., ECW2). The salinity meter was calibrated in the lab using a commercially available salinity standard (Vernier Science Education); the accuracy and resolution of the meter were +/−1 ppt and +/−0.1 ppt, respectively. δ18O measurements

400 microliters (µL) of sample water were pipetted into a 12 milliliter (mL) Exetainer (LabCo), then flushed with a mixture of helium and 0.3% CO2 before equilibrating for three days. Equilibrated CO2 from the headspace was then measured with a GasBench II coupled to a MAT 253 mass spectrometer, both Thermo Fisher Scientific. All δ18O values are expressed in units of per mille (‰) and reported relative to Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW) (Figs. 2b, 3b). Measurements were corrected for instrument drift and scale compression by comparison to United States Geological Survey (USGS) isotope standards USGS45 (Biscayne Aquifer Drinking Water: −2.238‰ VSMOW), USGS46 (Ice Core Water: −29.80‰ VSMOW), and USGS50 (Lake Kyoga Water: + 4.95‰ VSMOW). Eight consecutive CO2 injections were averaged per sample. Analytical precision was measured to be <0.1‰.

Data Records

The complete dataset named “Seawater salinity and oxygen isotopes (δ18Osw) along the U.S. East Coast from 24 °N to 44 °N (SeaSOx)”55 is readily available for download at https://doi.org/10.60520/IEDA/114403 in the EarthChem database and will be available separately on waterisotopes.org. The file name for this dataset is “Quizon_et_al_ECdw” and consists of two worksheets (“Individual Samples”; “Locality Averages”). Our data-gathering template consists of the following relevant columns for data entry: campaign; sampling ‘area’; site name; designation (marine, brackish-marine); hydrologic environment; collection year; collection month; collection day; collection time; latitude; longitude; depth; depth units; δ18Ow value; δ18Ow units; analytical 1 SD (standard deviation) in-house reproducibility (i.e., long-term 1 SD of standard USGS45); reference standard; sample ID; δ18Ow analysis technique (i.e., IRMS); δ18Ow analysis location; publication citation; publication DOI or URL; water isotope analysis notes (i.e., specific instrument setup); salinity value; salinity units; salinity error; precipitation (Y/N); other sampling notes. Individual samples were also averaged by locality and a complementary template contains many of the same data columns and additional columns for locality average, 1 SD, and 1SE (standard error), for both salinity and δ18Ow. Sampling ‘area’ is considered as the geographic region, with sampling ‘areas’ within states differentiated by the regional hydrology and oceanography (e.g., Boston vs. Cape Cod, West Florida vs. East Florida). Samples were designated as ‘marine’ or ‘brackish-marine’ (see Table 2). Sample environments were included to specify local hydrologic conditions that could have impacted salinity and δ18Ow. Technical Validation

The University of Michigan Stable Isotope Lab (SIL) participated in the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) 2024 Water Stable Isotope Intercomparison (WICO) test, for which results from the lab’s GasBench II/MAT 253 instrument setup were compared to the IAEA’s continuous-flow IRMS setup. Six unique water samples were compared, consisting of a variety of meteoric waters and lab tap waters with bulk δ18Ow compositions ranging from −22 to +1‰ (see Table 3). The average deviation from accepted δ18O for the six samples was 0.04‰, with the largest deviation being 0.11‰. This aligns with the analytical precision determined from repeat measurements of our check standard, which indicates consistent reproducibility at the <0.1‰ level. The lab received a “satisfactory” performance based on additional statistical tests.
