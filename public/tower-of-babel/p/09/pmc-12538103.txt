Prospects of CCUS technology under resource utilization and environmental risks
Authors: Lingna Liu, Chuanwang Liu, Jingxue Wei, Jianping Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCarbon dioxide capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) is a critical technology for global climate mitigation, supporting low-carbon fossil fuel use and decarbonization in hard-to-abate sectors. However, different CCUS technologies exhibit distinct trade-offs in terms of energy consumption, resource utilization, and environmental impacts. This review systematically evaluates energy consumption patterns, resource utilization implications, and associated environmental risks of CCUS technologies across the full chain. Key findings reveal that while CCUS helps address energy security and enables negative emissions, it imposes substantial additional energy demands. Resource efficiency improvements are possible through reduced water use and enhanced land productivity, contributing to socioeconomic benefits. However, large-scale deployment introduces environmental risks, such as CO2 leakage, acidification, and supply-chain vulnerabilities. Based on systematic assessment, we propose development pathways to optimize CCUS integration and risk management, supporting informed policy and technological advancement.

Introduction

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) synthesis report for the Sixth Assessment report noted that an increase in the global temperature will lead to an increase in damage.1 More severe heatwaves, heavier rainfall, and other extreme weather conditions increase the risks to human health and ecosystems. Implementing the right actions immediately will yield crucial changes toward building a sustainable and fair world. The main action needed to minimize climate change depends on the combination of two paths.2 One is to significantly increase the energy efficiency of all sectors to reduce the overall demand, and the second is to accelerate the transition to low-carbon and renewable energy sources, such as solar, wind, hydro, and geothermal energy.3,4 However, in certain hard-to-abate industries, such as the steel, cement, and chemical industries, decarbonization challenges are particularly severe. Process emissions in these industries are inherent, or the complete electrification of renewable energy is currently technically or economically challenging.

Carbon dioxide capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology has become a key technique for mitigating this complex environment because of its long-term and multifaceted effects.5,6 Its core function is to comprehensively decarbonize hard-to-abate industrial processes by capturing carbon dioxide.7,8 In addition, carbon dioxide removal (CDR) pathways can be provided through technologies such as bioenergy and carbon capture and storage (BECCS) and direct air capture (DAC). Therefore, CCUS technology occupies a prominent position in almost all cost-effective approaches modeled by the IPCC and the International Energy Agency to limit warming to 1.5° or 2°.

Driven by increasingly stringent climate targets and the awareness of the necessity of CDR, the application scope of CCUS technology has significantly increased. Extensive and in-depth research of the emission reduction potential, source-sink matching, economic costs, and other aspects of CCUS technology has been conducted.9,10,11 However, a key and relatively unexplored aspect of the large-scale deployment of CCUS technology is the inherent variability and trade-offs between different technological approaches, which yield broader sustainability impacts beyond the cost of simply reducing carbon dioxide emissions.12,13 As the transition from a high-carbon system to a low-carbon system progresses, selecting and implementing specific CCUS technologies may result in significantly different outcomes in terms of energy consumption, resource utilization, and the environment.14,15 These differences not only affect the technical details but also fundamentally affect the overall system efficiency, environmental synergies or trade-offs, social acceptance, and long-term sustainability and ultimately influence the commercial feasibility and scalability of CCUS solutions.16,17,18,19

Therefore, it is crucial to comprehensively understand and compare the energy, resource, and environmental impacts of different CCUS technologies throughout the entire chain. This requires the identification of targeted technology development and system integration paths to enhance the overall sustainability of CCUS technology. In this work, we systematically review the role of CCUS technology in ensuring energy security and increasing the resource utilization efficiency and evaluate the energy consumption and resource utilization patterns and environmental risks associated with different technologies. The technological path, economic viability, and scalability of CCUS technologies are identified. We emphasize advances in decarbonization pathways as key processes, thereby considering the heterogeneity in technical details, costs, resources, and the environment, which yields more specific and actionable insights.

CCUS technology and energy security

Advances in CCUS technology alleviate energy crises, indirectly curbing carbon emissions and offering a promising pathway for the rapid establishment of negative emissions.20,21,22,23,24 By 2050, CCUS technologies are projected to contribute 14% to global CO2 emission reduction.25 Certain geological sequestration techniques and BECCS technologies not only mitigate emissions but also generate oil or renewable energy, thus enabling the replacement of coal and other high-emitting fuels.26,27 The use of CCS-EOR technology could provide an extra 500 billion barrels of oil by 2050, enhancing recoverable reserves by 10%.26 Furthermore, in BECCS technology, renewable resources are employed to replace fossil fuels, indirectly reducing carbon emissions and presenting early opportunities for achieving negative emissions.22,23,24,25,26,27

Geological utilization and storage technologies include CO2-enhanced oil recovery (CO2-EOR), CO2-enhanced methane recovery, CO2-enhanced uranium leaching, CO2-based geothermal energy extraction, and underground CO2 bio-methanation. CO2-EOR is the most mature and widely applied method.28,29,30 In CO2-EOR technology, CO2 is injected into oil reservoirs for sequestration purposes, and the interaction between CO2 and crude oil is leveraged to achieve increased oil production and CO2 sequestration.12,31 Compared with traditional technologies, CO2-EOR technology can increase oil recovery rates by 7%–15%, extend the productive life of oil wells by 15–20 years, and thereby contribute to ensuring energy security.32,33,34,35 In China, approximately 20 billion tons of CO2 can be sequestered in proven oilfields, with a suitable oil reservoir capacity of approximately 5 billion tons of CO2.36 However, the implementation of CO2-EOR technology in China is still in the demonstration phase, and large-scale application has not yet been achieved. In contrast, the use of carbon dioxide for EOR purposes has already been implemented on a large scale in the United States.37 It is estimated that this technology could increase crude oil reserves in the United States by 60–80 billion barrels, effectively doubling the country’s oil reserves, which has immense significance for ensuring national energy security.38 Assuming that crude oil prices range from 50 to 100 per barrel and that CO2 costs vary between 35 and 60 per ton of CO2 injected, with a yield increase of 1 ton of crude oil per 3 tons of CO2 injected, the economic benefit of CO2-EOR technology can be estimated to range from 278.5 to 467 per ton of crude oil produced.39

CO2-enhanced methane recovery technology remains largely experimental, with large-scale industrial demonstrations still needed.40,41,42,43 This technology notably enhances methane recovery rates in conventional coalbed methane extraction, potentially increasing traditional recovery rates from 40%–50% to 90%–100% through CO2 injection.44 Compared with conventional in situ leaching, CO2-based uranium leaching technology is well established for industrial application45 and significantly reduces water and soil pollution, thereby facilitating postmining groundwater remediation.46 Currently, the costs of CO2-enhanced geothermal systems (EGSs) generally exceed those of traditional geothermal power generation systems, a status that is unlikely to change in the short term.31,47 However, in the medium term, advances in drilling technologies, increases in thermoelectric conversion efficiencies, decreasing CO2 prices, and the continuous development of other key technologies are projected to gradually decrease the overall costs of EGSs.48,49 CO2 bio-methanation can be used to convert stored CO2 into methane, resulting in resource reuse.50,51 At present, this technology is still at the laboratory stage. In the actual geological storage process, the metabolism of CO2 biomethane functional microorganisms is influenced by various specific environmental factors.52,53 On the one hand, microorganisms must tolerate certain environmental conditions such as high temperatures, high pressures, high salt contents, and nutrient deficiencies that are caused by deep soil. On the other hand, supercritical CO2 can affect the microbial living environment in reservoirs through hydrogeological characteristics, rock mechanical properties, reservoir physical properties, and other aspects, thereby affecting the functions of methane-related microorganisms.54,55,56

Geological storage increases energy security by increasing the use of fossil fuel resources, whereas BECCS technology mitigates energy crises from a biomass perspective. BECCS (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage) technology includes two crucial stages: biomass utilization and carbon capture. Projections indicate that the global potential resource base for biomass energy could reach 100–300 EJ per annum (EJ/a) by 2050.57,58,59,60 In China, projections suggest that the availability of biomass resources will reach 14.58, and 17.24 EJ/a by 2030, and 2050, respectively.61,62 In China and worldwide, numerous technologies that involve biomass utilization, including combustion, anaerobic digestion, and densification, have already been commercialized.63 These technologies are widely employed in various sectors, such as the energy supply, waste management, and fuel processing sectors. However, for carbon capture in BECCS technology, while the physical absorption methods for carbon dioxide capture have reached the commercial stage, other techniques, such as chemical adsorption, pressure swing adsorption (PSA), cryogenic distillation, and membrane separation, remain in the research, development, and demonstration phases.64 In the future, coal-fired power coupled with biomass generation + CCS/CCUS and biomass power generation + CCS/CCUS will emerge as two pivotal pathways for the advancement of BECCS technology.65 In terms of economic considerations, the CO2 avoidance cost associated with biomass combustion coupled with CCS technology is generally the highest, ranging from approximately 88 to 288 per ton. Similarly, the CO2 avoidance cost for biomass-to-ethanol production coupled with CCS technology is relatively high, estimated to range from approximately 20 to 175 per ton.64 Notably, among various industrial CCS technological applications, coal-fired (pulverized coal, or PC) power plants coupled with CCS generally have the lowest CO2 avoidance costs. By integrating biomass into coal-fired power generation and coupling it with CCS technology, the CO2 avoidance costs associated with biomass-based BECCS technology can be significantly reduced.1

Notably, additional primary energy is needed for the use of CCUS technology, with high energy demands increasing costs, compromising technical and economic feasibility and resulting in additional carbon emissions.66,67,68,69 The primary energy consumption in CCUS stems from the carbon capture stage, particularly the carbon dioxide absorption process. Among the established methods of precombustion capture, postcombustion capture, and oxy-fuel combustion, chemical absorption methods consume between 2 and 5.9 GJ/t of energy,70,71,72,73 membrane separation methods consume between 2 and 6.9 GJ/t of energy,74,75,76,77,78 and adsorption methods consume between 1.5 and 5 GJ/t79,80,81,82,83,84,85 of energy. The energy costs of carbon capture account for 60%–80% of the total costs.86,87 Thus, developing novel technologies to minimize capture-related emissions is vital for enhancing the emission reduction efficacy and economic viability of CCUS.

The absorption method is currently the most commonly employed carbon capture method. Using different types of phase change solvents and mixed absorbents results in different energy consumption levels. The energy consumption range of phase change solvents can reach 1.99–2.6 GJ/t CO2.88,89,90,91 If 30% monoethanolamine is used, the energy consumption can reach 4.0–6.0 GJ/t CO2.70 Chen et al. developed a phase change absorbent that comprises tetramethyldipropylenetriamine, polyethylene glycol dimethyl ether, and water, which contains both secondary and tertiary amines. The energy consumption for regeneration is 2.1 GJ/t CO2.88,90 Zhan et al. developed a phase change absorbent composed of a bifunctional ionic liquid and an aqueous solution of n-propanol, which reduced the energy consumption of regeneration to 1.99 GJ/t CO2.89,91 Jia et al. designed a novel four-component phase change absorption system with an energy consumption of 2.06 GJ/t.79 In chemical looping combustion capture technologies, the energy consumption is 60% lower than that of traditional capture methods.80,81,82,83

The carbon dioxide membrane separation method relies on the difference in selective permeation of gas molecules among membrane materials to achieve separation. It exhibits the characteristics of low pollution, easy operation, and small equipment volumes.84 Zhang et al. purified the permeated gas multiple times through a noncirculating flow secondary membrane separation system to obtain high-purity product gas and increase the CO2 capture rate of the system. The final energy consumption was 1.9 GJ/t.85 Zhao et al. proposed a two-stage membrane separation system with circulating flow, with an energy consumption of 0.7 GJ/t.92 Xu et al. optimized a two-stage membrane separation system with circulating flow by combining the Robeson upper limit, resulting in lower capture costs but increased energy consumption to 1.7 GJ/t.93 At present, the energy consumption of the membrane separation method is approximately 0.7–1.9 GJ/t.

The solid adsorption method involves the use of solid adsorbents to selectively adsorb CO2 in mixed gases and then desorb CO2 under certain regeneration conditions. The widely applied adsorption processes currently include PSA (VPSA), temperature swing adsorption (TSA), and vacuum temperature swing adsorption (VTSA).94,95,96 The energy consumption of the different methods ranges from 0.7 to 4.86 GJ/t CO2. Marc et al. studied the TSA CO2 capture process, which can achieve a CO2 purity of 95% and a CO2 recovery rate of 81%, with an energy consumption of 3.23 GJ/t CO2.97 JIANG et al. studied a VTSA process using zeolite 13× as an adsorbent, with CO2 purity and recovery rates both exceeding 97% and an energy consumption of 3.22 GJ/t.98 Wang et al. used a pilot-scale two-stage VPSA device in an existing coal-fired power plant, with a CO2 recovery rate of 90.2% and an energy consumption of 2.44 GJ/t CO2.99 George et al. investigated a two-stage VPSA process for capturing CO2 from dry flue gas using zeolite 13× and Mg-MOF-74 as adsorbents. The optimal performance of the two-stage VPSA process is a CO2 purity of 97.57% and a CO2 recovery rate of 90.16%. The energy consumption is 0.7 GJ/t CO2.100 Joss et al. designed a new TSA process for capturing CO2 from wet flue gas. This process achieves a purity of 95% and a CO2 recovery rate of 90%, with an energy consumption of more than 4.86 GJ/t CO2.101,102

The electricity and cement industries in China continue to face relatively high carbon capture costs, with capture costs ranging from 200 to 600 yuan per ton of CO2 for the electricity sector and 305 to 730 yuan per ton of CO2 for the cement sector. However, these costs are generally lower than those in international markets, which range from approximately 350 to 977 yuan per ton of CO2 for electricity and 686 to 1280 yuan per ton of CO2 for cement.103 Under the low-speed scenario, the cost of CO2 capture in China is projected to range from 70 to 400 RMB per tonne by 2035, and is expected to decline to between 30 and 150 RMB per tonne by 2060. Under the high-speed scenario, the cost is anticipated to fall within 60–350 RMB per tonne by 2035, and further decrease to 20–100 RMB per tonne by 2060.104

CCUS technology and resource utilization

Water use implications

CCUS results in a high demand for water, and the amount of water consumption varies with process heterogeneity; thus, water consumption and economic costs could be reduced through the optimization of CCUS technologies. The application of CCUS technology has led to 2%–3% increases in freshwater withdrawal and 50%–90% increases in water consumption.105,106,107,108,109 The capture process is the primary contributor to water consumption, accounting for 90% of the total water usage in the process.110 There are large differences in water consumption between different process designs and capture technologies111,112,113,114 (Figure 1). In terms of the process design, different cooling systems exhibit substantial differences in terms of water consumption. These cooling systems primarily include the following three types: once-through cooling systems, wet cooling towers (WCTs), and air-cooled condensers (ACCs). Across all capture types, once-through cooling systems result in the lowest water consumption, whereas WCTs yield the highest wastewater discharge, and ACCs and once-through systems result in comparable wastewater discharge levels.115 For example, with respect to the postcombustion amine capture method, a comparison of the water consumption between coal-fired power plants with and without CCUS technology revealed that in plants with traditional WCTs, approximately 86% of the plant’s water consumption is dedicated to cooling. The addition of an amine-based CCUS system doubles the water consumption of a plant. However, replacing WCTs with ACCs for dry cooling can reduce plant water consumption by approximately 80% (without CCUS) to approximately 40% (with CCUS). Nevertheless, under these scenarios, the capital cost of cooling systems increases by approximately 3-fold.116 According to China’s Annual Report on CCUS, WCTs offer lower net emission reduction and capture costs than ACCs do but consume significantly more water. When capture equipment is installed in power plants, the total water consumption of cooling systems increases by nearly 49.6%.104 In summary, for process designs aimed at reducing water consumption, a once-through cooling system is the optimal choice because it results in minimal water usage. WCTs exhibit the highest wastewater discharge because of their high evaporation rates. There are also differences in water consumption among different capture technologies, with precombustion capture consuming 0.9–2.0 L/kWh of water, postcombustion capture consuming 1.84–5.04 L/kWh of water, and oxy-fuel combustion capture consuming 2.97–3.01 L/kWh of water.117 Notably, postcombustion capture technology results in greater water consumption than the other two capture technologies do.

Among precombustion capture technologies, the main methods include solution absorption, solid adsorption, membrane separation, and cryogenic distillation. The water footprint for precombustion capture is 0.65–0.80 m3/t of CO2 captured.118 Sharma et al. analyzed the average water consumption and water usage per unit of electricity generated by coal-fired power plants in India under different capture solvent configurations (e.g., amines, ammonia, and membranes).115 For amine capture, ammonia capture, and membrane capture under once-through cooling systems, the water consumption levels ranged from 0.375 to 0.397, 0.342–0.360, and 0.37–0.39 kg/kWh, respectively; under WCTs, the water consumption levels ranged from 3.557 to 3.471, 4.035–3.961, and 4.03–3.97 kg/kWh, respectively; and under ACCs, the water consumption levels ranged from 2.116 to 1.998, 0.389–0.369, and 0.42–0.40 kg/kWh, respectively. Overall, amine-based capture is most common, whereas membrane capture technology is relatively costly. Ammonia capture technology is the most water-intensive process, followed by membrane capture and amine-based capture. Compared with traditional monoethanolamine (MEA) absorbents, novel absorbents, such as aminomethyl propanol (AMP)+MEA,119 N-dethylethanolamine (DEEA)+MEA,120 and N-methyldiethanolamine (MDEA) + MEA121 can reduce energy consumption and water consumption during the capture process to a certain extent.122,123 In the future, the advancement of precombustion capture technologies will depend on the development of novel materials, the optimization of process flows, and advances in large-scale industrialization for increasing the absorption efficiency and reducing energy and water consumption levels.

Compared with other CO2 capture technologies, postcombustion capture is more widely applied and is a more mature carbon capture technology. The separation processes involve chemical absorption, physical adsorption (including PSA, TSA, and combined pressure and temperature swing adsorption [PTSA]), and membrane separation and require significant amounts of medium-to low-temperature saturated steam for the regeneration of absorbents. Amine absorption and TSA are the most water-intensive processes among the capture technologies, whereas the PSA and membrane technologies are the least water-intensive processes.118 Brandl et al.124 evaluated the cooling water requirements for postcombustion capture in PC power plants and reported that the cooling water requirements for subcritical, supercritical, and ultrasupercritical power plants increased by 47%, 33%, and 31%, respectively, compared with those of plants without CO2 capture. The challenges associated with postcombustion capture technology include the development of novel composite capture solvents and materials, the optimization of capture solvents or materials and equipment and processes for energy savings, and the comprehensive integration of the capture process with the original industrial process.

The use of oxy-fuel combustion technology increases water consumption more than the use of other technologies does.119 The water consumption of CCUS power plants using oxy-fuel combustion technology exceeds those of traditional thermal power plants by 34.482%, with an increase from 2.84 L/kWh to 3.79 L/kWh.113 Studies have revealed that water consumption during oxy-fuel combustion ranges from 2.5 to 98.4 L/kWh.110 The water footprint of oxy-fuel combustion technology is similar to that of postcombustion capture technology, ranging from 1.93 to 2.69 m3/t.125 Although the capability for a commercial-scale demonstration has been shown for oxy-fuel combustion technology, high additional investment costs, operating costs, and carbon capture costs remain critical technical challenges that hinder further research and development and the widespread adoption of this technology.

With respect to the transportation processes in CCUS, owing to a lack of specific water usage data, existing studies generally refer to water usage data from natural gas transportation, which includes a value of approximately 8.4 gallons per ton of CO2 captured.126,127 In terms of the storage and utilization processes in CCUS, compared with water-enhanced geothermal systems (W-EGSs), technologies such as CO2-EOR, in which water is replaced with CO2 for oil extraction, and CO2-enhanced geothermal systems (CO2-EGSs), can reduce water consumption. Some studies suggest that water intake increases by 174.9% when a power plant is retrofitted with CCUS technology and that water usage increases by 36.9% (with CO2-enhanced water recovery (CO2-EWR)) or 150.5% (without CO2 -EWR). The recovery of underground saline water through CO2-EWR technology can effectively reduce water consumption by 75.5%.126 Moreover, the water consumption of coal-fired power plants increased from 25% to 200% after CCUS technology was adopted,128,129 with a water consumption rate of 0.42 m3/t CO2.105,106,130

BECCS and DACCS (direct air capture with carbon storage) technologies have higher water consumption rates than postcombustion capture technologies in coal-fired power plants do. The application of BECCS technology requires a significant amount of additional energy and water resources. In one study, Rosa reported that among CCS technologies, BECCS has the largest water footprint, with a water footprint of 333 m3/t of CO2 captured in low-efficiency configurations and 575 m3/t of CO2 captured in high-efficiency configurations.118 The water demand for BECCS technology can be divided into two components: the water footprint for the growth of bioenergy crops and the water demand for CCS, with a much higher water consumption for bioenergy crop growth than that for CCS.125 Although DACCS technology currently costs more than BECCS technology does, its water consumption is not as high as that of BECCS technology, with a blue water footprint of 4.01 m3/t of CO2 captured. In DACCS, water resources are indirectly consumed through the use of water for cooling, cleaning, or maintaining equipment during operation.131 There is regional heterogeneity in water consumption, with DACCS process water accounting for more than 10% of the total water use in Russia, the United States, Mexico, and Argentina.132 Overall, attention needs to be given to the tradeoff between the ability of BECCS to mitigate global warming and the growing global water crisis. Finding a balance between future water security, human livelihood needs, and BECCS is particularly important. To address this potential water crisis, further research into advanced technologies and methods for biofuel extraction is necessary.

Land and resource use implications

CCUS technology has a positive effect on the belowground space utilization and the protection of water resources, promoting technological innovation and enhancing socioeconomic benefits. In terms of land utilization, CCUS technology increases the demand for industrial land and drives the development of related industries, such as carbon capture equipment manufacturing and CO2 transportation services.133,134 In belowground space utilization, CCUS technology involves geological CO2 sequestration and injection, enhances energy production and promotes resource extraction; for example, CCUS technology increases oil and gas recovery and promotes the recovery of geothermal, uranium, and other resources.135,136,137,138

Compared with conventional in situ leaching techniques, CO2-enhanced uranium leaching (CO2-EUL) can significantly reduce water and soil pollution levels during uranium mining, facilitating groundwater environmental restoration after mining. By adding ammonium bicarbonate and increasing the CO2 dosage, the leaching efficiency can be markedly improved; for example, the amount of HCO3- in the leachate can be increased from 850 mg/L to 1200 mg/L, and the uranium concentration can be increased by 1.73–44.33 mg/L per borehole and 8 mg/L in composite samples.139 Unlike conventional uranium mining techniques (e.g., agitation leaching and heap leaching), the CO2-EUL method avoids the transport of underground ore to the surface and eliminates tailings, waste residue, and dust pollution.140 Moreover, owing to the relatively small scale of CO2 injection, the CO2-EUL technique is safe. Compared with ex situ mineralization technologies, in situ mineralization technologies provide advantages such as greater sequestration capacities and lower costs.141

CO2-based heat extraction technologies refer to the process of geothermal resource exploitation using CO2 as the working medium and include CO2-containing geothermal systems (CPGSs) and CO2-EGS technology.142,143 Currently, the cost of CO2-EGS technology is generally higher than that of traditional geothermal power generation, and this status is unlikely to change in the short term. However, in the medium term, with continuous advances in drilling technology, increases in thermoelectric conversion efficiency, reductions in CO2 prices, and the sustained development of other key technologies, the overall cost of CO2-EGS could gradually decrease. Additionally, by optimizing the allocation of CO2 sources, geothermal sites, and power generation systems, the overall cost of CO2-EGS technology can be effectively reduced. If CO2-EGS technology is successfully implemented, it will result in significant environmental and social benefits.144,145 As important renewable and clean energy sources, geothermal resources can indirectly reduce CO2 emissions during fossil fuel utilization. CO2 can be sequestered via this technology, thereby achieving direct CO2 emission reduction. Compared with traditional enhanced geothermal systems (W-EGSs), in which water is used to extract geothermal resources, CO2-EGSs can conserve substantial amounts of water resources, thus potentially significantly reducing wastewater generation and emissions, with favorable environmental and social benefits.146,147,148,149,150

The technology of CO2-enhanced deep saline water production and sequestration evolved due to the need to mitigate reservoir pressure and environmental risks during geological CO2 sequestration in deep saline aquifers. Compared with traditional injection methods, an injection-production balance process for pressure control is less likely to result in large-scale pressure accumulations, thereby reducing the risks of vertical leakage, lateral migration, and large-scale surface deformation induced by pressure accumulation. Water desalination can effectively alleviate the dual dilemma of CO2 emissions and water scarcity in energy-rich but water-scarce regions, representing a future trend in CO2 sequestration technology for saline aquifers.151,152 Moreover, water production via CO2-enhanced deep saline technology can be applied to liquid mineral deposits, thereby extracting high-value liquid mineral resources and various valuable elements from brine or concentrated saltwater, generating significant economic and social benefits and reducing carbon emissions.153

However, there is a tradeoff between BECCS and land ecosystems. The large-scale deployment of BECCS technology will result in the occupation of a significant amount of agricultural and forestry land, and the large-scale cultivation of carbon-fixing plants may also induce imbalances in regional ecosystems. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically balance the considerations of land use, ecological diversity, and the deployment of BECCS.154 Additionally, notable issues exist in the use of BECCS technology in terms of technological maturity and economic-ecological uncertainties. For example, BECCS is still in the early stages of research and development, and only a few engineering demonstrations have been conducted globally. The transportation of biomass, as well as the use of land and water resources, may also contribute to carbon emissions from BECCS technology deployment.155 The challenges that must be addressed for the large-scale implementation of BECCS technology in the future include enhancing the efficiency of biomass energy utilization, reducing the operational costs of CCUS technology, mitigating the impact of BECCS projects on biodiversity, and alleviating the constraints on biomass resources imposed by land and water resources.

Risks of CCUS technology

CCUS technology faces constraints in large-scale deployment because of physical risks (e.g., carbon leakage, ocean acidification, soil acidification, and increased greenhouse effects) and supply chain risks (e.g., lack of key materials and infrastructure and labor shortages).156,157 The physical risks of CCUS technology, ranked in descending order of accident severity, are carbon emissions sources, chemical usage, capture, transportation, and biological/geological modules158,159,160,161 (Figure 2). In terms of time, owing to factors such as temperature, the risk of accidents is lowest in autumn and highest in winter.162 Conducting risk assessments in advance is key to the success of CCUS technology.163Figure 2CCUS leakage risk

CCUS leakage risk

In the capture stage, in particular, significant energy consumption occurs across various CO2 capture methods, leading to the emission of additional CO2 and other pollutants.164,165 Chemical methods, such as solvent absorption, generate volatile amines, nitrosamines, and aldehydes that impact the environment.166,167,168 The use of low-concentration absorbents, such as solutions with less than 5% ammonia, can significantly reduce fugitive emissions and control ammonia escape.168 In addition, for economic reasons, most equipment and pipelines are constructed of carbon steel, and these parts of the devices may be subject to corrosion from various reagents, such as wet acidic gases, oxygen, and amine degradation products. The correct selection of materials is crucial for ensuring the long-term performance, safety, and operational availability of CO2 capture devices.169,170

During transportation, risks exist during the transport of captured CO2 to storage sites because of leakage from pipelines, ships, or transport vehicles. If such leaks occur, CO2 and other potentially harmful substances may be released into the environment, posing threats to ecosystems and human health.156,157 For pipeline transportation, the primary safety concern lies in the potential leakage of supercritical CO2, which would primarily affect areas along transportation routes.171 In ship-based CO2 transportation, ultralow-temperature CO2 can lead to the formation of ice-water mixtures, triggering strong air currents, while high concentrations of CO2 can stall ship engines. A larger leak size leads to a broader dispersion region with a more pronounced presence of all CO2 phases.172 Real-time monitoring of CO2 leaks can be achieved through temperature and gas concentration monitoring methods coupled with online monitoring technologies.173,174

The sequestration and utilization stages represent crucial junctures for CO2 leakage monitoring.175,176,177,178 Under the chemical utilization mode, the leakage and fires of CO2 to methanol storage tanks affect areas within a radius of 337 m, and the leakage of carbon monoxide can affect areas within a radius of 2 km, which more directly influences human health.162,179 Inherent leakage risks exist during underground sequestration, with consequences ranging from ocean acidification and soil acidification to exacerbation of the greenhouse effect, which depends on the specific sequestration site.180,181 Within the context of CCS-EOR technology deployment, CO2 leakage along faults is among the most critical leakage paths.182,183,184 The absence of wellhead recovery and reinjection processes can lead to substantial CO2 emissions from wellheads, which intensify with increasing injection volumes and durations.185,186 Furthermore, low integrity of wells and cap rocks, insufficient trapping mechanisms, and geological variations can contribute to significant CO2 leakage from underground storage reservoirs, necessitating monitoring.187

In addition to the risk of physical leakage of carbon dioxide, its supply chain risk also deserves full attention. The current lack of risk quantification for CCS projects globally has resulted in their inability to meet their budget and timeline expectations. The CCUS technology chain is relatively long and is susceptible to technical, economic, environmental, political, and other issues, leading to supply chain disruptions.188 Its main issue is the fragility of multilink coupling—the failure of any link can lead to paralysis of the entire chain. These issues mainly include spatial and temporal mismatches between sources and sinks, bottlenecks caused by inadequate planning of transportation networks, coordination failures between regions, overload of underground resources, public acceptance problems, and potential chain reactions caused by any partial interruption of future business models.189,190,191 Therefore, conducting security assessments of supply chains to balance economic, environmental, and safety objectives is crucial.192,193,194

Currently, the methods for monitoring leakage risks can be divided into subsurface monitoring and surface monitoring methods.195 Subsurface monitoring mainly determines whether a leak has occurred by measuring the carbon dioxide concentrations in groundwater and soil, as well as changes in the terrain. The main methods include pressure monitoring, electromagnetic monitoring, thermal conductivity monitoring, monitoring of CO2 residual saturations, logging microseismic monitoring, and ecosystem monitoring.196,197

Among them, the pressure monitoring method ensures the safety of CO2 storage by monitoring the pressure of the storage layer and even the pressure of the previous permeable layer. This method is widely adopted, and almost all CCS projects adopt this technology.198,199 The electromagnetic monitoring method determines possible CO2 leaks by monitoring the conductivity of groundwater but is affected by the depth of penetration. This technology has been successfully applied in the Mansfield Sandstone Reservoir CCS project, which is located in Indiana, USA.200 The CO2 residual saturation monitoring method can be used to determine the underground migration and changes in CO2 by monitoring the residual CO2 saturation. Currently applied to injection wells and monitoring wells in the Frio geological storage project,201 ecosystem monitoring methods detect carbon leakage by monitoring changes in the ecosystem at the burial site and have been used in CCS projects in Italy and controlled injection projects in Nottingham, UK. The ZERT CCS project, located in Bozeman, Montana, USA, relies on ground-based and high-altitude hyperspectral methods to observe the response of plants in visible and near-infrared reflectance spectra.202,203

Surface monitoring technology is more complex. The main methods include infrared gas analyzer technology, long-range open path infrared detection and modulation laser detection technology, vorticity-related monitoring methods, logging microseismic monitoring methods, lidar detection technology, tracer tracking monitoring methods, carbon stable isotope monitoring methods, hyperspectral imaging detection technology, and wireless sensing monitoring technology.204,205,206

The logging microseismic monitoring method relies on microseismic fault photography technology to apply microseismic waves to the source well and aims to detect microseismic waves that are transmitted from different cross-sections at the corresponding receiving wells, finally determining the movement of underground fluids. This method exhibits high accuracy, but the instrument and equipment costs are high, and real-time monitoring cannot be achieved. This technique has been used to monitor leaks in the Frio CCS testing project in the United States.207,208 The carbon stable isotope monitoring method uses the different abundances of carbon isotopes in different carbon sources to monitor the changes in isotope content in the in situ carbon spectrum of the atmosphere and infers the amount of CO2 leakage through calculations. The carbon stable isotope monitoring method is highly accurate, but the monitoring process is cumbersome, the detection instruments are expensive, and long-term results analysis is needed, making it difficult to achieve online real-time monitoring. This technology has been applied to CO2 leakage monitoring in CO2 storage projects at the ZERT experimental center.209,210 In the Salah project, surface deformation analysis was employed, and the results conformed with modeling predictions. Experimental airborne applications of differential absorption lidar exhibited satisfactory accuracy in leak detection. Laboratory tests via Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy have also indicated successful leak detection.211,212

In terms of supply chain risk monitoring, existing quantitative methods include the use of numerical data and mathematical models to quantify and analyze risk.213 These approaches include expert evaluation methods to assess the relative impacts of various risks and uncertainties on failed investments in CCS projects214 and expected monetary value methods to calculate emergency reserves, arrange procurement activities, allocate the impacts of various research and development activities, and promote further research into the use of decision trees.215 However, the challenge of quantifying risk lies in the uncertainty in probability and accurately measuring the impact of cost or time, which heavily relies on human intuition and may vary between individuals. The calculations for large-scale CCS projects involving numerous variables or outcomes are very complex.216 Although risks can be quantified to some extent, it is impossible to provide absolute guarantees.217

Future development paths and challenges

In the future, research on CCUS technology will continue to focus on technological innovations, diversified applications, industrial integration, and other areas (Figure 3).Figure 3Development of CCUS technology in the different fields

Development of CCUS technology in the different fields

With respect to technological innovations, technological advances drive the optimization and improvement in CCUS technology. Key areas of focus include increasing carbon capture rates, utilization efficiencies, and storage capacities while minimizing costs and risks. In the capture process, first-generation carbon capture technologies include primarily chemical absorbents, membrane separation, and adsorption methods. Second-generation capture technologies refer to those that are anticipated to be commercially deployable by 2025.218 In terms of these technologies, innovations have been achieved in the development of capture solvents, catalysts, and overall technology, with the incorporation of advanced materials such as ionic liquids, phase-change absorbents, and heterogeneous catalysts. As a result, the energy consumption for carbon capture via these second-generation technologies is expected to be more than 30% lower than those of first-generation technologies.219 Third-generation technologies significantly differ from first-generation capture technologies in terms of techniques and methodologies, enabling a reduction in capture energy consumption of more than 50% compared with first-generation technologies.80,81,82,220,221 Research into novel adsorption materials such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and porous organic polymers (POPs), as well as innovative absorption materials and membrane separation technologies.222,223

In the transportation sector, we should vigorously develop high-pressure, large-diameter, and long-distance pipeline technology to reduce unit transportation costs.224 It is also important to accelerate the construction of regional and even national-level CO2 transportation backbone networks and gathering systems.225 Moreover, specialized CO2 transport ships with greater capacity should be developed. Low-temperature liquefaction and insulation technology should be optimized to reduce evaporated gas generation and energy consumption during transportation.226 It is also important to explore the feasibility of combining CO2 transportation pipelines with other energy infrastructure, such as hydrogen pipelines, or develop pipeline technologies that can simultaneously transport multiple media.227

In the process of storage and utilization, enhanced oil recovery technology and in situ uranium extraction technology have reached commercial levels and should be expanded from petroleum to enhanced natural gas/shale gas recovery (EGR/ESGR), CO2-EGS, and CO2-deep saltwater storage-driven brine extraction (CO2-DRBR).228 the promotion of chemical and biological utilization technologies, such as methanol production, mineralization utilization, and microbial conversion technologies, such as converting CO2 into methanol, synthetic natural gas (SNG); synthetic aviation fuel (SAF); olefins (e.g., ethylene and propylene); formic acid, polymers, and the use of engineered microorganisms or algae to convert CO2 into biofuels (e.g., ethanol and diesel); bioplastics (Polyhydroxyalkanoates, PHA); high-value chemicals (e.g., pigments and proteins); and feed or food raw materials should also be accelerated.151,229,230,231

In addition, in terms of environmental monitoring technology, most existing CO2 storage leakage monitoring technologies focus on monitoring CO2 leakage, but CO2 leakage significantly lags behind the structural damage and instability of the geological body used for storage.232 To achieve safe monitoring of CO2 storage, it is necessary to conduct full cycle monitoring of CO2 storage. In the initial stage of CO2 storage, the direction of CO2 transport is studied through tracer monitoring or numerical simulations. At the later stage of CO2 storage, continuous three-dimensional environmental monitoring is conducted to achieve real-time and continuous monitoring of CO2 storage leaks. After CO2 storage is completed, key environmental indicators should be monitored regularly on the basis of the CO2 storage mechanism and migration laws to ensure the effectiveness of CO2 storage.233,234 In terms of diversified applications, the use of CCUS technology will continue to expand into various sectors, including power generation, industrial processes, transportation, agriculture, and environmental remediation. For example, CCUS can be used to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in coal-fired power plants, convert CO2 into valuable chemicals or materials, and even assist in land management and crop production.218

Notably, many challenges remain to be resolved with respect to CCUS technology in terms of cost and infrastructure. One of the primary barriers to the adoption of CCUS technologies is the high cost associated with carbon capture, transportation, and storage.235 Further research and technological advances are needed to reduce these costs and render CCUS technology economically viable.236 In addition, significant challenges exist in terms of developing the necessary infrastructure for large-scale CCUS projects, including CO2 transportation networks and storage sites.237 Identifying suitable storage sites and ensuring their long-term safety and integrity are crucial.

CCUS technology represents a promising solution for mitigating climate change and enabling the realization of a low-carbon future. However, several challenges exist in promoting its widespread adoption, including economic viability and infrastructure development. To overcome these challenges, continued research and development efforts, supportive policies and regulations, and robust public engagement are essential. CCUS technology could fulfill a critical role in reducing greenhouse gas emissions and achieving sustainable development goals, as the global community works together to address climate change.
