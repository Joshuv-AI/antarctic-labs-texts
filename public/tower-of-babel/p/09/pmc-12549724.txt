The seagull excision technique for pilonidal sinus disease
Authors: C Sahin, S Leventoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPilonidal sinus disease is a common condition affecting the skin and subcutaneous tissue in the upper natal cleft. Bascom’s cleft lift procedure, an established surgical technique, treats the disease by excising the sinus tract, flattening the gluteal cleft, and displacing the incision off the midline (Immerman in Cureus, 2021. 10.7759/cureus.13053; Leventoglu et al. in Colorectal Dis 25:1938–1939, 2023. 10.1111/codi.16701). The seagull excision technique, developed as an alternative to the proven cleft lift procedure, aims to achieve similar surgical goals while offering a different flap design and closure strategy. Introduction

Pilonidal sinus disease is a chronic infection of the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the natal cleft, affecting approximately 0.7% of the population, with an incidence of around 25–26 per 100,000 individuals. It is at least twice as common in men as in women and typically occurs between the ages of 15 and 30 years [1, 2]. Clinical symptoms include pain, swelling, erythema, and discharge, and the condition can significantly reduce quality of life and, in severe cases, lead to sepsis [3]. A wide array of surgical techniques have been described for its treatment, ranging from simple excision with or without primary closure to various flap-based closure methods [4–8].

Despite the variety of surgical approaches, recurrence and wound-related complications remain significant concerns, particularly in cases of previous interventions. Off-midline closure techniques have gained popularity owing to their lower complication and recurrence rates compared to midline closures [9, 10]. Among these, Bascom’s cleft lift and Karydakis procedures are widely accepted methods [6, 8, 11]. These two techniques are well-established advancement flap techniques for the surgical management of pilonidal disease. Both procedures aim to flatten the natal cleft, achieve off-midline closure, and reduce wound tension by mobilizing adjacent tissues, thereby minimizing the risk of recurrence. However, in complex or recurrent cases, modifications of these approaches may offer additional benefits. Our seagull excision technique similarly belongs to the category of advancement flap methods, sharing these fundamental objectives. This technique was developed as an alternative to the traditional cleft lift, incorporating a gluteal advancement design that resembles the shape of a seagull’s wings. This technique allows for tension-free, off-midline closure and enhanced tissue mobilization. It is preferred because it provides greater lateralization of the natal cleft and effectively displaces the incision away from the intergluteal sulcus and the anal verge, thereby potentially reducing local complications and recurrence.

Methods

Patient selection

Patients aged 18 years and older with pilonidal disease classified as type 3, 4, or 5 according to the Tezel classification were included [12]. Patients with asymptomatic disease or acute abscess corresponding to stage 1 or 2 were excluded. In our clinical practice, cleft lift or seagull excision techniques are selected for patients without acute abscess, with single or multiple pits demonstrating midline tracts, and in those who have experienced recurrence following simple excision, unroofing, or midline closure techniques. The choice of surgical technique (cleft lift or seagull excision) was randomly assigned to patients. Exclusion criteria also included severe comorbidities precluding surgery and inability to attend follow-up visits. All patients provided informed consent before surgery.

Surgical procedure

The procedure was performed under spinal anesthesia with the patient in the prone jackknife position. We did not perform preoperative bowel cleansing. Cefazolin (2 g) was administered for antibiotic prophylaxis. After the buttocks were shaved and taped for optimal exposure, the pilonidal area and excision margins were marked. The upper limit of our off-midline incision begins at the superior edge of the navicular area and should extend inferiorly to the point where the sinus tracts end, which is typically 4–5 cm superior to the anal verge. If the sinus tracts are located closer than this distance to the anal verge, we do not recommend this technique, as increased tension on the incision may occur. This seagull-shaped incision encompassing all pathological tissue was made, followed by complete excision of the diseased area. A flap approximately 1 cm thick and 3 cm wide was elevated laterally and advanced medially to achieve tension-free closure. Manual medial compression was applied to ensure adequate flap mobility. The adipose tissue was closed with interrupted 2–0 absorbable sutures, and a Jackson-Pratt drain was placed. Subcutaneous tissues were approximated with 3–0 absorbable sutures, and the skin was closed using a subcuticular 4–0 absorbable suture. The operative time ranged from 30 to 45 min.

Surgeons performing the seagull excision technique should have experience with advancement flap procedures, a thorough understanding of the natal cleft anatomy, and proficiency in achieving meticulous hemostasis. Familiarity with flap elevation, tension-free closure, and proper wound drainage techniques are essential technical prerequisites to ensure optimal outcomes and minimize complications. On the basis of our experience, we estimate that surgeons already experienced with pilonidal surgery can become proficient with the seagull technique after performing approximately 7–8 cases.

Follow-up

Pain scores were evaluated on postoperative day 1, while cosmetic outcomes were assessed at the 6-month follow-up visit. All patients underwent comprehensive evaluations at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively to monitor wound healing, detect potential complications, and assess recurrence. The drain was typically removed on postoperative days 4–5, and daily wound dressings were recommended until drain removal. Patients were advised to avoid sitting for the first week after surgery. No medication was prescribed for bowel control. Washing the surgical area was not recommended until after the drain was removed.

Patients with sedentary occupations are advised to return to work approximately 2 weeks after surgery, whereas those performing physical labor are recommended to resume work no earlier than 4 weeks postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate, based on expected cell frequencies. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software.

Results

In our retrospective series of 100 patients treated between January 2021 and July 2024, 50 patients underwent the seagull excision technique and 50 underwent the Bascom cleft lift. The mean age was 32.0 years (range 18–58) in the seagull group and 30.2 years (range 18–49) in the cleft lift group. The proportion of male patients was 64% in the seagull group and 84% in the cleft lift group. The mean follow-up period was 14.9 months for the seagull group and 15.9 months for the cleft lift group.

We found that the seagull technique resulted in significantly lower rates of surgical site infection (2%) and overall wound complication (12%) compared to the cleft lift group (p < 0.05). Hospital stay, drain removal time, postoperative pain scores, and cosmetic satisfaction scores were similar between groups. Discussion

This study compared the surgical outcomes of the Bascom cleft lift and seagull excision techniques in the treatment of pilonidal sinus disease. The Bascom cleft lift is widely regarded as one of the most effective surgical approaches for pilonidal disease, with low rates of recurrence and wound-related complications reported in the literature [4, 13, 14]. However, in our case series, the seagull excision technique demonstrated even lower rates of surgical site infection and overall postoperative complications compared to the cleft lift. These findings suggest that the seagull approach may offer additional advantages in minimizing wound morbidity, possibly owing to its greater lateralization of the incision and enhanced tissue mobilization. In contrast, rates of minor wound separation, hematoma, and recurrence were not significantly different between the two groups.

Recent cohort data further support these findings, showing that off-midline flap techniques are associated with significantly lower rates of wound complications and recurrence compared to midline excisions [15]. The seagull excision technique was independently designed to fulfill the same core surgical principles—off-midline closure, tension reduction, and optimized healing—while incorporating a gluteal-based flap configuration that may provide enhanced lateralization and lower incision tension.

While the seagull technique appears promising, particularly in its capacity to reduce early postoperative complications, further prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate recurrence rates and long-term cosmetic outcomes. As a newly introduced technique, the seagull excision requires further evaluation through multicenter randomized trials to establish its efficacy, reproducibility, and broader applicability across diverse patient populations.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old male patient with pilonidal disease presented with a 2-year history of persistent discharge and pain in the intergluteal sulcus. The patient presented with type 3 disease according to the Tezel classification, with tracts primarily confined to the midline and limited lateral extension. The depth of the tracts was approximately 2 cm. The seagull excision technique was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 1 with instructions for daily wound care. The surgical drain was removed on postoperative day 4. No complications were observed during the follow-up.
