The Impact of Hinge Position and Wedge Orientation in Zadek Osteotomy across Different Foot Morphologies: A Virtual Surgical Simulation Study
Authors: Giammarco Gardini, Carla Carfì, Wolfram Grün, Jonathan Kaplan, Gustavo Araujo Nunes, Emily J Luo, Tommaso Forin Valvecchi, Roberto Zambelli, Cesar De Cesar Netto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The Zadek osteotomy (ZO) is a well-established surgical option for insertional Achilles tendinopathy. By modifying the position of the posterior calcaneal tuberosity, ZO may reduce posterosuperior calcaneal prominence, mechanical conflict at the Achilles insertion, and tensile stress on the tendon. Nevertheless, the biomechanical impact of wedge orientation and hinge position—and their relationship with underlying foot morphology—has not been clearly defined.Methods:A weightbearing computed tomography (WBCT)–based virtual simulation was performed on 60 asymptomatic feet, categorized as cavus (n = 20), neutral (n = 20), or planus (n = 20) based on the calcaneal inclination angle. Nine ZO configurations were modeled by combining 3 hinge positions (posterior, A; mid-tuberosity, B; anterior, C) with 3 wedge inclinations. Pre- and post-simulation measurements were compared for calcaneal inclination angle (CIA), talocalcaneal angles, hindfoot moment arm (HMA), hindfoot alignment angle (HAA), Fowler-Philip (FP) angle, X/Y ratio, and plantar fascia (PF) length.Results:Wedge inclination showed no significant influence on any variable (P ≈ .99). Hinge position, however, markedly affected sagittal alignment. Hinges B and C significantly reduced CIA (hinge B −0.5° [95% CI −0.66° to −0.4°]; hinge C −2.1° [95% CI −2.26° to −1.96°], both P < .001). Hinge B showed the largest reduction in FP angle (−7.6° [95% CI −8.63° to −6.59°], P = .16) and produced the greatest increase in X/Y ratio (+0.57 [95% CI +0.46 to +0.68], P = .007). In the pre- to post-simulation analysis, HMA increased slightly with hinge B (P = .03), and PF length increased only with hinge C (P < .001). Subgroup analysis revealed that cavus feet exhibited the greatest sagittal correction, particularly with hinge C.Conclusion:ZO significantly affected the Fowler-Philip angle, the primary radiographic outcome, with its overall foot alignment corrective power depending principally on hinge position rather than wedge inclination. These changes occurred predominantly in the sagittal plane while sparing coronal and axial alignment.Clinical Relevance:WBCT-based virtual simulation may help surgeons tailor Zadek osteotomy hinge position to baseline foot morphology and anticipate morphology-specific alignment changes before surgery.Level of Evidence:Level IV, pre-clinical simulation case series.Keywords: Haglund syndrome, insertional Achilles tendinopathy, minimally invasive surgery, weightbearing CT, virtual simulation, dorsal closing wedge calcaneal osteotomy

Introduction

Insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT) and Haglund syndrome are painful conditions of the heel associated with the presence of a bony prominence at the posterior tuberosity of the calcaneus.

Nonoperative management is effective in most patients, but surgical treatment may be indicated in refractory cases.1,2 Traditional procedures such as calcaneoplasty and tendon debridement yield variable results, particularly when the underlying calcaneal morphology is not addressed. 3 Several bony, soft tissue, and combined procedures have been described in the literature, but no consensus has been reached regarding the most effective approach.4,5

Zadek osteotomy (ZO) is a dorsal closing-wedge osteotomy of the calcaneus first described in 1939.6,7 By modifying the posterosuperior calcaneal contour, the ZO procedure theoretically reduces the pressure of the calcaneal tuberosity on the Achilles tendon and shortens the calcaneus. By repositioning the posterior calcaneal tuberosity, ZO may reduce posterosuperior prominence, mechanical conflict at the Achilles insertion, and tensile stress on the tendon. Since its introduction by Zadek, the technique has shown favourable outcomes and has therefore undergone continuous refinement.8,9 Keck and Kelly 10 reintroduced it in 1965, and Miller and Vogel 11 later combined the wedge resection with smoothing of the posterosuperior calcaneal process. In 1995, Manin 12 proposed a re-elaboration with excision of a 4-6-mm rectangular bone block distal to the plantar calcaneal tubercle. More recently, attention has shifted toward technical aspects rather than wedge morphology: Maffulli et al 13 described a modification performed without Achilles detachment or calcific debridement, while Kaplan et al 14 described a minimally invasive percutaneous technique, which has been associated with shorter recovery and fewer complications. 15 Clinical series have demonstrated favourable outcomes, with improvements in pain, function, and radiographic parameters.16,17

Recently, virtual simulation studies using weightbearing computed tomography (WBCT) and surgical planning software, have provided novel insights in foot and ankle deformity comprehension.18,19 Nunes et al 20 showed that ZO significantly decreases the calcaneal length, Fowler-Philip angle, and sagittal talocalcaneal angle, while sparing coronal and axial alignment. These findings suggest that ZO produces a predominantly sagittal correction; however, the influence of wedge position and inclination remains unclear. 3

Despite recent advances, the impact of baseline foot morphology on the biomechanical outcomes of ZO remains unclear. This study sought to answer 3 important questions through WBCT-based virtual simulation: (1) does wedge inclination and (2) hinge position affect key radiographic parameters, and (3) do specific osteotomy configurations offer corrective benefits or biomechanical disadvantages in patients with varying hindfoot alignments?

We hypothesized that the position and inclination of the wedge resection may significantly influence hindfoot alignment and calcaneal morphology, potentially offering an additional corrective tool in selected patients based on their preoperative hindfoot morphology.

Methods

This retrospective comparative virtual simulation study has obtained an institutional review board approval (IRB no. Pro00113556), complying with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Patient Selection

Sixty patients who underwent WBCT between February 2022 and May 2024 at our institution were identified. The scans (CurveBeamAI) were conducted with a voxel size of 0.37 mm, a 350-mm field-of-view diameter, a 200-mm field-of-view height, an exposure time of 9 seconds, and a total scan time of 54 seconds. All scans were obtained from bilateral WBCTs performed for the evaluation of the contralateral symptomatic, injured, or operated side. Inclusion criteria included adults over 18 years who had undergone WBCT scans, while exclusion criteria comprised prior ankle or foot surgery, previous injury, or any radiographic signs of osteoarthritis.

Imaging was analysed using the semi-automated software Bonelogic (Disior; Zimmer Biomet). DICOM files for each WBCT scan were processed within the software, which automatically generated a preliminary 3D reconstruction of the foot and ankle. All bones were then manually reviewed and marked when necessary to ensure anatomical accuracy, resulting in a semi-automated segmentation. The software subsequently computed the mathematical 3D model of the foot, defining bone axes, centre points, and the radiographic parameters. Patients were finally stratified into 3 groups according to their foot morphology (planus, neutral, and cavus ; n = 20 per group), based on the semi-automatically measured calcaneal inclination angle: feet with a pitch <10° were classified as planus, those between 10° and 20° as neutral, and those >20° as cavus.21-23

Virtual ZO

Virtual ZO procedure was performed using 3D models generated by Bonelogic software. 24 The region of the calcaneus located behind the posterior subtalar facet was divided into 6 segments. Its upper margin was defined by the Y-line, as originally described by Tourné et al. 25

The posterior boundary was established by a line drawn perpendicular to the posterior point of the Y-line and extending to the inferior cortex of the calcaneus. The anterior boundary was defined by a parallel line starting from the posterior landmark of the posterior subtalar joint. The superior cortex was then equally divided into 3 equal sectors (1, 2, and 3). At the level of the inferior cortex, this rectangular area was further subdivided into 3 subregions: region A corresponded to the area posterior to the plantar tubercle of the calcaneal tuberosity; region C was located anterior to the insertion of the plantar fascia and intrinsic plantar musculature; and region B represented the intermediate zone between these 2 landmarks (Figure 1).

Representation of the Y and X lines described by Tourné, the boundaries and the 6 regions of subdivision of the calcaneus.

The calcaneal 3D model was visualized in a standardized lateral orientation to define the osteotomy plane, while all simulations and measurements were performed on the reconstructed 3D model. The virtual osteotomies were defined by combining 3 hinge positions (A, B, and C) with 3 different wedge orientations (1, 2, and 3), resulting in 9 distinct configurations (Figure 2). The wedge orientations (1, 2, and 3) determined the inclination of the osteotomy plane, with progressively more anteriorly directed cuts from configuration 1 to 3. For each configuration, the osteotomy was performed perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the calcaneus, with the apex located at the plantar cortex, which was preserved as a hinge. The posterior fragment was then rotated 10° anteriorly, thereby standardizing the wedge size across specimens. The software then automatically calculated the radiographic parameters after the procedure. All simulations were performed by a fellowship-trained foot and ankle surgeon experienced in WBCT analysis and 3D planning software, and reviewed by a senior expert to ensure anatomical consistency.

Schematic representation of the 9 virtual Zadek osteotomy configurations. Osteotomies were defined by combining 3 hinge positions (A: posterior calcaneal tuberosity; B: mid-tuberosity; C: anterior tuberosity, closer to the plantar fascia insertion) with 3 different wedge orientations (1, 2, and 3), corresponding to progressively more anteriorly directed osteotomy planes. Each combination of hinge position and wedge orientation generated a distinct osteotomy configuration (A1-A3, B1-B3, C1-C3).

Measurements

Adhering to previously published protocols and original descriptions,26-29 a total of 9 variables were semiautomatically measured before and after the virtual osteotomy. The calcaneal inclination angle (CIA) was measured in the sagittal plane as the angle between the inferior surface of the calcaneus and the supporting surface (Figure 3). Talocalcaneal angles were assessed in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, reflecting the spatial relationship between the talus and calcaneus in 3 dimensions. Hindfoot moment arm (HMA) and hindfoot alignment angle (HAA) were used to quantify coronal plane alignment of the hindfoot relative to the tibial axis. The Fowler-Philip angle (FP) was measured in the sagittal plane to evaluate the prominence of the posterosuperior calcaneus. Calcaneal length and the calcaneal X/Y ratio, as described by Tourné, were used to assess calcaneal morphology and elongation. To assess potential overstretching of the plantar structures, plantar fascia (PF) length was defined as the linear distance between the most plantar-proximal aspect of the first proximal phalanx and the insertion of the plantar fascia at the calcaneal tuberosity (Figure 4). All measurements were obtained using semi-automated WBCT-based 3D analysis software. Patient demographic characteristics were also recorded, including age, body mass index, gender, and laterality. To minimize potential sources of bias, only structurally intact feet without prior surgery or degenerative changes were included. All measurements and simulations were performed using standardized semi-automated software and reviewed by an experienced foot and ankle specialist to ensure consistency.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.6; University of Düsseldorf), based on Fowler-Philip angle values reported in a previous pilot study. 20 Assuming an effect size of f = 1.765, a sample size of 20 feet per group was sufficient to achieve a statistical power of 98% (1–β = 0.98). The power analysis was based on the Fowler-Philip angle, which was considered the primary radiographic outcome. Given the multiple radiographic parameters, osteotomy configurations, and subgroup comparisons, all other analyses were considered exploratory, and their P values were interpreted descriptively rather than as confirmatory hypothesis testing. Descriptive statistics (mean, median, and SD) were calculated for the radiographic measurements. Subgroup analyses were performed according to foot morphology (planus, neutral, and cavus), based on calcaneal inclination angle thresholds, to assess morphology-dependent differences. The normality of variable distributions was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-sided 95% CIs were computed for all mean changes using the t distribution. A paired t test or paired Wilcoxon was used to compare pre- and post-virtual surgery values. Continuous outcomes were compared with analysis of variance (ANOVA), Welch ANOVA, or Kruskal-Wallis tests according to normality distribution of the data. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed with EasyMedStat (version 3.42).

Results

Sixty feet from 60 patients were included in the study, with 20 feet in each morphology subgroup: cavus (n = 20), neutral (n = 20), and planus (n = 20). Of these, 24 were male and 33 corresponded to right feet. The mean age was 48.1 ± 17.6 years, with no statistically significant differences among the 3 subgroups (planus, neutral, and cavus). However, the planus group showed significantly higher body mass index values compared with the other groups (P < .001).

Before virtual ZO, statistically significant differences among subgroups were observed in CIA, HMA, and HAA (P < .001, P = .007, and P < .001, respectively), with lower CIA and higher HMA and HAA values in the planus group, supporting the subgroup stratification based on foot alignment. Detailed baseline values are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

When assessing the impact of wedge inclination within the same hinge position using the measured WBCT parameters, no statistically significant differences were observed for the primary radiographic outcome, the Fowler-Philip angle, within hinge A (configurations 1, 2, and 3), nor within hinge B or C. Similarly, no differences were observed for the other key variables analysed, including CIA, hindfoot moment arm, and X/Y ratio. With P ≈ .99 for all analysed parameters, the 3 wedge inclinations were highly correlated and statistically indistinguishable within each hinge configuration.

In examining whether hinge configuration affects the analysed parameters following virtual ZO, we first observed consistent changes across all configurations, independent of foot morphology. Specifically, the Fowler-Philip angle decreased, whereas the sagittal talocalcaneal angle and the calcaneal X/Y ratio increased significantly in all hinge positions (A, B, and C; all P < .001). When analysing the effect of hinge position, CIA values decreased significantly in hinge configurations B and C (both P ≤ .001). HAA showed a small decrease with hinge B and an increase with hinge C in the absolute-change analysis, while pre- to post-simulation comparisons were statistically significant for both configurations (hinge B P = .001; hinge C P < .001). Subgroup analysis showed that the magnitude of these changes differed across foot morphologies, as reported in Table 1. In the pre- to post-simulation analysis, the HMA increased significantly only in hinge configuration B (P = .03), but not in the flatfoot group, whereas plantar fascia length increased exclusively in hinge configuration C (P < .001), regardless of foot type. No changes were observed in CIA or axial talocalcaneal angle in hinge A, nor in coronal talocalcaneal angle and plantar fascia length in hinges A and B. Subgroup analysis revealed that these changes were not uniformly distributed across foot types. Although pre- to post-simulation changes in CIA and HAA were statistically significant in several subgroup analyses (Supplementary Table 2), their magnitude varied according to baseline foot morphology, with the flatfoot group generally showing smaller absolute corrections. Detailed numerical results, including pre- and post-simulation values for each parameter across hinge configurations and subgroups, are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Morphology-specific absolute changes and between-subgroup comparisons are reported in Table 1.

Finally, having identified which parameters varied according to hinge configuration, we evaluated whether these changes differed significantly by foot type. The calcaneal X/Y ratio showed significant variation with hinge A (P < .001) and hinge B (P = .007), with greater increases observed in neutral feet and, to a lesser extent, in cavus feet compared with the flatfoot group. Moreover, in hinge type C, both CIA and sagittal TC angles were significantly reduced, with the most pronounced effect in the cavus group. No other parameters showed significant morphology-dependent differences according to hinge configuration. The numerical representations of the analysed values can be consulted in Table 1, supporting a morphology-dependent response to hinge positioning.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the primary radiographic outcome, the Fowler-Philip angle, decreased significantly after virtual ZO, with the largest absolute reduction observed with the intermediate hinge position. More broadly, wedge inclination did not significantly affect any radiographic parameter, whereas hinge position had a marked influence on sagittal plane alignment of the calcaneal tuberosity and foot.

Specifically, the most distal hinge position produced the greatest reduction in CIA and sagittal talocalcaneal angle, whereas the intermediate hinge position also produced the greatest increase in calcaneal X/Y ratio. In contrast, the most posterior hinge position induced only modest modifications, with limited impact in the alignment. Importantly, coronal and axial alignment remained largely unaffected regardless of configuration, supporting the predominantly sagittal plane corrective potential of ZO. Some of these findings can be explained by basic geometric principles of rigid body rotation. In this model, a standardized 10° rotation around a fixed plantar hinge largely determines the final position of the posterior calcaneal fragment; therefore, the limited effect of wedge inclination is expected when rotation magnitude and hinge point are kept constant. Similarly, more anterior hinge positions increase the distance between the hinge and posterior tuberosity, producing a larger arc of displacement and explaining the greater reduction in calcaneal inclination angle and the increase in plantar fascia length observed with hinge C. Because the virtual rotation was performed in the sagittal plane around a mediolaterally oriented axis, limited coronal and axial changes were also expected. The added value of WBCT-based 3D simulation is therefore to quantify the absolute magnitude of these effects across clinically relevant radiographic parameters and identify morphotype-specific differences among cavus, neutral, and planus feet, under the assumption that the calcaneus behaves as an approximately rigid body in the simulated model, which remains to be validated against postoperative imaging data.

Building on this quantitative interpretation, the corrective potential of ZO is primarily driven by hinge position and should be interpreted in light of baseline foot morphology. In cavus feet, the most distal hinge position on the calcaneus appeared most effective in reducing CIA and sagittal talocalcaneal angle, favouring a flatter heel profile. In neutral feet, the intermediate hinge position produced balanced corrections in Fowler-Philip angle and calcaneal X/Y ratio, without adverse effects. Conversely, in flatfoot morphology, changes were limited across all hinge positions, underscoring the reduced capacity of ZO to address sagittal malalignment in this subgroup. Overall, these findings demonstrate a hinge-driven and morphology-dependent correction mechanism, supporting the need for morphology-based hinge selection in surgical planning.

Only a limited number of studies have evaluated the radiographic consequences of ZO. Tourne et al 17 reported a mean decrease of 5.5° in CIA and a significant reduction in calcaneal length, associated with favourable functional outcomes in patients with insertional Achilles tendinopathy.

Among prior works on ZO, Syed and Perera 3 were the first to hypothesize that hinge position could modulate calcaneal morphology. They suggested that a posterior hinge (comparable to hinge A in our study) induces anterior rotation of the tuberosity, whereas an anterior hinge (hinge C) elevates the tuberosity, flattens the calcaneus, and reduces calcaneal inclination angle, an effect potentially advantageous in cavo varus feet. They also suggested a linear relationship between wedge closure and tuberosity elevation. However, their contribution remained purely theoretical, lacking quantitative validation. Our findings partially support these theoretical assumptions.

The first true biomechanical analysis was provided by Nunes et al 20 who performed WBCT-based simulations in patients with insertional Achilles tendinopathy. They reported decrease in CIA, a significant reduction in the Fowler-Philip angle, and a shortening of calcaneal length, with changes confined to the sagittal plane. The present work expands on this study by incorporating different baseline foot morphotypes. Our results are in line with observations of Nunes et al 20 supporting the conclusion that ZO predominantly influences sagittal plane alignment while sparing coronal and axial planes. Importantly, our study builds on their work by showing that these effects are highly dependent on hinge location, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of the ZO corrective mechanism. From a clinical standpoint, our findings reinforce the role of ZO as a procedure capable of reducing posterior calcaneal prominence and improving Achilles insertional mechanics. Improvements in Fowler-Philip angle and calcaneal X/Y ratio, particularly with hinge B, are consistent with radiographic targets associated with symptom relief in insertional Achilles tendinopathy. Conversely, the increased plantar fascia length observed with hinge C warrants caution, as excessive elongation may predispose to plantar overload.

This study has several limitations. First, it represents a virtual simulation without clinical correlation to symptoms or functional outcomes. Moreover, as the simulation was based on reconstructed 3D bone models, it allowed assessment of osseous alignment but did not account for soft tissue tension or biomechanical forces across the Achilles tendon. Second, the study population consisted of asymptomatic patients, limiting direct extrapolation to patients with insertional Achilles tendinopathy. Third, a uniform 10° wedge was applied, in contrast with the original dorsal resection described by Nordio et al 30 of 7 to 10 mm; in our model, the 10° wedge corresponded to a mean resection of 6.2 mm. Although this approach was useful for standardization across different calcaneal sizes, it may not fully reflect surgical practice, where wedge size is typically tailored intraoperatively. Finally, high baseline X/Y ratios in our cohort may reduce comparability with conventional radiographic thresholds.

Future research should aim to validate these findings through clinical outcome studies stratified by hinge position and foot morphology. WBCT-based preoperative simulations could serve as valuable planning tools to individualize hinge positioning, optimize correction, and minimize complications. Furthermore, comparative studies between open and percutaneous Zadek techniques should investigate whether hinge-dependent corrections can be reliably reproduced across surgical approaches.

Conclusion

This study suggests that ZO significantly affects the Fowler-Philip angle, the primary radiographic outcome, and that its overall corrective capacity is primarily driven by hinge position, whereas wedge inclination does not significantly influence hindfoot alignment or calcaneal morphology. The effects of the osteotomy are predominantly confined to the sagittal plane.

By systematically quantifying these changes, our findings provide objective evidence that supports a hinge-driven and morphology-dependent correction mechanism. Tailoring hinge position to baseline foot morphology may help optimize surgical planning and represents a step toward more individualized, evidence-based correction strategies. Given the reconstructed 3D bone-model nature of this simulation, further clinical validation including in vivo assessment of soft tissue tension and Achilles tendon loading would be desirable.
