Effects of drying methods on the emulsifying properties of micellar casein powder
Authors: Yang Song, Zhishen Mu, Xuhui Fan, Shengbo Yu, Shuang Wang, Kerong Wang, Hanyu Chai, Zhongyao Du, Weibo Zhang, Pengjie Wang, Yun Chen, Chong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micellar casein powder (MCP), obtained by drying casein micellar solution, has gained attention as a natural functional food ingredient. This study systematically compared the effects of freeze drying (FD), spray drying (SD), and electrostatic spray drying (ESD) on the emulsifying properties of MCP. Emulsions prepared from MCP using different drying methods exhibited significant differences in particle size, zeta potential, and stability. Compared with emulsions prepared with sodium caseinate, the emulsions stabilized with both FD and ESD MCPs had higher stability but larger average particle sizes and lower absolute zeta potential values. The ESD MCP-stabilized emulsion had the highest stability, whereas the SD MCP-stabilized emulsion had the largest particle size and lowest stability. Confocal laser scanning microscopy combined with rheological analysis indicated that all MCP-stabilized emulsions formed a unique weak gel network structure. The ESD MCP-stabilized emulsion showed the highest viscosity and most densely packed protein network, which were consistent with its stability. These findings suggest that the formation of a weak gel structure and high viscosity are crucial in stabilizing MCP-stabilized emulsions. This study provides theoretical and practical insights for the development of high-performance casein-based emulsifiers.

Introduction

Protein-stabilized emulsions, particularly oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions, are a common food system formulation, found in products such as dairy beverages, ice cream, and condiments (McClements and Rao, 2011). Casein is an ideal natural emulsifier in these emulsions because of its unique amphiphilic structure and exceptional surface activity (Han et al., 2023). In milk, casein mainly exists as its natural assembly, casein micelles, which are nanoscale complexes formed by self-assembly of multiple casein isoforms and colloidal calcium phosphate (Qi, 2007). This micellar structure gives casein outstanding emulsifying, foaming, and gelling properties by allowing it to adsorb effectively at the oil-water interface, greatly reducing interfacial tension and increasing the physical stability of emulsions (Yin et al., 2023). Consequently, casein micelles are invaluable in the food industry as emulsifiers, thickeners, and nutritional fortifiers.

Casein micelles typically exist in liquids in their natural state, but this causes problems with storage and transportation. However, advances in drying technology have allowed micellar casein powder (MCP) to emerge as an important functional food base material. MCP is increasingly used across diverse applications because of its advantages of convenient storage, low transport costs, and extended shelf life. In MCP preparation, membrane filtration techniques, such as microfiltration and ultrafiltration, are typically used to separate and remove whey proteins, lactose, and soluble minerals (Carter et al., 2021). The retained liquid is then dried to produce a high-protein powder (Beckman and Barbano, 2013). However, varying physical conditions during drying can induce denaturation and restructuring of the casein micelle architecture, thereby affecting functional performance. For example, spray drying (SD) temperature can strongly affect protein solubility (Abdullah et al., 2021; Hoehne and Gaukel, 2024), whereas spray freeze drying (SFD) has been reported to better preserve micelle structural integrity and hydration properties (Ren et al., 2022). Nevertheless, SFD generally involves atomization into a cryogenic medium followed by sublimation drying (Singh et al., 2024), which increases process complexity, processing time, and energy consumption, potentially limiting its practical scalability for routine dairy powder production (Sartzi et al., 2024). Therefore, selecting an appropriate drying method is essential for tailoring the structural and functional properties of MCP and enhancing its potential value in food applications.

SD is one of the most widely used drying methods for milk protein concentrates because of its high efficiency and industrial scalability. However, SD is energy-intensive, and both its energy consumption and product quality are strongly influenced by processing parameters such as inlet air temperature (Abdullah et al., 2021) and feed concentration (Gawalek, 2022). Previous studies have shown that these conditions can affect protein aggregation, particle morphology, solubility, rehydration behavior, and the functional properties of dairy protein powders (Schuck, 2009). In contrast, freeze drying (FD) removes water through sublimation under low-temperature and vacuum conditions, which is generally beneficial for preserving heat-sensitive protein structures, although its long processing cycle limits its industrial application (Pang et al., 2017). Electrostatic spray drying (ESD) uses high-voltage electrostatic fields to assist atomization and drying at relatively low temperatures, making it suitable for thermosensitive food components and milk protein matrices and shows great potential for industrial applications. (Chutani et al., 2024; Jayaprakash et al., 2023). Chutani et al. (2024) reported that ESD of a milk protein matrix resulted in lower Maillard reaction markers than conventional spray drying, indicating reduced heat-induced chemical modification and better preservation of protein quality. Therefore, different drying technologies may affect protein functionality through distinct mechanisms related to thermal exposure, dehydration pathway, particle formation, and the state of protein aggregation.

As MCP retains the supramolecular structure of native casein micelles, it may serve as a promising protein-based emulsifier in dairy emulsions, high-protein beverages, and nutritional formulations. Although the effects of drying on the structure, solubility, and rehydration behavior of casein micelles have been reported (Beghdadi et al., 2022; Carter et al., 2018; Song et al., 2022), previous studies have primarily focused on changes in physicochemical or powder properties. How these drying-induced changes further affect the emulsifying behavior of MCPs and the underlying mechanisms of emulsion stabilization remains to be clarified.

In the present work, O/W emulsions were prepared by ultrasonic emulsification using sodium caseinate (SC) or MCPs produced by SD, FD, and ESD as emulsifiers, with the aim of comparing the effects of different drying methods on the emulsifying properties of MCPs. The particle size, zeta potential, microstructure, rheological properties, and storage stability of the resulting emulsions were characterized to systematically evaluate the emulsifying properties of MCPs produced by different drying methods. The novelty of this study lies in clarifying the stabilization mechanisms of MCP-based emulsions from structural and rheological perspectives, thereby linking drying-induced changes in MCP structure with interfacial adsorption, network formation, and emulsion stability. This work provides insight into the structure-function relationship of MCP as a protein-based emulsifier and offers theoretical and technical guidance for selecting suitable drying processes for specific emulsion applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Skimmed milk was provided by Inner Mongolia Mengniu Dairy Group Co., Ltd. (China). Sodium caseinate (SC, ≥95%, bovine) and ProClin™ 950 biocide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) standard solutions (1.0 M) were purchased from Bolinda Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Food-grade corn oil was purchased from Yihai Kerry Group (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of MCPs

The MCPs were prepared from a single batch of skimmed milk. The skimmed milk was microfiltered using a membrane filtration system equipped with a ceramic membrane (0.1 μm; ISOFLUX, TAMI Industries, France) at 50 °C and a transmembrane pressure of 0.12 MPa until a volume concentration factor (VCF) of 3 was reached, yielding a casein micelle concentrate (Carter et al., 2021). The concentrate was then dried using three methods. For FD, a 15% (w/w) sample solution was frozen and lyophilized at −50 °C for 72 h using a vacuum freeze dryer (CHRIST Epsilon 2-10D, Germany). For SD, a 15% (w/w) feed was dried using a spray dryer (GEA Niro Mobile, Denmark) at an inlet air temperature of 190 °C and an outlet air temperature of 100 °C. For ESD, a 20% (w/w) feed was dried using an electrostatic spray dryer (ESP-5, Dasheng Innovation Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) with a feed temperature of 70 °C, inlet air temperature of 80 °C, and outlet air temperature of 50 °C (Beaupeux et al., 2024). These drying conditions were selected based on preliminary trials and previously reported conditions for dairy protein powders, aiming to ensure sufficient dehydration while minimizing excessive protein aggregation and thermal damage (Carter et al., 2018; Chutani et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2022; Song et al., 2022). The resulting powders were denoted FD MCP, SD MCP, and ESD MCP, respectively, and stored in sealed containers at −20 °C until analysis.

Preparation of casein micelle solutions

The MCPs and SC were separately dissolved in deionized water, and the solutions were adjusted to pH 7.0, stirred magnetically at 55 °C for 1 h and then stirred at 25 °C until completely dissolved to obtain a 1% mass concentration casein micelle solution. To inhibit microbial growth, 0.1% ProClin™ 950 preservative was added. The solution was stored overnight at 4 °C to ensure thorough hydration.

Particle size and zeta potential of casein micelles

The particle size of the casein micelle solution (prepared from MCPs) was measured at 25 °C. Samples were diluted 100-fold and analyzed using a four-sided transparent cuvette on a particle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano-ZS 90, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK).

The zeta potential values of the diluted samples were evaluated at 25 °C, using Zetasizer Nano ZS 90 Malvern with zeta potential cell. Subsequently, approximately 1 mL of sample was added to the sample cell for testing, with a premeasurement equilibration period of 120 s.

Microstructural observation of casein micelles

The reconstituted casein micelle dispersion was applied to a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) copper grid and air-dried at room temperature. The samples were observed using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1200, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at a voltage of 100 kV. Images were acquired at various magnification levels to analyze the morphological and structural features of the casein micelles.

Emulsion preparation

The emulsions were prepared by ultrasonic homogenization according to Shi et al. (2023) with slight modifications. The casein micelle solutions prepared in Section 2.3 were mixed with food-grade corn oil at an aqueous phase-to-oil phase ratio of 3:1 (v/v) (Liu et al., 2023). The mixture was pre-emulsified using a rotor-stator homogenizer (T18 digital ULTRA TURRAX, IKA, Staufen, Germany) at 10,000 rpm for 7 s at 25 °C to obtain a coarse emulsion before ultrasonication (Yang and Cheng, 2024). The total volume of the pre-emulsion was 20 mL. Ultrasonic emulsification was then performed using an ultrasonic homogenizer (SCIENTZ-IID, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China) equipped with a titanium probe (amplitude transformer 6) at an output power of 300 W. The total elapsed treatment time was 2 min, and an intermittent cycle of 3 s on and 5 s off was used to obtain a homogeneous O/W emulsion. The ultrasonic energy density was calculated using equation (1).(1)E=P×teff/Vwhere E is the ultrasonic energy density (J/mL), P is the ultrasonic output power (W), teff is the effective sonication time (s), and V is the emulsion volume (mL). Considering the intermittent mode, the effective sonication time was 45 s, and the corresponding ultrasonic energy density was 675 J/mL. The resulting emulsions were allowed to equilibrate for 30 min before further analysis.

Emulsion particle size

The droplet size was determined using a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern, UK). After ultrasonic emulsification, 1 mL of each emulsion was slowly added to a sample cell containing deionized water (500 mL) to maintain an optical density of 10%-20% and a refractive index of 1.436. Measurements were taken with steady stirring at 2000 rpm. The results are expressed as the surface-area mean diameter (D[3,2]) (Ellouze et al., 2019).

Emulsion zeta potential

The zeta-potential of the emulsions prepared in Section 2.6 was measured at 25 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS 90 (Malvern, UK), as per the method detailed in Section 2.4.

Emulsion stability analysis

The stabilities of O/W emulsions stabilized by casein micelle powders prepared using different drying methods were evaluated by static multiple light scattering (Celia et al., 2009). Measurements were performed using a stability analyzer (Turbiscan LAB, Formulaction, Toulouse, France). Approximately 20 mL of each emulsion was placed in a cylindrical glass vial and scanned from bottom to top every 10 min for 24 h at 25 °C. Because the samples were opaque, the emulsion stabilities at different time points were determined by monitoring the change in the percentage of reflected light over time.

The Turbiscan instability index (TSI) of the emulsion system was calculated using the built-in software on the instrument with equation (2).(2)TSI=∑i=1n(xi‐xBS)2n‐1where xi is the backscattered light (BS) intensity of the emulsion system obtained from a single measurement at a specific height, xBS is the average value of xi after scanning the emulsion system from top to bottom, and n is the total number of scans.

Emulsion microstructure

The microstructures of the emulsion samples were examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; TCS SP8, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). A dual fluorescence staining scheme of Nile red and Fast Green was adopted (Swaminathan et al., 2021). Lipids were stained with Nile Red (1 mg/mL in ethanol), and proteins were labeled with Fast Green (1 mg/mL in distilled water). The same volume of the emulsion sample was placed on a glass slide, followed by sequential addition of the two fluorescent dyes. The sample was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min and then covered with a coverslip. Fluorescence imaging was performed using excitation wavelengths of 488 and 633 nm (Raudsepp et al., 2014) with a 20× objective lens to visualize the emulsion microstructure on the first day of preparation and after 50 days of storage under ambient conditions. Droplet size distribution was analyzed using ImageJ software to evaluate changes in emulsion stability. For each sample, images were acquired from three randomly selected fields of view (n = 3), and a total of 60 droplets were measured for quantitative analysis.

Shear rheological measurement

The viscosity of the emulsions was measured using an AR1500ex rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) with parallel-plate geometry (60 mm diameter) at 25 °C (Xie et al., 2023). After a 30 s equilibration period, flow curves were obtained by sweeping the shear rate from 0.1 to 100 s−1.

Interfacial tension

Interfacial tension between the casein micelle solutions and corn oil was measured using a contact angle system (Data Physics OCA 25, Audelino, Beijing, China) via the hanging drop method (Amine et al., 2014). The casein micelle solution was added to the sample cell. An automatic dispensing system formed an oil droplet at the tip of a syringe needle suspended in the aqueous phase. The test was conducted at 25 °C for 3 h. The interfacial tension was automatically calculated based on the droplet profile captured by a charge-coupled device camera using the Young-Laplace equation (3).(3)γ(1R1+1R2)=ΔP=ΔP0‐Δρgzwhere R1 and R2 are the principal radii of curvature; ΔP=Pin-Pout is the Laplace pressure across the interface; Δρ=ρd‐ρ is the density difference; and ρd and ρ are the drop phase density and continuous phase density respectively. This can be written in terms of a reference pressure ΔP0atz=0 and a hydrostatic pressure Δρgz (Berry et al., 2015).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times. The corresponding data were recorded as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences between samples were analyzed using SPSS software with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Particle size of casein micelles

It has been reported that SC exists primarily as dissociated casein molecules and self-associated aggregates rather than intact native casein micelles (Cheng et al., 2022). Its apparent particle size varies widely depending on pH, ionic strength, calcium concentration, protein concentration, and measurement method. For example, Nash et al. (2002) reported that the typical size range of nanoaggregates formed by SC at pH 6.3 was 50-80 nm, while other studies have shown that caseinate aggregates may range from several to tens of nanometers to larger aggregates exceeding 100 nm (Cheng et al., 2022; Lucey et al., 2000).

In this study, the protein dispersions exhibited significant differences in particle size (Fig. 1A). Among the MCP samples, the SD sample showed the largest average particle size (∼330 nm), which was likely related to drying-induced structural rearrangement and micellar aggregation under thermal stress (Arzuaga et al., 2022). In contrast, the FD and ESD samples exhibited smaller particle sizes (180-200 nm), comparable to those of native casein micelles. The ESD sample had the smallest particle size among the MCP samples, which may be attributed to reduced thermal exposure and rapid drying during electrostatic spray drying, thereby limiting protein-protein interactions that promote micellar aggregation (Zhang et al., 2025).Fig. 1Structural characteristics of MCPs prepared by different drying methods. Structural characteristics of MCPs prepared by different drying methods. (A) Particle size; (B) Zeta potential; and (C) TEM micrographs of SD-, FD-, and ESD-MCPs.

Zeta potential of casein micelles

The zeta potential of the dispersion systems for MCPs produced by different drying methods exhibited significant differences (Fig. 1B). All samples had a negative charge at neutral pH, indicating that negatively charged amino acids predominated over positively charged amino acids on the casein micelle surface. The absolute zeta potential values of all MCP dispersions exceeded 15 mV, indicating a dynamically stable system (Greenwood, 2003). Notably, the FD and ESD samples exhibited zeta potentials around −22 mV, which aligns closely with values reported for native casein micelles in suspension (around −22.4 mV, pH 6.92) (Zhang et al., 2025), suggesting better preservation of the natural surface charge. In contrast, the SD sample showed a lower absolute zeta potential (−17 mV), a pattern consistent with studies reporting that conventional spray drying can reduce surface charge due to thermal-induced conformational changes and aggregation (Choi et al., 2025).

Microstructure of casein micelles

TEM observations (Fig. 1C) show that the drying methods strongly affected the microstructure of MCPs. Although all MCPs retained the fundamental spherical micelle morphology, they exhibited marked variations in structural integrity. SD treatment induced noticeable micelle aggregation, consistent with literature reports that spray drying often leads to particle fusion in dairy proteins (Ren et al., 2022). FD samples retained relatively intact structures, aligning with the recognized ability of freeze-drying to preserve the native morphology of heat-sensitive colloids (Bhatta et al., 2020). In contrast, ESD samples exhibited the most native-like micellar structure, with clearly defined and dispersed subunits, indicating that the low-temperature, low-shear process best preserved the original protein conformation. This structural advantage is in agreement with the superior morphological conservation reported for ESD when processing other sensitive protein systems compared to high-temperature methods (Zhang et al., 2025). These structural differences directly demonstrate the impact of different drying processes on protein conformation.

Emulsion particle size

The average particle size of emulsions stabilized by MCPs prepared via different drying methods is shown in Fig. 2A. The SC-stabilized emulsion exhibited the smallest average particle size (D[3,2]) of approximately 2.5 μm. Conversely, the SD MCP-stabilized emulsion displayed the largest particle size of approximately 6.8 μm. The FD and ESD MCP-stabilized emulsions fell between these two values (3.5 − 4.0 μm). This trend aligns with the micelle particle size data and is consistent with studies reporting that the size and structural integrity of protein-based emulsifiers directly determine the droplet size of the resulting emulsions (Gonzalez-Jordan et al., 2016). According to Stokes’ law, the sedimentation rate of droplets is proportional to the square of their diameter (Ding et al., 2023). Consequently, smaller droplet sizes imply slower phase separation behavior and higher emulsion stability (Miyagawa et al., 2015).Fig. 2Properties of emulsions stabilized by SC and MCPs. Emulsion zeta potential

Zeta potential is an indicator of the overall surface charge of particles, providing insights into particle aggregation and stability in emulsions (Zhao et al., 2022). Emulsions stabilized by MCPs prepared by different drying methods showed significant differences in zeta potential (Fig. 2B). The SC-stabilized emulsion showed the most negative zeta potential value (−52.28 ± 0.83 mV), which is consistent with the well-documented strong negative charge of caseinate/casein-based systems at neutral pH (de Paula et al., 2025). In contrast, emulsions stabilized by MCPs exhibited less negative zeta potential values. Specifically, the zeta potential values of the FD-, ESD-, and SD-MCP-stabilized emulsions were −26.93 ± 0.67 mV, −27.77 ± 0.36 mV, and −29.38 ± 1.11 mV, respectively. Although these values were lower in magnitude than that of the SC-stabilized emulsion, the absolute zeta potential values of all MCP-stabilized emulsions exceeded 25 mV. This threshold is widely recognized as indicative of good electrostatic stability in colloidal systems (Midekessa et al., 2020; Tirgarian et al., 2022). The results indicated that the emulsion droplets carried appreciable net surface charges, which could enhance electrostatic repulsion among droplets and thereby help suppress flocculation and coalescence, contributing to improved physical stability of the emulsions (Yan et al., 2022).

Emulsion stability

The real-time stability of MCP-stabilized emulsions was monitored with a stability analyzer (Fig. 3A and B). BS intensity is positively correlated with the concentration of the dispersed phase, and phase separation behavior in an emulsion is quantitatively assessed by scanning BS changes at different heights in a sample tube (Huck-Iriart et al., 2016). During the 24-h monitoring period, all emulsion systems exhibited a significant decrease in BS values in the bottom and middle layers, whereas BS values in the top layer increased markedly (Fig. 3A). These changes indicated droplet aggregation and upward migration, which decreased the proportions of the dispersed phase in the lower layers and increased it in the upper layers. This trend was particularly pronounced in the SD MCP- and SC-stabilized emulsions. In contrast, the overall BS variation was small in the ESD and FD MCP-stabilized emulsions, indicating lower droplet migration and aggregation, and thus highest physical stability of the emulsions.Fig. 3Stability of emulsions stabilized by SC and MCPs. (A) Changes in the backscattering intensity profiles of emulsions within 24 h; (B) Turbiscan instability index (TSI) curves.

Stability of emulsions stabilized by SC and MCPs. (A) Changes in the backscattering intensity profiles of emulsions within 24 h; (B) Turbiscan instability index (TSI) curves.

The TSI, which is a key indicator for quantitatively evaluating emulsion stability, was obtained by fitting BS photodynamic data. A higher index value indicates greater phase separation or aggregation in the system, and thus poorer emulsion stability (Almeida et al., 2024). The TSI values for emulsions stabilized by MCPs prepared via different drying methods decreased in the order SD > SC > FD > ESD (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that SD samples exhibited the poorest stability, whereas ESD samples had the lowest TSI value and optimal physical stability. This trend was consistent with the BS intensity analysis and particle size measurements in MCP-stabilized emulsions. The FD and ESD MCP-stabilized emulsions both exhibited significantly higher stability than those stabilized by the commercially available emulsifier SC. This demonstrates that FD and particularly ESD preserve the structural integrity of casein better, thereby improving its emulsifying functional properties.

Theoretically, emulsions stabilized by SC should exhibit the highest stability because they have the highest absolute zeta potential and smallest emulsion particle size. However, the experimental results indicate that their actual stability is lower than those of emulsions stabilized by MCPs produced via ESD or FD. This result can be explained by the depletion flocculation effect induced by high concentrations of linear macromolecules in SC emulsions (Grundy et al., 2018). Under this mechanism, even in the presence of strong electrostatic repulsion, the entropic driving force generated by non-adsorbed polymers induces attraction between droplets, thereby accelerating aggregation and phase separation.

Emulsion microstructure

CLSM images revealed the microstructural characteristics of the emulsion droplet distribution and interfacial proteins (Fig. 4A). Following dual staining with Nile red (oil phase) and Fast Green (proteins), the images demonstrate that the MCP-stabilized emulsions exhibited a characteristic weak gel network structure. The red oil droplets were tightly encapsulated by a green fluorescent protein layer, forming a continuous three-dimensional network consistent with the protein-stabilized milk gel properties reported in the literature (C. H. Liu et al., 2023). The ESD MCP-stabilized emulsion exhibited the most compact and uniform network, which restricted droplet migration and aggregation. In contrast, although the SC-stabilized emulsion contained fine droplets, its protein distribution was discontinuous and fragmented and did not form a complete spatial network. Localized oil droplet flocculation was observed, indicating poorer structural stability, which may have been caused by depletion flocculation induced by high SC concentrations (Li et al., 2021).Fig. 4Microstructure and storage stability of emulsions stabilized by SC and MCPs. (A) CLSM micrographs of fresh emulsions; (B) CLSM micrographs after 50 days of storage; and changes in droplet size distributions of SD-emulsion (C), FD-emulsion (D), ESD-emulsion (E), and SC-emulsion (F) during 50 days of storage.

Microstructure and storage stability of emulsions stabilized by SC and MCPs. (A) CLSM micrographs of fresh emulsions; (B) CLSM micrographs after 50 days of storage; and changes in droplet size distributions of SD-emulsion (C), FD-emulsion (D), ESD-emulsion (E), and SC-emulsion (F) during 50 days of storage.

The emulsion stability results showed that FD- and ESD-stabilized emulsions demonstrated superior overall performance compared with the SC-stabilized emulsion. Weak gelation altered the spatial distribution of the aqueous and lipid phases, mainly confining oil droplets through protein-coated oil phases, thereby inhibiting droplet migration and aggregation. In contrast, the SC-stabilized emulsion relied on interfacial adsorption stabilization mechanisms. Although the SC-stabilized emulsion contained fine droplets, the absence of a continuous gel network support made the emulsion susceptible to depletion flocculation induced by entropic forces at high concentrations, leading to reduced stability. The SD MCP-stabilized emulsion exhibited the lowest stability because of its structural heterogeneity, larger droplets, and increased aggregation. Thus, different interactions among emulsion microstructures alter the emulsion stability (Huck-Iriart et al., 2016).

Emulsion storage stability

The initial emulsions were stored at room temperature under light-protected conditions for 50 days. Changes in droplet size were observed using CLSM (Fig. 4B), and quantitative analysis of the microstructural images was performed using ImageJ to calculate the Feret diameter of the oil droplets (Fig. 4C–F). After 50 days of storage, the average droplet size increased in all emulsions, indicating an inevitable tendency for droplet aggregation during storage. The primary mechanism probably involved Ostwald ripening (Koroleva and Yurtov, 2021). Notably, the emulsion prepared with ESD MCP-stabilized emulsion (Fig. 4E) showed the smallest increase in droplet size (from approximately 6.42 μm to 7.84 μm), followed by FD MCP (Fig. 4D) and SC-stabilized emulsions (Fig. 4F), while SD MCP-stabilized emulsion (Fig. 4C) exhibited the most pronounced growth. This stability hierarchy can be attributed to the superior preservation of the native micellar structure by ESD. The intact micellar structure forms a denser and more elastic protein layer at the oil-water interface, which effectively resists droplet flocculation and coalescence (Beaupeux et al., 2024).

Apparent viscosity

The rheological properties of emulsions, particularly apparent viscosity, are a key determinant of their physical stability (Xie et al., 2023). The viscosities of SC- and MCP-stabilized emulsions prepared by different drying methods are shown in Fig. 5. Notably, irregular fluctuations in apparent viscosity were observed at low shear rates (from 1 to 10 s−1). This non-ideal response is common in concentrated emulsions with a gel-like protein network and can be attributed to the competition between shear-induced disruption and the rapid structural reformation of the weak, yet elastic, interfacial protein layer (Jiang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2022). The ESD MCP-stabilized emulsion displayed the most pronounced viscoelastic solid-like character (highest initial viscosity) and the most stable flow profile among the samples at these low shear rates. This indicates a more cohesive and resilient interfacial network, which aligns with the dense, continuous protein matrix observed via CLSM. This continuous matrix formed by high viscosity suppressed droplet migration, aggregation, and Ostwald ripening (Zhang et al., 2024), revealing the intrinsic mechanism underpinning the optimal stability of ESD MCP-stabilized emulsions.Fig. 5Apparent viscosity of emulsions stabilized by SC and MCPs as a function of shear rate.

Interfacial tension

Interfacial tension reflects the emulsifying capacity of surfactants: a lower value indicates a greater ability to reduce oil-water interfacial energy, resulting in superior emulsifying properties (Ho et al., 2022). SC exhibited the lowest interfacial tension (Fig. 6), which is consistent with the observation of the smallest stable emulsion droplets in the particle size analysis (Fig. 2A). This can be attributed to the abundance of flexible, low-molecular-weight caseinate molecules in SC, which rapidly adsorb and unfold at the interface, effectively displacing oil molecules and lowering interfacial tension (Kheynoor et al., 2025; Perugini et al., 2018). However, this system had poor storage stability, probably because of the depletion flocculation mechanisms induced by high concentrations of free casein (Grundy et al., 2018).Fig. 6Dynamic interfacial tension of SC and MCP dispersions prepared by different drying methods at the oil-water interface.

Dynamic interfacial tension of SC and MCP dispersions prepared by different drying methods at the oil-water interface.

In contrast, the SD, FD, and ESD MCP dispersions showed no significant differences in interfacial tension (Fig. 6). This similarity suggests that the core mechanism for interfacial tension reduction by casein micelles is largely preserved across drying methods. Consequently, surface activity was not the main factor causing the stability differences among the MCP-stabilized emulsions.

Conclusions

This study compared the effects of SD, FD, and ESD on the emulsifying properties of MCP. The drying methods significantly affected the particle size, zeta potential, microstructure, rheological behavior, and stability of MCP-stabilized emulsions. Among the three drying methods, ESD produced MCP with the smallest particle size, and the corresponding emulsion exhibited the best physical stability. Emulsions prepared from all three MCPs formed weak gel-like network structures, while the ESD-MCP-stabilized emulsion showed higher viscosity and a denser protein network, which contributed to its enhanced stability. The core contribution of this work is to establish a structure-function relationship between drying-induced protein states and interfacial stabilization behavior. These findings provide practical guidance for selecting suitable drying strategies to produce MCPs with improved emulsifying functionality for dairy-based emulsions, high-protein beverages, and nutritional formulations. Future studies should further evaluate the long-term storage stability, digestion behavior, sensory quality, and scale-up feasibility of MCPs prepared using different drying technologies.
