Integrative analysis of T cell subset-specific ICOS expression and tumor HLA class I/II expression in lung adenocarcinoma: implications for their interaction and clinical outcome
Authors: Hiroyuki Yamada, Rin Yamada, Yoshihiro Komohara, Cheng Pan, Daiki Yoshii, Hiromu Yano, Eri Matsubara, Yusuke Shinchi, Hirotake Tsukamoto, Yukio Fujiwara, Koei Ikeda, Makoto Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesInducible T cell costimulator (ICOS) is a CD28-family costimulatory receptor with bidirectional therapeutic effects on antitumor immunity, and HLA-mediated tumor recognition is essential for effective T cell responses. However, their clinicopathological significance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is not fully understood. We investigated subset-specific pattern of ICOS expression and examined whether ICOS+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density and tumor HLA expression provide immune context and prognostic information in LUAD.Materials and MethodsWe examined 228 resected LUADs by immunohistochemistry. ICOS+ TIL density and HLA class I and HLA-DR status were compared with the densities of CD8+, CD3+CD8−, and FOXP3+ TILs and with postsurgical outcomes. Subset-specific ICOS expression was evaluated using multiplex pseudocolored immunohistochemistry in 10 representative cases selected from the ICOS+ TIL-high group and validated using a publicly available single-cell RNA-seq dataset.ResultsICOS+ TIL density correlated with each T cell subset. Multiplex analysis showed the highest ICOS positivity among Tregs, followed by CD4+ non-Tregs and CD8+ TILs. Because CD4+ non-Tregs were numerically predominant, ICOS+CD4+ non-Tregs constituted the largest ICOS+ fraction. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis corroborated these findings. ICOS+ TIL density was significantly higher in HLA-DR-strong tumors, and tumor HLA class I and HLA-DR were associated with longer recurrence-free survival. ICOS had no overall prognostic impact; however, among HLA-DR-strong tumors, ICOS-high patients tended toward longer cancer-specific survival.ConclusionIn LUAD, ICOS+ TIL density largely reflects ICOS+CD4+ non-Treg infiltration and is enriched in HLA-DR-strong tumors. Tumor HLA expression was associated with favorable prognosis, and ICOS may have context-dependent clinical significance that warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04327-w.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Inducible T cell costimulator, CD278, HLA class I, HLA class II, Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Although surgical resection is the most effective curative option, many patients are diagnosed at unresectable, advanced stages, despite improved detection by computed tomography screening [2]. Along with conventional nonsurgical treatments such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and molecular targeted therapy, treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has increased markedly in recent years [3]. In particular, blockade of programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) and its ligand, PD-L1, is now a standard treatment for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [4]. Nevertheless, only a subset of patients respond, with typical overall response rates of approximately 15–40% for NSCLC. To increase the proportion of patients who benefit from ICI therapy, additional immune targets and predictive biomarkers must be identified, and a more detailed understanding of antitumor immune mechanisms is needed.

Inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS, CD278) is a CD28-family costimulatory receptor induced after T cell receptor activation across T cell lineages, and its ligand, ICOSL (B7-H2/CD275), is normally expressed on antigen-presenting cells [5]. ICOS exerts dual, subset-dependent effects in antitumor immunity, including interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor alpha induction in effector T cells (Teff) while reinforcing immunosuppression via IL-10-producing regulatory T cells (Tregs) [6]. Constitutive ICOS expression in intratumoral Tregs is well established and correlated with tumor progression, in line with evidence indicating that Treg-specific ICOS depletion improves the Teff to Treg balance and enhances Teff cytotoxicity [7, 8]. ICOS also facilitates antitumor activity, as PD-1+ICOS+CD4+ cells are enriched among CD4+ T-helper (Th) tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) for tumor reactivity in human solid tumors [9]. Based on these observations, both agonistic and antagonistic ICOS-targeting antibodies are being tested in clinical trials in patients with solid cancers, including NSCLC [10]. However, only a few studies have specifically examined ICOS+ TILs in NSCLC; thus, their clinical significance remains unclear. In particular, there are no reports that clearly quantify the ICOS+ TIL density in NSCLC.

The recognition of tumor antigen-derived peptides presented by HLA class I by cytotoxic CD8+ T cells is essential for tumor cell elimination in adaptive immunity. In NSCLC, loss of heterozygosity in HLA class I is a common phenomenon and is associated with a high subclonal neoantigen burden and increased immune activity, suggesting an adaptive immune escape mechanism [11]. Although HLA class II is typically expressed on antigen-presenting cells, they are also frequently expressed in NSCLC [12]. HLA class II have mechanisms that allow the presentation of endogenous antigens, and tumor antigens presented through this pathway can be recognized by tumor-specific CD4+ Th cells [13, 14]. Notably, the PD-1+ICOS+CD4+ T cells described above also spatially correlate with tumor HLA class II expression, suggesting a role for ICOS in local antigen recognition.

The present study revealed that ICOS exhibits a T cell subset-specific expression pattern in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). We then examined how the density of ICOS+ TILs relates to tumor HLA class I and class II expression and assessed the prognostic impact of each factor, exploring a potential interaction between ICOS and tumor antigen presentation in LUAD.

Materials and methods

Samples

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues from 228 patients with LUAD who underwent surgical resection at Kumamoto University Hospital between 2010 and 2013 were analyzed using immunohistochemistry (IHC). The study used tissue microarrays previously constructed by our group, in which the regions of interest were restricted to tumor areas [15].

Single and double IHC

IHC was performed on paraffin-embedded sections using the following primary antibodies: anti-ICOS (clone D1K2T; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-CD3 (clone SP7; Nichirei, Tokyo, Japan), anti-CD8 (clone C8/144B; Nichirei), anti-FOXP3 (clone 236A/E7; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-HLA-A/B/C (clone EMR8-5; Medical & Biological Laboratories, Tokyo, Japan), and anti-HLA-DR (clone TAL1B5; Dako, Agilent Technologies). Sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse #424,131, anti-rabbit #R24141; Histofine, Nichirei) and visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (#425,011; Nichirei). For double staining of CD3 and CD8, sections were first stained for CD3 using DAB and then heat treated in 1 mmol/L EDTA buffer (pH 8.0), followed by CD8 staining and visualization using HistoGreen substrate (#AYS-E109; Linaris, Heidelberg, Germany). Because CD4 is expressed on both T lymphocytes and macrophages, interpretation of staining in tumor tissue can be complicated; therefore, we identified T cells by staining for CD3 and CD8 and regarded CD3+/CD8− cells as CD4+ T cells. Slides were digitized for subsequent analysis using a NanoZoomer S20 whole-slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan). For all markers except ICOS, we re-analyzed per-case IHC data from our previous studies [16, 17].

Cell counting and criteria for assessing tumor HLA class I and HLA-DR

Cell counting and HLA class I/HLA-DR assessment were performed on digitized slides. For each case, four non-overlapping areas of 0.81 mm2 were randomly selected. Detection of nuclei, cell segmentation, setting of chromogenic intensity thresholds, and subsequent counting of TILs were carried out using HALO (version 4.0.5107.318; Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA). To avoid inappropriate dilution of cell density, residual air spaces were identified using HALO and excluded from the denominator area. Tumor cell expression of HLA class I and HLA-DR was scored based on staining intensity. Because immune cells are clearly positive for HLA class I and HLA-DR is commonly expressed on macrophages, these cells were used as internal positive controls, and tumor areas with weaker staining were judged as reduced. Staining intensity was graded on a 0–2 scale (2, strong; 1, reduced; 0, negative). When staining was heterogeneous, an intermediate value (e.g., 1.5 for strong/reduced) was assigned. Patients were then classified according to HLA class I as follows: loss, 0 or 0.5; reduced, 1; strong, 1.5 or 2. Because tumor expression is generally considered inducible, the categories for HLA-DR were as follows: negative, 0 or 0.5; weak, 1; strong, 1.5 or 2. All HALO parameter settings and HLA evaluations were performed by H.Y. and independently verified by K.Y., both of whom were blinded to the clinical data.

Multiplex pseudocolored IHC

For 10 cases exhibiting high ICOS+ infiltration, consecutive staining for ICOS, CD3, CD8, and FOXP3 was performed on the same slide. In this procedure, 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC; Nichirei, Tokyo, Japan) was used as the chromogen instead of DAB, and the initial staining steps were identical to those described in Sect. "Single and double IHC" above. After each round of antibody staining, destaining and antibody stripping were carried out according to a previously reported method [18]. Briefly, slides were mounted using a water-soluble medium, scanned, decoverslipped in Tris-buffered saline (TBS), dehydrated through an alcohol series to 95% ethanol, and kept in 95% ethanol until the AEC reaction product disappeared. Slides were then rehydrated through 70% ethanol and transferred to the appropriate antigen retrieval buffer, followed by microwave irradiation at full power until boiling. After cooling and two TBS washes, the next round of staining was started, beginning with blocking. Pseudocolored images showing the staining intensities of hematoxylin and AEC were generated using the color deconvolution function of HALO, and multilayered composite images were generated by fusing the pseudocolored images from each staining cycle. Cell counting was performed as described in Sect. "Cell counting and criteria for assessing tumor HLA class I and HLA-DR" above, except that air spaces were not excluded in this analysis.

Single-cell RNA-seq

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data from a LUAD cohort (GSE131907; 11 patients) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus [19]. Data processing was performed using R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with Seurat (version 5.1.0) and Harmony for batch correction [20, 21]. Low-quality cells (≤ 200 or ≥ 5000 detected genes, or ≥ 15% mitochondrial reads) were removed. Each sample was converted to a Seurat object and merged using the merge function, followed by layer integration (JoinLayers), normalization (NormalizeData), and identification of variable features (FindVariableFeatures). Gene expression was scaled using ScaleData, and batch effects were regressed using RunHarmony with orig.ident as the covariate. Principal component analysis (15 principal components) was carried out for dimensionality reduction and clustering, followed by FindNeighbors and FindClusters at a resolution of 1.0, which yielded 25 clusters. Clusters annotated as T cells were then subsetted and subjected to reference-based, per-cell annotation using the Azimuth workflow to distinguish CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and Tregs [22]. These T cell subsets were further refined using the ProjecTILs pipeline, which projects query cells onto a tumor-infiltrating T cell reference to assign functional states [23].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.0). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess associations between continuous variables. For comparisons between two groups, Fisher’s exact test was applied to categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to continuous variables. For comparisons among three related groups in the multiplex analysis, the nonparametric Friedman test was used, followed by post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. Survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank tests. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

ICOS expression is predominantly associated with CD3+CD8−and FOXP3+TILs

Patients were stratified into ICOS-low (n = 114) and ICOS-high (n = 114) groups according to the median ICOS+ TIL density. Staining results for representative cases from each group are shown in Fig. 1a. Investigating the association between ICOS+ TIL density and individual T cell markers revealed that ICOS+ TIL density was correlated with the densities of FOXP3+ TILs and CD3+CD8− cells and more weakly correlated with CD8+ TIL density (Fig. 1b). ICOS+ TIL density was positively associated with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutant status but not other clinicopathological variables (Table 1). EGFR gene status was not associated with the densities of the other three T cell subsets (supplementary Fig. 1). Regarding prognosis, a higher density of FOXP3+ TILs, CD3+CD8− TILs, or ICOS+ TILs was not associated with postsurgical outcomes, whereas a higher CD8+ TIL density was associated with longer recurrence-free survival (RFS) (supplementary Fig. 2).

Most common ICOS-expressing cells are CD4+non-Tregs, whereas subset-level positivity is highest in Tregs

Given the differential associations between ICOS+ TIL density and individual T cell subsets, we investigated the frequency of ICOS+ cells in each T cell subset using multiplex IHC analysis of 10 selected cases from the ICOS-high group. Among these cases, five were EGFR wild-type, four were EGFR mutant, and one case lacked EGFR mutation data, with no apparent imbalance in EGFR mutation status. Figure 2a shows a representative pseudocolored multiplex image. In this analysis, CD3+CD8−FOXP3− cells and CD3+CD8−FOXP3+ cells were regarded as CD4+ non-Tregs and Tregs, respectively. ICOS positivity within each subset was highest among Tregs, followed by CD4+ non-Tregs and then CD8+ T cells (median 81.6% [70.0–91.5], 41.3% [31.6–54.5], and 24.4% [20.3–34.0], respectively), and the differences were statistically significant (Fig. 2b). As shown in Fig. 2c, CD4+ non-Tregs constituted the predominant intratumoral T cell population, whereas Tregs and CD8+ T cells accounted for smaller proportions. Reflecting this composition, ICOS+CD4+ non-Tregs formed the largest ICOS+ fraction within tumor tissues, followed by ICOS+ Tregs and ICOS+CD8+ T cells (median 62.5% [53.6–71.3], 17.2% [11.3–20.9], and 14.1% [9.1–27.9], respectively) (Fig. 2d), despite the higher frequency of ICOS+ cells among Tregs.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis shows subset-variable ICOS expression consistent with IHC findings

To validate the IHC findings at the transcriptomic level, we analyzed a publicly available LUAD scRNA-seq dataset. Figure 3a shows the results of clustering of 43,742 cells from 11 patients. According to the clinical information provided in the original publication of the scRNA-seq dataset, six cases were EGFR wild-type, four were EGFR mutant, and one case lacked EGFR mutation data. A total of 19,476 T cells were extracted from this cluster and subjected to reference-based, per-cell annotation. When cells with a nonzero ICOS count were defined as ICOS+, CD4+ non-Tregs constituted the largest proportion of ICOS+ T cells (Fig. 3b). Both the ICOS expression level and proportion of ICOS-expressing cells followed the same pattern observed in the multiplex IHC analysis, being highest in Tregs and lowest in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3c). These observations supported the IHC results. Furthermore, among CD4+ T cells, ICOS expression appeared to be linked not only to Tregs but also to T follicular helper-like and exhausted cytotoxic CD4+ states, whereas among CD8+ T cells, ICOS expression was confined primarily to terminally exhausted cells (Fig. 3d).

Tumor HLA-DR-strong cases exhibited higher ICOS+TIL density

Because tumor antigen presentation is critical for antitumor immune responses, we examined the relationship between ICOS+ TIL density and tumor expression of HLA class I (HLA-A/B/C) and class II (HLA-DR) molecules (Fig. 4a). Cases were classified into three groups according to tumor staining intensity, using constitutive HLA expression in immune cells as an internal reference. Among the total cohort, 61 tumors (26.8%) showed strong HLA-DR expression, and these tumors exhibited significantly higher ICOS+ TIL density than tumors with negative or weak HLA-DR expression (n = 167, 73.2%). In a multivariate logistic regression model that included clinicopathological factors significant in univariate analysis along with CD8+ TIL density and CD3+CD8− TIL density, only pathological stage (IB–IV vs 0–IA3) and ICOS+ TIL density remained independent predictors of strong tumor HLA-DR expression (Table 2). Notably, although EGFR-mutant status was associated with both high ICOS+ TIL density and strong tumor HLA-DR expression in univariate analysis, it was not an independent factor in the multivariable model. This finding suggests that the association between tumor HLA-DR expression and ICOS+ TIL infiltration is largely independent of EGFR mutation status. In contrast, no such association was observed for tumor HLA class I expression (strong, n = 78, 34.2% vs. loss or reduced, n = 150, 65.8%). Prognostic implication of tumor HLA expression and ICOS+TIL density by tumor HLA-DR status

Both HLA class I-strong and HLA-DR-strong groups showed better recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared with the loss/reduced and negative/weak groups, respectively (Fig. 5a,b). In a multivariate Cox regression analysis for RFS, HLA class I-strong status remained an independent prognostic factor, whereas HLA-DR did not, and pathological stage was a strong prognostic factor in both models (Table 3). Given the marked association between ICOS+ TIL density and HLA-DR-strong status, we performed a subgroup analysis according to tumor HLA-DR expression. In the HLA-DR-negative/weak group, survival did not differ between ICOS-low and ICOS-high patients (Fig. 5c). By contrast, in the HLA-DR-strong group, the ICOS-high group showed a trend toward longer cancer-specific survival (CSS), although this trend did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.060). When the analysis was stratified by the densities of specific T cell subsets, no significant survival differences were observed (supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

As shown in Fig. 1, ICOS+ TIL density correlated with the densities of CD8+, CD3+CD8−, and FOXP3+ TILs, but only CD8+ TILs were identified as a prognostic factor, correlating with longer RFS. This result is consistent with reports from studies of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) indicating that higher densities of intratumoral CD8+ CTLs are associated with better clinical outcomes [24, 25]. A few studies of LUAD using TCGA data or postoperative IHC scoring with intensity and proportion have reported better survival among ICOS-high patients [26, 27]. In our cohort of resected LUAD patients with relatively good cure rates, ICOS+ TILs were not significantly associated with prognosis. The prognostic significance of CD4+ T cells is less clear, but several lines of evidence suggest that effector CD4+ T cells function in helping maintain functional CD8+ T cells, for example through IL-2-mediated support [28].

We also characterized ICOS expression across intratumoral T cell subsets. ICOS positivity was highest among Tregs (median ~ 80%), intermediate among CD4+ non-Tregs (~ 40%), and lowest among CD8+ T cells (~ 25%), consistent with the speculation that Tregs constitutively express ICOS and depend on ICOS signaling for maintenance [29]. In addition, ICOS+ cells were clearly present among CD4⁺ non-Tregs, and because this subset was numerically predominant in our multiplex IHC analysis, the overall ICOS⁺ TIL density largely reflected ICOS+CD4+ non-Tregs rather than ICOS+ Tregs or ICOS+CD8+ cells. This is biologically important because ICOS is a costimulatory receptor that enhances both effector CD4 responses and Treg-mediated suppression, and its role in antitumor immunity therefore depends on the cell subset in which it is expressed. By visualizing and quantifying subset-specific ICOS expression in LUAD tissue, our findings may provide guidance for designing ICOS-targeted strategies (agonistic or antagonistic): CD4⁺ non-Treg effectors could be targeted to promote antitumor immune responses [30]; however, ICOS engagement on Tregs enhances immunosuppression somewhat [29]. Each case should therefore be evaluated for the proportions and spatial distributions of CD4⁺ subsets. Notably, scRNA-seq analysis also supported the finding that CD4⁺ non-Tregs represent the major ICOS⁺ T cell subset. However, the proportions of ICOS+ cells (approximately 50% in Tregs, 30% in CD4⁺ non-Tregs, and 20% in CD8⁺ T cells) were lower than those observed by multiplex IHC, and ICOS transcript levels in ICOS⁺ cells were low (log-normalized UMI counts generally in the range of 0–1; mean, 0.51). These findings suggest that the threshold distinguishing ICOS+ and ICOS− cells may depend on low-level expression changes that are not detectable by scRNA-seq, making strict classification based solely on transcriptomic data technically challenging.

In this study, ICOS+ TIL density was positively associated with EGFR-mutant status. Although EGFR-mutant lung cancers are generally characterized by a relatively “cold” immune phenotype, including reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [31, 32], an association between EGFR mutation and ICOS expression has not been previously reported. Whether the CD4⁺ non-Treg-predominant pattern of ICOS⁺ TILs observed in our multiplex IHC analysis is also applicable to EGFR-mutant tumors remains unclear, but this distinction is an important consideration for optimizing ICOS-targeted therapeutic strategies. With the current widespread implementation of multigene panel testing, subset-specific ICOS expression across different oncogenic driver alterations warrants further investigation in more recent cohorts. In this context, ICOS expression on Tregs should be interpreted with caution, given prior reports indicating increased Treg infiltration in EGFR-mutant tumors [31].

In examining the relationship between ICOS and tumor HLA expression (class I and class II), we found a significant correlation, specifically with HLA-DR expression. HLA class II is constitutively expressed in multiple NSCLC cell lines [12]; in surgically resected NSCLC patients, cancer cell-specific downregulation of HLA-DR occurs in 17.7% of cases and is associated with reduced TILs and shorter overall survival [33]. In our cohort, the densities of ICOS⁺ TILs were increased in HLA-DR-strong tumors, whereas total CD8⁺ or CD3⁺CD8⁻ TIL densities were not correlated with HLA-DR status, indicating a selective link between HLA-DR and the ICOS⁺ fraction. Multiplex IHC analysis suggested that ICOS⁺ cells are predominantly ICOS⁺CD4⁺ non-Tregs, suggesting this cell subset drives the association. Two non-exclusive mechanisms may explain this link. The first mechanism involves cytokine-driven induction of HLA class II expression on tumor cells. Although constitutive HLA class II expression is typical of professional antigen-presenting cells, diverse cell types, including tumor cells, exhibit upregulated HLA class II expression in response to immune cytokines, particularly IFN-γ [34]. ICOS costimulation promotes the expression of Th1-type cytokines, including IFN-γ [35], and Liakou et al. reported that ICOS+ Th1-like cells are the majority of tumor-specific, IFN-γ producing CD4 T cells in bladder cancer tissues treated with anti-CTLA-4 therapy [36]. ICOS⁺CD4⁺ non-Tregs in our study may exhibit Th1-like features, produce IFN-γ, and induce tumor HLA-DR expression. In the second mechanism, tumor HLA class II may recruit or retain tumor-reactive CD4⁺ T cells. Key studies have shown that in addition to conventional exogenous peptides, endogenous proteins can access the HLA class II pathway and generate peptides recognized by CD4+ T cells [13]. Indeed, Sartolis et al. showed that CIITA (class II transactivator), the master regulator of HLA class II expression, enables these cells to present antigens to previously activated Th cells when ectopically expressed in human tumor cells [37]. Murine models have also shown that tumor expression of HLA class II increases the densities of intratumoral CD4⁺ subsets, enhances CD4⁺-mediated tumor control, and elevates the expression of chemoattractant cytokines such as CXCL13 and CCL5 [13, 14, 38]. These observations indicate that tumor cells expressing MHC class II are directly recognized in vivo and can elicit tumor-specific CD4⁺ Th responses.

Furthermore, a report indicating that PD-1⁺ICOS⁺CD4⁺ Th cells are tumor-reactive and enriched in tumor HLA class II-rich regions supports observations suggesting that HLA class II is recognized by ICOS+ cells [9]. In lung cancer, tumor neoantigen presentation by HLA class II has not been conclusively demonstrated, but in silico predictions indicate that numerous peptides, including those derived from KRAS, TP53, and EGFR, could be presented by HLA-DR [39]. Considered together with these prior findings, the positive correlation between ICOS+ TIL density and strong HLA-DR expression observed in our study allows us to propose the hypothesis that ICOS+CD4+ non-Tregs (acting as effector cytokine-producing Th cells) and tumor HLA-DR expression (potentially induced by cytokines and presenting tumor antigens) may be functionally linked and mutually reinforced within the LUAD tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). We acknowledge that this proposed ICOS⁺CD4⁺ TIL-HLA-DR axis is based on observational associations and lacks direct functional validation. In addition, the spatial relationship between ICOS⁺ TILs and HLA class II-expressing tumor cells could not be evaluated because quantitative spatial analysis was not feasible in the present study and therefore remains an important direction for future investigation.

Finally, the clinical significance of tumor HLA expression and ICOS⁺ TILs according to tumor HLA-DR status were evaluated. Previous studies have suggested that tumor HLA class I and class II expression is associated with favorable prognosis in NSCLC, although evidence—particularly for class II—remains limited [33, 40]. In this study, HLA class I status remained an independent prognostic factor, suggesting that class I alterations may influence the tumor immune context and disease course. In contrast, HLA-DR was not independently associated with RFS, likely because its prognostic effect was largely accounted for by pathological stage, which is a strong determinant of survival. This indicates that the loss of HLA-DR can be more strongly related to intrinsic alterations derived from tumor progression, such as impaired IFN-γ-CIITA-mediated induction. In the HLA-DR-strong group, CSS tended to be longer in the ICOS-high subgroup, although this increase was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that ICOS+ TILs are not uniformly beneficial; rather, the relevance of ICOS⁺CD4⁺ non-Tregs may become apparent in an IFN-γ-responsive context that supports HLA-DR induction. Both CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells can produce IFN-γ, but CD8+ CTLs are generally regarded as the principal antitumor effector population in solid tumors, which is consistent with our finding that higher CD8+ TIL density is prognostically favorable. Within the above model of an ICOS+CD4+ TIL-HLA-DR axis, IFN-γ derived from CD8+ CTLs likely also contributes, whereas ICOS+CD4+ TILs may act not only as CTLs but also as helpers that support CD8+ CTL responses. Further studies will be required to clarify the CD4-CD8 T cell interactions in this TIME.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Multiplex IHC analysis was restricted to ICOS-high cases to ensure reliable quantification of subset-specific ICOS expression, given the limited tissue area provided by 5-mm tissue microarray cores; although no marked bias in EGFR mutation status was observed, ICOS⁺ TILs were correlated with CD8⁺ TILs, CD4⁺ TILs, and Tregs, and this approach may therefore preferentially capture tumors with an inflamed TIME. Finally, we acknowledge the potential contribution of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) to ICOS⁺ TIL density, given the established role of ICOS in T follicular helper-like CD4+ T cell subsets and TLS-associated immune responses. These limitations highlight the need for future studies using whole-section specimens to assess spatial immune architecture in greater detail.

Conclusions

The overall ICOS+ TIL density in LUAD primarily reflects the abundance of ICOS+CD4+ non-Tregs. Tumor HLA expression was associated with favorable prognosis, and ICOS+ TILs were enriched in HLA-DR-strong tumors. Consideration of subset-specific evaluation and tumor immunogenicity may refine the therapeutic effectiveness of ICOS-targeted therapy.
