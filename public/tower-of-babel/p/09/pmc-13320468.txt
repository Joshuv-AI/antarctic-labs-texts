APOE Genotype Modifies the Predictive Performance of Plasma Biomarkers for Amyloid Plaque Burden in Subjective Cognitive Decline
Authors: Hyuk-je Lee, Bora Yoon, Yun Jeong Hong, Jee Hyang Jeong, SangYun Kim, Min Jeong Wang, Seong Hye Choi, Ji Sun Ryu, Sungmin Kang, Kee Hyung Park, Dong Won Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 is a well-known risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with ε4 carriers exhibiting distinct clinical and biological characteristics compared to non-carriers. This study investigated whether the predictive performance of plasma biomarkers for amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) outcomes varies by APOE ε4 carrier status in individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed baseline data from the Cohort Study to Identify Predictors for the Clinical Progression to Mild Cognitive Impairment or Dementia from Subjective Cognitive Decline. Plasma levels of the amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40 ratio, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and phosphorylated tau (pTau) 181 were quantified using Single Molecule Array technology. We assessed predictive performance of plasma biomarkers for amyloid PET outcomes using receiver operating characteristic analysis and linear regression models.ResultsThe study included 104 participants, with 20 APOE ε4 carriers and 84 non-carriers. Among ε4 carriers, NfL (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.909), GFAP (AUC = 0.904), and pTau181 (AUC = 0.828) showed the highest predictive accuracy. For non-carriers, the most predictive biomarkers were pTau181/Aβ42 (AUC = 0.872), Aβ42/Aβ40 (AUC = 0.794), and pTau181 (AUC = 0.761). Significant interactions between APOE and biomarkers were noted for GFAP (P = 0.005) and NfL (P = 0.010) in predicting amyloid plaque burden.ConclusionPlasma biomarkers exhibit differential predictive performance for amyloid pathology based on APOE genotype, with GFAP and NfL showing interaction with APOE genotype. These findings suggest the importance of considering APOE genotype when interpreting plasma biomarker results for AD diagnosis in individuals with SCD.Trial RegistrationClinical Research Information Service Identifier: KCT0003397Keywords: Alzheimer’s Disease, Plasma Biomarker, Apolipoproteins E, Subjective Cognitive Decline

INTRODUCTION

Recent advances in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) research have established plasma biomarkers as valuable diagnostic tools, leading to their inclusion in the 2024 revised Alzheimer’s Association Workgroup criteria.1,2 These plasma biomarkers, including amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40 ratio, phosphorylated tau (pTau), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL), are increasingly used in the actual clinical trials as pre-screening or screening tools.3 It is notable that the 2024 revised criteria emphasize that the diagnostic accuracy and cutoff values of plasma biomarkers must be population-specific.2 This consideration is crucial as predictive performance varies depending on cognitive stage and underlying conditions.4,5

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) is a glycoprotein comprising 299 amino acids, with three isoforms—APOE ε2, APOE ε3, and APOE ε4.6 The APOE ε4 allele is present in 64% of sporadic, late-onset AD cases.7 APOE ε4 contributes to AD pathogenesis through multiple mechanisms. APOE ε4 enhances Aβ production from amyloid precursor protein and Aβ fibrillization while impairing cellular uptake and clearance of soluble Aβ.6,8 In the context of tau pathology, induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons show increased tau phosphorylation in APOE ε4 carrier AD patients9 and tau propagation is promoted by APOE ε4.6 In addition, APOE ε4 enhances inflammatory responses and increases blood-brain barrier permeability.6,10,11 As a consequence, APOE ε3/ε4 individuals have a 5-fold increased risk of AD among East Asian populations.12 Studies have shown that the ε4 allele accelerates cognitive decline.13,14

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) is a cognitive stage in which a person reports a self-experienced persistent decline in cognitive capacity in comparison with a previously normal cognitive status, despite normal performance on standardized cognitive tests.15,16 Classified as stage 2 in the AD continuum,2 individuals with SCD show a 4.5-fold increased risk of progression to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia compared to cognitively normal individuals.17 In the era of disease-modifying therapy, early diagnosis at AD continuum stages 1 and 2 has become critical, underscoring the need to assess the diagnostic performance of plasma biomarkers specifically at these stages. Furthermore, biomarker levels and interrelationships differ between individuals with and without SCD,18 emphasizing the need for specific evaluation of biomarker performance in this population.

However, SCD is a heterogeneous condition with diverse etiologies and prognoses. APOE ε4 carriage increases the risk of progression within this group,18 suggesting that genotype-stratified testing could enhance diagnostic precision—especially given that the relationship between plasma biomarkers and cerebral amyloid may differ between ε4 carriers and non-carriers.

This study aimed to investigate two key aspects among individuals with SCD: 1) whether the predictive performance of plasma biomarkers for amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) positivity varies based on ε4 carrier status and 2) whether APOE genotype interacts with plasma biomarkers in association with amyloid plaque burden, acknowledging that these relationships extend beyond simple linear correlations.

METHODS

Participants

This study analyzed baseline data from the Cohort Study to Identify Predictors for the Clinical Progression to Mild Cognitive Impairment or Dementia From Subjective Cognitive Decline (CoSCo). The study’s objectives and detailed design have been previously described.19 In brief, CoSCo aims to identify risk factors predicting progression to MCI or dementia in patients with SCD.

Participants were recruited from six dementia centers in Korea. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients aged 60 years or older who reported persistent cognitive decline; 2) individuals not diagnosed with dementia or MCI; 3) participants whose cognitive domain scores were at or above the 7th percentile, while verbal memory scores ranged from the 7th to 50th percentile, as assessed by the comprehensive neuropsychological test, Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery-II (SNSB-II)20,21; 4) individuals who had completed at least elementary school education; and 5) participants who provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria included the following: 1) the presence of other neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus, or brain lesions likely to cause cognitive decline; 2) metabolic or infectious conditions affecting cognitive function, including hypothyroidism, vitamin deficiency, or syphilis; 3) uncontrolled or severe psychiatric disorders such as depression, schizophrenia, or substance abuse; and 4) refusal to participate by the participant or their legal representative.

History taking and physical examination

To identify comorbid conditions, each participant’s prescribed medications were reviewed. A family history of dementia was recorded if any first-degree relative was reported to have had dementia by the participant or their legal representative. Vital signs and blood chemistry analyses were conducted to calculate Framingham cardiovascular risk scores.22 Additionally, physical performance was assessed through a hand grip strength test using a dynamometer (TKK-5401; TAKEI, Niigata, Japan) and by measuring gait speed over a 7-meter walk.23

Neuropsychological test and questionnaires

All participants underwent cognitive assessment using the SNSB-II.21 Task scores were adjusted for age, sex, and education and reported as percentiles. Participants also completed validated questionnaires, including the Korean-Everyday Cognition (K-ECOG) for functional change related to cognitive decline,24 the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression,25 and the Brief Encounter Psychosocial Instrument (BEPSI) for stress measurement.26

Plasma biomarker quantification

Peripheral blood samples were centrifuged and frozen within one hour. Plasma levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, pTau181, NfL, and GFAP were quantified using the Single Molecule Array (Simoa) platform, employing a paramagnetic microbead-based ELISA. Aβ, GFAP, and NfL levels were measured using the Simoa® Neurology 4-Plex E assay, and pTau181 was analyzed with the pTau181 Advantage V2.1 assay (PN 104111; Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA). All samples were run in duplicate with automated 4× dilution in 96-well plates. Assays were performed on the Simoa HD-X instrument using a two-step immunoassay, where target proteins were captured and detected simultaneously via antibody-coated beads and biotinylated detectors. The lower limits of quantification were 4.08 pg/mL for Aβ40, 1.51 pg/mL for Aβ42, 11.6 pg/mL for GFAP, 1.6 pg/mL for NfL, and 2.00 pg/mL for pTau181. Data acquisition was conducted with HD-X software version 3.1.2011.30002.

Amyloid PET acquisition and processing

All participants underwent [18F]-florbetaben (FBB) PET imaging according to standardized protocols.27 Brain magnetic resonance imaging scans were acquired following the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative protocol.28 Amyloid PET scans were interpreted visually by nuclear medicine professionals at each research site using the brain amyloid plaque load scoring system.29

Quantitative PET analysis was performed using MATLAB 2013a and SPM8.30 T1-weighted images were segmented and co-registered to corresponding PET scans. A template with 90 regions of interest (ROIs), known as automatic anatomical labeling (AAL), was aligned to each subject’s T1-weighted image.31 FBB PET voxel intensities were normalized to the mean uptake value in the cerebellar cortex to calculate standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs), and partial volume correction excluded voxels with a gray matter probability below 20%. Twenty-eight AD-specific cortical ROIs from the AAL atlas were used to calculate regional SUVR values, and the average of regional SUVR values were computed to determine the global SUVR.31,32

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics between APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality.

ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of plasma biomarkers for amyloid positivity, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated. Cutoff values were determined based on the highest Youden’s index. ROC analysis was performed for the total population and stratified by APOE ε4 carrier status. Differences of AUC between APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers are compared using DeLong’s test. Jackknife analysis was performed, and post-hoc power was calculated from DeLong’s variance.

A multivariable linear regression model was used to evaluate the relationship between plasma biomarkers and amyloid plaque burden determined by global SUVR and to examine the presence of interaction between APOE genotype and plasma biomarker, with adjustments for age, sex, education, APOE genotype, and APOE genotype * plasma biomarker. APOE genotype was treated as a binary variable with two levels: ε4 carrier and ε4 non-carrier. Amyloid plaque burden and levels of GFAP, NfL and pTau181 were logarithmically transformed to approximate normality assumptions. Statistical power for the interaction was estimated post-hoc with Cohen’s f2 method. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to investigate the association between amyloid plaque burden and plasma biomarkers according to amyloid PET positivity by using separate linear regression models for Aβ+ and Aβ− participants.

Two-sided P values < 0.05 are considered significant and family-wise error rate was controlled using Bonferroni method if required. The analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board in each center (Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, #KC18ONDI0394; Gil Medical Center, GAIRB2021-097; Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital, 2021-02-011-010; Seoul National University Bundang Hospital and Roa Neurology Clinic, B-2107-694-002; Inha University Hospital, 2021-03-028). Informed consent was submitted by all subjects when they were enrolled. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and principles of Good Clinical Practice. This study was registered at Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS) (cris.nih.go.kr/cris, KCT0003397).

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

From November 2018 to November 2019, 120 participants were enrolled in the CoSCo study, among whom plasma biomarker data were available for 104 individuals. Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. The cohort comprised 20 APOE ε4 carriers (3 homozygote and 17 heterozygote carriers) and 84 ε4 non-carriers. The mean age was 71.4 years for ε4 carriers and 70.4 years for ε4 non-carriers, with females comprising 60.0% and 54.8% of each group, respectively. Both groups had an average of 11.5 years of education (Table 1). Amyloid PET positivity (Aβ+) was observed in 55.0% of ε4 carriers compared to 15.5% of ε4 non-carriers. Both groups showed minimal comorbidities and intact physical performance, both of which were not significantly different between the groups.

Plasma biomarker level

Significant differences in the level of plasma biomarkers between Aβ+ and Aβ− groups were observed and the differences were varied by APOE genotype (Fig. 1). Among ε4 carriers, GFAP and NfL levels were significantly elevated in the Aβ+ group after Bonferroni correction (GFAP: corrected P = 0.013; NfL: P = 0.012) (Fig. 1B and C). Conversely, in ε4 non-carriers, Aβ42/Aβ40, pTau181, and pTau181/Aβ42 levels were significantly higher in the Aβ+ group (Aβ42/Aβ40: corrected P < 0.001; pTau181: P = 0.041; pTau181/Aβ42: P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A, D and E). Additionally, GFAP levels were higher in Aβ+ ε4 carriers compared to Aβ+ ε4 non-carriers (corrected P = 0.016).

Predictive performance of plasma biomarkers

Plasma biomarkers showed different predictive performances for amyloid PET positivity according to APOE genotype. In the total population, pTau181/Aβ42 (AUC = 0.864), pTau181 (AUC = 0.810), GFAP (AUC = 0.794), and Aβ42/Aβ40 (AUC = 0.791) demonstrated good predictive ability (Table 2). Among ε4 carriers, NfL (AUC = 0.909), GFAP (AUC = 0.904), and pTau181 (AUC = 0.828) demonstrated good predictive performance (Table 2, Fig. 2). In contrast, pTau181/Aβ42 (AUC = 0.872), Aβ42/Aβ40 (AUC = 0.794), and pTau181 (AUC = 0.761) were the most predictive biomarkers in ε4 non-carriers. However, the AUC estimates in ε4 carriers proved sensitive to individual outlier (Supplementary Table 1). Acceptable power was not achieved for any plasma biomarker when comparing AUC differences between two groups.

The accuracy of plasma biomarker was generally higher in ε4 carriers than in ε4 non-carriers, except for pTau181, which showed the highest accuracy in ε4 non-carriers due to its high specificity despite a low sensitivity of 0.462 (Table 2). In ε4 non-carriers, where the prevalence of amyloid PET positivity was only 15.5%, Aβ42/Aβ40 had the lowest accuracy (0.667) and a low positive predictive value (PPV; 0.308) due to its low specificity (0.620). Similarly, GFAP and NfL had low PPVs of 0.333 and 0.304, respectively, while all biomarkers had negative predictive values exceeding 0.9.

Interaction between biomarkers and APOE genotype

To further elucidate the relationship between plasma biomarkers and amyloid plaque burden, we conducted a multivariable regression analysis incorporating APOE genotype interactions. Table 3 shows that the interaction between APOE genotype and biomarkers significantly affects the association of GFAP and NfL with amyloid plaque burden (APOE * GFAP: unstandardized regression coefficient [B] [standard error (SE)], 0.19 [0.07], P = 0.005; APOE * NfL: B [SE], 0.19 [0.07], P = 0.010), while the models of Aβ42/Aβ40 and pTau181 did not include the interaction term as a significant predictor (APOE * Aβ42/Aβ40: B [SE], −8.32 [4.53], P = 0.069; APOE * pTau181: B [SE], 0.10 [0.08], P = 0.210). However, the power analysis showed that neither APOE * Aβ42/40 nor APOE * pTau181 reached adequate statistical power (Supplementary Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study assessed the influence of APOE genotype on the predictive performance of plasma biomarkers for amyloid plaque burden in individuals with SCD. Our findings are consistent with genotype-specific differences in biomarker predictive performance for amyloid PET positivity and indicate statistically significant APOE * GFAP and APOE * NfL interactions related to amyloid plaque burden.

From a practical standpoint, because performing APOE genotyping followed by five plasma assays would be burdensome, we propose a two-stage algorithm: 1) APOE testing (routinely tested in Korea) 2) genotype-guided biomarker panels—GFAP + NfL for ε4 carriers and pTau181/Aβ42 for non-carriers. Further validation in diagnostic performance and prospective cost-effectiveness analyses are required before clinical adoption.

The APOE ε4 allele is known not only to influence Aβ or tau pathology but also to drive neurodegeneration through glial-mediated neuroinflammatory mechanisms.6,33 Our finding that GFAP and NfL, an astrocytic marker and a neuro-axonal injury marker respectively, demonstrated better predictive performance and stronger associations with amyloid plaque burden in APOE ε4 carriers aligns with previous evidence indicating that ε4 carriers had increased proinflammatory responses, astrocytic activation, and upregulation of genes correlated with brain atrophy.33

GFAP level has been consistently associated with cortical Aβ deposition, longitudinal cognitive decline, and brain structural changes even from the preclinical AD34,35,36,37 and particularly elevate in the early disease course.38,39 The significant APOE * GFAP interaction observed in our study underscores a closer association between astrocytic activation and amyloid pathology in APOE ε4 carriers, which is especially pronounced during the SCD. Previous studies have reported mixed results regarding this interaction.40,41,42,43,44,45 These conflicting findings may be attributed to differences in disease stage, as cerebrospinal fluid proteins change with cognitive stage progression, and these alterations vary by APOE ε4 carrier status.46

NfL showed high predictive performance for amyloid PET positivity in APOE ε4 carriers, with a significant interaction with APOE genotype noted for amyloid plaque burden. In linear model, however, NfL levels were not independently predictive of amyloid plaque burden unlike GFAP, reflecting its broader association with general neurodegeneration rather than specific amyloid pathology.47 This is consistent with findings from other studies that report variable associations between NfL and amyloid pathology.48,49 In our study, stringent exclusion criteria may have enhanced the apparent predictive performance of NfL among ε4 carriers. The interaction between APOE and NfL in relation to amyloid pathology has not been thoroughly investigated, though some research indicates higher NfL levels in cognitively unimpaired APOE ε4 homozygotes compared to APOE ε4 non-carriers.48,49

Unlike GFAP and NfL, we observed no significant APOE * Aβ42/Aβ40 interaction for amyloid plaque burden, aligning with previous reports that found no genotype interactions for plasma Aβ in individuals diagnosed with SCD.50 However, our sensitivity analysis identified APOE * Aβ42/Aβ40 interaction as well as Aβ42/Aβ40 as the significant predictors for amyloid plaque burden in the Aβ- group, suggesting early decline of plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 precede detectable amyloid deposition.51

The study has several limitations. First, the overall sample was modest, thereby reducing the precision of AUC estimates and power to detect between-genotype differences in AUC. Apart from NfL, AUC differences were not significant, and the APOE * biomarker interactions for Aβ42/40 and pTau181 were likewise non-significant—findings we attribute primarily to limited power rather than definitive absence of effect. Accordingly, all non-significant AUC differences and interaction terms should be interpreted with caution. These results need be regarded as exploratory. Second, the focus on a Korean cohort might restrict the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities. Third, this study is cross-sectional, precluding inferences about temporality and causality.

Despite these limitations, the study’s strengths include a well-characterized cohort and the cautious control of confounding variables, providing support for the observed genotype-specific effects. In addition, we presented the separate predictive performance of plasma biomarkers by APOE genotype, which has been seldom examined, not combining the genotype as one of the predictors in a model. The ε4 allele may alter—not merely add—amyloid pathology in the association with blood biomarkers.

Future longitudinal studies with larger, more ethnically diverse populations and a sufficient number of APOE ε4 homozygotes are necessary to assess the dose effect of APOE ε4 alleles more comprehensively. Further research into astrocytic and neurodegenerative responses by APOE genotype in relation to amyloid pathology will help clarify the mechanisms underlying our observations.

In conclusion, APOE genotype influences the predictive performance of plasma biomarkers for amyloid pathology in individuals with SCD. Specifically, APOE * GFAP and APOE * NfL interactions suggest the importance of considering APOE status when interpreting these biomarkers. These findings support integrating APOE genotype into the selection and interpretation of plasma biomarkers.
