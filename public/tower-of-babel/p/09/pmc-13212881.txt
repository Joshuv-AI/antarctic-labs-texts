Pore structure of deep high-rank coal and its impact on coalbed methane adsorption: a case study of the Daning-Jixian block, Eastern Ordos Basin
Authors: Haonan Chen, Hailong Wang, Jing Fan, Sijie He, Liangliang Wang, Zhanping Li, Kaiwen Sun, Xin Tong, Jiahang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The pore structure of deep coal reservoirs is the key to evaluating the potential of coalbed methane resources and optimizing the development strategy. In this study, the 8# coal sample of Benxi Formation in the DJ57 well of the Daning-Jixian block in the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin was taken as the research object. Based on the microscopic components and industrial analysis, the multi-scale quantitative characterization of nano-scale pores was conducted by using CO2 and N2 adsorption experiments. On this basis, the V-S (Volume-Specific Surface Area) and FHH (Frenkel Halsey Hill) fractal models were used to reveal the multi-scale fractal characteristics of pores. Combined with the CH4 isothermal adsorption experiment, the main controlling factors affecting the CH4 adsorption gas content were systematically discussed. The results show that: (1) The thermal evolution degree of deep coal in the study area is high, and the whole is in the stage of high rank coal (the average Ro, max is 2.44%). The material composition of coal rock is mainly vitrinite (average content is 81.0%), inertinite, and mineral group content is 12.5% and 6.5%, respectively. Industrial analysis shows that it has the characteristics of low moisture (the average Mad is 0.97%), low ash (the average Aad is 21.7%), low volatile (the average Vad is 7.36%), and high fixed carbon (the average FCad is 69.95%). (2) The micropores are highly developed, with an average CO₂ adsorption capacity of 17.52 cm³/g. The average total micropore volume and specific surface area are 0.05301 cm³/g and 180.57 m²/g, respectively. The pore morphology is primarily composed of cylindrical pores with openings at both ends and flat slit shaped pores with open cylindrical on all sides. (3) The coal samples exhibit strong CH₄ adsorption capacity, with an average Langmuir volume (VL) of 28.77 cm³/g. (4) The fractal characteristics of multi-scale pores are significant. The average pore fractal dimension (DC) based on CO2 adsorption is 2.4686, and the average pore surface fractal dimension (DN−1) and pore structure fractal dimension (DN−2) based on N2 adsorption are 2.5943 and 2.7202, respectively. (5) The degree of thermal evolution, material composition, pore structure, and heterogeneity of coal rock jointly control the CH4 adsorption capacity. With the increase of Ro, max, the content of vitrinite and the proportion of micropores increase, and the complexity and heterogeneity of pore structure increase, providing more CH4 adsorption sites, thus improving the adsorption capacity. There is a strong positive correlation between VL and DC, and DC can be used as a key parameter to evaluate the adsorbed gas content of deep coal in the study area. The research results have theoretical guiding significance for deep coalbed methane resource evaluation, “sweet spot” identification, and efficient development.

Introduction

China has abundant deep coalbed methane (CBM) resources. The results of the fourth round of coalbed methane resource evaluation in China show that the resource volume of coalbed methane at burial depth less than 2000 m is 29.82 × 1012 m³, while the resource volume of deep coalbed methane at burial depth greater than 2000 m is 40.47 × 1012 m³. This is approximately equivalent to the conventional natural gas resource volume, indicating promising prospects for exploration and development1–3. In recent years, China has made significant progress in the exploration of deep coalbed methane resources4. With increased exploration investment and advancements in exploration techniques, the steady growth of coalbed methane production has been realized. Deep coalbed methane has become an important pillar of China’s energy structure adjustment and clean energy development5–10. However, deep coalbed methane exhibits the coexistence of adsorbed gas and free gas, and its occurrence state, accumulation characteristics, and development law are significantly different from those of middle-shallow coalbed methane. The unclear accumulation evolution law restricts its efficient exploration and development11–13.

Pore structure is a key factor influencing the occurrence and production of coalbed methane. It not only directly affects the occurrence ratio and gas content of free and adsorbed coalbed methane, but also controls the adsorption-desorption, diffusion, and seepage characteristics of coalbed methane14. The pore structure of coal affects the adsorption and migration of CH4 in coal. The main purpose of coalbed methane reservoir research is to clarify the distribution characteristics of pore structure and its influence on gas adsorption. IUPAC classifies pores into three types: micropores (< 2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (> 50 nm)15. Coal contains pores and microfractures of various scales. It is difficult to accurately characterize the development and distribution characteristics of pores at different scales using a single method16. At present, the commonly used coal pore structure characterization techniques include the fluid injection method, the image analysis method, and the non-destructive characterization method. The fluid injection method can be used to evaluate porous media containing non-wetting fluids (N2, CO2, and Hg), and the pore structure parameters can be calculated according to the relevant models17,18. Image analysis requires the use of optical microscopy (OM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and other microscopic imaging techniques to observe the pore morphology, size, and distribution characteristics of coal19. Non-destructive characterization methods mainly include qualitative and quantitative characterization techniques such as small-angle/ultra-small-angle neutron scattering (SANS/USANS) and computer tomography (CT), which are used to evaluate the multi-scale pore space in coal reservoirs. All these methods have the advantages of being non-destructive, fast in operation, and providing rich information20–24. Due to the variety of testing methods for pore structure and distribution in coal, different analysis principles and sample requirements may lead to certain limitations when using the obtained pore distribution range information25,26. Therefore, it is urgent to use various auxiliary techniques to characterize the complex pore structure of coal.

This study selected the deep coal reservoirs of the Danning-Jixian block as the research object. Combining microscopic components and industrial analysis, low-pressure CO2 and low-temperature N2 adsorption experiments, CH4 isothermal adsorption test, and fractal theory methods. The study systematically characterized the coal rock material composition, multi-scale pore structure, and its heterogeneity, and further revealed the main controlling factors of methane adsorption gas content in deep coal reservoirs. The aim is to provide a theoretical basis for deep coalbed methane resource evaluation, “sweet spot” identification, and efficient development in the study area.

Geological setting

The study block is located in the southeastern margin of the Ordos Basin, spanning the southern section of the Jinxi flexure fold belt and the southeastern part of the Yishan slope. The administrative division belongs to Daning County and Jixian County in Shanxi Province, and is connected to the southern block of Yanchuan (Fig. 1a and b). The regional structure as a whole is a gently inclined monocline that strikes NNE and dips NW, and develops a fault system dominated by NE-trending reverse faults8. The Permian in the study area is a marine-continental transitional facies deposit, which is divided from bottom to top into the Benxi Formation, Taiyuan Formation, and Shanxi Formation. Among them, the Shanxi Formation is dominated by delta deposits, and the Taiyuan Formation and the Benxi Formation develop delta, lagoon, tidal flat, and shallow marine shelf deposits from north to south27. Vertically, mudstone is frequently interbedded with coal seam and sandstone, with pure mudstone layers being relatively rare. The coal seams in the area are mainly distributed in the Shanxi Formation, Taiyuan Formation, and Benxi Formation. The thickness of coal seam in the Shanxi formation is relatively thin (1–3 m), the thickness of coal seam in the Taiyuan formation is 1–6 m, and the thickness of coal seam in the Benxi formation is large, generally 5–12 m, which is the main layer of coalbed methane exploration in the Ordos basin. Among them, the 8# coal seam of Benxi Formation is widely distributed, has a large thickness, and is continuous and stable. The distribution area in the range of buried depth of 2000–3500 m is about 6.9 × 103 km2, which is the most favorable target layer for deep coalbed methane exploration in Benxi Formation28(Fig. 1c).

Experimental method

Gas adsorption method

The low-pressure CO2 adsorption experiment and low-temperature N2 adsorption experiment were conducted using the ASAP 2460 automatic gas adsorption instrument produced by Micromeritics Company of the United States, according to the Chinese national standard GB / T 19,587 − 2017. The low-pressure CO2 adsorption experiment was conducted at 273 K. Since CO2 does not undergo capillary condensation, this method is not suitable for analyzing pores larger than 2 nm. Because of its small molecular diameter (0.33 nm) and high saturated vapor pressure, it is mainly used to detect the distribution of micropores less than 2 nm. The pore volume of micropores was calculated by the DFT (Density Functional Theory) model, and the specific surface area of micropores was calculated by the D-A (Dubinin-Astakhov) model.

The low-temperature N2 adsorption experiment can characterize the pore structure through physical adsorption and capillary condensation, and is mainly suitable for mesopores of 2–50 nm and some macropores (50–200 nm). The temperature condition of the low-pressure N2 adsorption experiment is 77 K. At this temperature, as the relative pressure (P/P0) gradually increases, N2 undergoes physical adsorption and capillary condensation on the solid surface, and the adsorption amount also increases. Based on this theory, the adsorption amount of N2 can be quantitatively calculated. In the lower relative pressure range (P/P0 < 0.5), the specific surface area of the coal samples can be calculated using the BET (Brunauer-Emmett-Teller) equation, based on the relationship between adsorption amount and relative pressure. In the higher relative pressure range (P/P0 > 0.5), where capillary condensation occurs, the pore volume of the coal samples can be calculated using the BJH (Barrett-Joyner-Halenda) equation.

CH4 isothermal adsorption method

The methane isothermal adsorption experiment was conducted according to the volume method of the Chinese national standard GB/T 35210.1–2017. Before the experiment, the sample was subjected to humidity balance treatment, and 120–150 g was weighed. Then, the pressure was gradually increased to 25 MPa at a constant temperature of 35℃ to obtain a high-pressure methane adsorption isotherm curve. Fractal theory

The fractal theory was proposed by the Mandelbrot29, aiming to describe the irregular but self-similar complex systems in nature, and its structural complexity can be quantitatively characterized by fractal dimension. This theory has been widely used in the characterization of the pore structure of porous media, such as coal reservoirs, in the early stage. The fractal dimension can be calculated by the mercury intrusion method, the gas adsorption (CO2, N2) method, and the scanning electron microscope method30.

Experimental results

Microscopic components and industrial analysis

The analysis of coal rock components shows that the vitrinite content is 75.9% -86.1% (average 81.0%), the inertinite content is 7.4%-19.8% (average 12.5%), the mineral group content is 3.2%-11.7% (average 6.5%), and there is no exinite. The coal rank belongs to the category of lean coal-anthracite, and the maximum vitrinite reflectance (Rₒ,ₘₐₓ) is 1.91%-2.88%, with an average of 2.44%. According to the metamorphic degree of coal, coal can be classified into three ranks: low-rank coal (Rₒ,ₘₐₓ < 0.65%), medium-rank coal (Rₒ,ₘₐₓ = 0.65%-2.0%), and high-rank coal (Rₒ,ₘₐₓ > 2.0%)14. According to this classification, the deep coal rock in the study area belongs to the category of high rank coal. The industrial analysis shows that the coal samples have the characteristics of low moisture (Mad average of 0.97%), low-medium ash (Aad average of 21.7%), low volatile (Vad average of 7.36%), and high fixed carbon (FCad average of 69.95%).

Low-pressure CO2 adsorption experiment

CO₂ can effectively enter micropores smaller than 2 nm in coal samples due to its relatively high activation energy. Therefore, the CO₂ adsorption method can accurately characterize the micropore structure (< 2 nm) of coal samples33. According to the classification of the IUPAC, the CO2 adsorption isotherms of deep coal samples in the study area are characterized by a typical type I curve (Fig. 3). The adsorption capacity of the curve increases rapidly in the low-pressure section and tends to be gentle in the high-pressure section, indicating that a large number of micropores are developed in the coal rock samples34.

The CO2 adsorption capacity of coal samples is between 6.98 cm3/g and 23.86 cm3/g, with an average of 17.52 cm3/g. The total pore volume of micropores is between 0.02124 cm3/g and 0.07242 cm3/g, with an average of 0.05301 cm3/g. The specific surface area is between 71.99 m2/g and 247.05 m2/g, with an average of 180.57 m2/g. The pore structure parameters based on CO2 adsorption are shown in Table 2.

Low-temperature N2 adsorption experiment

Low-temperature N2 adsorption is one of the main methods for characterizing the pore structure of coal samples, especially suitable for analyzing mesopores in the 2–50 nm range32. The low-temperature N2 adsorption-desorption curves for coal samples in the study area are shown in Fig. 4. According to the classification criteria of IUPAC, the N2 adsorption-desorption curves can be divided into six types (I-VI), and the hysteresis loops can be divided into four types (H1-H4)35. The curve shapes and hysteresis loops of different types reflect the pore structure and type information of the sample36. The N2 adsorption isotherm curves of deep coal samples in the study area show the mixed characteristics of type II and type III (Fig. 4), indicating a relatively low degree of mesopore development. In addition, the adsorption-desorption hysteresis loop of coal rock is small, indicating that the pore morphology is mainly composed of cylindrical pores with openings at both ends and flat slit shaped pores with open cylindrical on all sides37. The BJH pore volume ranges from 0.0024 cm³/g to 0.0137 cm³/g, with an average value of 0.0065 cm³/g, while the BET specific surface area varies between 1.20 m²/g and 6.10 m²/g, averaging 3.08 m²/g. CH4 isothermal adsorption experiment

Methane adsorption is the main occurrence form of coalbed methane in the reservoir. Its essence is a physical adsorption process, in which methane molecules and the coal rock surface are combined with each other through intermolecular forces38. Laboratory methane isothermal adsorption experiments can effectively determine the methane adsorption capacity of coal samples. The adsorption and desorption of this process are two reversible but different characteristics stages, both of which together reveal the occurrence and migration characteristics of CH4 in the reservoir. The key evaluation index of its comprehensive performance is the content of adsorbed coalbed methane39.

The methane isothermal adsorption curve drawn according to the experimental results conforms to the Langmuir model law (Fig. 5). The fitting degree (R2) of the Langmuir equation of each coal sample is greater than 0.990, indicating that the model can better characterize the methane adsorption behavior of coal rock samples. Based on the Langmuir equation, the corresponding methane adsorption capacity can be fitted under different pressure conditions. Among them, the maximum adsorption capacity is the Langmuir volume (VL), reflecting the adsorption potential. Under the condition that the pressure does not exceed 25 MPa, the methane adsorption capacity gradually increases with the increase of pressure (Fig. 5). The methane adsorption capacity of deep coal samples in the study area is between 22.47 and 33.68 cm3/g, with an average of 28.77 cm3/g (Table 4).

Fractal dimension

Micropore fractal dimension

Comparative studies have shown that when the FHH model is used to calculate the fractal dimension of micropores by processing CO2 adsorption data, systematic errors will occur due to the lack of applicability of the basic adsorption theory of the model at the micropore scale40,41. Therefore, this study selected the V-S fractal model based on CO2 adsorption experiments to accurately characterize the fractal characteristics of micropores. According to the slope of the fitting line of Ln (V) and Ln (S), the fractal dimension DC of micropores can be quantitatively obtained (Fig. 6). The linear fitting coefficient R2 is more than 0.99, which reflects the good fitting effect and indicates that the fractal characteristics are very significant. The calculation results show that the DC of coal rock samples in the study area is between 2.4402 and 2.4988, with an average of 2.4686. The detailed data are shown in Table 5.

Mesopore fractal dimension

Based on the experimental data of N2 adsorption, the fractal dimension (DN) of mesopores was calculated by the FHH fractal model. In the N2 adsorption isotherm, two different linear ranges can be identified according to the relative pressure (P/P0), which are 0-0.5 and 0.5-1.0, respectively, and two fractal dimensions are calculated accordingly42. Among them, DN−1 corresponds to the range of P/P0 = 0-0.5, and the N2 molecule is dominated by monolayer adsorption at this stage, and its fractal dimension reflects the roughness of the pore surface. DN−2 corresponds to the range of P/P0 = 0.5-1.0, in which capillary condensation occurs, and its fractal dimension characterizes the complexity of pore space structure. The larger the values of DN−1 and DN−2, the rougher the pore surface of the reservoir and the more complex the pore structure.

According to the slope of the fitting line of Ln (V) and Ln [Ln(P0/P)], the fractal dimensions DN−1 and DN−2 can be quantitatively obtained (Fig. 7). The linear fitting coefficient R2 is more than 0.97, which reflects the good fitting effect and indicates that the fractal characteristics are very significant. The calculation results show that the DN−1 of coal samples in the study area is between 2.5284 and 2.6575, with an average of 2.5943; DN−2 was between 2.6990 and 2.7702, with an average of 2.7202. Discussion

As a key parameter reflecting the upper limit of adsorbed gas content, VL is controlled by multiple geological factors. Therefore, clarifying its relationships with thermal evolution, material composition, pore structure, and heterogeneity is essential for understanding the adsorption capacity of deep coal reservoirs.

The relationship between Ro,max and VL

The adsorption capacity of coal to methane generally increases with the increase of thermal maturity (coal rank), especially in the high rank coal stage12. As shown in Fig. 8, the VL of coal samples in the study area increases linearly with the increase of Ro, max (R2 = 0.8570). This is because during the coalification process, the chemical structure of coal undergoes a transition from aliphatic to aromatic: hydrogen-rich aliphatic chains and functional groups (such as -CH3, -OH) gradually fall off, and carbon atoms are continuously enriched in aromatic structures to form larger and more ordered aromatic cluster sheet layers43–45. Low rank coal contains a higher proportion of aliphatic components, and its adsorption capacity for methane is weak. The highly aromatic carbon skeleton in high rank coal not only produces a stronger “methane-philic” adsorption potential field, but also directly promotes the development of micropores (< 2nm )46.

Previous studies have shown that when Ro, max is less than 1.4%, aliphatic components mainly control micropores, and their volume decreases with increasing maturity. When Ro, max exceeds 1.4%, the aromatic structure dominates the development of micropores, and the increase of aromatic cluster size directly leads to the rapid increase of micropore volume47. Yan et al.14 showed that the critical Ro, max was 1.5%, while Wang et al.12 identified the critical value to be 1.7%. In any case, previous studies have shown that there is such an inflection point of Ro, max. Beyond the inflection point, the micropores of coal rock begin to develop extensively, and the Ro, max values of deep coal rock samples in the study area are all above 1.9%, which provides superior conditions for the enhancement of CH4 adsorption capacity. Therefore, there is a significant linear positive correlation between VL and Ro, max.

The relationship between material composition and VL

The CH4 adsorption capacity of coal rock is also controlled by the material composition, and changes regularly with the evolution of coal rank. In the evolution process from low rank coal to high rank coal, the vitrinite content increased significantly, while the content of inertinite and exinite decreased correspondingly, and the methane adsorption capacity increased accordingly12,26.

The contribution of different microscopic components to methane adsorption is significantly different, and the adsorption capacity is: vitrinite > inertinite > exinite48. The vitrinite is rich in a large number of micropores and has a large specific surface area, showing a strong adsorption capacity for methane molecules. Therefore, the methane adsorption performance of coal increases significantly with the increase of its content (Fig. 9). The inertinite group mainly develops irregular macropores, and the pore wall spacing is large, resulting in a relatively low adsorption capacity. The overall adsorption capacity of coal decreases with the increase in its content. The content of exinite group in coal is usually low, the pores are not developed, and the overall effect on methane adsorption capacity is weak.

In the process of coalification, with the increase of coal rank, the chemical composition and physical structure of coal have undergone systematic evolution. Its essence is that hydrogen-rich and oxygen-rich organic macromolecules continue to undergo devolatilization, functional group shedding, and structural rearrangement under temperature and pressure conditions, and gradually change to a highly carbon-rich graphitized structure49. The study of Wang et al.12 showed that with the increase of coal rank, the moisture content (Mad) of coal showed a positive U-shaped change, while the ash content (Aad) showed an inverse U-shaped change. Specifically, in the high rank coal stage (Ro, max > 1.7%), with the further increase of Ro, max, the content of Mad increases, and the content of Aad decreases. In the coalification process, as the non-carbon components escape in the form of gas, the volatile matter is reduced, and the carbon element is continuously enriched and ordered in the form of aromatic structure, thereby significantly increasing the content of fixed carbon. This study also verified this law (Fig. 10). The methane adsorption capacity of coal is closely related to its thermal evolution degree. With the increase in maturity, the micropore content increases significantly. Therefore, VL was positively correlated with Mad and FCad, and negatively correlated with Aad and Vad (Fig. 11).

The relationship between pore structure parameters and VL

The highly aromatic carbon skeleton in high-rank coal works synergistically with the developed micropore network to form an efficient methane adsorption reservoir50. As shown in Fig. 12, there is a significant positive correlation between VL and CO2 adsorption capacity (AD-CO2), and a certain negative correlation between VL and N2 adsorption capacity (AD-N2), indicating that with the increase of micropore content, the methane adsorption capacity of deep coal is significantly enhanced. Since the CO2 adsorption capacity is a reliable indicator for characterizing the micropore structure, the above relationship confirms that micropores are the key pore types that control the methane adsorption capacity of coal reservoirs.

Relationship between VL and adsorption capacity.

The correlation analysis results between VL and pore structure parameters are shown in Fig. 13. VL was significantly positively correlated with the pore volume (PV-CO2) and specific surface area (SSA-CO2) obtained from CO2 adsorption experiments (Fig. 13a and b), while negatively correlated with BJH pore volume (PVBJH) and BET specific surface area (SSABET) based on N2 adsorption (Fig. 13c and d). This result is consistent with the research conclusion of Wang et al.12, namely, when the vitrinite reflectance (Ro, max) exceeds 1.7% of the high-rank coal stage, with the further increase of Ro, max, the methane adsorption capacity increases, the micropore content and its proportion increase, the macropore change is not significant, and the mesopore content and its proportion decrease as a whole. Wang et al.12 further confirmed the positive correlation between VL and micropore volume and specific surface area, indicating that micropores are the key pore type that controls methane adsorption capacity. It is worth noting that the correlation between specific surface area and VL (R2 = 0.8773) is stronger than that of pore volume (R2 = 0.8703), which is mainly because adsorbed methane mainly occurs on the pore surface. The more the number of micropores, the greater the specific surface area, and the stronger the adsorption capacity.

The relationship between pore structure heterogeneity and VL

The correlation analysis results between VL and fractal dimension D are shown in Fig. 14. There is a significant positive correlation between VL and fractal dimension (DC) based on CO2 adsorption experiments (Fig. 14a). This is mainly due to the well-developed of deep coal micropores in the study area. On the one hand, micropores become the main place for methane adsorption due to their large specific surface area. A higher micropore content leads to a more complex pore structure, a larger Dc value, and a stronger methane adsorption capacity51. On the other hand, methane adsorption mainly occurs on the pore surface. The stronger the pore surface roughness and heterogeneity (the higher the DC value), the more adsorption sites it provides, and the corresponding adsorption capacity is enhanced52. Therefore, the fractal dimension DC can be used as an important parameter to characterize the methane adsorption capacity of coal reservoirs.

Based on the N2 adsorption data, the fractal dimension was calculated in two relative pressure ranges of P/P0 = 0-0.5 (DN−1) and 0.5-1.0 (DN−2). DN−1 reflects the roughness of pore surface dominated by monolayer adsorption under low pressure, and DN−2 characterizes the complexity of pore structure when capillary condensation occurs under high pressure. The higher the two values, the rougher the pore surface and the more complex the structure. However, this study did not observe a significant correlation between VL and DN−1 or DN−2. It is speculated that the reason is that N2 adsorption is mainly suitable for mesopore-macropore characterization, while the deep coal in this study area has a high degree of thermal evolution and a low content of mesopores and macropores. Therefore, the fractal dimension based on N2 adsorption is difficult to effectively reflect the methane adsorption behavior dominated by micropores.
