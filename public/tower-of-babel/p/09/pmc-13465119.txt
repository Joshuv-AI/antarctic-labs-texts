Salt-saturated supramolecular solvent-assisted dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction for the efficient enrichment and determination of resveratrol in water and grape juice
Authors: Zahra Bagheri, Sayyed Hossein Hashemi, Ahmad Jamali Keikha, Massoud Kaykhaii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA salt-saturated supramolecular solvent–assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (SUPRAS-SS-DLLME) method was developed for the efficient enrichment and spectrophotometric determination of resveratrol in water samples, and grape juice. The extraction strategy is based on the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules into supramolecular solvent architectures, whose extraction capability is significantly enhanced by a salt-induced reduction of analyte solubility in the aqueous phase.ResultsA mixture of NaCl, Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, and KNO3 was employed to achieve salt saturation, resulting in an average enhancement of extraction efficiency of approximately 30% compared to salt-free conditions. Key experimental parameters affecting the extraction process, including pH, extraction solvent volume, and disperser solvent volume, were systematically optimized using response surface methodology. Under optimal conditions, the method exhibited a linear response in the concentration range of 10–5000 µg/L with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9962. The limit of detection and limit of quantification were 1.6 and 5.3 µg/L, respectively, with an enrichment factor of 124. The method demonstrated good precision (RSD < 4.4%) and excellent recoveries (97.3–99.9%).ConclusionThe proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method requires low solvent volumes, short extraction time (< 10 min), and competitive LOD for spectrophotometric detection, in accordance with green analytical chemistry principles. The method was successfully applied to the determination of resveratrol in tap water, wastewater, and red and green grape juice samples, demonstrating its reliability and potential for routine analysis of bioactive compounds in complex matrices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01859-1.

Introduction

Resveratrol (3,5,4′-trihydroxy-trans-stilbene) is a naturally occurring polyphenolic phytoalexin produced by more than 70 plant species, including grapes and peanuts. It has been widely studied due to its reported antioxidant, cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, and chemopreventive properties, demonstrating inhibitory effects on several stages of carcinogenesis including initiation, promotion, and progression. As a major bioactive constituent of grape juice and wine [1], resveratrol has also been associated with the “French Paradox,” referring to the relatively low incidence of coronary heart disease in certain French populations despite a diet rich in saturated fats. However, despite its health benefits, resveratrol exhibits limited aqueous solubility, instability, and rapid metabolism, leading to poor bioavailability [2–4]. Driven by its diverse therapeutic potential, ranging from cardioprotective and anticarcinogenic effects to antiaging properties, quantification methods of resveratrol in various matrices are evolving rapidly. These are including liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry for determination of resveratrol in red wines, peanuts and biological samples [5, 6], gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (wine samples) [7, 8], capillary electrophoresis (natural sources) [9] and paper spray mass spectrometry (red wine) [10]. Although these techniques offer high sensitivity and selectivity, they typically require expensive instrumentation, toxic organic solvents, and does not fully align with green analytical chemistry principles. Spectrophotometric methods, in contrast, are simpler, more economical, and suitable for routine analysis; however, their performance may be compromised by matrix complexity and low analyte levels in real samples. Therefore, efficient sample preparation and preconcentration are often necessary prior to spectrophotometric determination to ensure high sensitivity, accuracy, and matrix cleanup [11, 12].

Dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (DLLME), first introduced by Rezaee and Assadi [13], is a miniaturized sample preparation technique analogous to cloud-point and homogeneous liquid–liquid extraction. In DLLME, a mixture of disperser and extraction solvents is rapidly injected into an aqueous sample, forming a cloudy solution of fine extraction solvent droplets that greatly increase phase contact area and dramatically reduce extraction time. DLLME has been widely applied for the enrichment and determination of numerous analytes and is characterized by short extraction time, high enrichment factors, and low solvent consumption [14].

Supramolecular solvents (SUPRASs) are nanostructured, non-volatile, non-flammable, and cost-effective green solvents formed through the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules. They possess high extraction capability owing to hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, and hydrophobic effects. Long-chain alcohols such as decanol are commonly used as amphiphilic constituents in SUPRAS microextraction. In mixtures of tetrahydrofuran (THF) and water, decanol spontaneously forms reversed micellar aggregates that organize into restricted-access supramolecular structures capable of simultaneously extracting analytes and performing partial matrix cleanup [15–17].

In SUPRAS-based DLLME systems, the supramolecular solvent acts as the extraction medium and THF as the disperser, while vortex agitation can further enhance extraction efficiency by improving mass transfer between phases. Another important strategy to improve extraction efficiency is salting-out. Increasing the ionic strength decreases analyte solubility in the aqueous phase and favours its transfer into the supramolecular solvent. Unlike conventional salting-out procedures, which typically employ a single salt at moderate levels, salt-saturated DLLME (SS-DLLME) applies high concentrations of mixed inorganic salts to intensify this effect and enhance analytical response by promoting stronger interactions and limiting analyte solubility [14].

Despite the extensive application of DLLME and SUPRAS-based extraction approaches, the effect of salt saturation has not yet been widely explored in supramolecular solvent-based DLLME systems.

In this work, we report a salt-saturated supramolecular solvent–assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (SUPRAS-SS-DLLME) strategy for the efficient enrichment of resveratrol prior to spectrophotometric determination. The proposed approach is based on the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules into supramolecular solvent architectures, whose extraction performance is further enhanced through a salt-induced reduction of analyte solubility in the aqueous phase. By integrating supramolecular interactions with dispersive microextraction, the method enables rapid phase formation, high enrichment efficiency, and effective matrix clean-up using low solvent volumes. The experimental conditions affecting the extraction process were systematically optimized using response surface methodology to achieve optimal analytical performance. The applicability and reliability of the developed method were finally demonstrated through the determination of resveratrol in complex matrices, including tap water, wastewater, and red and green grape juice samples.

Experimental

Apparatus

A Unico 2100 S spectrophotometer (China) was used for absorbance measurements. The detection wavelength for spectrophotometric analysis was set at 325 nm. A Metrohm 630 pH meter (Switzerland) was employed to measure the pH of all solutions and samples. For accuracy evaluation of the proposed method, an HPLC system from Knauer (Germany) equipped with an EA4300F Smartline Autosampler 3950 and a diode-array detector (DAD) was employed. Chromatographic detection of resveratrol was carried out at 325 nm. Separation was achieved using a 250 × 4.6 mm Eurospher 100-5 C18 analytical column coupled with a corresponding guard column of the same stationary phase. The injection volume was 20 µL. ChromGate V3.1.7 software was used for chromatographic data acquisition and processing.

Reagents

Resveratrol (purity ≥ 99%) was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemicals were of analytical grade and purchased from Merck Company (Darmstadt, Germany). A 500 mg/L stock solution of resveratrol was prepared by dissolving 50 mg of the analyte in 0.5 mL of methanol, followed by dilution to 100 mL with ultra-pure water. Standard solutions were freshly prepared daily by appropriate dilution of the stock solution in ultra-pure water. The pH of the solutions was adjusted using either 1 M HCl or 1 M NaOH, as required.

Chromatographic conditions

An isocratic elution was performed using a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (45:55, v/v). The flow rate was maintained at 1.0 mL/min with UV detection to ensure the accuracy of the technique.

SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure

Figure 1 schematically illustrates the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure. A 10 mL aliquot of standard solution or sample containing resveratrol was transferred into a 50 mL Falcon tube, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0. The solution was then sequentially saturated with a mixture of 1.12 g NaCl, 1.74 g Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, and 3.32 g KNO3. Under these conditions, a small amount of undissolved material (sediment) was observed, indicating that the mixed-salt system had reached the salt-saturated state. These salts were dissolved under room-temperature conditions prior to extraction. The approximate solubilities of the individual salts at room temperature are 360 g/L for NaCl, 420 g/L for Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, and 383 g/L for KNO3. The selected salt amounts were intended to create a high-ionic-strength environment and enhance the salting-out effect rather than achieve independent thermodynamic saturation of each salt individually. The combination of mono- and divalent cations (Na⁺, K⁺, and Mg2⁺) together with different anions (Cl⁻ and NO3⁻) enhances ionic strength and promotes a stronger salting-out effect compared to single-salt systems, while also maintaining solution stability and preventing premature crystallization.

To generate the supramolecular solvent phase, 300 µL of 1-decanol and 300 µL of THF were added to the solution. The mixture was vortexed for 60 s, producing a cloudy solution, and subsequently centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 4 min to separate the supramolecular solvent phase from the aqueous phase. Finally, 50 µL of upper supramolecular solvent layer was collected by a microsyringe and introduced to the spectrophotometer for analysis.

Although detailed physicochemical characterization of the supramolecular solvent phase (e.g., droplet size distribution, zeta potential, and microscopic morphology) was beyond the scope of the present analytical study, the observed extraction enhancement is consistent with the formation of reversed micellar supramolecular aggregates and salt-induced strengthening of analyte partitioning into the SUPRAS phase.

Real samples preparation

The protocol was applied to real samples, including wastewater, tap water, red and green grape juices, to evaluate its performance in complex media.

Tap water samples were filtered through a 450 μm nylon membrane filter to remove suspended solids, with no further treatments applied. Wastewater samples were obtained from municipal sewage and a student dormitory. These samples were filtered under vacuum pressure utilizing 11 μm particle-size retention glass fiber filters (Whatman No. 1, Sigma-Aldrich, Castle Hill, NSW, Australia). The filters were rinsed with methanol and deionized water prior to use to prevent cross-contamination. Sample preparation (for red and green grape) followed a modified version of the procedure described by Wang et al. [18]. Briefly, 5 mL of THF was added to 1 g aliquots of each juice sample. After stirring overnight, the mixtures were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 6 min. The supernatants were collected and evaporated using a rotary evaporator, and the resulting residues were redissolved in 10 mL of deionized water. The sample preparation time for grapes was 12 h and 30 min. Samples were protected from direct light during preparation. Additionally, to evaluate the effect of real sample preparation, various concentrations of the analyte (100, 2500, and 4000 µg/L) were spiked, yielding recoveries greater than 99%. These recoveries reflect the performance of the entire analytical workflow, including sample preparation, solvent extraction, evaporation, reconstitution, and SUPRAS-SS-DLLME enrichment. Then, the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure was applied to the prepared solution under the optimized conditions.

Results and discussion

Optimization studies

The efficiency of the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure is influenced by several experimental parameters that greatly effect supramolecular solvent formation, phase separation, and analyte partitioning. In the present study, the effects of pH, supramolecular solvent composition (volumes of decanol and THF), vortex time, and centrifugation conditions were systematically investigated. Following optimization, the developed method was applied to the determination of resveratrol in real samples including tap water, wastewater, and red and green grape juices. All optimization experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) to ensure reproducibility, and the corresponding results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Effect of vortex time

Vortex-assisted dispersion plays a critical role in enhancing mass transfer between the aqueous phase and the supramolecular solvent. The effect of vortex time on extraction efficiency was investigated over the range of 15–90 s. The analytical response increased with vortex time up to approximately 60 s and showed no significant further enhancement at longer extraction times, suggesting that extraction equilibrium or a near-equilibrium state was effectively achieved under the optimized conditions. Consequently, a vortex time of 60 s was selected for subsequent experiments. This short extraction time and neutral pH conditions reduce the likelihood of resveratrol degradation during sample preparation.

Effect of centrifugation time and rate

Efficient phase separation is essential for achieving high extraction recovery in DLLME methods. The influence of centrifugation time (1–6 min) and centrifugation rate (3000–6000 rpm) on the extraction efficiency of resveratrol was therefore examined. The results showed that the analytical response increased progressively with increasing centrifugation time and rate, reaching a plateau at 4 min and 5000 rpm. Beyond these values, no further improvement in extraction efficiency was observed. Accordingly, a centrifugation time of 4 min at 5000 rpm was selected as optimal. These conditions provide effective separation of the supramolecular solvent phase from the aqueous phase while maintaining rapid overall analysis. Under the optimized conditions, the total extraction time of the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure was approximately 7 min, highlighting the suitability of the method for rapid routine analysis.

Response surface methodology optimization

To evaluate the combined effects of key experimental variables and their interactions on the extraction efficiency of resveratrol, response surface methodology based on a Box–Behnken experimental design was applied. The investigated variables included pH (X1 or A), volume of the supramolecular extraction solvent decanol (X2 or B), and volume of the disperser solvent tetrahydrofuran (THF) (X3 or C). Each factor was examined at three coded levels (−1, 0, and +1), corresponding to low, middle, and high values, respectively. Method validation

The analytical performance of the optimized SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method was evaluated in terms of linearity, sensitivity, precision, enrichment factor (EF), limit of quantification (LOQ), and limit of detection (LOD). External calibration curves were constructed over the concentration range of 10–5000 µg/L, yielding a linear regression equation of A = 0.3499 C + 0.1093 with a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.9962 with a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.9962, indicating excellent linearity. (A=instrument response; C=concentration of the analyte). LOD and LOQ were calculated based on signal-to-noise ratios corresponding to 3Sd/m and 10Sd/m, respectively, where Sd represents the standard deviation of ten consecutive measurements of a blank solution [16] and determined to be 1.6 and 5.3 µg/L, respectively, demonstrating the adequate sensitivity of the proposed method for spectrophotometric detection.

The EF was calculated as the ratio of the concentration of resveratrol in the collected supramolecular solvent phase CSUPRAS to its initial concentration in the aqueous sample C0 (EF=CSUPRAS/C0) and found to be 124. After centrifugation, approximately 50 µL of the upper SUPRAS phase was collected using a microsyringe and analyzed spectrophotometrically. This high EF confirms the strong preconcentration capability of the developed microextraction method. Precision was assessed in terms of repeatability (intra-day precision) and precision (inter-day precision) by analysing spiked samples at three selected concentration levels. Intra-day precision, evaluated from five replicate measurements performed on the same day, resulted in relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 4.4%. Inter-day precision, assessed over three consecutive days, yielded RSD values below 5.9%. These results indicate good method precision and reproducibility. The accuracy of the proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method was evaluated through recovery studies using spiked samples. Recoveries obtained at different concentration levels ranged from 98.0% to 99.9%, confirming the reliability of the method for quantitative analysis.

The results demonstrate that the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method offers competitive sensitivity and precision while benefiting from operational simplicity, shorter extraction times, a better LOD, a wider linear range, lower RSD, and reduced solvent consumption. Comparison of the performance of extraction with and without salt saturation

The performance of SUPRAS-SS-DLLME, supramolecular solvent-based liquid–liquid microextraction (SUPRAS-LLME), and conventional salting-out (addition of NaCl) was compared for the extraction of resveratrol at concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.5, and 5.0 mg/L under optimized conditions. As shown in Fig. 3, saturation of the solution with a mixture of salts significantly enhanced the analytical signal, indicating improved extraction recovery. A preliminary comparison with a single-salt system (NaCl) showed lower extraction efficiency, indicating that the mixed salt system provides a more effective salting-out environment; however, a detailed investigation of individual salt contributions was beyond the scope of this study.

Fig. 3Comparison of the extraction performance of resveratrol with and without salt saturation in the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure

Comparison of the extraction performance of resveratrol with and without salt saturation in the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure

To evaluate the influence of salt saturation on extraction efficiency, supramolecular solvent–based dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (SUPRAS-DLLME), conventional salting-out (addition of NaCl) and SUPRAS-SS-DLLME were compared under identical experimental conditions. Standard solutions of resveratrol at concentrations of 100, 500, 1000, 2500, and 5000 µg/L were subjected to both extraction procedures using the optimized parameters. As Fig. 3 shows, the presence of salts resulted in a consistently higher analytical response across the entire concentration range investigated. On average, an enhancement of approximately 30% in extraction efficiency was observed when salt saturation was applied. This improvement is attributed to the salting-out effect, whereby the increased ionic strength of the aqueous phase reduces the solubility of resveratrol and promotes its transfer into the supramolecular solvent phase. In the salt-saturated system, the use of a mixture of inorganic salts (NaCl, Mg(NO3)2 ·6H2O, and KNO3) introduces a diverse population of ions, which further intensifies the salting-out effect compared to the use of a single salt. The resulting decrease in analyte - water interactions enhances analyte - SUPRAS interactions, leading to improved partitioning into the extraction phase. The enhanced extraction efficiency observed in the salt-saturated system can also be attributed to synergistic ion-specific effects. In particular, Mg2+, due to its higher charge density, exhibits a stronger ability to structure water molecules and reduce analyte solubility, thereby intensifying the salting-out effect. Furthermore, the coexistence of chloride and nitrate ions provides a balanced ionic environment that improves phase separation and analyte partitioning into the supramolecular solvent phase. These results clearly demonstrate that salt saturation plays a significant role in improving the extraction performance of supramolecular solvent–based microextraction systems and validates the incorporation of salt saturation as a key component of the proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method. The salt concentrations were selected to achieve saturation conditions rather than being individually optimized, in order to maximize ionic strength and the salting-out effect. Under these conditions, an enhancement of 30.2 ± 1.1% increase in analytical response was observed. Statistical evaluation using a Student’s t-test confirmed that this improvement is significant (p < 0.05), with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).

Comparison of the performance of SUPRAS-SS-DLLME and supramolecular solvent-based reversed-phase DLLME

The performance of the proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method was further evaluated by comparison with a supramolecular solvent–based reversed-phase dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (SUPRAS-RDLLME) approach. In the SUPRAS-RDLLME procedure, resveratrol was first reacted with 4-aminoantipyrine in decanol, and the resulting complex was subsequently microextracted into an aqueous extraction phase. For this purpose, 10.0 mL of standard solution (containing various concentrations of resveratrol in decanol) was transferred to a glass test tube with an elongated conical bottom. In a microvial, 200 µL of THF (as dispersive solvent) was mixed with 50 µL of 0.7 M NaOH solution (as extraction solvent), and the mixture was rapidly injected into the standard solution. This step caused the extraction solvent to disperse into the sample as fine droplets, forming a cloudy solution. The glass test tube was then placed inside a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 8 min. The sedimented phase was transferred to a microvial, after which 50 µL of 0.9% w/v 4-aminoantipyrine and 50 µL of 1.4% w/v potassium peroxodisulfate were added. The pH was adjusted to 9.5, and the mixture was shaken in a water bath at 30 °C for 10 min. Finally, 50 µL of the resulting aqueous phase was withdrawn and analysed spectrophotometrically. As shown in Fig. 4, the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method provided a clearly higher analytical response than the SUPRAS-RDLLME approach under comparable conditions. This enhanced performance may be attributed to the direct partitioning of resveratrol into the supramolecular organic phase formed in situ, eliminating the need for chemical derivatization and multiple transfer steps. In contrast, the reversed-phase procedure requires additional reaction and extraction stages, which may reduce overall extraction efficiency and increase procedural complexity. Furthermore, the salt-saturated supramolecular system promotes stronger analyte partitioning due to the combined effects of supramolecular interactions and reduced aqueous solubility, leading to improved enrichment. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method offers superior extraction efficiency, simplicity, and analytical sensitivity compared with the SUPRAS-RDLLME technique.

Selectivity in the presence of interferences

The selectivity of the proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method was evaluated in the presence of compounds that commonly coexist with resveratrol in real matrices, particularly phenolic compounds with similar chemical structures. Curcumin, fructose, glucose, citric acid, and quercetin were selected as representative potential interferences due to their comparable aromatic and polyphenolic characteristics. To assess possible interference effects, aliquots of 10 mL standard solutions containing 1000 µg/L of resveratrol were spiked with equal concentrations and concentration ratios (10:1 interferent-to-analyte) of each interferent and subjected to the optimized SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure. The analytical responses obtained in the presence of these compounds were compared with those obtained for resveratrol alone. No significant change in the analytical signal of resveratrol was observed, indicating that the presence of the mentioned compounds does not interfere with the extraction or spectrophotometric determination of resveratrol under the optimized conditions (Table SI1). These results show that the proposed method exhibits satisfactory selectivity for resveratrol, even in the presence of structurally related compounds, supporting its applicability to complex matrices such as juices. As shown in Fig. 5, the UV–Vis spectrum obtained after extraction exhibits a well-defined absorption maximum at approximately 325 nm, corresponding to resveratrol. The absence of additional distinct absorption bands or significant spectral distortion suggests minimal interference from co-extracted matrix components. This observation is consistent with the selective extraction capability of supramolecular solvents, which preferentially isolate target analytes based on specific intermolecular interactions. Statistical comparison using Student’s t-test showed that the differences between the analytical signal of resveratrol alone and in the presence of the tested interferents were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Accuracy of the protocol

To evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME spectrophotometric method, the obtained results were compared with those achieved using the HPLC-DAD reference method. Real samples including red grape juice, green grape juice, wastewater, and tap water were analyzed after spiking with known concentrations of resveratrol. The concentrations determined by the proposed method were in good agreement with those obtained by HPLC analysis, with no statistically significant difference observed at the 95% confidence level according to Student’s t-test (p > 0.05). In addition, the satisfactory recoveries and low RSD values obtained for both methods confirmed that the proposed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME procedure provides accurate and reliable determination of resveratrol in complex matrices. These results demonstrate that the developed spectrophotometric approach can serve as a simple and cost-effective alternative to chromatographic analysis for routine applications.

Greenness considerations of the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method

The environmental profile of the developed SUPRAS-SS-DLLME method was evaluated according to the principles of Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC). Although THF is classified as a hazardous organic solvent, the method utilizes only a micro-volume amount (300 µL per extraction), thereby significantly reducing solvent consumption, hazardous waste generation, and operator exposure compared with conventional extraction approaches. In addition, the method completely avoids chlorinated solvents and does not require derivatization, heating, or complex clean-up procedures.

To provide a quantitative assessment of greenness, the proposed method was evaluated using the Analytical GREEnness metric, yielding an overall score of 0.78, indicating good compliance with GAC principles. The favorable score is mainly attributed to low solvent usage, short extraction time (< 10 min), minimal waste generation, spontaneous formation of the supramolecular solvent phase without additional synthesis steps, and the use of UV–Vis spectrophotometric detection with low energy requirements.

These results demonstrate that the SUPRAS-SS-DLLME approach represents a relatively sustainable and environmentally considerate alternative for routine resveratrol determination in aqueous and juice samples.

From a practical perspective, this method offers lower consumable cost than conventional SPE methods because no extraction cartridges or sorbent materials are required. In addition, the total extraction procedure requires less than approximately 10 min, allowing relatively high sample throughput. Compared with QuEChERS-based approaches, the method also involves lower solvent consumption and fewer clean-up steps, thereby simplifying the analytical workflow.

Conclusion

This study presents a robust and efficient salt-saturated supramolecular solvent–assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction strategy for the enrichment and spectrophotometric determination of resveratrol in tap water, wastewater, and green and red grape juice. The method exploits the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules into supramolecular solvent architectures, which, in combination with a salt-induced phase separation effect, enables rapid and highly efficient transfer of the target molecule from aqueous media into a compact extraction phase. The proposed approach provides high enrichment efficiency, acceptable LOD for routine analysis, good precision, and excellent recoveries, demonstrating its applicability across chemically diverse matrices. The use of low solvent volumes, short extraction time, and simple UV–Vis detection contributes to reduced solvent consumption, minimal waste generation, and operational simplicity, supporting the method’s alignment with key principles of green analytical chemistry. Beyond its analytical performance, this work highlights the functional role of supramolecular solvent self-assembly in enhancing extraction efficiency through cooperative molecular interactions, offering insight into the structure–function relationship of supramolecular systems in sample preparation. The developed strategy may be readily extended to the extraction and analysis of other bioactive compounds with limited aqueous solubility, suggesting broader applicability of salt-saturated supramolecular microextraction systems in various samples.
