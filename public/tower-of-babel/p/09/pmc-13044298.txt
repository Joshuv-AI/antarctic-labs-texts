Introduction

COPD, including emphysema, remains a significant contributor to global morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Emphysema is characterised by irreversible alveolar wall destruction, leading to hyperinflation, impaired gas exchange and progressive respiratory failure. Despite advances in medical therapy, many patients with severe emphysema continue to experience debilitating dyspnoea, reduced exercise capacity and diminished quality of life (QoL).

Lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) offers the potential to restore the overinflated lung to a more normal size, by targeting excess residual volume (RV). In a well-selected subset of severe emphysema patients, already on optimal conventional therapy, LVRS improves lung function, exercise capacity and QoL, and has the potential to delay or prevent lung transplantation [4].

The National Emphysema Treatment Trial (NETT) provided crucial insights into the efficacy and safety of LVRS [5, 6]. However, it also demonstrated high 90-day mortality in patients with diffuse emphysema and low baseline exercise capacity. As a result, LVRS was reserved for a highly selective subset of patients: primarily those with heterogeneous, upper lobe predominant emphysema and low exercise tolerance.

LVRS has evolved significantly since the NETT trial. Improved thoracoscopic techniques, better understanding of chest computed tomography (CT) emphysema morphology, thorough multidisciplinary patient selection [7] and the implementation of an enhanced recovery protocol (ERP) [8] have led to better patient outcomes, and the risks associated with LVRS have been reduced.

Although morbidity and mortality are no longer of major concern in experienced centres, the use of LVRS remains limited [9, 10] and many eligible patients are not considered for this potentially life-changing intervention. Furthermore, heterogeneous upper lobe emphysema remains the preferred morphology for treatment but patients exhibiting different emphysema patterns have also been treated successfully [11]. Given these novel insights and progressive improvements in current LVRS procedures, it is key to monitor its impact on patient outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate single-centre outcomes of a consecutive 5-year series, including patients beyond the classical NETT criteria (non-upper lobe and non-heterogeneous morphology). The primary objective was to evaluate post-operative mortality and morbidity. Pulmonary function, exercise tolerance and QoL were evaluated up to 3 years post-intervention.

Patients and methods

Ethical statement

The prospective observational study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee UZ/KU Leuven (Leuven, Belgium) on 22 January 2021 (S64530). Data were obtained after individual informed consent.

Study population and design

This single-centre study included all consecutive patients who underwent elective LVRS at University Hospitals Leuven (Leuven, Belgium) from 1 August 2019 to 31 July 2024. Redo procedures, bullectomies and exploratory cases were excluded.

The multidisciplinary LVR programme started with the introduction of endobronchial valves in 2017 [12] and an LVRS-tailored ERP was implemented in 2019 (supplementary figure S1) [8, 12]. Our eligibility criteria were previously reported [8]. In summary, we included patients who were ex-smokers, symptomatic, under optimal medical and non-pharmacological therapy, who had severe airflow obstruction (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) <45% pred) and hyperinflation (RV >150% pred, total lung capacity (TLC) >100% pred and RV/TLC >55%). Additionally, patients needed to have diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) >20% pred (one patient with DLCO 18% pred), without bronchiectasis, no more than two moderate or severe exacerbations in the past year and no comorbid disease that would render them unfit for surgery. Morphology was classified based on multidisciplinary evaluation of the CT scan according to Weder et al. [13] as 1) markedly heterogeneous, when a significant regional difference in emphysema severity occurs in at least two adjacent lung segments; 2) intermediately heterogeneous, when differences may occur in one or more non-adjacent segments; and 3) homogeneous, where differences in regional emphysema severity are minimal or absent (supplementary figure S2). Quantitative CT analysis with StratX measurement was applied as standard for each case but was not used for surgical decision making, as it primarily reports interlobar heterogeneity and does not account for intralobar variation, which is critical for resectability assessment. Perfusion scintigraphy was assessed to confirm the target zone.

Patients with homogeneous emphysema required well-preserved DLCO and FEV1 as well as marked hyperinflation with RV >200% pred. Additionally, we also evaluate patients at the opposite end of the spectrum, extending criteria to include FEV1 >45% pred and 6MWD >450 m [14].

Surgical procedure

The patients were divided into three groups according to surgical intervention: unilateral, one-stage bilateral or staged bilateral. The option of a two-stage procedure was considered pre- or intra-operatively in case of extensive pleural adhesions, hypercapnia, cardiopulmonary instability or extended surgical time, and sequential bilateral treatment was carried out through a delayed approach.

All procedures were performed by the same surgeon using video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), employing the “no-touch” concept with minimally invasive instruments to avoid tissue damage during manipulation. Stapling was performed with the Signia stapling system and Tri-Staple 2.0 Reinforced Reload (purple 45 and 60 mm) (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Polyglycolic acid sheet (Neoveil; Gunze, Kyoto, Japan) and Progel Pleural Air Leak Sealant (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) were systematically used since 2022. Patients with homogeneous morphology underwent a bilateral “horseshoe-like” resection of the upper lobe apex, an approach aimed at relieving hyperinflation and restoring diaphragmatic respiratory mechanics in carefully selected patients with marked hyperinflation, preserved gas exchange (DLCO >20% pred) and without significant cardiac comorbidity. To better delineate the target volume intra-operatively, resection is performed on a semi-inflated lung, achieved by pausing ventilation just before incision, leaving the most destroyed parenchymal areas inflated due to airway obstruction.

Surgical outcomes

Surgical outcomes included surgical targets, number of staplers, complications (Clavien–Dindo classification), chest tube duration (days), prolonged air leak (PAL) (>7 days), length of stay (days), and in-hospital, 30- and 90-day mortality.

Functional outcomes

Pulmonary function testing, including spirometry and body plethysmography, was performed pre- and post-operatively at 3 and 6 months, and then yearly according to the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines [15–18]. Exercise tolerance was assessed with a standard 6-min walk distance (6MWD) test; the pre-operative test was performed after completion of an outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation programme. The post-operative 6MWD measurements were obtained at 3 and 6 months, and then annually. At follow-up, dyspnoea was assessed by the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea scale; patient-reported QoL assessment by the patient used the St George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) and COPD Assessment Test (CAT).

Statistical analysis

Procedures or patients were compared based on type of analysis. Surgical outcomes were assessed based on number of procedures. Functional outcomes were evaluated for each individual patient during follow-up (in two-stage LVRS, after the second procedure).

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are described as numbers (percentage) and comparisons were conducted using Fisher's exact test. Functional outcomes were compared with baseline up to 3 years after treatment. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for within-group comparisons and the Holm–Bonferroni method was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. In the subgroup analysis, between-group comparisons for continuous data were executed using the non-paired Mann–Whitney U-test.

The analysis was restricted to complete cases, including only participants with available measurements at both baseline and the relevant follow-up time-point. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Macintosh version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and graphical representations were produced with Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA), with a significance level of 0.05.

Subanalysis

Patients were divided according to morphology into markedly heterogeneous and non-markedly heterogeneous emphysema. Surgical target zones were grouped into isolated upper lobe and non-isolated upper lobe emphysema.

Markedly heterogeneous versus non-markedly heterogeneous morphology

In unilateral procedures, morphology was markedly heterogeneous if present on the treated lung side. In bilateral procedures, LVRS was classified as markedly heterogeneous if both lungs were affected; otherwise, it was considered non-markedly heterogeneous.

Isolated upper lobe versus non-isolated upper lobe

Unilateral procedures were classified as isolated upper lobe, when the target zone for resection, as assessed on CT, was in the upper lobe of the treated lung. Bilateral procedures were classified as upper lobe if both upper lobes were treated, lower lobe if both lower lobes were treated and combined if multiple lobes were treated, all according to the target zones. Upper lobe combined with middle lobe was categorised as upper lobe.

Results

Study population

A total of 253 LVRS procedures were performed at University Hospitals Leuven between 1 August 2019 and 31 July 2024. 13 procedures were urgent and excluded. Of 240 elective procedures, 11 (4.6%) bullectomies, four (1.7%) explorative (extensive adhesions) and two (0.8%) redo LVRS cases were excluded. The remaining 223 LVRS procedures in 191 patients were included in our analysis (figure 1). Median follow-up was 19 months. Over the same timeframe 554 patients were discussed at our multidisciplinary emphysema meeting, of which 83 were treated by endoscopic LVR (supplementary figure S3).

Functional outcomes

Paired pre- and post-operative data at 3, 6, 12, 24 and 36 months were available for 191, 176, 159, 125, 76 and 42 patients, respectively. Loss to follow-up for these time-points was limited, with number and reason for dropout summarised in supplementary table S2. At 1 year, pulmonary function compared to baseline improved regarding FEV1 (relative improvement: +18.4% to 37% pred (30–51% pred); p<0.001) and RV reduction (−24.5% to 172% pred (141–199% pred); p<0.001). 6MWD was 414 (321–486) m (absolute improvement: +56 m) (p<0.001), mMRC dyspnoea scale improved to 1 (1–2) from 3 (2–4) (p<0.001) and SGRQ score was 45 (34–56) (absolute reduction: −17) (p<0.001).

At 3 years, patients in follow-up (22%) had persistent relative improvements in pulmonary function (FEV1 +17.2% up to 38% pred (29–48% pred); p<0.001; RV −19.5% to 173% pred (148–199% pred); p=0.002); as well as CAT score (absolute reduction: −2) 20 (17–24) (p=0.018), mMRC dyspnoea scale 2 (1–3) from 3 (2–4) (p<0.001) and SGRQ score (−7) 55 (39–68) (p=0.046) (table 3 and figure 2). Five patients were transplanted during follow-up with a median time to transplantation of 731 (571–1031) days after LVRS. Median values achieved minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) for FEV1 and RV at all time-points, for 6MWD at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months, and for mMRC dyspnoea scale at all time-points (table 3).

Discussion

This 5-year prospective study of consecutively treated emphysema patients by LVRS shows almost zero mortality and limited morbidity. It demonstrates significant clinical, functional and QoL improvements. Moreover, carefully selected patients beyond classical NETT criteria, including those with non-markedly heterogeneous or non-upper lobe predominant emphysema, can also benefit comparably from LVRS as the classic upper lobe heterogeneous morphology candidates.

More than 25 years ago, NETT, the largest randomised controlled trial on LVRS, compared surgery with medical therapy in patients with severe emphysema. In an early analysis, a small high-risk subgroup was defined: patients with FEV1 ≤20% pred and either DLCO ≤20% pred or homogeneous emphysema. This group showed a 30-day post-operative mortality of 16% compared to 0% in the medically treated group [5]. Although these patients were subsequently excluded from randomisation, it was this early finding that stuck in the mind of many clinicians. As a result, LVRS was widely perceived as dangerous, and largely abandoned in centres around the world. However, the completed study, which included 1218 randomised patients [6], demonstrated that LVRS significantly improves lung function, exercise capacity and QoL, especially in patients with upper lobe predominant emphysema and low baseline exercise tolerance. Importantly, the study also showed a survival benefit in appropriately selected surgical candidates.

Following the trial, the number of LVRS procedures dropped significantly worldwide with minimal research executed, except for a few centres that continued to carry out procedures [19–21]. In these centres, notable advancements and modifications have occurred, including improvement in patient selection, surgical approach and CT scan interpretation, which have been shown to substantially increase the efficacy of emphysema surgery [22, 23].

Initial large LVRS series reported in-hospital mortality ranging from 4% [24] to 5.6% [25] and despite the adoption of more minimally invasive techniques such as VATS, more recent series reported similar rates, e.g. Seadler et al. [19] in 2019 reporting an in-hospital mortality of 5% and Buttery et al. [26] in 2024 reporting a 90-day mortality of 6%. The best reported outcomes were by Weder et al. [20] in 2009, with a 30-day mortality of 2.4%, and Horwood et al. [21], reporting a 90-day mortality of 2.2%. The CELEB trial (41 LVRS procedures) reported one death at 5 months, and demonstrated meaningful and durable improvements in QoL and functional capacity after LVRS, equal to patients treated by endobronchial valves [27]. In our cohort, the mortality rate post-LVRS was exceptionally low, with a 30-day mortality of 0.5% (one patient) and a 5-year patient survival of 84.7%, exceeding the mortality rates anticipated from the natural course of the disease [28]. For comparison, patients who were evaluated but not accepted for LVR had a 5-year survival of 74.1%.

In recent years, a multidisciplinary team has been implemented at our centre with the aim of improving LVRS outcomes through a structured framework to optimise patient selection for LVRS and endobronchial valve procedures. As an integral component of this initiative, the ERP adopted from Zurich was further developed [8]. Several factors that exert a key role can be identified.

First, the implementation of a multidisciplinary team of surgeons, pulmonologists, radiologists and anaesthesiologists involved to select the optimal candidates for surgical intervention substantially contributes to a lower intervention risk. Moreover, quantitative CT improves selection by assessing emphysema heterogeneity and supporting tailored treatment, although it requires experience for evaluating surgical resectability. In the future, single-photon emission CT (SPECT) could help to improve assessment of the pulmonary vascular bed. During the reported study period, only one in three patients presented at the multidisciplinary board was referred for LVRS. We excluded patients with a recent history of repeated exacerbations, bronchiectasis, moderate-to-severe pulmonary hypertension and excessive comorbidities. Patients non-compliant to optimised medical therapy, smoking cessation (for ≥6 months) or active rehabilitation were also renounced from LVRS.

Second, surgical morbidity remains minimal, with an infection rate of 2.2%. This can be attributed to the implementation of a detailed ERP, optimisation of surgical and anaesthesiological techniques, focusing on prevention and early treatment of PAL, limited to 2% in the last 20% of patients. This resulted in a chest tube duration of 4 days and a hospital stay of 7 days. In contrast, Ciccone et al. [29] reported PAL in 45%, infections in 10% and need for tracheostomy in 4%, although their findings are based on procedures performed >20 years ago by median sternotomy. More recent series such as Seadler et al. [19] reported PAL in 59% of 123 bilateral VATS LVRS cases, with 30% lasting >14 days. Horwood et al. [21] observed complications in 35% of patients, most commonly PAL (17%), pneumonia (5.2%) and respiratory failure or reintubation (5.1%), with reintervention in 5.1%. In the most recently reported LVRS series, chest tube duration varied between 7 and 9 days with a hospital stay of 8–15 days [19, 21, 22, 30].

Third, although LVRS has traditionally been recommended for upper lobe predominant emphysema and markedly heterogeneous lung destruction, our experience supports the previous finding [20] that it can be successfully applied to other morphologies as well. This is especially pertinent when factors such as TLC, DLCO and hyperinflation are meticulously considered in patient selection.

Emphysema distribution has been argued to be a significant determinant of physiological outcomes following LVRS, with heterogeneity often being considered as more effective than homogeneity, despite recent reports that indicate potential benefits for these patients [20, 31]. A critical point to consider is that homogeneous emphysema can only be considered for LVRS in cases with marked hyperinflation and better preserved DLCO >25% pred. The goal is to remodel both lungs to the predicted volume to allow the diaphragm to regain its normal shape and improve the breathing mechanics. Our subanalysis revealed no increased risk in post-operative complications in the non-markedly heterogeneous emphysema group. However, we observed greater improvements in FEV1 at all time intervals evaluated in the markedly heterogeneous group, although functional outcome and QoL improvements did not differ between groups. The difference in FEV1 may partly be explained by variations in lung mechanics. In heterogeneous emphysema, regional volume reduction could lead to localised changes in compliance and increased alveolar pressure, potentially improving expiratory flow. Additionally, better preserved and partially compressed lung areas in markedly heterogeneous emphysema may experience less residual hyperinflation after LVRS compared to homogeneous disease. In homogeneous cases, similar volume reductions may not significantly alter compliance, possibly resulting in improved forced vital capacity without corresponding changes in FEV1.

In the NETT trial, patients with non-upper lobe emphysema and higher exercise capacity had higher mortality after surgery compared to medical therapy (risk ratio 2.06; p=0.02). In 2017, Perikleous et al. [32] reported on 36 patients undergoing lower lobe LVRS via VATS, but lung function returned to baseline after 24 months. Our findings show that carefully selected patients with non-upper lobe isolated disease can achieve similar long-term benefits (as seen in pulmonary function, exercise capacity and QoL) as those with upper lobe targets, without increased surgical risk. A key difference we suggest is the surgical approach: in NETT, only 20% of procedures were minimally invasive, while most were performed open via median sternotomy, limiting lower lobe access. With VATS, lower lobe access can be optimised by performing an anterior approach at intercostal level 8–9 and release of the pulmonary ligament. Moreover, most NETT centres performed fewer than 10 cases annually, whereas our centre consistently performs 50–80 cases per year.

Despite these promising results, it should be realised that LVRS remains a specialised procedure that requires an understanding of the physiology related to emphysema, meticulous attention to detail and the intervention performed by a highly experienced multidisciplinary team to ensure both patient safety and optimal outcomes.

The study has limitations. Strict selection criteria reduce generalisability to wider populations. Moreover, in the evaluation of procedural results, any possible correlations between surgical outcome for patients undergoing either bilateral or staged operations were not considered. Furthermore, the interpretation of emphysema morphology on CT, both for patient selection and identification of targets for resection, is based on the physician's experience and not standardised by software. The study reflects the experience of a single surgeon, which may limit reproducibility, although efforts are ongoing to train surgeons and harmonise the new standard across centres. The ongoing refinement of our ERP resulted in variability across the study period. Long-term data are limited, but dropout rates are low. Most patients without 2/3-year follow-up data were not lost, but had not yet reach the time-point due to later inclusion. Therefore, the apparent attrition should not be interpreted as true dropout, but rather is because of the prospective design and ongoing patient recruitment. This distinction is important when interpreting long-term outcome data and supports the overall robustness of follow-up in this cohort. Follow-up loss is also due to patients living further away in Belgium and the Netherlands, with limited possibility to return for evaluation.

In conclusion, based on our growing LVRS experience resulting in significant improvements for our patients with limited morbidity and almost no mortality, we are confident that this will lead to a resurgence of appropriate and “extended” use of the procedure, provided it is performed within expert centres or structured networks with established multidisciplinary expertise to ensure patient safety.
