Does preoperative anxiety influence endodontic skill performance and students’ subjective perceptions in an observational simulation-based study?
Authors: Eren Var, Yaren Cam, Eren Pektas, Cihan Kuden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative state anxiety is known to influence student performance across diverse dental educational contexts. Yet, its specific impact on root canal treatment outcomes in simulation-based environments remains insufficiently explored. This study investigated the association between preoperative anxiety and students’ technical performance and subjective outcomes during their first root canal treatment on extracted molars.MethodsNinety-three dental students performed root canal treatments on extracted permanent mandibular and maxillary molars. Prior to each procedure, students completed the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-State (STAI-S) to assess preoperative state anxiety levels. Scores were recorded before the procedure and grouped during the analysis as low (< 44) or high (≥ 45) anxiety. Preclinical performance was evaluated based on total procedure score (access, shaping, obturation, apical accuracy, and cleanliness), treatment duration, distance to the apex, and occurrence of procedural errors. Post-procedure measures included STAI-S, perceived procedural difficulty, self-confidence, and self-reported comprehension. Statistical comparisons were conducted using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests (t-test, Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank, chi-square/Fisher’s exact), correlations were examined with Spearman’s rank test, and inter-rater reliability was assessed with Cohen’s Kappa (α = 0.05).ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed between low- and high-anxiety groups in total root canal treatment performance scores for either mandibular or maxillary molars (p > 0.05). Treatment duration also showed no meaningful variation between groups (p > 0.05). However, during mandibular procedures, the incidence of over-instrumentation was higher in the low-anxiety group (p = 0.040), whereas a non-significant trend toward increased instrument separation was noted in the high-anxiety group (p = 0.060). High-anxiety groups showed a significant post-procedure decline in anxiety, though scores stayed above the clinical threshold. For subjective outcomes, high-anxiety students reported lower self-confidence during mandibular procedures and lower comprehension ratings during maxillary procedures (p < 0.05). Correlation analyses further indicated that higher anxiety was weakly associated with lower self-confidence in mandibular procedures, and with reduced comprehension and greater perceived obturation difficulty in maxillary procedures.ConclusionPreoperative state anxiety did not compromise technical performance outcomes, though limited associations were noted in subjective measures. Anxiety at the preclinical stage may influence perceptions more than procedural skills.Keywords: Anxiety, Dental students, Preclinical dental education, Root canal therapy, Self-perception, Technical performance

Background

Anxiety is a commonly encountered psychological response that may influence cognitive and motor functions, particularly in educational and clinical settings [1, 2]. Among the various forms of anxiety, state anxiety refers to a transient emotional state characterized by feelings of apprehension and tension experienced in response to a specific situation [3]. Elevated levels of state anxiety have been linked to compromised performance in tasks requiring fine motor control, decision-making, and sustained attention [4]. In the context of educational psychology, it has also been associated with decreased working memory capacity, attentional lapses, and suboptimal motor planning, all of which may impair task execution [1, 5]. More recent findings indicate that anxiety not only impairs academic achievement but may also adversely affect clinical decision-making accuracy among dental students, especially under emotionally demanding conditions [6].

Given these cognitive and psychomotor implications, anxiety holds particular relevance in preclinical dental education, where students are required to acquire precise manual skills in high-stakes, time-constrained environments [7, 8]. Endodontics, in particular, is initially perceived by students as one of the most stressful fields within the dental curriculum due to its technical complexity and low tolerance for procedural errors [8, 9]. Studies have also documented a high prevalence of perceived stress among dental students, particularly during the transition from preclinical to clinical training, where fear of making mistakes, patient interaction, and assessment pressure become dominant stressors [10–12]. Such stressors have been correlated with reduced self-efficacy in clinical performance, even when academic metrics remain unaffected [10]. In addition to these objective outcomes, state anxiety may further undermine students’ perceived comprehension, confidence, and ability to successfully complete technically demanding phases such as obturation [5, 6, 10].

Simulation-based training has been widely adopted in dental curricula to address these challenges. It allows students to gain procedural confidence and refine technical skills through repeated, feedback-rich practice aligned with clinical duties [13, 14]. While artificial teeth are commonly employed in simulation environments, extracted human teeth offer greater anatomical realism and tactile feedback, thus more closely replicating actual clinical conditions [15, 16]. Practicing root canal treatment on extracted teeth, in particular, provides a highly suitable model for investigating the influence of emotional variables on technical performance owing to its procedural complexity, anatomic variability, and inherent susceptibility to error, even among experienced clinicians [15–18].

Although the general effects of anxiety on academic and clinical performance have been explored [5, 19, 20], evidence remains scarce regarding its specific impact on technical performance during preclinical dental procedures. This gap is especially relevant in endodontic education, where students often interact with extracted teeth for the first time in simulation-based environments. These settings offer a controlled yet realistic opportunity to assess students’ psychomotor performance under emotionally salient conditions, devoid of patient-related stressors. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate whether preoperative state anxiety levels, affect the technical performance as well as the perceived procedural difficulty and self-confidence of undergraduate dental students during root canal treatment procedures on extracted permanent molars. The null hypotheses posited that state anxiety would not significantly influence (1) total performance scores, (2) procedure duration, (3) apical fill accuracy, (4) the occurrence of procedural errors, or (5) students’ perceived procedural difficulty and self-confidence. Each hypothesis was tested independently for mandibular and maxillary molars, as procedures were conducted at separate time points.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This observational cross-sectional study was conducted in the simulation laboratory of the Faculty of Dentistry, Cukurova University. The study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative state anxiety levels and technical performance outcomes during root canal treatment performed on extracted permanent molars. The primary educational purpose of the simulation was procedural skill acquisition. No alternative instructional design or anxiety-regulation intervention was implemented; rather, the focus was on observing how situational anxiety related to students’ performance scores and self-perceptions.

All procedures were conducted under simulated clinical conditions using extracted mandibular and maxillary molars. These teeth were deliberately selected because their complex anatomical morphology renders root canal treatment particularly challenging for students and is associated with lower success rates compared with anterior or premolar teeth [21]. Efforts were made to ensure standardization of tooth selection and procedural conditions, thereby minimizing variability in the experimental setup.

Ethical approval for the study was initially granted by the Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (Ethics No: 2024/148/20) in accordance with institutional and national research ethics standards. The present analysis is derived from the originally approved study protocol. A subsequent approval (Ethics No: 2025/154/29) was obtained to expand the scope of data analysis. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Although not a clinical trial, the reporting of the study followed applicable aspects of STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Participants and setting

A total of 116 third-year undergraduate dental students enrolled in the preclinical endodontics course at Cukurova University were considered for inclusion in this study. Students who did not successfully complete the required theoretical and practical training on root canal treatment using artificial mandibular and maxillary molar teeth in the simulation laboratory were excluded from the outset.

Eligible participants were those without known neurological or psychological conditions beyond the normal range of anxiety assessed in this study, and who had not previously repeated the preclinical endodontics course. Students were also excluded if they reported the use of stimulant or psychotropic medications in the past six months, or if they declined to participate. A total of 93 students met the inclusion criteria and began the mandibular root canal simulation session. The study sample consisted of 50 females and 43 males, with a mean age of 21.1 ± 1.1 years. Theoretical preparation and preclinical training

Before the experimental phase of this study, all participating students had previously undergone standard preclinical training in root canal treatment procedures using artificial mandibular and maxillary molars. However, at the time of data collection, none of the participants had performed root canal treatments on extracted natural molar teeth.

To establish a consistent level of preparedness, students initially attended a structured theoretical course held outside the simulation laboratory. This course was Aligned with international guidelines for preclinical dental education and emphasized instructor support and timely feedback. In addition, a 45-minute focused lecture was delivered within the simulation laboratory immediately before the procedures. This session offered detailed instruction on key aspects of molar endodontic therapy, including molar tooth anatomy, access cavity design, working length determination, canal shaping, and obturation techniques.

Simulation-based root canal treatments

Following the preparatory phase, each participant completed two root canal treatment procedures on extracted permanent molars under simulated clinical conditions, with a one-week interval between the mandibular and maxillary sessions.

All extracted teeth were stabilized on simulation units using a consistent support method. A standardized set of endodontic instruments and materials—including access burs, hand files, irrigation solutions, and obturation materials—was provided to all participants. Each procedure required students to perform all key stages of endodontic treatment, beginning with access cavity preparation and proceeding through working length determination, canal shaping, irrigation, and obturation. Students were instructed to follow standard clinical protocols throughout all steps of the procedure.

The 116 students did not perform the procedures simultaneously; instead, the training was organized into three consecutive sessions (n = 38, 39, and 39), with each session comprising one mandibular and one maxillary exercise, resulting in a total of six sessions. The course cohort consisted of 116 students, all of whom participated in the endodontic simulation training. For pedagogical reporting purposes, the educator-to-student ratio was calculated on the full cohort. All sessions were supervised by four instructors, corresponding to an instructor-to-student ratio of approximately 1:10. To create a psychologically safe learning environment, students were explicitly informed that the procedures would not be graded, thereby reducing performance pressure. Instructors remained available throughout each session for real-time, individualized guidance. Importantly, students were permitted to pause the procedure at any time to request instructor support, ask questions, and then resume their work without penalty or disruption to procedural flow. Furthermore, all procedures were performed individually, with no peer interaction, minimizing potential peer influence on anxiety and performance.

Anxiety measurement

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory – State form (STAI-S) served as the sole instrument for assessing situational anxiety in the current study. This validated self-report scale was administered both immediately prior to and immediately after each root canal procedure. The STAI, originally developed by Spielberger et al. [22], comprises 40 items rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale and includes two subscales: the STAI-S, which evaluates anxiety experienced at a particular moment, and the STAI-T, which measures an individual’s general disposition toward anxiety. The STAI was adapted into the native language and psychometrically validated by Öner and Le Compte in 1985 [23], ensuring its linguistic and cultural appropriateness for the present population. Its clinical utility in dentistry has also been supported by Ayaz et al. [24].

The 20-item STAI-S form was administered separately before and after each root canal session, as procedures were performed on different days in this study. This approach allowed for the assessment of state anxiety at two specific time point. Total scores on the STAI-S range from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater levels of situational anxiety. Scores between 38 and 44 are commonly interpreted as reflecting moderate anxiety, whereas scores of 45 or above are considered indicative of high anxiety levels [25, 26]. For analytical purposes, a cut-off score of ≥ 45 was used to stratify participants into two groups: low anxiety (≤ 44) and moderate-to-high anxiety (≥ 45).

Performance evaluation

Two calibrated examiners (E.P. and Y.Ç.) independently evaluated all procedures while being blinded to participants’ anxiety status to minimize potential assessment bias. Prior to data collection, calibration exercises were conducted to standardize the scoring approach. A total of 186 extracted molar teeth were assessed by two experienced examiners, and inter-examiner agreement was calculated across all performance evaluations.

Performance scores were determined using a structured checklist comprising five procedural domains: (1) access cavity preparation, (2) canal instrumentation, (3) quality of root canal obturation (reflecting density and homogeneity), (4) accuracy of the apical termination point (length), and (5) cleanliness of the access cavity following treatment. Each parameter was scored dichotomously as adequate (1 point) or inadequate (0 points).

To incorporate procedural safety and precision, specific intraoperative errors—such as canal transportation, instrument separation, hourglass formation, ledge formation, and perforation—were penalized by deducting one point per occurrence. The total performance score for each student was calculated by summing the scores for the five domains and subtracting any penalty points, yielding a possible score range from 0 to 5.

In addition, apical fill accuracy (classified as optimal, short, or over-instrumented) and the occurrence of procedural errors were also reported separately, in order to highlight clinically significant deviations that could affect treatment outcomes beyond the composite score.

Post-procedural self-reported measures

Immediately after completing each root canal procedure, participants completed a structured, ten-item questionnaire designed to assess both task-specific performance perceptions and cognitive-affective factors. The first seven items assessed whether the participant encountered any difficulty during specific procedural stages—access cavity preparation, reaching the working length, root canal shaping, irrigation, obturation, excess gutta-percha removal, and pulp chamber cleaning—using a binary format (“Yes”/“No”). The remaining three items evaluated:

Comprehension – “I understood how to perform root canal treatment on molar teeth, including all procedural steps.” (Yes, I understood/Partly understood/No, I did not understand).

Self-confidence – “I feel confident in performing root canal treatment on molar teeth.” (Yes, I am confident/Partly confident/No, I am not confident).

Associations between anxiety, performance, and self-efficacy

The impact of preoperative anxiety on objective measures of root canal treatment success and on subjective measures of perceived procedural difficulty and self-efficacy was evaluated separately for mandibular and maxillary procedures using correlation analysis. Objective measures included performance score, procedure duration, apex accuracy, and procedural error rates. To avoid excessive complexity in the analysis of subjective outcomes, only parameters directly aligned with the primary research aim were included perceived difficulty during access cavity preparation, root canal shaping, and obturation, as well as comprehension and self-confidence. Correlation coefficients were interpreted according to the thresholds proposed by Mukaka [27].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS for Mac version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were calculated for continuous and ordinal variables, which were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on the distribution. The distribution of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Independent samples t-test was used to compare age between low- and high-anxiety groups, while gender distribution was assessed using Fisher’s exact test. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pre- and post-procedural STAI-S scores for both mandibular and maxillary sessions. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare performance scores, and procedure durations as well as subjective measures between low-anxiety and high-anxiety groups, separately for each procedure. Categorical variables such as apical fill accuracy and procedural errors were compared between anxiety groups using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were performed to explore associations among preoperative anxiety, subjective difficulty ratings, and key performance parameters. The inter-rater agreement between the two examiners was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Apex accuracy and procedural errors

Table 4 presents the comparison of apex accuracy and procedural error types between students with low and high anxiety levels during mandibular endodontic procedures. Among the apex accuracy categories, a statistically significant difference was found in the proportion of over-instrumented cases, which was higher in the low-anxiety group (30.4%) compared to the high-anxiety group (11.4%) (p = 0.048). However, no significant differences were observed between groups in the proportion of optimal (30.4% vs. 35.7%; p = 0.801) or short apex placements (39.1% vs. 52.9%; p = 0.337). Regarding procedural errors, although the total error rate appeared higher in the high-anxiety group (20.0%) than in the low-anxiety group (8.7%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.341). Similarly, no significant group differences were identified for specific error types, including file fracture (15.7% vs. 0.0%; p = 0.060), perforation (1.4% vs. 8.7%; p = 0.150), or ledge formation (2.9% vs. 0.0%; p = 1.000). Among the different types of procedural errors, file fracture was observed exclusively in the high anxiety group (15.7%), while no such errors occurred in the low anxiety group.

Similarly, the analysis of maxillary root canal treatment scores showed that most parameters did not differ significantly between low- and high-anxiety groups (p > 0.05), with median values generally overlapping (Table 7). Two items, however, reached statistical significance. First, Obturation scores were lower in the high-anxiety group (mean rank = 43.14) compared to the low-anxiety group (mean rank = 55.10), with median scores of 1 (IQR = 1–2) versus 2 (IQR = 1–2), respectively (U = 702.0, Z = − 2.322, p = 0.020). Second, Comprehension was also reduced in the high-anxiety group (mean rank = 43.33) relative to the low-anxiety group (mean rank = 54.70), with identical medians of 3 (IQR = 3–3) (U = 714.0, Z = − 2.276, p = 0.023).

Associations between anxiety, performance, and self-efficacy

Spearman’s rho analysis for the mandibular procedures revealed that confidence was moderately and positively associated with comprehension (r = 0.506, p < 0.001) and showed weak negative correlations with perceived difficulty during root canal shaping (r = – 0.330, p = 0.001) and obturation (r = – 0.293, p = 0.004). Confidence was also weakly negatively correlated with anxiety (r = − 0.230, p = 0.026), indicating that higher anxiety was linked to lower self-confidence. Apex accuracy exhibited a weak association with fewer reported difficulties during access cavity preparation (r = − 0.209, p = 0.044). Comprehension measures showed weak negative relationships with perceived difficulty of root canal shaping (r = – 0.258, p = 0.012) and obturation (r = – 0.201, p = 0.053; borderline significance). In addition, perceived difficulty during root canal shaping and obturation were themselves weakly correlated (r = 0.212, p = 0.042).

In maxillary procedures, time to complete the treatment was weakly positively correlated with perceived difficulty during root canal shaping (r = 0.245, p = 0.018), indicating that greater ease during root canal shaping corresponded to shorter completion times. Confidence demonstrated a moderate positive association with comprehension (r = 0.441, p < 0.001), and weak negative correlations with perceived difficulty during root canal shaping (r = − 0.347, p = 0.001) and obturation (r = − 0.317, p = 0.002). Comprehension was also weakly correlated with perceived root canal shaping difficulty (r = − 0.298, p = 0.004). Anxiety showed a weak negative correlation with comprehension (r = − 0.237, p = 0.022) and a weak positive correlation with perceived difficulty during obturation (r = 0.242, p = 0.019), indicating that students with higher anxiety tended to report lower understanding and greater difficulty during obturation. Lastly, root canal shaping and obturation difficulty were positively associated (r = 0.269, p = 0.009). No further statistically significant associations were identified among the subjective variables within either procedure.

Discussion

Root canal therapy, known for its complexity and susceptibility to procedural errors, poses a valuable context in which to explore the potential impact of students’ emotional states on psychomotor outcomes during training [28, 29]. In this context, simulation-based training represents the final preparatory stage before clinical practice, offering a critical opportunity to observe whether students with elevated anxiety levels are at risk of compromised technical performance. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether preoperative state anxiety influences performance outcomes during root canal treatment procedures performed on extracted permanent molars within a simulation-based preclinical setting. The null hypotheses, which posited that state anxiety would not significantly affect total performance scores, treatment duration, apical fill accuracy, or procedural errors, were largely retained across both mandibular and maxillary procedures. No statistically significant differences were observed between low- and high-anxiety groups for any of the primary outcomes, except for a higher frequency of over-instrumentation in the low-anxiety group during mandibular procedures, which represented an isolated deviation from the overall pattern. These findings suggest that preoperative anxiety, within the range observed in this cohort, did not systematically impair students’ technical execution in this controlled simulation environment.

This study offers a novel contribution to dental education by stratifying an entire cohort based on their pre-procedural anxiety levels and subsequently comparing their psychomotor performance in a preclinical setting. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to apply such psychological profiling before the onset of technical skill tasks, thereby highlighting the feasibility of integrating emotional assessments into the structure of simulation-based training research. A key strength lies in its iterative design: procedures were conducted on both mandibular and maxillary molars, allowing cross-regional validation and minimizing the risk of region-specific biases. Moreover, by using extracted human teeth instead of artificial models, the study enhanced ecological validity and better approximated clinical conditions. The finding that performance did not systematically differ between anxiety groups suggests that baseline emotional states may exert limited influence under structured simulation conditions. In this sense, the study contributes to growing evidence that well-designed simulations may serve as leveling environments where transient anxiety does not translate into measurable technical disadvantage. A similar interpretation was reported by El-Kishawi et al. [8], who found that although undergraduate students exhibited both subjective and physiological stress responses during clinical endodontic procedures, these stressors had no significant impact on their technical performance. Their findings suggest that students may retain procedural competence even under emotionally charged conditions, once core skills are sufficiently established.

According to Cognitive Load Theory, anxiety can increase extraneous cognitive load, thereby impairing attention, reducing working memory capacity, and elevating the likelihood of errors during complex tasks [3]. However, in our study, no significant performance differences were observed between low- and high-anxiety students during root canal procedures. One plausible explanation is the highly structured nature of the simulation environment, which may have stabilized cognitive demands and enabled students, regardless of anxiety level, to maintain procedural focus. Lugassy et al. [30], for example, reported a strong inverse correlation between anxiety and manual performance, accounting for 74% of variance in repeated plastic-tooth exercises. However, their longitudinal design involved artificial models and measured evolving stress levels over time using multiple psychological inventories (DASS-21/DES). In contrast, our study captured a more advanced stage of simulation: after students had already completed artificial-tooth training, they performed root canal treatments on extracted human molars for the first time, a task more closely resembling clinical conditions. Rather than tracking changes in anxiety during introductory training, we stratified students by state anxiety before the procedure and directly assessed psychomotor output. The lack of group differences in our study suggests that realistic yet controlled tasks, particularly when repeated across anatomical regions, may limit the disruptive influence of preoperative anxiety. Supporting this interpretation, Barakat et al. [28] found that students who received structured preclinical training produced significantly higher-quality root canal fillings compared to those without such preparation. Although anxiety was not directly assessed in their work, their findings highlight how prior exposure and task familiarity can improve technical outcomes, potentially buffering students against emotionally driven performance variability. Collectively, these observations suggest that advanced simulation stages may provide a stabilizing framework that supports both technical development and emotional consistency among learners.

Interestingly, a significantly higher incidence of over-instrumentation was observed among low-anxiety students during mandibular procedures. This counterintuitive finding suggests that lower anxiety levels may be associated with diminished threat sensitivity or even overconfidence during psychomotor tasks. This interpretation aligns with behavioral inhibition and vigilance theories, which propose that anxiety—while often perceived negatively—can serve adaptive functions by enhancing caution, promoting risk aversion, and increasing attentional monitoring [1, 4]. In contrast, lower levels of anxiety may encourage more automatic, habitual, or assertive responses which, although efficient in routine settings, may predispose individuals to errors in complex and unfamiliar contexts such as molar instrumentation. In the present study, it is also plausible that students with higher anxiety levels, despite experiencing increased cognitive load, adopted more deliberate and cautious approaches, thereby reducing the likelihood of apical overextension. This aligns with emerging perspectives suggesting that moderate anxiety may exert a protective influence by enhancing procedural vigilance, particularly in high-risk clinical tasks [2]. Moreover, this over-instrumentation trend might have been expected in maxillary procedures, which typically pose greater challenges in terms of access and visibility. These anatomical complexities may amplify the consequences of minor lapses in judgment or motor control [6]. However, the absence of similar findings in maxillary procedures may be explained by the greater familiarity students often report when dealing with mandibular molar. This interpretation is supported by Luz et al. [31], who found that undergraduate dental students expressed heightened anxiety and reduced confidence particularly when treating molars for first time. This interpretation, based on anatomical familiarity, is reinforced by the corresponding reduction in the number of highly anxious individuals prior to maxillary root canal treatments, compared to the higher anxiety levels observed before mandibular procedures.

When procedural errors were examined, instrument separation clustered in the mandibular exercise among high-anxiety students (15.7% vs. 0%; p = 0.060). Although this p value narrowly missed the conventional α = 0.05 threshold, the absolute gap suggests a clinically relevant effect, consistent with evidence that sympathetic arousal amplifies cyclic fatigue, apical pressure, and loss of tactile feedback—factors predisposing novices to file fracture. Supporting this view, more than 60% of dental interns attribute instrument separation to anxiety [32], and undergraduate audits list broken files among the most frequent stress-related mishaps [33, 34]. The borderline p value likely reflects limited power, only eleven separations occurred, and one additional event would have reached significance. In the subsequent maxillary session, however, anxiety-related differences disappeared (all p > 0.20), plausibly because initial mandibular practice rapidly improved students’ force modulation and psychomotor control. Larger studies that integrate physiological stress markers and haptic-sensor data are needed to confirm whether the anxiety–separation association persists clinically and to define the threshold at which anxiety becomes a measurable risk for iatrogenic error.

Gender-based distribution analyses revealed a significant overrepresentation of female students in the high-anxiety groups, particularly in the mandibular cohort (p = 0.002), echoing findings from recent studies that report a greater psychological burden among female dental students during preclinical procedures [35, 36]. Although procedural stress appeared to be alleviated post-operatively, as evidenced by significant reductions in STAI-S scores among high-anxiety students in both mandibular (p < 0.001) and maxillary groups (p = 0.002), their post-treatment median scores (48.00) remained above the established clinical threshold (≥ 44). This suggests that, while acute stress diminished, baseline trait-anxiety may continue to influence their psychophysiological state beyond procedural completion. In contrast, no significant changes were noted in low-anxiety groups, which may reflect a psychological ceiling or habituation effect previously described in low-reactive individuals. Furthermore, although female students were more likely to fall into high-anxiety categories, gender was not associated with differential performance outcomes in this study. This echoes findings from Wu et al. [37], who reported that while female dental undergraduates exhibited significantly higher test anxiety than males, their objective performance in OSCEs was unaffected by these emotional differences. Similarly, Baechle et al. [38] demonstrated that consistent simulation-based practice predicted exam performance independently of students’ anxiety levels, underlining the equalizing effect of repeated exposure on technical outcomes. These studies suggest that, when tasks are well-structured and opportunities for repeated practice are provided, psychomotor performance may remain stable across gender and anxiety profiles. However, future research should explore interaction effects between gender, anxiety reactivity, and motor precision under stress, particularly through objective metrics such as task timing, error rates, or eye-tracking.

From the perspective of self-reported (subjective) measures, the influence of anxiety showed additional nuances when examined across anatomical sites and procedural phases. The present findings suggest that preoperative anxiety exerts a nuanced influence on students’ self-perceived performance during endodontic procedures, with effects varying according to the anatomical site. In the mandibular procedure, anxiety status did not significantly affect most self-reported parameters, except for confidence, which was significantly lower in the high-anxiety group. Interestingly, although the mandibular procedure represented the students’ first clinical encounter with a natural tooth, anxiety was associated with a broader range of significant differences in cognitive domains in the maxillary procedure, with higher-anxiety students reporting greater perceived difficulty during obturation and reduced comprehension. This pattern suggests that factors beyond initial exposure, such as anatomical access or perceived technical complexity, may more strongly shape the impact of anxiety on self-perceived performance. These associations are consistent with prior evidence showing that anxiety can impair procedural performance in dental education by increasing cognitive load and diverting attention during complex tasks [39], and that elevated stress levels are linked to poorer academic outcomes in clinical skill acquisition [40].

The absence of more widespread differences in our study may reflect the uniformity of prior training, as all participants had successfully completed simulation-based exercises on both mandibular and maxillary molars before inclusion, ensuring a baseline of procedural familiarity. From an educational perspective, identifying procedural phases, such as obturation in the maxilla, where anxiety most strongly influences perception offers opportunities for targeted intervention. Evidence-based strategies include structured rehearsal with simulation-based patient cases, immediate and specific feedback, and integration of anxiety-reduction methods such as mindfulness and contemplative practice, which have been shown to improve confidence, procedural understanding, and perceived competence in dental students [14, 41, 42]. Embedding such approaches at points of greatest vulnerability may enhance strengthen self-efficacy, facilitating smoother transitions from preclinical to clinical practice.

The comparative analysis of specific objective and subjective parameters across mandibular and maxillary procedures revealed both overlapping and region-specific correlation patterns, indicating that some performance-related associations are robust across anatomical sites, while others may be influenced by anatomical nuances. In both mandibular and maxillary interventions, the perceived difficulty of root canal shaping demonstrated a consistent inverse association with both confidence and comprehension, suggesting that experiencing less difficulty during this stage is linked to greater procedural understanding and a stronger sense of self-efficacy, regardless of the operative site. Additionally, in both regions, perceived ease of filling was positively and significantly correlated with self-confidence, underscoring that smoother obturation experiences may enhance the operator’s confidence in their clinical capabilities. However, certain parameters were found to be region-specific. In the mandibular group, anxiety level (classified as high or low) showed a weak but statistically significant negative correlation with feeling confident (r = − 0.230, p = 0.026), whereas in the maxillary group, this association was weaker and approached significance (r = − 0.191, p = 0.067). This difference may be attributed to the fact that real-tooth experience was introduced first in the mandibular region, potentially making this procedure more intimidating for participants with higher anxiety levels, whereas the maxillary procedure may have benefited from increased familiarity with the process. Moreover, starting with the mandibular molar was an intentional choice, as the maxillary molar is often perceived by both practitioners and students as a more challenging tooth [43]. In addition, a positive correlation between procedure duration and perceived shaping difficulty was detected in the maxilla (r = 0.245, p = 0.018), while no such correlation existed in the mandible, indicating that time efficiency in the maxilla may be more sensitive to variations in technical proficiency. Taken together, these findings indicate that while certain associations between perceived task difficulty (e.g., shaping/obturation) and students’ confidence or comprehension are preserved across both anatomical regions, other patterns—particularly those related to anxiety status and time efficiency—appear to be region-dependent. Notably, objective performance scores did not show consistent correlations with the self-report measures. Further studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to clarify the drivers of these region-specific trends and to assess their clinical relevance.

Taken together, these observations provide a multifaceted view of how anxiety interacts with technical and cognitive aspects of student performance. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, although the performance rubric was structured and covered multiple domains, its binary nature (adequate/inadequate) may have lacked the sensitivity to detect subtle variations in psychomotor performance related to anxiety. Second, while the STAI-S was administered immediately prior to each procedure, other potential stressors, such as academic workload or personal concerns, were not controlled and may have influenced anxiety levels. Although the STAI-S has demonstrated convergence with physiological markers such as heart rate variability in prior research, its reliance on self-report remains a limitation of this study. Third, the use of a simulation-based environment, while valuable for standardization, limits generalizability to clinical contexts where patient interaction, time pressure, and real consequences may alter student behavior. Future studies may benefit from integrating physiological stress markers (e.g., cortisol, heart rate variability) or exploring performance in real patient care settings to assess whether the observed buffering of anxiety persists in more complex environments.

Conclusion

Preoperative state anxiety, as measured by the STAI-S, did not significantly affect overall performance outcomes or procedure durations in root canal treatments on extracted molars. While between-group differences in self-reported measures were limited, the maxillary procedure showed anxiety-related effects in a slightly broader set of parameters, particularly in cognitive domains. These findings suggest that the influence of acute preoperative anxiety may be phase- and task-specific rather than globally detrimental, highlighting opportunities for targeted instructional support at the procedural steps most susceptible to anxiety-related performance changes.
