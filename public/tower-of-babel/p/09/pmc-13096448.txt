Dietary astragalus stems polysaccharides improve antioxidant capacity and lactation performance in dairy goats through integrated metabolomic and mechanistic analyses
Authors: Ziqi Meng, Yifeng Song, Qier Mu, Jingwei Qi, Xiaoping An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Astragalus stems are abundant yet underutilized in livestock feeds. This study optimized the extraction of Astragalus stem polysaccharides (ASPS) and assessed their efficacy as a natural antioxidant in improving milk yield and metabolic profiles in dairy goats. Hot-water extraction was optimized (solid-to-liquid ratio of 1: 30, 2 h duration, 85 °C) to achieve a polysaccharide yield of 119.38 ± 4.27 mg/g. Mid-lactation dairy goats supplemented with ASPS [100 mg/kg dry matter (DM) daily] showed increased milk yield and milk protein content, along with reduced blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Supplementation also elevated immunoglobulin A (IgA), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and catalase (CAT). Serum metabolomics identified 307 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism and D‑amino acid metabolism. Six key metabolites were selected for molecular docking against relevant targets; all bound with affinities ≤ − 5.0 kcal/mol, with phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine-d-amino acid oxidase (DAO) showing strongest affinity (− 8.34 kcal/mol). Molecular dynamics simulation suggested stable binding of phenylacetyl-l-glutamine to DAO, with MM-PBSA estimating a favorable free energy (− 92.051 ± 0.366 kJ/mol) dominated by van der Waals interactions. This findings provide valuable insights into the utilization of AS as well as its application in enhancing productivity within the dairy goat industry.

Introduction

Consumer awareness of the nutritional benefits of goat milk is increasing demand, necessitating improvements in dairy goat productivity, milk composition, and overall health to support sustainable industry growth1. Concurrently, the widespread use of antibiotics in livestock has raised concerns about food safety and antimicrobial resistance, prompting regulatory restrictions. Consequently, there is an urgent need for alternative feed strategies to maintain animal health and performance. Plant-derived additives have attracted attention due to their natural origin, safety, and efficacy2. Rich in bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides, flavonoids, and saponins, these botanical ingredients can modulate gut health, immune responses, and antioxidant status, thereby enhancing milk yield and quality3,4. Adopting such plant extracts aligns with consumer preferences for eco-friendly, residue-free products and facilitates the shift toward sustainable, antibiotic-free goat farming.

Astragalus, a traditional Chinese herb, is abundant in bioactive components, including polysaccharides, saponins, flavonoids, and amino acids5–7. Astragalus polysaccharides are well-recognized for modulating cytokine secretion and enhancing immune function8. They also regulate the gut microbiome, influencing host metabolism and immunity9. In dairy goat research, Astragalus polysaccharides have been shown to protect sperm from oxidative damage via AMPK signaling pathways10. Moreover, incorporating Astragalus into diets has been demonstrated to scavenge free radicals, thereby enhancing antioxidant capabilities in dairy goats11, leading to improved growth rates and higher milk quality12. Traditionally, Astragalus roots are used medicinally, while the Astragalus stems (AS) remain underutilized despite their abundance and lower cost compared to roots alone. Emerging evidence indicates that AS contain similar active components to roots13, indicating substantial value. Utilizing AS in animal husbandry thus offers an opportunity to transform agricultural byproducts into valuable resources and provides a novel alternative to antibiotics in livestock feeds. Moreover, metabolomics‑guided feed evaluation has emerged in recent years as a powerful approach for elucidating host metabolic responses to functional feed additives in ruminants, enabling the linkage of dietary interventions with systemic metabolic pathways, immune modulation and production performance14,15. However, the effects of AS polysaccharides (ASPS) on dairy goats—especially their regulatory roles in lactation performance and metabolic pathways—remain largely unexplored, particularly when investigated through integrated serum metabolomics and molecular docking/dynamics analyses.

To bridge this knowledge gap, ASPS were extracted by hot-water extraction and optimized for maximum polysaccharide yield, followed by characterization of bioactive components and monosaccharide composition. A feeding trial was conducted in mid-lactation dairy goats to evaluate the impacts of dietary ASPS supplementation on milk yield, composition, and blood biochemical, immune, and antioxidant parameters. Serum metabolomic profiling was subsequently performed to identify differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) and the pathways they affect. Finally, structure-based molecular docking of key metabolites with pathway-specific target proteins was performed, with the top complex validated through molecular dynamics simulations for binding stability and specificity. This comprehensive approach provides a scientific basis and reference for the further development and utilization of AS in goat farming practices (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Material source

Fresh Astragalus was procured from a local market in Hohhot City, Inner Mongolia, China (40°48′N, 111°39′E). Specifically, the AS were mixed with water in a 1:1 (w/w) ratio and subjected to a pretreatment process at 120 °C and 101 kPa for 15 min. After pretreatment, the samples were dried in an oven at 45 °C, ground into a fine powder, and stored in a desiccator for future use.

Screening of extraction conditions for polysaccharides from AS

For the purpose of optimizing the extraction of ASPS, single-factor experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of solid-to-liquid ratio, extraction time, and extraction temperature. (1) Solid-to-liquid ratio: 1 g of pretreated stem powder was mixed with distilled water at ratios of 1:10, 1:20, 1:30, and 1:40 (w/v). Mixtures were extracted in a 55 °C water bath for 1.5 h, cooled, and centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was then collected for polysaccharide quantification. (2) Extraction time: 1 g of pretreated powder was mixed with distilled water at 1:30 ratio and extracted in a 55 °C water bath for 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 h. Samples were then processed as described above. (3) Extraction temperature: 1 g of pretreated powder was mixed with distilled water at 1:30 ratio and extracted for 2 h at 40, 55, 70, or 85 °C. Samples were processed similarly.

Determination of soluble proteins, polyphenols, and flavonoids in ASPS

Total polysaccharides, polyphenols, soluble proteins, and flavonoids in ASPS were determined leveraging ultraviolet (UV)-Vis spectrophotometry. Polysaccharides were measured by the phenol-sulfuric acid method with glucose as the standard16. Soluble proteins were determined by the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method17. Total polyphenols were assayed via the Folin-Ciocalteu method with gallic acid as the standard18. At last, flavonoids were quantified using the sodium borohydride-4-chlorophenol method with quercetin as the standard reference19.

Determination of monosaccharide composition

Monosaccharide composition was determined using pre-column derivatization with 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP) followed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)20. Briefly, 2.0 mg of polysaccharide sample was hydrolyzed with 0.2 mol/L trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in a sealed tube at 120 °C for 2 h. Excess acid was removed by nitrogen evaporation. The resulting hydrolyzed sample was then dissolved in 0.1 mol/L sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. The monosaccharide derivatives were prepared by reacting the hydrolyzed sample with 0.5 mol/L PMP in methanol at 70 °C for 30 min. The reaction mixture was subsequently cooled to room temperature, neutralized with 0.3 mol/L hydrochloric acid (HCl), and extracted three times with chloroform to remove excess reagents. The aqueous phase was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The PMP-labeled monosaccharide derivatives were analyzed using an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) and a diode-array detector (DAD). The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and acetonitrile (82:18, v/v) with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Detection was performed at 250 nm.

Animal

A total of 46 mid‑lactation Saanen goats (2–3 parities; body weight 57.38 ± 1.64 kg; daily milk yield 2.27 ± 0.12 kg/d) were selected from an intensive farm near Hohhot City, Inner Mongolia. All goats were clinically healthy, with similar days in milk. No animals met exclusion criteria or were withdrawn during the experiment, and all 46 goats completed the study. Goats were housed under standardized management conditions in well‑ventilated pens bedded with straw. The housing environment was maintained at a temperature of 18–25 °C and relative humidity of 50–65%, under a 12‑h light/12‑h dark photoperiod. Animals were group‑housed at a density of approximately 5–6 goats per pen, with ad libitum access to fresh water and environmental enrichment, including visual contact with conspecifics and free‑choice mineral blocks. Goats were randomly allocated to control [n = 23; dry matter intake (DMI): 2.49 ± 0.27 kg/day] or treatment (n = 23; DMI: 2.43 ± 0.28 kg/day) groups using a computer-generated sequence. Each goat served as an experimental unit. Outcome assessors for milk and biochemical analyses were blinded to allocation; farm staff administering diets were not. The treatment group received the basal diet supplemented with 100 mg/kg DM of ASPS extract daily. Both groups were fed concentrate twice daily (8:00 and 16:00) with ad libitum roughage and water. The trial included a 7-day adaptation period followed by a 60-day experimental period. The basal diet was formulated according to Chinese goat feeding standards (2004) (Table 1).

Sample collection and processing

Milk yield was recorded on days 20, 40, and 60 of the experiment using a milking carousel. Prior to milking, udders were thoroughly disinfected with povidone-iodine and alcohol. Morning and afternoon milk samples were collected, preserved in 50 ml centrifuge tubes with one preservative tablet (Aiyuexing, Tianjin Technology Co., Ltd.) per sample. The morning and afternoon milk samples were mixed at a 6:4 ratio, thoroughly homogenized, and stored at 4 °C. Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein before morning feeding on the 60th day of the experimental period, using disposable vacuum blood collection tubes. A 5 ml blood sample was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum and the serum was aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

Milk composition analysis

Milk composition parameters, including milk fat, protein, lactose, total solids, and solids-not-fat, were analyzed using a rapid milk composition analyzer (LACTOSCAN LWA). Collected milk samples were first brought to room temperature, then incubated in a 40 °C water bath for 20 min to ensure thorough homogenization. After equilibrating to 25 °C, samples were loaded onto the analyzer. Each sample was measured three times, and the average of these three readings was recorded as the final result.

Assessment of blood biochemical, immune, and antioxidant parameters

For the measurement of blood biochemical parameters, frozen serum samples were thawed and equilibrated to room temperature before analysis. The following parameters were assessed: glucose (GLU), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (CHO), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (TBIL), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), creatinine (CRE), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). In addition to evaluating the effects on lactation performance and blood biochemical parameters, this study also investigated the impact of dietary ASPS supplementation on immune and antioxidant indices in dairy goats. The specific markers assessed included immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin M (IgM); interleukin 2 (IL-2) and interleukin 6 (IL-6); tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α); and antioxidant enzymes total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), as well as malondialdehyde (MDA). Concentrations of immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, IgM) and cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α), along with T-AOC and MDA levels, were quantified using Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits purchased from Wuhan ColorfulGene Biological Technology Co., Ltd. according to the manufacturer’s instructions (including standard curve preparation via serial dilution, sample incubation at 37 °C, washing, chromogenic reaction, and absorbance measurement at 450 nm). Activities of antioxidant enzymes CAT, T-SOD, and GSH-Px were determined using spectrophotometric assay kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, according to the manufacturer’s protocols visible light method for CAT at 405 nm based on hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) decomposition, colorimetric inhibition assay for T-SOD at 550 nm, and 5,5’-Dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB)-based method for GSH-Px at 412 nm). All assays were standardized across samples to minimize variability.

Serum metabolomics analysis

In an effort to conduct a rigorous and precise metabolomic analysis of blood samples from treatment (n = 6) and control (n = 6) groups of dairy goats, we utilized an UPLC-MS/MS platform coupled with a custom LC-MS/MS database provided by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan. Their DMI and milk yield were comparable to the group averages, and this sample size is consistent with established practices in metabolomics research. This setup allowed for accurate qualitative and quantitative assessment of metabolites. Quality Control (QC) measures were rigorously applied throughout the analysis to ensure data integrity. Total Ion Chromatograms (TIC) were generated for each sample, and the consistency across measurements was evaluated by analyzing the coefficient of variation (CV) among different samples. Further statistical analyses included principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). These multivariate techniques were employed to explore underlying patterns in the metabolite composition and assess variations attributable to treatment effects. Subsequent differential expression analysis identified DEMs between the treatment and control groups [P < 0.05 and variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1]. These DEMs were then subjected to cluster analysis to discern patterns or groupings within the data, providing insights into metabolic pathways significantly influenced by the treatment. Finally, pathway enrichment analysis through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)21,22 was performed on these DEMs. This step helped elucidate biological pathways that are potentially modified by the intervention, offering deeper understanding into the metabolic impact of treatments administered to dairy goats.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

For the purpose of exploring the potential molecular mechanisms of DEMs, we performed structure-based docking against key proteins identified by KEGG analysis and followed established protocol23,24. In the beginning, human target proteins were retrieved from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) and their highest-resolution crystal structures from PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/), with structures prepared in PyMOL (v 3.1.1)25. Ligand structures were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in SDF format and converted to PDBQT via AutoDock Tools. Docking was conducted with AutoDock Vina (v 1.5.7)25, and binding poses were analyzed in PyMOL. Generally, lower binding energies indicate more stable ligand–receptor conformations, and any compound with a binding energy of ≤ − 5.0 kcal/mol was deemed likely to exhibit appreciable binding activity. In addition, a hydrogen bond count of ≥ 2 suggested higher binding specificity.

Building on this, molecular dynamics simulations were carried out in GROMACS (v 2022). Proteins were modeled with AMBER14SB force field and solvated in TIP3P water. Ligands were parameterized with AM1-BCC charges and GAFF2 atom types via Antechamber, converted to GROMACS topologies with ACPYPE, and ion parameters followed Joung–Cheatham. Complexes were placed in a truncated octahedral box with a minimum distance of 1.2 nm between the protein surface and box edge, solvated with TIP3P water, and neutralized/adjusted to 0.15 M NaCl. The system underwent steepest descent energy minimization (Fmax < 1000 kJ/mol/nm), followed by 200 ps of staged NVT and NPT equilibration at 298 K. Production runs lasted 100 ns under NPT conditions with a 2 fs time step and Verlet integration. Long-range electrostatics were treated with PME; van der Waals and Coulomb interactions were truncated at 1.2 nm. All bonds involving hydrogens were constrained with LINCS, and temperature and pressure were maintained by Nosé–Hoover and Parrinello–Rahman at 298 K and 1 bar, respectively. Trajectories were saved every 10 ps, and analyses of structure and interactions were performed using GROMACS tools along with VMD and PyMOL; when needed, MM-PBSA energy estimates were computed with gmx_MMPBSA.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were initially processed using Microsoft Excel for basic calculations and summaries. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed implementing general linear model (GLM) in SAS (v 9.2) to assess group differences. The results are presented as least squares means with corresponding standard errors. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the metabolomic analysis, UPLC-MS data were processed using Analyst software (v 1.6.3). Subsequent statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v 4.2.1).

Results

Optimization of ASPS extraction conditions

In order to optimize the extraction conditions of ASPS while maximizing yield and minimizing costs, single-factor experiments were conducted. For the solid-to-liquid ratio, the polysaccharides content was maximized at a ratio of 1:30, reaching 84.27 ± 4.01 mg/g, and showed a quadratic variation (P < 0.05) (Table 2). Extraction time also showed a quadratic effect (P < 0.05), with the highest polysaccharides yield (89.94 ± 1.6 mg/g) observed at 2 h (Table 3). Afterwards, polysaccharides content increased significantly with temperature (P < 0.05), peaking at 119.38 ± 4.27 mg/g at 85 °C (Table 4). Therefore, the optimal extraction temperature was set at 85 °C. In summary, the optimized extraction conditions for ASPS were determined to be a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30, an extraction time of 2 h, and an extraction temperature of 85 °C. Under these conditions, the polysaccharides content reached 119.38 ± 4.27 mg/g, representing the highest yield obtained in our study.

Comprehensive characterization of ASPS

After the extraction conditions for ASPS were optimized, an analysis was conducted to determine its polysaccharides, soluble proteins, polyphenols, flavonoids, monosaccharide composition, and molecular weight (Table 5). Briefly, the polysaccharides content in ASPS was found to be 119.38 ± 4.27 mg/g, soluble protein content was 3.49 ± 0.41 mg/g, polyphenols content was 2.13 ± 0.14 mg/g, and flavonoids content was 1.23 ± 0.05 mg/g. Monosaccharide composition analysis revealed 11 components: glucuronic acid, mannose, glucosamine, ribose, rhamnose, glucuronic acid, galacturonic acid, glucose, galactose, arabinose, and fucose, with glucose being the most abundant at 71.49%, followed by arabinose at 10.05% and galactose at 9.96%. The average molecular weight of the polysaccharides in ASPS was 217.64 kDa.

Effects of ASPS supplementation on milk yield and composition in dairy goats

The effects of dietary supplementation with ASPS on the lactation performance of dairy goats were presented in Table 6. DM intake did not differ significantly between groups at any time point (P > 0.05), although a slight numerical increase was observed in the treatment group on days 40 and 60 (P = 0.0877 and P = 0.0782, respectively). Milk yield and composition were unaffected by ASPS supplementation during the first 20 days of the trial (P > 0.05). However, from day 40 onward, the treatment group exhibited significantly higher milk yield and milk protein content compared with the control group (P < 0.05). These improvements persisted through day 60 (P < 0.05). On day 60, milk fat content also showed a trend toward increase in the treatment group (P = 0.0966). No significant differences were observed in lactose, total solids, or solids-not-fat percentages at any sampling point (P > 0.05). Importantly, the enhanced milk yield and protein content in the treatment group occurred independently of any significant increase in DMI, indicating that the beneficial effects of ASPS supplementation on lactation performance were attributable to the bioactive properties of the polysaccharides rather than to greater feed consumption.

Influences of dietary ASPS supplementation on blood biochemical, immune, and antioxidant parameters in dairy goats

The subsequent analysis focused on the effects of dietary ASPS supplementation on the blood biochemical parameters in dairy goats (Table 7). ASPS supplementation significantly reduced serum BUN levels (P < 0.05). Trends toward increased HDL levels and decreased ALP levels were observed, although not statistically significant (P = 0.0503 and 0.0749, respectively). These findings suggest that ASPS may have beneficial effects on specific metabolic markers in dairy goats. Following that, we analyzed immune and antioxidant parameters in the blood of dairy goats to gain a deeper understanding of how dietary supplementation with ASPS affects the functionality of the immune system and oxidative stress status. Notably, the addition of ASPS significantly increased the concentration of IgA levels in the blood during mid-lactation (P < 0.05), and there was a trend toward higher levels of IgM levels (P = 0.0665) (Table 8). Furthermore, antioxidant status was markedly improved, with significant increases in T-AOC and CAT activity during this period (P < 0.05), and a significant reduction in MDA levels, indicating improved antioxidant defense mechanisms in dairy goats (P < 0.05) (Table 9).

Assessment of instrumental precision and data stability in mixed QC samples employing TIC

Following the aforementioned analyses, we conducted a metabolomic analysis on the serum of dairy goats supplemented with ASPS. This investigation aimed to identify significant metabolites that could elucidate the biochemical impacts of ASPS supplementation. At the outset, in the analysis of mixed QC samples, TIC were overlaid to compare spectral patterns at each time point. The summed intensities of all ions and their retention times showed substantial overlap, indicating minimal variation attributable to instrumental error throughout the experimental process (Fig. 2A). Subsequent Pearson correlation coefficients among QC samples approached 1, confirming excellent repeatability (Fig. 2B). Moreover, over 85% of detected metabolites had coefficient of variation (CV) < 0.5, and > 75% had CV < 0.3, demonstrating high data stability (Fig. 2C).

Identification and functional enrichment analyses of DEMs

In differential expression analysis between the treatment and control groups of dairy goats, a total of 307 DEMs were identified. Among these, 134 metabolites were upregulated and 173 were downregulated (Fig. 4A). The most abundant upregulated metabolite class was glycerophospholipids with 38 instances, followed by amino acids and their derivatives (29), and phenyl derivatives (18). Conversely, the predominant downregulated classes included amino acids and their derivatives (45), organic acids and their derivatives (35), and nucleotides and their derivatives (20). Noteworthy DEMs included LPC(22:3), L-Ornithine, L-Tryptophan, caffeic acid, shikimic acid, and phenylacetyl-l-glutamine (Table 10). To further understand the metabolic pathways associated with these DEMs, cluster analysis was conducted on a selected set of 307 DEMs. The clustering results indicated that samples from the treatment group aggregated well together, suggesting minimal intra-group variability and similar metabolic profiles among samples. Particularly, amino acids and their derivatives, organic acids and their derivatives, fatty acyls, as well as nucleotides and their derivatives were predominantly higher in the control group compared to the treatment group. In contrast, glycerophospholipids, phenyl derivatives, and heterocyclic compounds showcased higher levels in treatment group than in the control (Fig. 4B). KEGG analysis revealed that these DEMs were primarily involved in 20 distinct metabolic pathways including glycerophospholipid metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and D−Amino acid metabolism (Fig. 4C).

Structural basis of metabolite–protein interactions in energy homeostasis pathways revealed by molecular docking

Preliminary non-targeted metabolomic profiling identified several DEMs—notably LPC(22:3), L-ornithine, L-tryptophan, caffeic acid, shikimic acid and phenylacetyl-l-glutamine—that are implicated in regulating energy homeostasis in dairy goats through glycerophospholipid metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids and D-amino acid metabolism. To clarify the underlying mechanisms, we docked each DEM against the corresponding pathway’s core proteins (Fig. 5 and Table 11). Binding energies ranged from − 4.687 to − 8.339 kcal/mol; most were ≤ − 5.0 kcal/mol. Remarkably, phenylacetyl-l-glutamine bound most tightly within the D-amino acid metabolism (DAO) (binding energy = − 8.339 kcal/mol), stabilized by three hydrogen bonds (Gly312, Leu316 and Thr317) (Fig. 5F). Consequently, this complex was selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations. These docking results are used as qualitative, hypothesis-generating evidence due to the cross-species nature of the structures.

Energetically favored and stable complex formation between phenylacetyl-l-glutamine and d-amino acid metabolism (DAO) driven by van der Waals interactions

Molecular dynamics simulation of the phenylacetyl-l-glutamine and DAO complex was carried out for 100 ns to assess its stability and binding characteristics. The backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the complex stabilized at 0.38 ± 0.05 nm after ~ 20 ns, indicating equilibration of the system; the ligand RMSD remained below 0.10 nm, while the apo-protein RMSD leveled off around 0.25 nm (Fig. 6A). The radius of gyration (Rg) fluctuated only slightly around 2.75 nm throughout the trajectory, confirming that the overall compactness of DAO was maintained (Fig. 6B). Residue-wise flexibility analysis by root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) revealed elevated mobility in loop regions spanning residues 308–322, with a maximum fluctuation of ~ 0.60 nm (Fig. 6C). The center-of-mass distance between ligand and binding pocket remained constant at ~ 1.45 ± 0.03 nm (Fig. 6D), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of complex averaged 6 ± 0.5 nm2, both indicating a stable binding interface (Fig. 6E). Superposition of snapshots from 0 ns to 100 ns, colored blue to red, further confirmed minimal structural drift over the simulation (Fig. 6F).

In vacuum simulations, both van der Waals and electrostatic interaction energies stabilized over time, reflecting sustained affinity (Fig. 6G). Under solvated conditions, MM-PBSA calculations yielded a total binding free energy of − 92.051 ± 0.366 kJ/mol, predominantly driven by van der Waals interactions, followed by hydrophobic and electrostatic contributions. Per-residue decomposition identified TYR-224 and TYR-314 as key contributors (Fig. 6H and Table 12). The number of hydrogen bonds between DAO and phenylacetyl-l-glutamine averaged 3 throughout the run (Fig. 6I). Detailed interaction mapping revealed that the ligand formed six hydrogen bonds with Gly312, Gly313, Gly315, Leu316, Thr317 and Leu51. Key hydrophobic contacts—including π–π stacking, T-shaped π–π and π-alkyl interactions—were observed with Tyr224 and Tyr228, while van der Waals interactions involved Ile230, Tyr314, Thr44 and neighboring residues (Fig. 6J). Essential dynamics via PCA showcased two dominant conformational clusters accounting for 75% of the motion, indicating restricted sampling around the bound state (Fig. 6K). Electrostatic surface potential mapping revealed a positively charged binding groove that complements the ligand’s polar groups (Fig. 6L). The free energy landscape exhibited a single deep energy basin, confirming the prevalence of one stable conformer (Fig. 6M), which B-factor analysis corroborated (Fig. 6N). Finally, hydrogen-bond frequency analysis further affirmed the persistence of key electrostatic contacts (Fig. 6O). Overall, these results confirm a highly stable, energetically favorable complex driven primarily by van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions.

Discussion

Astragalus, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, is rich in bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides, proteins, and flavonoids. Historically, only the roots have been used medicinally, while the abundant stems have been largely discarded. Recent studies, however, have shown that AS also contains substantial levels of polysaccharides, saponins, and flavonoids13,26,27, highlighting their potential as an underutilized resource. In this study, we optimized hot-water extraction to obtain ASPS and evaluated their effects in dairy goats via lactation performance, serum metabolomics and molecular modeling. Supplementation with 100 mg/kg DM of ASPS extract daily increased milk yield and protein content, enhanced antioxidant capacity and elevated immune markers. Besides, metabolomic profiling uncovered shifts in energy-related metabolites, particularly phenylacetyl-l-glutamine. Structure-based docking and molecular dynamics simulation of this metabolite with DAO supported a stable complex, providing a potential mechanistic hypothesis linking ASPS‑associated metabolite changes with enzyme activity. These findings position ASPS as a sustainable, value-added feed additive that enhances goat productivity while reducing reliance on synthetic compounds.

Hot-water extraction is a simple, cost-effective method commonly employed for polysaccharide isolation28. We used a single-factor experimental design to systematically evaluate the influence of solid-to-liquid ratio, extraction time, and temperature on ASPS yield. A rational solid-to-liquid ratio enhances the diffusion rate between internal materials and external solvent, facilitating the dissolution of polysaccharides29,30. In our study, the optimal ratio of 1:30 (w/v) yielded 84.27 ± 4.01 mg/g polysaccharide. Deviating from this optimal ratio may result in either an excessive dilution effect with too much solvent, or inefficient extraction of polysaccharides from the solid matrix due to insufficient solvent. Extraction for 2 h maximized yield at 89.94 ± 1.60 mg/g, allowing adequate time for solvent penetration and polysaccharide release until equilibrium was reached31. Higher temperatures accelerate molecular motion and mass transfer, explaining the peak yield of 119.38 ± 4.27 mg/g at 85°C32. The optimized conditions (1:30 ratio, 2 h, 85 °C) achieved the highest ASPS recovery in this study and offer a scalable, economical protocol suitable for industrial application.

Characterization of the optimized ASPS extract confirmed its predominance of polysaccharides, accompanied by minor bioactive components. Soluble protein content (3.49 ± 0.41 mg/g) may confer additional enzymatic or peptide-related bioactivity. Furthermore, the presence of 2.13 ± 0.14 mg/g polyphenols and 1.23 ± 0.05 mg/g flavonoids in ASPS is noteworthy due to their recognized antioxidant properties. Nevertheless, polysaccharides were the primary target of extraction optimization and biological evaluation9 and are widely regarded as the principal bioactive constituents responsible for the immunomodulatory and antioxidant effects of Astragalus‑derived products8,11. At the same time, the presence of polyphenols and flavonoids may provide additive or synergistic support to antioxidant‑related responses33. The individual biological contributions of these minor components were not independently assessed in the present study and warrant further investigation in future work. Polysaccharides with a complex array of monosaccharides might exhibit superior biological activities34. Although variations exist in molecular weights among different types of Astragalus polysaccharide, they predominantly consist of glucose, arabinose, and galactose35–37. In our research, glucose was most abundant in ASPS at 71.49%, followed by arabinose at 10.05% and galactose at 9.96%. This glucose-rich glucan backbone may influence solubility, viscosity, and biological activity.—a finding consistent with previous studies on Astragalus root polysaccharides where similar monosaccharide compositions were identified38. The average molecular weight of 217.64 kDa suggests good stability and potential for effective interaction with biological targets. Future extraction strategies could explore advanced techniques—such as ultrasound-assisted or enzymatic methods—to further improve yield, bioavailability, and structural preservation while maintaining environmental sustainability.

Lactation performance is a key determinant of dairy goat profitability, heavily influenced by dietary factors. Previous work has shown that dietary Astragalus polysaccharides at 100 mg/kg enhance lactation in dairy goats39. In our trial, ASPS supplementation had no effect in the first 20 days but significantly increased milk yield and protein content by days 40 and 60, with a near-significant rise in milk fat (P < 0.1). This delayed but sustained improvement suggests gradual enhancement of mammary gland function or a cumulative effect of ASPS bioactives, ultimately boosting both production volume and nutritional quality. ASPS supplementation also favorably modulated blood biochemical, immune, and antioxidant parameters. Significant reductions in serum BUN and a trend toward lower ALP indicate improved protein utilization and potential hepatoprotective effects, aligning with known benefits of Astragalus polysaccharides40. Elevated BUN levels can indicate excessive protein catabolism or poor renal function41, while increased ALP is often associated with liver damage or disease42. Immunologically, elevated IgA and a trend toward higher IgM are consistent with enhanced lymphocyte and macrophage activity reported for Astragalus polysaccharides43,44. Furthermore, antioxidant capacity was also improved: T‑AOC and CAT activities rose while MDA decreased, reflecting reduced lipid peroxidation and stronger ROS scavenging—findings concordant with earlier studies showing Astragalus polysaccharide elevates SOD, GSH‑Px and CAT and lowers MDA45. Of which, increased T-AOC reflects heightened non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses and enzymatic levels which suggests an elevated ability to neutralize reactive oxygen species thereby protecting cellular components from oxidative damage46,47. Additionally, lower MDA levels imply reduced lipid peroxidation within cell membranes critical for maintaining cellular integrity and functionality48. Collectively, these changes highlight ASPS as a multifunctional additive supporting metabolic efficiency, immune competence, and oxidative resilience in lactating goats.

Metabolic alterations are primary risk factors for functional abnormalities and diseases in tissues, such as cancer49, metabolic diseases50, and cardiovascular diseases51. Dietary supplementation with plant-derived polysaccharides has been shown to induce coordinated shifts in lipid, amino acid, immune system, gut microbiota, and energy metabolism52–55, reflecting improved nutrient partitioning and metabolic efficiency rather than isolated metabolite changes. Our metabolomic analysis identified 307 DEMs. The predominant upregulation of glycerophospholipids in the treatment group suggests that dietary supplementation with ASPS may influence enhanced membrane synthesis or remodeling activities. This could indicate improved cellular integrity or altered signaling pathways, which are crucial for maintaining optimal physiological conditions in lactating goats56,57. Conversely, the downregulation of amino acids and their derivatives in the treatment group might suggest that these building blocks are being diverted towards more direct metabolic needs such as protein synthesis or energy production rather than accumulating in the serum58. This hypothesis is supported by our earlier observations of increased milk protein content in dairy goats. KEGG enrichment implicated pathways including glycerophospholipid metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and D-amino acid metabolism. Docking of six key DEMs to pathway-specific proteins yielded favorable affinities ( ≤ − 5.0 kcal/mol). Phenylacetyl-l-glutamine bound most strongly to DAO (− 8.339 kcal/mol) via three hydrogen bonds. Phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine is a gut microbiota‑derived amino acid conjugate that has attracted increasing attention due to its immunomodulatory and metabolic regulatory properties. Previous studies in mammalian systems have shown that altered Phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine levels are associated with systemic inflammatory status and immune signaling, and that Phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine can modulate cytokine production and immune‑related pathways through host–microbiota interactions59,60. In ruminants, glutamate and glutamine are increasingly recognized as functionally important amino acids despite being considered nutritionally non‑essential61. They play key roles in protein synthesis, immune regulation, and antioxidant defense via glutathione biosynthesis62. Therefore, changes in phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine abundance may reflect altered amino acid utilization and immune‑metabolic crosstalk. In the context of this study, the enrichment of Phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine in response to ASPS supplementation suggests a potential link between polysaccharide‑mediated modulation of gut microbial metabolism, host amino acid turnover, and systemic immune or oxidative status during lactation. Subsequent molecular dynamics confirmed a stable DAO–ligand complex: backbone RMSD plateaued at ~ 0.38 nm, Rg remained near 2.75 nm, and MM-PBSA calculations yielded a binding free energy of -92.051 ± 0.366 kJ/mol dominated by van der Waals interactions. Per-residue analysis highlighted Tyr224 and Tyr314 as critical hot spots, while persistent π–π and π–alkyl contacts further reinforced complex stability.

Despite the promising findings presented, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the extraction optimization of ASPS was based on a single‑factor experimental design, which does not account for potential interactions among extraction temperature, time, and solid–liquid ratio; therefore, the obtained conditions may not represent a global optimum. Second, possible variations in ASPS composition resulting from geographic origin or batch differences were not evaluated. Third, although monosaccharide composition and molecular weight distribution were analyzed, detailed structural characterization of ASPS was not conducted, which limits deeper interpretation of structure–activity relationships. In addition, molecular docking and molecular dynamics analyses were conducted using human protein structures due to the lack of available goat crystal structures. Although these models are suitable for hypothesis generation, species‑specific structural differences may influence binding characteristics, and conclusions should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, only a single ASPS supplementation level (100 mg/kg DM) was evaluated, and dose–response relationships remain to be determined. Lastly, the observed relationships between ASPS supplementation, serum metabolite profiles, and enzyme‑related targets were derived from correlative and in silico analyses, and direct causal relationships were not experimentally verified. Future studies should incorporate multivariate extraction optimization, assess ASPS source variability, investigate dose‑dependent effects, and conduct targeted mechanistic experiments to better elucidate the biological actions of ASPS in dairy goats.

Conclusions

In this study, hot-water extraction of ASPS was optimized using single-factor experiments, yielding optimal conditions of a 1:30 solid-to-liquid ratio, 2 h duration, and 85 °C temperature, resulting in a polysaccharide content of 119.38 ± 4.27 mg/g. Dietary supplementation with ASPS extract at 100 mg/kg DM in mid‑lactation dairy goats was associated with increased milk yield and milk protein content from day 40 onward, along with improved antioxidant status and immune‑related indices, as indicated by reduced serum BUN and MDA concentrations and elevated IgA, T‑AOC, and CAT activity. Serum metabolomics identified 307 DEMs enriched in key pathways, with molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealing a potentially stable interaction between phenylacetyl‑l‑glutamine and DAO, providing a plausible molecular hypothesis linking metabolite changes to observed physiological responses. Collectively, these results indicate that ASPS supplementation is correlated with favorable production performance and metabolic profiles in dairy goats, while the proposed molecular mechanisms remain to be verified through targeted functional and mechanistic studies.
