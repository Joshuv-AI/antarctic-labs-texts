3D imaging contribution in pediatric surgical oncology: a multi-stakeholder assessment study
Authors: Luca Pio, Rani Kassir, Giammarco La Barbera, Cecile Lozach, Enzo Bonnot, Thomas Isla, Juan Pablo de la Plata Alcalde, Pietro Gori, Isabelle Bloch, Sabine Sarnacki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medical imaging is crucial for surgical planning, yet surgeons struggle with mental transformation of 2D images into 3D representations, particularly in complex pediatric pelvic anatomy. This study evaluated perceived benefits of 3D imaging with tractography compared to conventional 2D MRI in pediatric pelvic tumor surgery. A nationwide study assessed three groups: non-medical personnel (n = 30), medical trainees (residents and fellows; primary analysis n = 61, excluding 3 medical students), and senior pediatric surgeons (n = 12). Using 3-Tesla MRI with specialized protocols including high-resolution CoroT2cube and diffusion tensor imaging, participants evaluated five clinical cases in both 2D and 3D formats using 7-point Likert scales. Statistical analysis employed Wilcoxon paired tests with Bonferroni correction. All groups showed significant improvements in perceived understanding with 3D imaging. Non-medical personnel scores increased from 4.24 (± 0.69) to 6.27 (± 0.28) (p < 0.001), particularly in understanding disease and surgical objectives. Medical trainees improved from 5.08 (± 0.61) to 6.42 (± 0.49) (p < 0.001), with enhanced understanding of surgical objectives and anatomical relationships. Senior surgeons’ scores increased from 5.02 (± 0.69) to 6.33 (± 0.52) (p < 0.001), showing significant improvements in preoperative planning and family communication. Effect sizes were substantial across groups (Cohen’s d: 2.80, 1.90, and 1.52 respectively), though the within-subject design likely contributes to effect size inflation. This study provides preliminary evidence for perceived 3D imaging value in pediatric pelvic tumor surgery. Improved anatomical comprehension among non-medical personnel may benefit informed consent, while enhanced visualization aids surgical education and planning. High surgeon acceptance (92%) suggests strong acceptability, though these exploratory findings require validation before implementation recommendations can be made. Prospective studies evaluating objective clinical outcomes, workflow integration and cost-effectiveness require further study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44543-z.

Introduction

Medical imaging has revolutionized surgical planning and decision-making in modern medicine. While radiologists routinely perform cognitive three-dimensional reconstructions from two-dimensional computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance images (MRI), surgeons often find this mental transformation challenging1. This cognitive gap particularly impacts surgical planning in complex anatomical regions like the pelvis.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence and image processing have enabled patient-specific three-dimensional models through segmentation performed either manually or using automated recognition systems (3DSlicer, Myrian Studio, ITK-SNAP). Deep learning neural networks have accelerated this process, achieving near-automatic segmentation2,3. However, current automated systems struggle with nerve pathway visualization, crucial in regions where surgical intervention carries significant functional risks4–11.

The development of robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery and its high-definition 3D vision enables integration of patient-specific 3D imaging in the operative field. Numerous studies have evaluated this 3D representation and 3D printing for surgical planning and intraoperative guidance12,13. In 2017, Diment et al.7 identified 3,084 clinical trials evaluating 3D printing for surgical planning, with maxillofacial surgery (60%) and orthopedic surgery (25%) dominating these studies. This trend was then confirmed in recent years8.

In adult abdominal surgery, despite promising applications in hepatic and renal surgery, 3D modeling remains primarily research-focused10,11. Studies have shown that 3D-printed kidney tumor models significantly improve understanding of lesion characteristics among medical students and facilitate nephron-sparing surgery planning11,12. However, pediatric surgical conditions have not benefited equally from these technological advances, particularly concerning genetic, developmental, and oncological diseases.

Limited pediatric research has focused primarily on renal tumors and congenital heart disease13–15. Wellens et al.16 demonstrated significantly improved identification of tumor boundaries, vascular structures, and excretory systems using 3D imaging compared to conventional imaging in pediatric renal tumors.

Our multidisciplinary IMAG2 laboratory team has developed specialized techniques for 3D modeling of pediatric pelvic and renal anatomy using MRI and CT data. MRI was chosen for pelvic imaging due to its non-ionizing nature and capacity to provide functional information through specialized sequences. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequences, combined with tractography algorithms, enable visualization of nerve fiber networks and analysis of their pathological alterations17,18.

This research aims to assess perceived benefits and evaluate the contribution of 3D imaging (incorporating tractography) in pre- and intraoperative settings compared to conventional 2D MRI imaging in pediatric pelvic tumor surgery. We hypothesize that 3D modeling provides added value in three key areas: patient and family communication regarding pathology and planned surgery19–21; surgical planning and risk assessment22; and medical education for students, residents, and non-surgical physicians23. This study represents a critical step in exploring advanced imaging techniques in pediatric surgical care, with potential implications for improving surgical outcomes and enhancing medical education.

Methods

We conducted a nationwide assessment study to evaluate the perceived contribution of 3D imaging in pediatric pelvic tumor surgery across three distinct stakeholder groups. Our study population comprised non-medical personnel, medical trainees (residents and fellows), and senior pediatric surgeons, representing diverse professional backgrounds and geographical locations throughout France. The study included three distinct participant groups, each with specific eligibility criteria. The first group consists of non-medical personnel, which includes adults over 18 years of age who have no formal medical training or background. The second group encompasses medical trainees, specifically residents and fellows who are currently in surgical training. Medical students enrolled during the pilot phase were excluded from the final analysis cohort and are not presented in the descriptive tables. Two non-surgical physicians who participated were retained in the analysis as their clinical experience level was comparable to senior residents. The third group comprises senior surgeons, defined as board-certified pediatric surgeons who possess more than five years of professional experience in their specialty.

This prospective observational study was approved by the relevant Institutional Review Boards and an independent Ethics Committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile de France VII, N° 2015-A01705-44). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted according to the Helsinki guidelines and European Regulation 2016/679. Sample size was determined by convenience sampling based on available participants from pediatric surgery centers nationwide. Post-hoc power analysis revealed adequate power (> 0.80) for detecting the observed effect sizes given our sample sizes.

For image acquisition, we utilized a 3-Tesla GE MRI equipped with specialized coils adapted to patient age. The imaging protocol combined routine clinical sequences with two research-specific acquisitions: a high-resolution CoroT2cube sequence and a diffusion tensor imaging sequence (DTI 25 Dir b600 Axial). The CoroT2cube sequence provided isotropic voxels essential for precise 3D reconstruction, while the DTI sequence enabled detailed nerve tract visualization via a tractography algorithm24.

Our image processing pipeline integrated artificial intelligence-based semiautomatic segmentation with manual refinement as previously described24–26. The semiautomatic segmentation achieved high accuracy for anatomical structures, with Dice similarity coefficients ranging from 77% for vessels to 95% for bladder segmentation. Given the heterogeneous nature of pediatric tumors, these required manual segmentation. We developed a specialized AI-based algorithm integrating anatomical knowledge, the Visionerves technology (pending Patent n° EP22307002.0.2023), to isolate and recognize from the tractogram each individual nerve roots from L5 to S4 (Fig. 1).

To assess the impact of 3D modeling, we designed comprehensive electronic questionnaires tailored to each stakeholder group. The questionnaire items were developed based on relevant literature on surgical planning, anatomical understanding, and patient communication, though formal psychometric validation (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha, factor analysis) was not performed. These incorporated five representative clinical cases of pediatric pelvic tumors, including neurofibroma, rhabdomyosarcoma, presacral teratoma, and two cases of neuroblastoma (Table 1). While these cases were selected to represent the clinical spectrum of pediatric pelvic tumors, certain tumor configurations (e.g., nerve-encasing lesions) may particularly favor 3D visualization, and findings may not generalize equally to all tumor types. Each case was presented through standardized video presentations in both 2D and 3D formats, with randomized presentation order to control for learning effects (Supplementary Figure S1; video presentations available as Supplementary Material). Participants evaluated multiple parameters using seven-point Likert scales, followed by summary questions addressing overall utility and implementation preferences defined as general aspects score. To compare the perception of 3D imaging with that of 2D imaging, an average score (out of seven) was calculated for each questionnaire item separately for 2D and 3D imaging, based on responses from five clinical cases.

A sub-score for “anatomy” (out of 7) was determined by averaging the responses to the nine anatomy-related questionnaire items. Finally, a total score (also out of seven) was obtained by averaging the scores of all questionnaire items (Supplementary Figure S2). The averaging of Likert-scale items assumes interval-level measurement properties; while this is common practice, readers should interpret composite scores with this assumption in mind.

The scores obtained for 2D and 3D imaging (individual items, anatomy sub-score, and total score) were compared using paired Wilcoxon tests. The test was considered significant if the p-value was below 0.05.

Statistical analysis employed R version 3.6.1, incorporating Wilcoxon paired tests for comparing 2D and 3D visualization effectiveness, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. We calculated Cohen’s d effect sizes to quantify the practical significance of observed differences. Given that Cohen’s d assumes continuous, normally distributed data while Likert responses are ordinal, effect size estimates should be interpreted cautiously and may overestimate true effect magnitudes. The within-subject design, while controlling for individual differences, mathematically inflates standardized effect sizes by eliminating between-person variability. Future studies should consider reporting non-parametric alternatives such as Wilcoxon’s r alongside Cohen’s d.

Results

The study included 106 participants across three groups: non-medical personnel (n = 30), medical trainees and non-surgical physicians (n = 64; primary analysis conducted on n = 61 after excluding 3 medical students), and senior pediatric surgeons (n = 12) (Table 2). The questionnaire completion rate was 98.1% (106/108), with a mean completion time of 80 min (SD ± 12). The substantial questionnaire duration may have introduced fatigue effects that could bias responses; future studies should consider shorter formats or scheduled breaks to mitigate this risk.

In the non-medical personnel group, mean scores on the 7-point Likert scale increased from 4.24 (SD ± 0.69) with 2D imaging to 6.27 (SD ± 0.28) (p < 0.001) with 3D imaging, improving disease understanding, surgical objective comprehension and complication awareness (p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed in anxiety levels (p = 0.627) (Fig. 2). Structure-specific assessment showed improvements in vessels, nerves, tumor volume, and anatomical relationships (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Effect size analysis using Cohen’s d yielded 2.80 [95% CI: 2.16–4.39] for non-medical personnel, indicating that 99.7% of participants had a higher average score for 3D imaging compared to 2D imaging.

Medical trainees (residents and fellows, n = 61 after excluding medical students) demonstrated an increase in mean scores from 5.08 (SD ± 0.61) to 6.42 (SD ± 0.49) (p < 0.001). Educational metrics showed increased understanding of surgical objectives, anatomical learning efficiency and surgical complications (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Anatomy recognition improvements were documented in vascular anatomy, neural structures, tumor margins, and tumor’s spatial relationships (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Cohen’s d value was 1.90 [95% CI: 1.53–2.45] with 97.1% of participants having a higher average score for 3D imaging compared to 2D imaging.

Senior pediatric surgeons’ mean scores increased from 5.02 (SD ± 0.69) to 6.33 (SD ± 0.52) (p < 0.001). The comparison between 2D and 3D imaging revealed significantly better perceived outcomes across all measured parameters with 3D visualization. Preoperative planning showed marked improvement with 3D imaging (6.47 ± 0.61 vs. 5.08 ± 0.99, p = 0.009) (Fig. 4). The most notable difference was observed in family communication, where 3D imaging scored substantially higher (6.65 ± 0.41 vs. 5.32 ± 1.03, p = 0.002). Preoperative visualization also demonstrated superior results with 3D imaging (6.73 ± 0.58 vs. 5.93 ± 0.93, p = 0.045), as did intraoperative visualization (6.45 ± 0.78 vs. 5.48 ± 0.93, p = 0.016). The 3D imaging approach showed significant advantages in surgical strategy modification (6.17 ± 0.74 vs. 5.42 ± 0.85, p = 0.036) and risk assessment (6.28 ± 0.91 vs. 5.20 ± 1.13, p = 0.033). Additionally, the reassuring effect was notably stronger with 3D imaging (5.42 ± 1.36 vs. 4.22 ± 1.15, p = 0.004) (Table 4). The effect size analysis using Cohen’s d resulted in a value of 1.52 [95% CI: 0.98–2.75] for senior surgeons. The missing data rate across all questionnaires was less than 1%. Among surgeons, 92% indicated willingness to learn 3D software, and 90% considered it necessary for future practice.

The lack of significant improvement in bladder visualization among surgeons (p = 0.098) warrants careful interpretation. Given the high baseline scores (5.83 ± 0.57 for 2D), this finding likely reflects a ceiling effect and limited scale sensitivity. This should not be interpreted as evidence that 3D imaging provides no benefit for bladder visualization, as insufficient statistical power may have precluded detection of a meaningful difference.

Discussion

Our study provides preliminary evidence of the perceived value of 3D imaging across multiple stakeholder groups in pediatric pelvic tumor surgery. The results demonstrate significant improvements in self-reported anatomical understanding, surgical planning, and communication, with notable effect sizes across all evaluated populations. The present findings reflect perceived and self-reported benefits of 3D imaging and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of improved surgical or educational outcomes.

The striking improvement in anatomical comprehension among non-medical personnel (Δ = 2.62, p < 0.001) has important implications for patient care. This enhancement in understanding, particularly regarding complex structures such as vessels (Δ = 2.84) and nerves (Δ = 3.12), suggests that 3D imaging could potentially improve the informed consent process. Traditional informed consent relies heavily on verbal descriptions and 2D imaging that patients and families often struggle to interpret. Our findings align with previous studies by Bernhard et al. and Van de Belt et al., who reported improved patient understanding with 3D models in adult urological and neurosurgical procedures19,20. However, our study is the first to quantitatively demonstrate this perceived benefit in pediatric oncological surgery.

The absence of increased anxiety levels (p = 0.627) despite improved understanding of surgical complexity is particularly noteworthy. This finding challenges the common concern that detailed anatomical visualization might increase patient and family anxiety. Instead, the data suggests that better understanding may contribute to improved psychological preparation for surgery, as evidenced by the significant improvement in reassurance scores (p < 0.001).

For medical trainees (residents and fellows), the substantial improvement in anatomical visualization (p < 0.001) has significant implications for medical education. The marked enhancement in understanding neural structures and spatial relationships addresses a crucial challenge in surgical education: the development of three-dimensional anatomical thinking. This finding supports the work of Kong et al.22, who demonstrated improved learning outcomes with 3D visualization in hepatic surgery training. However, our study extends these findings to complex pediatric pelvic anatomy, where spatial relationships are more intricate and variations more common.

The educational impact is further emphasized by the improvement in surgical planning understanding among trainees. This suggests that 3D visualization could bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical surgical planning, potentially accelerating the learning curve for surgical trainees. The high effect size in this group (Cohen’s d = 1.90) indicates that this improvement is not just statistically significant but practically meaningful, though the within-subject design likely contributes to effect size inflation.

An interesting observation emerges from the anatomy sub-scores (Figs. 3B and 4B). With 2D imaging, the scores for medical trainees and senior pediatric surgeons were remarkably similar, whereas one might expect experienced surgeons to demonstrate superior anatomical understanding when interpreting conventional 2D images. This unexpected finding may reflect an overestimation of anatomical comprehension among trainees, who may perceive structures as more clearly identifiable than they actually are given their relative inexperience with complex pelvic anatomy. Furthermore, when examining the 3D scores across all three groups, the values converge toward similar high levels. This convergence suggests a “democratization” effect of 3D imaging: regardless of baseline expertise, 3D reconstruction appears to equalize anatomical perception across different stakeholder groups. This finding has important implications for multidisciplinary communication and suggests that 3D models may serve as a common visual language that transcends experience levels.

Among senior pediatric surgeons, the results reveal a nuanced picture of the technology’s utility. The significant improvement in overall visualization (p < 0.001) despite high baseline competence with 2D imaging underscores the value of 3D modeling even for experienced surgeons. The particular benefit in neural preservation planning and vascular relationships highlights the technology’s utility in the most challenging aspects of pelvic tumor surgery.

The lack of significant improvement in bladder visualization (p = 0.098) among surgeons likely reflects a ceiling effect given high baseline scores (5.83 ± 0.57) and limited scale sensitivity. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence that 3D imaging provides no benefit for bladder visualization, nor as proof that the observed advantages are specific to certain structures. Insufficient statistical power and scale constraints may have precluded detection of a meaningful difference.

The high willingness among surgeons to adopt this technology (92%) coupled with their strong belief in its necessity for future practice (90%) suggests feasibility and strong acceptability among experienced surgeons, but broader implementation should await studies linking 3D modeling to objective clinical outcomes and real-world workflow impact. However, this enthusiasm must be balanced against practical considerations of implementation costs, training requirements, and workflow integration. Based on internal time-motion analysis of our automated pipeline—including software licensing, computational resources, and operator time for quality control—the cost of generating a complete 3D model including tractography ranges from approximately €50–150 per patient when using fully automated processing pipelines such as those developed by our team, representing a significant reduction compared to traditional methods (€500-1,500) that require extensive manual processing and expert validation. These estimates include direct processing costs (software, computation, operator verification time) but exclude indirect costs such as MRI acquisition, institutional overhead, and infrastructure. This cost reduction, enabled by near-complete automation of the segmentation and reconstruction workflow, substantially improves the feasibility of routine clinical adoption. These preliminary cost estimates should be interpreted as indicative figures that set the stage for formal cost-effectiveness analyses, rather than as definitive evidence of affordability. The time required for model generation, significantly reduced by our AI-assisted segmentation pipeline, remains a consideration for routine clinical use.

An important methodological consideration is the within-subject design, where participants viewed both 2D and 3D presentations of the same cases. While randomization of presentation order was employed, this design introduces potential learning effects: once a participant has seen a case in one format, they have already formed a mental picture of the anatomy that may influence their evaluation of the subsequent format. Additionally, the novelty and visual richness of 3D presentations may introduce a preference bias independent of actual understanding improvements. These factors suggest caution in attributing observed differences solely to the imaging modality, and future studies should consider parallel-group designs to better isolate true effects.

The large effect sizes observed (Cohen’s d ranging from 1.52 to 2.80) exceed typical magnitudes in biomedical and educational research. While these may partly reflect genuine effects, they are likely inflated by the within-subject design, which eliminates between-person variability and mathematically increases standardized effect sizes. This should be considered when comparing our results to between-subject studies.

Our study has several strengths, including its multi-stakeholder approach, large sample size, and rigorous statistical analysis. The high completion rate (98.1%) despite the lengthy questionnaire (80 min) suggests strong engagement and reliable data. Although the questionnaire required a substantial completion time, the high response rate suggests strong participant engagement; nonetheless, potential fatigue effects remain a limitation, and future studies should consider shorter formats or scheduled breaks to mitigate this risk. The use of standardized video presentations and randomized case order minimized potential biases in the evaluation process.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study focused on pelvic tumors, and the results may not be fully generalizable to other anatomical regions or pathologies. The five clinical cases, while representative of pediatric pelvic tumors, may not capture the full spectrum of clinical presentations, and certain tumor configurations that particularly benefit from 3D visualization may be overrepresented. Secondly, while our study demonstrates improved perceived understanding and planning, it does not directly assess the impact on surgical outcomes; objective endpoints such as operative time, complication rates, and nerve preservation were not evaluated. Thirdly, the senior surgeon group, while representing major pediatric surgery centers, was relatively small (n = 12), potentially limiting the generalizability of findings in this subgroup; these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Furthermore, the small senior surgeon sample (n = 12) may have insufficient power to detect smaller but clinically meaningful differences (Type II error risk). This represents a significant limitation that should be addressed in future multi-center studies with larger surgeon cohorts. The study design, which evaluated 3D modeling in a multi-stakeholder cohort, did not stratify participants by level of medical training. Future studies should consider evaluating and quantifying the role of 3D imaging across varying levels of expertise. Future studies should employ multivariable analyses to identify which specific participant characteristics (years of experience, prior 3D exposure, etc.) predict greatest benefit from 3D imaging.

The questionnaire used in this study, while carefully designed based on existing literature, did not undergo formal psychometric validation. Internal reliability measures (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) were not calculated for composite scores, and factor analysis was not performed to confirm that grouped items measure unitary constructs. Future studies should employ validated instruments or conduct psychometric validation of novel questionnaires.

The within-subject design may introduce recall bias, though randomized presentation order and structure-specific improvements suggest genuine visualization benefits. Future studies should consider parallel-group designs despite the challenge of controlling for individual differences.

These findings have important implications for future practice. The strong effect sizes across all groups suggest that 3D imaging may represent a valuable adjunct to conventional imaging in pediatric pelvic tumor surgery, particularly for complex cases, improving perceived understanding, communication, and surgical planning. These exploratory findings in a small surgeon cohort (n = 12) suggest a favorable reception that warrants validation in larger, multi-center studies before implementation recommendations can be made. Future implementation will require careful consideration of practical aspects including workflow integration, training programs, and cost-effectiveness analysis.

Beyond static 3D models and video presentations, interactive applications allowing real-time manipulation of 3D anatomy could further enhance clinical utility. Such applications could be particularly valuable during informed consent discussions, enabling families to explore the surgical anatomy themselves. Our team is currently developing such interactive tools, and future studies will evaluate their added value compared to video-based presentations.

Future research should focus on several key areas. First, prospective studies evaluating the impact of 3D imaging on objective surgical outcomes (operative time, complication rates, nerve preservation) are needed. Second, cost-effectiveness analyses would help inform implementation decisions. Third, parallel-group randomized trials would help isolate the true effect of 3D imaging by eliminating learning and novelty biases inherent in within-subject designs. Fourth, studies examining the integration of this technology with intraoperative navigation systems could further enhance its utility in surgical practice.

Our results also suggest that medical education curricula should consider incorporating 3D imaging technology, particularly for complex anatomical regions like the pelvis. The strong perceived educational benefits demonstrated in our study indicate that early exposure to these tools could enhance surgical training and improve the learning curve for complex procedures.

Conclusions

3D modeling in pediatric surgical oncology may represent a valuable adjunct to conventional imaging, with participants perceiving improved family communication, medical education, and surgical planning. However, the present findings reflect self-reported benefits and should not be interpreted as evidence of improved objective clinical or educational outcomes. Prospective studies evaluating surgical outcomes are warranted to confirm these perceived advantages.
