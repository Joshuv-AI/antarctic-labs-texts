Advanced Pancreatic Cancer Complicated by Nasogastric Tube Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: CongBing Yan, Zhenzhen Chen, Biyun Xia, Yang Li, Junhong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn selected palliative care patients with nasogastric tube syndrome but no signs of acute airway obstruction, conservative management with corticosteroids and proton pump inhibitors may allow safe retention of the tube. This approach balances essential nutritional support and symptom control against the risks of removal.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a dismal 5‐year survival rate largely attributable to late‐stage diagnosis and its high metastatic potential [1, 2]. Early detection is paramount but challenging, as symptoms often remain silent until the disease is advanced [3, 4]. Consequently, many patients present with incurable disease, where the focus shifts to palliative care. Within this context, the placement of a nasogastric tube (NGT) is a fundamental intervention for managing malignant bowel obstruction and refractory emesis [5]. While critical for symptom palliation and nutritional maintenance, this procedure carries inherent risks. Among these, Nasogastric Tube Syndrome (NGTS) represents a rare but clinically significant complication, characterized by a triad of throat pain, hoarseness, and vocal cord paresis or paralysis stemming from postcricoid inflammation and potential neuropathic injury [6, 7]. Despite being described in the medical literature for decades, NGTS remains under‐diagnosed, particularly within palliative care settings where its manifestations can be erroneously ascribed to disease progression or treatment‐related side effects [8]. This diagnostic oversight is clinically consequential given the syndrome's potential to precipitate acute respiratory compromise.

Moreover, the emergence of NGTS introduces a classic palliative care conundrum: the tension between preserving a vital device for managing nutrition and core symptoms, and the obligation to eliminate a source of significant morbidity and potential mortality [9]. This case report delineates the clinical presentation and management of NGTS in a patient with advanced pancreatic carcinoma and multiple comorbidities. It seeks to elucidate the syndrome's clinical features, highlight the decisive contribution of systematic nursing evaluation to its timely identification, and examine a collaborative, multidisciplinary framework for navigating the intricate balance between competing therapeutic objectives in palliative medicine.

Case History

A 43‐year‐old female was admitted to the palliative care unit with a diagnosis of advanced pancreatic adenocarcinoma and hepatic metastases. Her medical history was complex, including nephrotic syndrome culminating in end‐stage renal disease (managed with regular continuous renal replacement therapy, CRRT), poorly controlled renal hypertension, and status post gastrectomy for a gastric schwannoma. Her primary admission complaint was severe, refractory neuropathic pain localized to the abdomen and back, rated 7–8 on a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) and inadequately controlled by an oral regimen of tramadol, celecoxib, and fentanyl transdermal patches.

To achieve superior analgesia, an intrathecal drug delivery system (IDDS) was implanted, which yielded substantial pain relief. Subsequently, the patient developed recurrent, high‐volume vomiting and markedly reduced oral intake. Given her surgical history, gastroparesis was initially considered the predominant etiology. A 12‐French polyvinyl chloride NGT was placed for gastric decompression and to facilitate enteral nutrition, leading to initial improvement of her vomiting.

The nursing team implemented a structured monitoring protocol, employing the Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 (NRS‐2002) alongside a comprehensive Symptom and Pain Assessment Record for daily evaluation [10]. While her nutritional risk score remained elevated due to a catabolic state and minimal intake, a distinct symptom cluster emerged approximately one week following NGT placement. The patient reported persistent, sharp odynophagia and a subjective sensation of pharyngeal constriction, followed shortly by the onset of pronounced hoarseness. Although subtle within her complex clinical picture, these symptoms were consistently captured and documented by nursing staff during routine assessments.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The meticulous documentation revealed a concerning temporal relationship between NGT placement and the development of new upper aerodigestive symptoms, prompting a formal interdisciplinary review. Differential diagnoses included gastroesophageal reflux disease, infectious laryngitis, and NGTS. An urgent otolaryngology consultation was obtained. Fiberoptic laryngoscopic examination demonstrated bilateral limitation of vocal cord abduction and mild erythema extending from the posterior pharyngeal wall to the laryngeal inlet—findings diagnostic of NGTS [7]. Crucially, the consultation ruled out imminent airway risk, as the patient exhibited no clinical signs of acute airway compromise, such as stridor, tachypnea, or oxygen desaturation.

The clinical team confronted a critical management decision. Standard therapeutic guidance for symptomatic NGTS advocates NGT removal [6]. However, in this specific context, the NGT served two indispensable functions: preventing vomiting and potential aspiration through gastric decompression and providing a crucial conduit for enteral nutrition in a patient with cachexia and uremia. Its removal risked precipitating a debilitating symptomatic crisis and exacerbating nutritional decline.

After comprehensive evaluation, the palliative care team, in concert with otolaryngology, nursing, clinical nutrition, and pharmacy specialists, endorsed a conservative, integrated management plan.

NGT Retention

The decision was made to maintain the NGT in situ, acknowledging its pivotal ongoing role in managing emesis and supporting nutritional status [11]. The clinical nutrition team formulated a viable enteral feeding plan that could be continued post‐discharge.

Adjunctive Pharmacotherapy

Intravenous corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) were administered to target local inflammatory pathology, and proton pump inhibitor therapy was optimized to address potential contributory gastroesophageal reflux [12]. Pharmacy specialists adjusted these regimens to account for the patient's renal impairment, ensuring drug safety.

Reassessment of Analgesia

The IDDS parameters were carefully adjusted to ensure adequate coverage of the newly developed somatic throat pain.

Enhanced Monitoring Protocol

A heightened nursing surveillance plan was instituted, focusing on frequent respiratory and symptom evaluations to promptly identify any clinical deterioration.

This coordinated intervention proved successful. Within 72 h, the patient's odynophagia and hoarseness demonstrated marked improvement and ultimately resolved. NGT‐facilitated drainage continued to control vomiting effectively, and the IDDS provided stable background analgesia. The patient was subsequently discharged to a home care setting with the NGT retained, having achieved a sustainable equilibrium between her multifaceted symptom burdens.

Conclusion and Results

This coordinated intervention proved successful. Within 72 h, the patient's odynophagia and hoarseness demonstrated marked improvement and ultimately resolved. NGT‐facilitated drainage continued to control vomiting effectively, and the IDDS provided stable background analgesia. No signs of airway compromise developed during the treatment course. The patient was subsequently discharged to a home care setting with the NGT retained, having achieved a sustainable equilibrium between her multifaceted symptom burdens. Follow‐up communication with the patient's caregivers confirmed that the NGTS symptoms did not recur, and the palliative care goals of comfort and symptom control were maintained.

Discussion

This case offers a pertinent clinical vignette for examining NGTS and the nuanced challenges of symptom management in advanced palliative care.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms and Risk Profiling in a Vulnerable Cohort

The pathogenesis of NGTS is attributed to sustained mechanical pressure from the NGT on the postcricoid mucosa, resulting in localized ischemia, inflammatory response, and potential compromise of the recurrent laryngeal nerves [7]. Our patient presented with several established risk factors highlighted in the literature [13]. Profound malnutrition and cachexia, intrinsic to her advanced oncological and renal disease, likely diminished tissue integrity and reparative capacity, amplifying susceptibility. Although midline tube positioning and cervical spine pathology were absent, her overall debilitated physiological state constituted a significant predisposing condition. This reinforces the necessity of considering NGTS in the differential diagnosis for any palliative patient with an indwelling NGT who develops new‐onset throat discomfort, voice alteration, or dysphagia.

Reconciling Therapeutic Priorities: A Deliberate Risk–Benefit Analysis

The standard management for symptomatic NGTS, as established in seminal literature, advocates for prompt NGT removal to alleviate pressure on the postcricoid region and prevent potential airway catastrophe [6, 12]. However, this case presented a therapeutic dilemma that necessitated deviation from this standard pathway. The decision to retain the NGT was not taken lightly but was the result of a structured, goal‐concordant risk–benefit analysis, explicitly weighing the potential risks of retention against the certain harms of removal.

The primary risks of retention, as highlighted by Juneja et al. [7], include progression to complete bilateral vocal cord paralysis and acute airway obstruction. Critically, our patient exhibited no clinical signs of impending airway compromise (e.g., stridor, desaturation), which significantly lowered the immediate threat of retention. Conversely, the consequences of removal were deemed both imminent and severe. First, the NGT was providing the sole effective management for intractable vomiting in a patient with a history of gastrectomy and uremia, preventing aspiration and maintaining metabolic stability. Second, it served as an indispensable conduit for enteral nutrition in a context of profound cachexia. Removal would have almost certainly precipitated a nutritional crisis and a return of debilitating symptoms, contradicting the core palliative care goal of comfort [9].

Therefore, in this carefully selected scenario, a conservative approach was adopted. We mitigated the risks of retention through a compensatory strategy: (1) deploying high‐dose corticosteroids to aggressively treat the underlying inflammation [8], and (2) implementing a heightened monitoring protocol with frequent respiratory assessments to ensure any clinical deterioration would be detected immediately. This case suggests that, in select patients where the NGT serves a life‐sustaining function and airway compromise is not imminent, a trial of conservative management with adjunctive therapy may be a viable option, provided there is a robust safety net in place.

The Integral Role of Systematic, Proactive Nursing Evaluation

The early detection of NGTS in this instance was fundamentally enabled by methodical nursing assessment. The routine application of validated instruments like the NRS‐2002 provided objective data on nutritional vulnerability, while meticulous symptom documentation captured the evolution of a new clinical entity [10]. Nurses, serving as consistent frontline clinicians, occupy a unique vantage point for identifying such subtle yet consequential changes. This case advocates strongly for the formal integration of specific oropharyngeal and laryngeal symptom screening into standard nursing assessment protocols for all patients requiring long‐term NGT placement.

Interdisciplinary Collaboration as the Cornerstone of Complex Care Management

The favorable outcome was predicated on seamless interdisciplinary cooperation. This interdisciplinary model was not merely descriptive but functionally essential. The otolaryngologist's risk stratification (absence of airway compromise) provided the clinical “permission” to deviate from standard care. The pharmacist's renal‐adjusted dosing ensured the safety of the pharmacologic intervention. The dietitian's pragmatic nutritional strategy guaranteed that the primary goal of NGT retention (nutrition) was achievable. Nursing, as the constant presence, integrated these inputs at the bedside, executing the enhanced monitoring protocol, assessing response to therapy, and communicating changes to the team in real time. Palliative care physicians synthesized these inputs into a cohesive, overarching goals‐of‐care framework [14]. This case exemplifies that true collaboration involves the synthesis of distinct, actionable expert opinions into a cohesive, value‐aligned care plan.
