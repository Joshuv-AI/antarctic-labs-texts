Development and validation of the multidimensional elderly health status assessment and screening (EHSAS) tool for community-dwelling older people in rural India
Authors: Jaya Singh Kshatri, Soumya Ranjan Sahu, Supriya Darshini Behera, Daisy Janssen, Susan Deborah Shenkin, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA), a cornerstone of geriatric care, is challenging to implement in low-resource settings due to workforce limitations and the absence of suitable screening tools to identify older adults who require CGA.ObjectivesTo develop and validate the Elderly Health Status Assessment and Screening (EHSAS) tool, a concise, culturally adapted, community-based, multidimensional screening tool, delivered by Community Health Workers (CHWs) to identify the need for CGA amongst rural older adults in India.MethodsWe employed a three-phase approach: tool development (literature review, expert consensus, face and content validation), scale development (exploratory factor analysis, convergent and discriminant validity testing), and scale evaluation (cut-off score determination, diagnostic accuracy testing, reliability assessment). The tool’s performance was compared to the criterion variable of moderate/severe frailty status based on Fried’s phenotype.ResultsThe final EHSAS tool comprises 11 items spanning the key geriatric domains, plus a frailty item not included in the score. A cut-off of ≥3 demonstrated good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha >0.7), substantial test–retest reliability (Cohen’s kappa >0.79), and balanced diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 76.3%, specificity 76.5%, negative predictive value 93.2%). Field testing confirmed that EHSAS was usable by and acceptable to CHWs and older adults.ConclusionThe EHSAS tool fills a critical gap in geriatric care in low-resource settings by offering a validated, brief and culturally appropriate screening instrument for CHWs. Its adoption can strengthen timely referrals for CGA, ultimately improving health outcomes for older adults.Keywords: elderly health status assessment and screening (EHSAS), comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), screening tool, community health workers, tool validation, older people

Introduction

Most lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) are characterised by a rapidly ageing population [1–3]. This includes India, where most of the older adults reside in rural areas with limited specialised geriatric care [4, 5]. Older adults often experience a range of interrelated health issues, including multimorbidity, frailty, cognitive decline, sensory impairments and functional limitations [5–7]. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive approach to assessment and management [8, 9].

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is recognised as the gold standard for evaluating and addressing the health status of older adults [10, 11]. CGA is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary process to evaluate an older person’s health and develop a coordinated care plan [12]. Synthesised evidence suggests that CGA can lead to improved health outcomes [8, 11]. Despite its routine use in high-income countries (HICs), the implementation of CGA in LMICs remains limited [11]. Challenges to implementing CGA include a lack of specialists, inadequate infrastructure and the absence of culturally appropriate tools [12–14]. In most LMICs, performing a lengthy CGA in all older people is not feasible.

India’s public health system is organised as a three-tier structure of primary, secondary, and tertiary care [15, 16]. Primary care is delivered through sub-centres and Primary Health Centres (PHCs), with each covering 30 000 people in rural areas. Community Health Workers (CHWs), known as Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA), are responsible for 1000–1250 people, facilitating health promotion, basic screening and referral linkages. Geriatric services are coordinated under the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE), where community-based services are delivered through the ASHA workers, and specialised clinics are run at PHCs [17]. However, the implementation of the NPHCE is inconsistent and the programme is continuously working out delivery models to improve standards of care [18, 19].

Considering the scale of population, a model where the ASHAs screen older people to identify eligible candidates for CGA at PHC is a practical solution [20]. Such screening needs to be multidimensional, while being brief and simple in language and usage, while culturally appropriate. No such tools are currently available for use in LMICs [13, 21]. The development of such community-based screening tools aligns with the global commitment to healthy ageing, particularly the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) and the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework [22, 23]. The UN Decade of Healthy Ageing has identified delivering integrated care as one of its key action areas, while the ICOPE framework emphasises the importance of community-level interventions and basic assessment tools that can be implemented by trained health workers, including CHWs, to identify older adults who require more comprehensive care.

This study aims to develop and evaluate the validity and reliability of a concise, culturally appropriate, and multidimensional screening tool, referred to as the Elderly Health Status Assessment and Screening (EHSAS), for referral to primary-care based CGA in rural India. ‘EHSAS’ is a common word that translates to ‘feeling’ in many local South Asian languages. This is part of a larger Wellcome Trust/DBT India Alliance-funded implementation research project on improving health outcomes amongst older adults in rural India, led by an international, multidisciplinary team including community physicians, geriatricians, general practitioners, public health specialists, and programme managers from India, the UK, and the Netherlands [20].

Methodology

Study design and setting

This study employed a multiphase, mixed-methods approach to develop and validate the EHSAS tool. The study was conducted in rural villages served by the field practice area of the Model Rural Health Research Unit (MRHRU) in Tigiria, Cuttack District, Odisha. The MRHRU is a government-funded research platform under the Department of Health Research, embedded within the National Health Mission framework [24]. This included five PHC (serving 10–15 thousand population each) and 14 sub-centres (serving 3–5 thousand population each) providing primary healthcare. Each village has an ASHA worker, a female community volunteer appointed by the State health department. ASHAs receive 23 days of training and are monitored monthly.

The participants were ASHAs and older people selected from a rural cohort of older people-DISHA G, established in the study site. The DISHA-G rural cohort of 18 389 residents aged 50+ was established between June 2021 and October 2022. Data collection was digital, and the cohort will be followed annually from 2024 to 2028. This has been described in further detail elsewhere [25].

Tool development and validation

We followed best practices in tool development and validation [20, 26], described in three phases: Tool development; Scale development; Scale evaluation.

Tool development

Tool development

Domain identification and item generation: The conceptual framework for EHSAS was developed by identifying key domains relevant to the health and well-being of older adults in low-resource settings in an overview of reviews [11]. Twelve primary constructs were defined [11, 12, 21] (Figure 1). See Appendix 1 for details.

Face validation: Face validity was assessed through field testing with 18 CHWs and 40 older adult participants. For CHWs, permission was first obtained from the district health authorities and their immediate supervisors were informed. Investigators then contacted CHWs directly, explained the study purpose, and obtained written informed consent before participation. For older adults, participants were recruited from the DISHA-G cohort but provided additional written informed consent specifically for this validation exercise, separate from the original cohort consent. Structured feedback was collected using a Likert scale between 1–5 (for item understanding, relevance, ease of use and comfort). This was supplemented by two focus group discussions (FGDs) amongst older people and five in-depth interviews with CHWs. Items were reduced to the best 5–6 per construct. Scale evaluation

Scale evaluation

Cut-off derivation and scoring: The multidisciplinary international study team (comprising geriatricians, elderly care physician, programme managers, clinicians and public health researchers from India, the UK, and the Netherlands) decided to determine criterion validity using frailty status (moderate or severe), defined by the Fried phenotype, as this is a predictor of adverse health outcomes and widely accepted indication for CGA referral in primary care [31–34]. Using logistic regression, the relationship was modelled between EHSAS total scores and frailty status (moderate or severe).

Using logistic regression, the relationship was modelled between EHSAS total scores and frailty status (moderate or severe).

Diagnostic accuracy testing: ROC analysis was performed to determine the optimal cut-off for classifying high-risk individuals and accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values were estimated at that point. Youden’s Index was used to identify the point that maximised sensitivity and specificity, a standard approach for cut-off selection in diagnostic accuracy studies [35, 36].

Participant survey

To evaluate the psychometric properties of the EHSAS tool, we undertook a community-based survey amongst older adults in rural Odisha. This cross-sectional validation survey was conducted with a sample of 200 older adults (≥60 years) selected using stratified random sampling from the DISHA cohort [25]. The survey allowed for comprehensive construct validation of the tool. Its robustness derived from (i) the use of the DISHA-G cohort, a census-based sampling frame of over 18 000 older adults ensuring representativeness; (ii) the sample size of 200 participants, which exceeded recommended thresholds for stable EFA (typically ≥150 participants or 5–10 participants per item) [37]; and (iii) the inclusion of all candidate items spanning the predefined geriatric domains.

Reliability testing

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with a threshold of ≥0.7 considered acceptable for newly developed scales [26, 38]. For test–retest reliability assessment, a randomly selected subsample of 60 participants (around 30% of the original sample) was re-interviewed after a 2-month interval, with values above 0.7 indicating substantial agreement [39]. This sample size exceeds the minimum recommendations for Cohen’s kappa reliability testing, which suggest at least 25–50 cases for stable estimates with acceptable confidence intervals [39]. The subsample was selected using computer-generated random numbers to ensure representativeness of the larger cohort. Test–retest interviews were conducted by the same CHWs who completed the initial assessments.

Analysis and reporting

Data analysis was carried out using R software. Findings are reported in accordance with standard recommendations for reporting the results of studies of instrument and scale development and testing [38].

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Human Ethics Committee of ICMR-RMRC Bhubaneswar [ICMR-RMRC/IHEC-2022/109] and followed the National Ethical Guidelines for biomedical research in human participants [40]. Separate consent procedures were followed for each group. CHWs provided written informed consent after being contacted directly by the investigators, following permission from state health authorities. Older adult participants, although already part of the DISHA-G cohort, were approached again for the present validation study and provided additional written informed consent, distinct from the cohort-level consent—with thumb print impression alongside witness signature if illiterate. All data were anonymized prior to analysis and reporting. Participants who screened positive for geriatric syndromes using the EHSAS tool were referred to their nearest public primary healthcare facility (PHC, or sub-centre) via a structured referral slip completed by the community health worker. In most cases, PHCs provide basic assessment by medical officers but lack in-house specialist geriatric services; onward referral to district hospitals, where needed, were done. These referral pathways are consistent with operational protocols for care for older people in rural India’s public health system, though practical barriers such as distance, availability of services, and need for accompaniment are acknowledged.

Tool development: face and content validity

CHWs reported high confidence in using the tool following a half-day orientation and practical training. (Appendix 3) The tool was consistently administered in under 12 minutes, and the accompanying instruction manual (Appendix 6) provided clear guidance for field use.

Following initial refinements in items, community participants rated the version of the tool with 5–6 items per construct (Appendix 2) highly on understanding (mean score: 4.91), relevance (4.87), and ease of use (4.85), with slightly lower scores for comfort with language of items (4.32), indicating overall good acceptability. FGDs and interviews helped refine item wording and sequencing.

The item-level content validity indices (I-CVI) ranged widely and most items failed to achieve the prespecified acceptability threshold of ≥0.78. Items with an I-CVI < 0.78 were revised for language and cultural adaptation or removed entirely after discussion. After substantial revision and removal of low-rated items, the remaining 11 items (plus general frailty item) were re-evaluated by the panel. In this final round, all retained items achieved an I-CVI ≥ 0.91 for clarity, ≥ 0.96 for relevance, and ≥ 0.91 for simplicity (Appendix 3).

Scale development: construct validity

The EFA of the proposed 12-item tool supported a unidimensional structure and demonstrated acceptable sampling (KMO = 0.73). (Appendix 3 and Table 2 for the English version).

Correlations between EHSAS domains and standard comparator instruments (Appendix 1) were variable (Appendix 4). The strongest correlation was observed in the hearing domain (r = 0.86) while moderate associations were found for mobility (r = 0.50), nutrition (r = 0.43), continence (r = 0.48) and depression (r = 0.31). Although vision and delirium showed weak correlation with comparator tools, they were retained based on their relevance during face validation and high field endorsement. These domains were consistently identified as important concerns by older adults and CHWs during qualitative interviews. Items selected for each domain had acceptable to excellent discriminative ability (Appendix 4).

Scale evaluation: diagnostic accuracy and cut-off score

Using moderate to severe frailty as the criterion variable, the EHSAS tool score showed balanced diagnostic performance. Overall, the threshold of 2.5 weighted points (rounded practically to 3) offers a good trade-off based on the ROC and Youden analysis. The tool achieved an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI: 0.71–0.88, P < .001), with sensitivity of 76.3% and specificity of 76.5% at the threshold of ≥3 [Negative Predictive Value (NPV): 0.93; Positive Predictive Value (PPV): 0.43]. See Table 2 for comparison with two and four responses.

Reliability testing

Internal consistency of the final tool was acceptable (Cronbach’s alpha for each >0.7). Item-total correlations supported retention of all selected items. Test–retest reliability was excellent. Cohen’s kappa ranged from 0.790 to 1.000. Eleven of twelve items showed excellent agreement (kappa >0.90), while one item (Q7) had good agreement (kappa = 0.79). All retest interviews were performed by the same CHW to assess intra-rater reliability and the resulting kappa statistics demonstrate high consistency.

Discussion

This study presents the development and validation of a novel EHSAS tool, a concise, culturally adapted, and multidimensional screening instrument designed for CHWs. The name of the tool has been chosen purposefully as it translates to ‘feeling’, and is easily recognisable in many languages of South Asia. The tool, tested in Odia and translated here, showed strong psychometric properties in 200 older residents in rural India (mean age 67.7 years). It had high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha >0.7), good reliability (Cohen’s kappa >0.79), and satisfactory construct validity compared to a reference standard of frailty. A practical cut-off score of ≥3 balanced sensitivity (76.3%) and specificity (76.5%), with a high negative predictive value (93.2%). This threshold provides a good trade-off for public health screening, indicating strong rule-out capability and making it useful for primary care referrals.

The EHSAS tool directly supports the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) goals by enabling CHWs to deliver integrated care through systematic screening and appropriate referrals [22]. The tool complements the WHO Integrated Care of Older People (ICOPE) approach, which advocates for community-level screening to identify declines in intrinsic capacity [23]. The implementation of EHSAS can advance several ICOPE principles, including the integration of care across different levels of the health system, the empowerment of CHWs, and the promotion of person-centred care. Furthermore, the tool’s emphasis on cultural adaptation and linguistic appropriateness aligns with the UN Decade’s commitment to ensuring that no older person is left behind, particularly those in rural and underserved communities.

This study is from a setting where older adults have limited access to specialised geriatric services [2, 9, 41]. In contrast to HICs, where geriatric care has gained prominence, LMICs continue to face barriers such as inadequate infrastructure, workforce shortages, and the absence of culturally sensitive assessment instruments [14, 42, 43].

Most older adult screening tools focus on a single syndrome, while multidimensional tools like G8, VES-13, and RGA have been validated in HICs [21, 44–46]. A suite of tools is also available from interRAI, a global not-for-profit network [47, 48]. While these comprehensive assessment instruments are globally accessible, their implementation typically requires licencing arrangements with royalty payments for operational use, which can create financial barriers for routine deployment in LMICs.

The available tools often assume the availability of geriatricians and robust referral networks, or may not account for cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic differences in LMICs. In contrast, the EHSAS tool is specifically designed for the community-based primary care settings in LMICs, and is easy for CHWs to administer.

The integration of the EHSAS tool into the National Programme Healthcare of Elderly in India, can significantly improve early identification of older adults requiring CGA [17]. This equips programme managers with tools for operationalising CGA based care models in India [49]. Community-based referrals in India are typically directed to PHCs for further assessment. In our study setting, CHWs completed a structured referral slip summarising EHSAS findings, which older adults presented at the PHC for follow-up. While access to geriatricians or specialist services remains limited in rural India, this structured referral process ensures that older adults identified as high risk by EHSAS can undergo a facility-based CGA delivered by medical officers, with onward referral to district hospitals or specialist centres where required [50, 51].

The potential integration of the EHSAS tool within India’s health system is both feasible and timely. ASHA workers, who already conduct routine household visits for maternal, child health, and NCD programmes, can incorporate the 10–12 minute EHSAS screening into their workflow with minimal additional training, as demonstrated in our face validation phase [52]. This aligns closely with the goals of the national programme and guidelines in India [17, 49]. At a systems level, scaling up EHSAS nationally would not require substantial new resources but could be embedded into existing supervisory and reporting structures for ASHAs.

Beyond India, EHSAS offers a scalable model for other LMICs where CHWs are central to primary care delivery [53]. Future implementation research, including pilot integration studies and cost-effectiveness analyses, will be important to guide scale-up and adaptation in diverse health system contexts.

The study’s strengths include a rigorous, multiphase approach and engagement with CHWs and older adults. Limitations include the geographic scope and potential challenges in ensuring consistency across diverse languages (particularly non-Indic languages) and regions. The study did not include inter-rater comparisons; however, rigorous CHW training, detailed administration protocols, and periodic supervisory fidelity checks were implemented to standardise administration across CHWs. EHSAS shows good predictive validity for frailty but requires further exploration for predicting other outcomes. Future studies should validate EHSAS in diverse LMIC settings and explore digital adaptations for integration into primary care.

The EHSAS tool represents a significant advancement in bridging the gap between community-based care and specialised geriatric services in LMICs. It is a culturally attuned and brief geriatric screening tool for resource-limited settings. Its adoption can improve geriatric healthcare delivery and enhance older adults’ health and quality of life in LMICs.
