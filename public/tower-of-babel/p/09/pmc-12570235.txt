Triptolide-mediated immunomodulation of macrophages: from pathophysiology to therapy
Authors: Yi Chen, Qiuyu Zhu, Ankang Yin, Wei Wang, Juan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriptolide (TP), a potent bioactive compound from the Chinese herb Tripterygium wilfordii, demonstrates strong anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects. Evidence indicates that TP modulates macrophage polarization and regulates inflammatory responses through multiple signaling pathways, exerting significant immunomodulatory effects in various diseases.ObjectiveThis review summarizes the immunomodulatory mechanisms of TP on macrophage function, with a focus on its therapeutic potential in diverse diseases. We also explore strategies to mitigate TP’s toxicity, including formulation enhancements, targeted delivery, ligand conjugation, and structural modifications. Finally, we highlight the therapeutic utility and clinical progress of TP derivatives, offering insights into their broad clinical applications.ResultsTP modulates macrophage migration, polarization, phagocytosis, and the production of cytokines and chemokines, thereby alleviating various macrophage-centric diseases. Strategies to reduce TP’s toxicity not only mitigate systemic adverse effects but also enhance therapeutic efficacy through macrophage-targeted delivery. The clinical progress of TP derivatives demonstrates their broad therapeutic potential.ConclusionTP’s macrophage-targeted immunomodulation shows promising therapeutic potential for macrophage-centric diseases. However, its limited clinical use due to poor water solubility and significant toxicity highlights the need for further research to develop effective toxicity-reduction strategies.

Introduction

Macrophages are highly plastic cells that can polarize into classically activated (M1) or alternatively activated (M2) macrophages under different conditions [1,2]. In an organ overwhelmed with inflammation, macrophages first exhibit the M1 phenotype, releasing a large amount of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) α, interleukin (IL) 1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-18, and IL-23, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response. They also eliminate pathogens by activating the NADPH oxidase (NOX) system to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), inducible nitric oxide (NO) synthase (iNOS), and NO, mediating tissue damage and inhibiting wound healing. Although the M1 phenotype is beneficial initially, it can elicit an unrestrained inflammatory response that damages tissues and the immune system, thus contributing to various chronic inflammatory diseases [3].

As an infection enters the repair phase, M2 macrophages secrete large amounts of anti-inflammatory cytokines [IL-10 and transforming growth factor (TGF) β], contributing to angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and tissue repair [4–6]. Thus, reprogramming macrophage polarization via targeted immunomodulators may have therapeutic potential in patients with chronic inflammatory diseases.

Tripterygium wilfordii Hook. f. is a medicinal plant predominantly used in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), and its roots have long been employed to ‘relieve stasis and internal warmth’. Specifically, “stasis refers to TCM “blood stasis, a state of impaired blood circulation and local stagnation linked to modern circulatory disorders and inflammatory damage. Meanwhile, ”internal warmth denotes TCM “internal heat, manifesting as symptoms related to modern inflammation. The herb alleviates these conditions to address diseases involving circulatory stagnation and excessive inflammation, and has been used in TCM for centuries to treat inflammatory disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [7–9]. As a key epoxy diterpenoid lactone isolated from Tripterygium wilfordii Hook. f., triptolide (TP) inherits and mediates the herb’s traditional therapeutic effects and exerts notable immunosuppression and anti-inflammatory effects [10,11].

Notably, TP modulates immune cell responses to inhibit inflammatory effects. For instance, TP directly modulates various immune cell populations. TP and its derivative broadly modulate regulate immune responses by suppressing B-cell differentiation [12,13], Th1/Th17 cell activity [14,15], and immune cell infiltration [12,13,16–19], as well as restoring the Th1/Th2 balance [14,20]; this demonstrates potent immunomodulatory effects of TP across innate and adaptive immunity. In addition, TP treatment can directly downregulate the expression of M1 polarization markers and concurrently upregulate the M2-associated phenotypes, thereby alleviating the effects of inflammation [21–23]. In contrast, in the tumor microenvironment, TP can reprogram tumor-associated M2 macrophages toward the tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype. These contradictory effects may be related to the differences in the environmental niches. In general, TP exhibits context-dependent functions in different pathological conditions, thereby influencing macrophage function.

In the current review, we focus on the effects of TP on macrophages in different microenvironments, the underlying mechanisms, and the clinical development of TP-based agents so far. We also discuss the detoxification strategies and design of TP derivatives to provide a reference for the clinical application of TP and relevant research.

Fundamental biology of macrophages

Recent fate-mapping studies in mice have revealed that tissue-resident macrophages originate from two progenitor sources: embryonic yolk sac-derived precursors and bone marrow (BM)-derived monocytes [24]. This discovery is pivotal for understanding the functional diversity of macrophages, particularly during infection processes. In the early stages of infection, circulating macrophages derived from the BM are rapidly recruited to the infection site and often perform proinflammatory functions. In contrast, tissue-resident macrophages contribute to inflammation modulation and tissue repair. Notably, because macrophage function strongly depends on the tissue microenvironment, both the aforementioned lineages display functional plasticity, transitioning between proinflammatory and reparative states depending on the stage of the infection [25]. For instance, during an acute pulmonary infection, BM-derived monocytes differentiate into macrophages that mediate initial pathogen clearance through enhanced proinflammatory cytokine production [26]; in contrast, resident alveolar macrophages (AMs) and interstitial macrophages subsequently restore tissue homeostasis by secreting immunosuppressive mediators, thereby mitigating excessive inflammation and preventing immunopathology [27–30]. In general, these cells orchestrate an immune response, maintain tissue homeostasis, and sustain effective immune surveillance [31,32].

Effects of TP on fundamental biology of macrophages

Migration and phagocytosis

During an infection, macrophage precursors migrate to the focus of the infection via chemokines and various adhesion molecules [31,33]. Then, they differentiate into macrophages to phagocytose pathogens, present antigens, and mediate inflammatory responses [34]. However, in certain pathological conditions, dysregulated macrophage migration and phagocytosis can exacerbate tissue damage and chronic inflammation, contributing to disease progression. Here, TP can significantly reduce the secretion of key chemokines (MIP-2/CXCL2, MCP-1/CCL2, LIX/CXCL5, MIP-1α/CCL3, and MIP-1β/CCL4) to impair macrophage recruitment [18,35,36]. In vivo studies have demonstrated that TP can reduce MCP-1 levels and subsequently inhibit tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) migration in intestinal tumors [16]. TP can also disrupt macrophage trafficking by downregulating the production of adhesion molecules (ICAM-1 and VCAM-1), compromising endothelial attachment and transendothelial migration [37–40]. In summary, TP can suppress inflammatory responses via a dual mechanism: disrupting chemokine–receptor interactions and preventing adhesion-dependent transendothelial migration [18,37].

In addition to regulating macrophage migration, TP can exert context-dependent control over macrophage phagocytic function, which is a key effector mechanism underlying both pathogen clearance and tissue homeostasis [41]. Notably, TP can either enhance or suppress phagocytosis depending on the pathological setting. In leukemia models, TP has been noted to promote the phagocytic clearance of tumor cells mediated by peripheral blood macrophages, potentially through the upregulation of scavenger receptor [42]. In contrast, in radiation-induced lung injury, TP can attenuate excessive phagocytosis by AMs, thereby curbing inflammatory cytokine release and preventing secondary tissue damage [35,43–46]. Taken together, these findings highlight TP’s therapeutic potential by indicating that TP can fine-tune macrophage phagocytic activity in a disease-specific manner.

Polarization

TP exhibits bidirectional control over macrophage polarization, which is fundamentally determined by disease pathophysiology. In neoplastic and angiogenic conditions, including colorectal cancer [47], glioblastoma [48], and choroidal neovascularization (CNV) [38,39], TP drives the transition of the immunosuppressive M2 macrophages toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype through downregulation of M2 markers (Arg-1, CD204, CD206) and upregulation of M1-associated mediators [16,39,47,49,50].

In contrast, in AMs, microglia, and LPS-activated macrophages, TP consistently reduces the levels of proinflammatory factors including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, ROS, and iNOS and enhances IL-10 secretion [10,15,20,21,23,35,40,51–56], thereby promoting the resolution of inflammation. This M2-skewing effect of TP is similarly applicable to sepsis [51], radiation pneumonitis [35], RA [10,15,21,23,57], diabetic nephropathy [20], atherosclerosis [53], and PD [52], as well as to tissue remodeling disorders, such as fibrosis [40,54] and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [55].

Th1 and Th17 responses, particularly their cytokines (IFN-γ and TNF-α), promote M1 polarization in macrophages [58–61]. TP suppresses the Th1 cytokines and subsequent M1 polarization, exerting anti-inflammatory effects [13–15,20]. ZT01, a TP derivative, effectively suppresses proinflammatory macrophage polarization by inhibiting Th1/Th17 differentiation [14]. Therefore, TP may indirectly modulate macrophage phenotypic switching.

Macrophage polarization is also influenced by oxidative stress responses. Changes in oxidative stress markers, such as decreases in glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels, lead to ROS accumulation, promoting M1 macrophage activation; malondialdehyde (MDA) has the opposite effect [62,63]. TP can reverse changes in oxidative stress markers, including GSH, SOD, GPx, and MDA, in vivo [19,64]. In vitro, it can upregulate the expression of Nrf2 and Nrf2-regulated redox genes (HO1, SOD1, GPX1, and NQO1), reducing intracellular ROS levels [19]. ROS are critical in M1 macrophage switching; because TP treatment reduces ROS levels, TP may indirectly influence M1 macrophage polarization [63]. In summary, TP can alleviate macrophage-mediated diseases by driving M1-to-M2 or M2-to-M1 transitions in a context-dependent manner, both in vivo and in vitro.

Cytokine and chemokine production

TP significantly inhibits the secretion of inflammatory mediators in M1 macrophages. TP may reduce NF-κB activation and translocation through the inhibition of not only AKT and IκB kinase activities but also IκB degradation, subsequently reducing the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, VEGF, CXCL2, CXCL8, CXCL12, and MCP-1, as well as adhesion molecules, such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, reducing monocyte and macrophage infiltration (Figure 1) [17,19,37,47,49,56,65,66].

MAPKs, including ERK, JNK, and p38, are well-known stress-activated kinases involved in the regulation of proinflammatory cytokine production [67–70]. TP has inhibitory effects on MAPK signaling in macrophages [21] and inhibits p38, ERK1/2, and JNK MAPK signaling pathways, reducing IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL2, CXCL8, and VEGF-A expression in M1 macrophages but increasing IL-4 and IL-10 expression in M2 macrophages (Figure 1) [21,47,66,71,72].

The TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), a crucial kinase for activating NF-κB and p38, typically requires complex formation with TAK1-binding protein 1 (TAB1) for its phosphorylation and subsequent activation [73]. TAK1–TAB1 complex blockade by TP via direct interaction holds promise in inhibiting the p38 and JNK pathways, reversing inflammatory responses (Figure 1) [51,73].

Nrf2, a critical endogenous regulator of cellular antioxidant response, is essential in redox homeostasis maintenance [74,75]. The effects of TP on the Nrf2 pathway have varied across studies—with some demonstrating upregulation of Nrf2 expression but others indicating its suppression (Table 1). TP can upregulate Nrf2 and its downstream redox genes, including HO1, SOD1, GPX1, and the NAD(P)H:quinone acceptor oxidoreductase 1 gene (NQO1), but limit the excessive TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, ROS, and MDA production (Figure 1) [17,19,64,77]. However, TP treatment can inhibit Nrf2 signaling in hepatic macrophages, increasing the levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (e.g. CHI3l3, CCL8, and CXCL10; Figure 1) [76]. These contrasting regulatory effects of TP may be related to its dosage (Table 1). At high doses, TP might be cytotoxic, impairing Nrf2 signaling.

TP also affects cytokine and chemokine production by modulating other signaling pathways, such as the JAK/STAT, cGAS/STING, AKT/PKA, Smad2/3, p53, lncRNA-p21, and E2F1 pathways, suppressing the expression of NO, iNOS, COX-2, TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and chemokines (MIP-2, MCP-1, and LIX) in AMs, U937 cells, LPS-induced macrophages, and microglia (Figure 1) [17,19,23,35,52,57,78–80]. Consequently, TP may alleviate inflammation related to excessive macrophage activation via multiple pathways.

NO and ROS production

M1 macrophages strongly express NADPH oxidase (NOX) and iNOS, and thus, produce abundant NO and ROS, fueling proinflammatory responses [81,82]. NO is a crucial regulator of inflammation predominantly produced by iNOS. In neurodegenerative diseases, excessive NO production exacerbates oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [83,84]. TP can inhibit iNOS production in microglia, subsequently reducing NO release and thus alleviating neuroinflammation [52,80]. Similarly, Zhou R et al. indicated that the TP derivative (5 R)-5-hydroxytriptolide (LLDT-8) can downregulate iNOS transcription, thereby reducing NO production and thus alleviating inflammation in macrophages [66]. In leukemic mice, TP can increase ROS to promote immune responses [42]. Similarly, in ovarian cancer, TP can repolarize macrophages to M1, increasing ROS and causing tumor cell death [50].

However, excessive NOX activation and ROS production may damage adjacent cells, resulting in chronic inflammation and tissue fibrosis [81,85]. TP reduces ROS in macrophages, mitigating intestinal inflammation [17]. TP also downregulates NOX2 and NOX4 expression in AMs, inhibiting ROS production and reducing fibrosis [86]. Thus, TP can switch its function according to the pathological or physiological context to suppress inflammation and protect healthy tissue.

Inflammasome regulation

TP attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome activity and the associated induced inflammatory response, thereby protecting the heart against pressure overload-induced damage (Figure 1) [40]. Similarly, TP derivatives can attenuate cytokine storms in Raw264.7 cells by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activity and TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β expression (Figure 1) [87]. In contrast, in liver injury models, TP can activate NLRP3 inflammasomes, triggering IL-1β release and caspase-3-mediated GSDME cleavage in Kupffer cells, thereby inducing acute liver injury (Figure 1) [88,89]. Thus, TP can modulate macrophages through NLRP3 inflammasomes in a context-dependent manner.

Choroidal neovascularization (CNV)

Chronic inflammation and angiogenesis are pivotal in CNV [90–92]. M2 macrophages promote angiogenesis by secreting proangiogenic factors [93]. TP reduces M2 macrophage infiltration and proangiogenic mediators at lesion sites, curbing inflammation, proliferation, migration, and tube formation and thus attenuating CNV [38,39]. Thus, as a modulator of macrophage activity, TP emerges as a promising therapeutic agent for CNV.

Cancers

Various studies have demonstrated the anticancer therapeutic effects of TP. As crucial tumor microenvironment components, M2-like TAMs promote tumor migration, invasion, immunosuppression, and drug resistance by modulating cytokine release, immune microenvironment shaping, and angiogenesis promotion [94]. TP reduces TAM infiltration and M2 polarization, reprogramming macrophages toward an anticancer phenotype and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment to repress cancer progression (Table 2) [16,47–50,72,97].

Moreover, in mice, TP can inhibit PI3K/AKT/NF-κB phosphorylation in TAMs, thereby downregulating anti-inflammatory cytokine expression and tumor burden, as well as prolonging survival (Table 2) [49]. In colon cancer, TP can reduce TAM infiltration by downregulating tumor-derived CXCL12 expression via the NF-κB/ERK1/2 axis, thereby remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment (Table 2) [47,72].

TP also reduced mRNA expression of representative M2 genes (ARG1, CD204, and CD206) in cancer tissues (Table 2) [16]. These results support the protective role of TP-treated macrophages against cancer.

Autoimmune diseases

TP has been used to treat autoimmune inflammatory diseases, such as RA, SLE, and psoriasis, over centuries [98].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)

RA is characterized by inflammation and matrix destruction in bones and cartilage [99]. Bone resorption, a hallmark of RA, is primarily caused by osteoclasts (OCs) [100]. OCs differentiate from macrophages in response to the cytokines RANKL and M-CSF, which may contribute to bone damage [101,102]. Therefore, inhibiting OC formation and activity is a key therapeutic strategy for RA.

TP and its derivative can reduce the expression of proinflammatory cytokines in macrophages, inhibit the differentiation of RAW264.7 cells into OC-like cells, thereby mitigating inflammation within RA-affected joints [21,23,65,103]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and their cytokines (e.g. IL-10, TGF-β) inhibit OC differentiation and maturation [104–107]. TP can suppress Treg-mediated osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption by increasing IL-10 and TGF-β1 secretion [108]. Wang S et al. reported that TP can induce OC precursor apoptosis and inhibit OC formation, alleviating RA effectively [109].

The crosstalk between macrophages and osteoclasts, mediated by the RANKL pathways, is critical for regulating bone destruction progression [110,111]. TP and its derivatives can inhibit the PI3K-AKT-NFATc1, p38, ERK, JNK, and NF-κB pathways, thereby suppressing RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis and the bone resorption functions of OCs [65,71,103,110,111]. Thus, TP may be an effective therapeutic agent for RA treatment.

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

Macrophages present antigens to T cells, driving T-cell differentiation toward proinflammatory phenotypes, such as Th1 and Th17; these phenotypes contribute to the pathogenesis of MS [112]. LLDT-8 suppresses macrophage-mediated proinflammatory responses, hindering T-cell activation and proliferation during the priming stage of MS and ameliorating experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [113].

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

SLE is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease causing various organ injuries. Lupus nephritis (LN), a severe complication of SLE, manifests as increased autoantibody and cytokine production, along with infiltration of leukocytes, including macrophages [114]. As key inflammatory cells, macrophages are crucial in the initiation and progression of kidney injury [115–118]. Both in vivo and in vitro, LLDT-8 can reduce macrophage proportions, downregulate inflammatory cytokine and related chemokine expression, suppress immune cell infiltration into the kidneys, and improve kidney function [18].

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

IBD is associated with an increase in proinflammatory macrophage activity [119]. TP attenuates macrophage activation and M1-to-M2 repolarization via the mTOR/STAT3 pathway, mitigating chronic inflammation (Table 3) [55]. Similarly, TP can mitigate macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization to ameliorate intestinal inflammation both in vitro and in vivo (Table 3) [17]. In the colitis mouse model, the TP derivative ZT01 was noted to inhibit M1 macrophage polarization, alleviating colitis (Table 3) [14]. Thus, TP may be a potential pharmaceutical candidate for treating IBD.

Organ damage

Organ damage is characterized by localized inflammation and oxidative stress injury, often driven by uncontrolled immune cell infiltration and abnormal proinflammatory cytokine expression [121–124]. TP can reduce NF-κB activity, upregulate Nrf2 and antioxidant gene expression, inhibit macrophage infiltration and proinflammatory cytokine production, and reverse oxidative stress markers, thereby alleviating colonic inflammation [17,19]. Radiation-induced pulmonary injury, a common complication of radiotherapy for thoracic cancer, is characterized by lung inflammation that can progress to pulmonary fibrosis [125,126]. AMs are the primary inflammatory cells infiltrating irradiated lung tissues, leading to pulmonary fibrosis by promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) through profibrotic cytokine secretion [127]. TP inhibits AM infiltration and profibrotic cytokines, such as IL-1β, TGF-β1, and IL-13, thereby reducing radiation-induced lung inflammation [35,54,86]. As such, TP may be an effective agent for treating organ damage.

Diabetes complications

Macrophages are closely associated with the development and progression of diabetic complications. Chronic hyperglycemia enhances inflammatory responses, promoting M1 macrophage recruitment and accumulation at inflammation sites, thereby increasing proinflammatory cytokine secretion and thus contributing to various complications associated with diabetes [128].

Diabetic nephropathy (DN)

TP has been shown to inhibit inflammation and macrophage infiltration in the kidneys, thereby attenuating albuminuria and podocyte injury considerably [129]. In a rat model of streptozotocin-induced DN, TP (12 mg/kg/day, 4 weeks) significantly suppressed macrophage infiltration and subsequent inflammatory responses [20]. Moreover, in deoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA)-salt-treated mice, TP (90 μg/kg/day) was reported to alter expression of proteins associated with macrophage adhesion and transendothelial migration in renal tissues [56]. These effects of TP may be mediated by dose-dependent inhibition of NF-κB activity [56]. Thus, TP can effectively suppress inflammation and protect renal tissues, demonstrating its potential as a therapeutic agent for kidney disease treatment.

Diabetes-related cardiovascular disease

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is characterized by excessive inflammation and myocardial fibrosis. TP can attenuate myocardial fibrosis and inflammation, alleviating DCM substantially. These effects are partly attributable to the inhibition of TLR4-induced NF-κB signaling in macrophages and TGF-β1-mediated fibrotic pathways, which result in decreased proinflammatory cytokine production, fibrotic protein accumulation, and macrophage infiltration [37].

Diabetes-related vascular complications

Atherosclerosis is a progressive inflammatory disorder of the arterial vessel wall, characterized by substantial infiltration of macrophages [130]. These macrophages can contribute to foam cell formation through the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) [131]. TP significantly reduces cholesterol accumulation in oxLDL-induced macrophages by enhancing ABCA1 expression, thereby reducing oxLDL uptake and increasing cellular cholesterol efflux [53,132]. These effects are critical for the inhibitory activity of TP on the transformation of macrophages into foam cells, thus reducing proinflammatory cytokine levels. In summary, TP-mediated modulation of macrophage chemotaxis and infiltration might be an effective approach to alleviating vascular complications associated with diabetes.

Nervous system

Microglia are the primary immune cells within the central nervous system. Neurodegeneration can polarize them into the proinflammatory M1 or the reparative M2 phenotype, affecting the pathological prognosis of the resulting neurodegenerative diseases considerably. TP can inhibit the activation of M1 microglia and induce their polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Moreover, TP can prevent M1 microglia–mediated necroptosis and apoptosis, downregulating the expression of proinflammatory molecules (iNOS, COX2, and NO) [52,80,133]. Metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGlu5) exerts neuroprotective effects in microglia by inhibiting inflammation [134,135]. TP can upregulate mGlu5 to suppress microglial activation, thereby reducing neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity in PD [52]. Moreover, LLDT-8 has potent neuroprotective effects, including reduction in infarct size in the ischemic cortex by acting on microglia and suppression of inflammatory mediator expression, ameliorating the effects of ischemic stroke [136]. In summary, as an immunomodulator, TP can mitigate the progression of neurodegenerative diseases by inhibiting M1 microglial activation and inflammatory mediator release.

Transplant immunity

Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR), a major cause of transplant failure [137–139], is characterized by an attack on the transplanted organ by donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) [140]. M1 macrophages have a major role in AMR pathogenesis; they infiltrate grafts, secrete proinflammatory cytokines, and activate T and B cells [137–139]. In a study, fewer T cells, B cells, NK cells, and macrophages were noted to infiltrate the grafts of TP-treated recipients, which resulted in more effective alleviation of DSA-mediated renal allograft injury [12]. Similarly, in patients with transplant vasculopathy, TP significantly reduced the infiltration of T cells and macrophages into the allografts and inhibited proinflammatory and profibrotic factors within grafts, alleviating the intimal thickening of the allografts [13].

In summary, by inhibiting macrophage infiltration and activation and reducing B-cell differentiation, TP attenuates antibody-mediated organ injury and prolongs allograft survival [12,13].

TP toxicity

As previously elucidated, TP exhibits a broad spectrum of therapeutic efficacy across diverse pathological conditions in both in vitro cellular experiments and in vivo animal studies. However, it is also recognized as a highly toxic compound, particularly causing hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, which greatly hinders its clinical application. TP toxicity is primarily due to the interference with cellular gene transcription. Extensive studies have demonstrated that TP can inhibit the activity of multiple transcription factors, including p53, NF-κB, and Nrf2 [141,142]. TP can also inhibit the ATPase activity of the XPB subunit of the transcription factor TFIIH [143,144], thereby suppressing transcription and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which underlie its remarkable therapeutic effects in anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor aspects.

In addition, RNA polymerase II is a broad-spectrum polymerase responsible for mRNA transcription [145,146]. TP indirectly inhibits RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription by targeting XPB, a subunit of the transcription factor TFIIH [142]. This inhibition alters the mRNA levels of short-lived genes, including NF-κB, p53, and Cyclin D1, which are critical for cell survival and cycle regulation. Consequently, these changes disrupt downstream physiological functions and homeostasis, leading to a cascade of toxic effects.

In recent years, researchers have employed strategies such as formulation modification, combination therapy, and structural modification to effectively adjust the pharmacokinetic properties of TP, thus optimizing its distribution, absorption, and metabolism in vivo, along with reducing its toxicity and improving its bioavailability.

Strategies for enhancing efficacy and reducing toxicity of TP

In clinical practice, TP is typically administered as a decoction or tablet. Newer formulations, such as dendrimers, exosomes, liposomes, hydrogels, nanomicelles, nanoparticles, and targeted drug delivery systems, have been developed for TP. These innovations have improved the oral bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of TP but reduced its toxicity. Formulation modifications

Dendrimers are highly branched nanomaterials that can carry drug molecules on their surface for targeted drug delivery [149]. Researchers have developed a drug targeting TAMs by introducing ester-linked dendrimers at the C-14 position of TP. This approach triggers TP release within TAMs, reducing its distribution in other tissues and minimizing the related systemic toxicity (Table 4) [48].

Exosomes are nanoscale vesicles secreted by cells that can carry various biomolecules and are widely used for drug delivery [156]. He D et al. constructed an exosome-encapsulated TP targeted drug delivery system (TP-Exos) and reported that TP-Exos activated the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, selectively inhibited M2 TAMs and their procancer mediators within a tumor microenvironment, and induced ovarian cancer cell apoptosis, thereby reducing toxicity to normal organs significantly (Table 4) [96].

To further address the toxicity and poor water solubility of TP, Zhang L et al. employed TP-loaded PGA-modified nanoparticles (PATs) and noted that PATs increased TP concentration at inflammatory joints, reduced IFN-γ-induced cytokines expression in macrophages both in vivo and in vitro; moreover, they alleviated joint inflammation, bone erosion, and cartilage loss effectively and exhibited lower toxicity (Table 4) [154]. Silvestrini AVP et al. enhanced this approach further by decorating liquid crystal nanoparticles (LCNs) with siRNA targeting TNF-α and IL-6, encapsulating TP, and embedding them in a hydrogel (Figure 3A) [78]. This method significantly increased TP concentration in the epidermis and dermis, reduced TNF-α and IL-6 expression in macrophages, and enhanced penetration and local drug efficacy (Figure 3A and Table 4) [78]. Wang K et al. designed a long-acting hydrogel that induced macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype after MI, maintaining anti-inflammatory effects for 28 days and alleviating hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity (Table 4) [155].

Nanoparticles can be conjugated with specific ligands, such as folic acid (FA), CD44, CD47, galactose, and Ala-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly peptide (APRPG), to modify the TP distribution pathway within the body, facilitating its accumulation in specific cells (Figure 3) [157,158]. In RA’s acidic milieu, CD44/galactose-decorated pH-sensitive nanoparticles can deliver TP selectively to macrophages, inhibiting macrophage infiltration and inflammatory cytokine release substantially (Figure 3E, 3H and Table 4) [15,151]. Several studies have modified nanoparticle carriers with FA (FA-TP-Lips) to demonstrate enhanced RA joint and macrophage targeting (Figure 3E). These FA-modified nanoparticles can enhance specific TP uptake, significantly reduce proinflammatory cytokine secretion, and minimize side effects in normal tissues (Table 4) [23,57,150]. FA-TP-Lips can prevent osteoclastogenesis and protect cartilage and bones by reprogramming the M1 macrophages to the M2 phenotype, boosting therapeutic efficacy (Figure 3E and Table 4) [22].

Tumor-derived exosomes strongly express CD47 [159]. The binding of CD47 with signal-regulatory protein alpha serves as a “do not eat me” signal to evade phagocytosis by macrophages [160,161]. TP-loaded CD47–exosome–liposome hybrids accumulate in tumors, reprogram macrophages to the M1 phenotype, and trigger ROS-driven apoptosis without toxicity (Figure 3G and Table 4) [50].

APRPG can specifically target vascular endothelial cells in CNV lesions [162–165]. A study designed and synthesized APRPG-modified, TP-loaded nanoliposomes and noted that they significantly reduced M2 macrophage infiltration at the lesion site, effectively suppressing CNV progression without inducing retinal toxicity (Figure 3D and Table 4) [39].

Combination with Western medicine

A combination of sorafenib (SFN) and TP loaded in cancer–platelet hybrid membrane liquid crystal nanoparticles affords suppression of synergistic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) at low sorafenib doses and evasion of mononuclear phagocyte clearance [166]. RAW 264.7 macrophages, the primary cells of the immune system, can enhance cellular uptake and exert immune-evasion capabilities [152]. Li Z et al. reported that platelet membrane-camouflaged cancer cell–platelet hybrid membrane-camouflaged liquid crystalline lipid nanoparticles and platelet membrane-camouflaged liquid crystalline lipid nanoparticles significantly reduced internalization in murine RAW 264.7 macrophages, thereby prolonging TP’s circulation effects (Figure 3B, 3C and Table 4B) [152]. Furthermore, it allowed TP to specifically bind to tumor cells, enabling tumor targeting and prolonged circulation, promoting tumor cell apoptosis, inhibiting tumor growth, and exerting synergistic therapeutic effects [152].

Another study combined coencapsulating glycyrrhizin (GL) and TP in lipid nanoparticles (GLLNP), leading to the formation of stable lipid nanoparticles TP/GLLNP. In addition, GLLNP was noted to improve tumor immunosuppression by promoting M2-to-M1 macrophage polarization, eventually leading to enhanced drug retention and accumulation in the tumor area, along with synergistic anti-HCC effects, in vivo (Figure 3F and Table 4) [153].

ZT01

Incorporating an NO-releasing moiety into anticancer and anti-inflammatory drugs may reduce toxicity and enhance the activity of the original drugs [167–171]. The C-14 hydroxyl group of TP has been implicated in enhanced aqueous solubility and is often used for modification [172]. As such, a study introduced a furoxan-based NO-donating moiety at the C-14 position of the TP core to design and synthesize a series of TP/furoxan hybrids. Of them, compound 33 demonstrated excellent anti-inflammatory, NO-releasing capabilities at low doses and substantially reduced the acute toxicity of TP [173]. Fu et al. reported that ZT01 targeted various activated macrophage models to reduce IL-6 and TNF-α production, exerting potent anti-inflammatory activity [14,51]. Furthermore, ZT01 inhibited macrophage polarization toward a proinflammatory phenotype by suppressing M1 marker expression without affecting M2 marker expression [51]. ZT01 was also noted to considerably inhibit macrophage infiltration into the colonic lamina propria of mice with DSS-induced acute colitis. Moreover, ZT01 suppressed macrophage polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, as indicated by the decreased percentage of CD11c+ cells in LPS- and IFN-γ-stimulated BM-derived macrophages [14]. According to the current experimental results, ZT01 has a strong anti-inflammatory effect and reduced toxicity compared to triptolide, which provides a reference for the clinical application of TP.

Omtriptolide

Omtriptolide (PG490-88 or F60008), a water-soluble semisynthetic TP-derived prodrug, is synthesized by conjugating the hydroxyl group of TP with succinic anhydride. This modification allows PG490-88 to be efficiently converted to TP, its active form, by plasma esterase in vivo. PG490-88 can attenuate acute humoral rejection by promoting macrophage infiltration, thereby significantly prolonging transplant survival in a canine renal allograft model [174]. Given that monocytes are precursors to macrophages and further differentiate into various macrophage subtypes within tissues, F60008 elicits significant pharmacokinetic responses in monocytes, encompassing apoptosis, immunosuppression, and cytokine level fluctuations. Thus, F60008 may substantially affect not only monocytes but also their mature macrophage subtypes, which are pivotal in mediating immune responses and maintaining tissue homeostasis [175].

Although omtriptolide demonstrated significant therapeutic potential, it exhibited multiple toxicities in clinical trials. In a phase I clinical trial for treating relapsed or refractory acute leukemia, a dose of 5.7 mg/m2/day administered for 5 consecutive days showed substantial therapeutic efficacy [176]. However, doses exceeding 5.7 mg/m2/day led to cerebellar toxicity, which was identified as the dose-limiting toxicity. These findings indicate a narrow therapeutic window and highlight the challenge of precisely managing its toxicity. In another phase I study of PG490-88 for advanced solid tumors, mild hematological side effects (grade 1–2 anemia) and gastrointestinal adverse reactions (fatigue, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation) were observed, with no renal or liver toxicities [175]. However, two fatal events were observed among 20 participants [175]. One patient, receiving a dose of 12 mg/m2, most likely died of neutropenic sepsis. Another patient receiving 18 mg/m2 died 29 h after omtriptolide administration, presenting a complex clinical syndrome possibly due to increased trauma-related cytokines [175]. Given the unpredictable, slow, and incomplete conversion process to its active metabolite TP, F60008 is not an optimal derivative of TP.

LLDT-8

LLDT-8 specifically modulates macrophage functions, thereby exerting therapeutic effects in various pathological conditions [18,36,57,65,66,103,113,136,177,178]. LLDT-8 can inhibit microglia-mediated neuroinflammation through the IκB/NF-κB cascade, have an anti-inflammatory effect, and protect against acute cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury [136]. LLDT-8 can effectively suppress chemokine expression in IFN-γ-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages and inhibit the subsequent immune response by interfering with the IFN-γ/STAT1/IRF-1 pathway, responsible for chemokine gene regulation. LLDT-8 can modulate macrophage function during the priming stage of MS, leading to a reduction in proinflammatory factor levels. This reduction subsequently influences T-cell activation, ultimately leading to the remission of EAE (Figure 3) [113].

Clinical trials have confirmed that LLDT-8 has anti-inflammatory activities through the RANKL/RANK/OPG pathway to reduce the number of OC-like cells derived from RAW 264.7 cells, thereby inhibiting osteoclastogenesis (Figure 3) [65]. It can also modulate macrophage infiltration and activation, thereby protecting mice against bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis [178]. Moreover, LLDT-8 can prevent fatal end-organ pathology by inhibiting the renal macrophage infiltration and suppression of renal inflammatory cytokines and chemokine expression, ameliorating LN [18]. In summary, LLDT-8 can robustly modulate key macrophage functions, including differentiation, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion, thereby enhancing its therapeutic potential.

One in vitro study showed that LLDT-8 had a 122-fold lower cytotoxicity than TP, with only a 20-fold reduction in immune cell proliferation inhibition, indicating preserved efficacy and reduced toxicity. However, in vivo studies found that LLDT-8’s acute toxicity was only 10-fold lower than TP, and its effective dosage needed a 10-fold increase [179]. These results raise safety concerns for clinical evaluation. Nonetheless, the overall toxicity reduction may still make LLDT-8 a viable alternative to TP, pending further investigation.

Conclusion and future directions

In summary, TP can alleviate inflammation-related diseases by re-educating M1 and M2 macrophages through modulation of their migration, phagocytosis, and polarization via adhesion molecules, chemokines, and key signaling cascades. However, TP’s regulatory effect on macrophage function is not uniform but varies with macrophage immune status, TP concentration, and timing of TP treatment/infection (Figure 4). For instance, in the colitis microenvironment, phosphodiesterase 4B (PDE4B) is highly expressed and serves as a critical target for TP. TP strongly inhibits the pro-inflammatory phenotype of M1 macrophages with high PDE4B expression, but has minimal effect on resting macrophages with low PDE4B expression (Figure 4, panel A) [17]. Furthermore, in Mtb-infected macrophages, TP’s inflammatory effects are concentration- and timing-dependent. Low-dose TP (1–10 nM) exerts anti-inflammatory effects via lncRNA modulation, whereas high-dose TP (100 nM) induces pro-inflammatory gene expression (Figure 4, panel B). Moreover, pre-infection TP treatment mitigates excessive Mtb-induced inflammation, while post-infection treatment enhances macrophage intracellular killing by boosting pro-inflammatory activation (Figure 4, panel C) [77].

Systemic toxicity caused by TP limits its clinical application. Therefore, many studies worldwide have focused on improving the clinical potential of TP through either a structural modification or a novel targeted delivery system to target macrophages. Currently, several TP derivatives are in the clinical trial stage, demonstrating promising anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects. Among these, Minnelide has been extensively studied and is considered to be a promising lead compound due to a broad antitumor spectrum and its low toxicity. It is currently undergoing Phase II trials for advanced pancreatic cancer and is anticipated to progress to Phase III. However, Omtriptolide exhibits poor clinical applicability due to its unpredictable TP conversion and fatal adverse reactions, necessitating pharmacokinetic enhancements before submission to clinical trials. Future research should continue to explore innovative strategies to enhance the therapeutic potential of TP derivatives, thereby expanding their clinical applications.
