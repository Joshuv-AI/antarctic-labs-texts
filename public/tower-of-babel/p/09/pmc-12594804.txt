ANN-based fault classification and localization with optimized PMU deployment for transmission systems
Authors: T Malini, P Thirumoorthi, K Lakshmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and timely Fault Detection (FD) remains a fundamental challenge in transmission systems due to the dynamic operating conditions of modern power grids, the diversity of fault types, and fast reclosure events. Conventional fault diagnosis techniques are often constrained by assumptions of static load profiles, dependence on high-rate sampling, or extensive data requirements, which hinder their real-time deployment. To address these limitations, this study proposes a novel Artificial Neural Network (ANN)-based framework for Fault Classification (FC) and localization using only bus voltage measurements. The distinctive contribution lies in the use of physically meaningful features rather than purely statistical ones, thereby ensuring robustness and interpretability. Simulation studies conducted on the IEEE 14-bus system show that the suggested method achieves FC accuracy above 98% and localizes faults with an error margin of less than 2% of line length, outperforming existing data-driven techniques. Furthermore, a new Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU) placement strategy is introduced, which improves observability while reducing hardware requirements, enabling reliable performance even under severely compromised measurement conditions. Achieving 0.89783 accuracy and notable reductions, ANN showed up to 5.66% higher accuracy and over 30% lower error rates, confirming its superior FC capability. These findings underline the potential of the suggested methodology as a scalable and resilient solution for real-time fault management in modern transmission networks.Keywords: Fault classification, Fault localization, Artificial neural network, Phasor measurement unit, MATLABSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In large-scale power grids, the rebuilding of the system is highly dependent on the efficient location and isolation of faulty lines. Although circuit breakers and relays are commonly used to monitor transmission line status, misoperation in some cases can cause severe cascading failures or blackouts. The 2003 blackout in America1 serves as a poignant reminder of the potential consequences of such failures. The shortcomings of conventional monitoring systems necessitate the development of smart monitoring systems. The devices have high-resolution data2, which can be used in advanced analytics. Fault-localization models are used to accurately locate faults in transmission lines. There are two types of traditional methods: (1) wide-area techniques and (2) standard procedures. Impedance-based, traveling wave-based as well as artificial intelligence-based algorithms are common for single-, double-, and multi-end lines3–5. Using the determined voltages and the known bus impedances, the impedance-based approach determines the fault current. Then, using the dispersed parameters, it calculates the fault’s distance from the measurement location6,7. However, the applicability of these methods is limited due to their requirement for high-sampling-rate measurements and exact time synchronization8,9. Optimized PMU deployment is essential for enhancing transmission system reliability, as random or dense placement increases costs and redundancy. Strategic allocation ensures maximum observability with minimal units, enabling accurate FD, state estimation, and system monitoring. This necessity is crucial for modern grids, which face dynamic operations and growing complexities.

Recent advancements in optimized PMU deployment focus on intelligent algorithms, such as metaheuristics, Machine Learning (ML), and graph-theory-based approaches, to minimize cost while maximizing observability. Incomplete data, measurement redundancy, and cyber-physical resilience are now addressed, which ensures FD improvement, real-time monitoring, and robust state estimation in more complex power transmission systems. ANNs10, Support Vector Machines (SVMs)11, as well as Extreme Learning Machines (ELMs)12 are shallow methods that have been considered in this application. Small-parameter-based shallow learning models, in particular, can be ineffective in trying to capture the nonstationary and nonlinear nature of a complex system, e.g., transmission networks13,14. To solve these shortcomings, shallow-based algorithms can be augmented with signal processing algorithms, e.g. Wavelet Transforms (WTs)15, or with alternative shallow networks, e.g. ANNs and SVMs16. Features derived by measurements can be used to train AI-based schemes (some of them include NNs or SVMs). Raw data may need prior processing, e.g., fourier, WTs, in order to obtain the convenient features, e.g., coefficients or fundamental components17. Extraneous noise and change in configuration as well as other disturbances, have the potential to greatly affect the fault data and also decrease the accuracy of hybrid fault-location techniques18,19.

Deep Learning (DL), is a groundbreaking advancement in data science and ML that has been developed as a promising tactic for various applications. By maximizing the number of hidden layers in NN architectures and developing innovative optimization techniques, deep neural networks have demonstrated significant potential in fields such as power transformer protection, time-series forecasting, PQ assessment, and WT monitoring. DL techniques are applied to both conventional and wide-area fault-location methods. Many conventional Fault Localization (FL) systems rely on measurements from at least one terminal bus of each line, making them impractical for large-scale power systems with limited PMU deployment. To address this challenge, wide-area algorithms have been developed that utilize information from the entire network rather than just local measurements. Literature review

Related works

Faza et al.20 demonstrated that employing ML classifiers such as SVMs and RF can achieve high accuracy in classifying fault type, location, and distance using only five PMUs on the IEEE 14-bus system. While effective, their approach remains dependent on carefully engineered features, limiting scalability to larger grids. Similarly, Perl et al.21 addressed the need for transparency by introducing an explainable AI framework with neural additive models. Their method identifies critical bus measurements for PMU placement, improving interpretability and computational efficiency, though at the cost of reduced adaptability under highly dynamic scenarios.

Other researchers have explored DL for enhanced robustness. Wenting Li et al.22 employed CNNs with physically meaningful voltage features, demonstrating superior FL accuracy on the IEEE 39- and 68-bus systems. However, CNN models require large, noise-free training datasets, making practical deployment challenging. Bhattacharya et al.23 advanced this line by tuning hyperparameters in diverse classifiers (RF, DT, XGB, LGBM) and using sophisticated feature selection strategies, which improved accuracy but introduced high computational overhead.

Graph-based learning has also emerged as a promising paradigm. Ukwuoma et al.24 introduced a Deep Gated Neural Network (DGNN) with multi-scale attention, enhancing generalizability across topologies and fault scenarios. Although efficient, GNN training remains complex and data-hungry. Complementary approaches, such as Sodin et al.25 virtual bus concept, achieved precise localization without additional PMUs, but their reliance on generalized fault models may not hold under uncertain operating conditions. Hybrid frameworks have also been reported: Najafzadeh et al.26 integrated fuzzy logic, adaptive neural networks, as well as metaheuristic optimization for rapid FD, yet the reliance on parameter tuning increases system complexity. Afrasiabi et al.27 further improved resilience under noise using an IGRNN with an informative loss function, but computational costs remain significant. Research novelty

While numerous studies have applied ML, DL and graph-based approaches for FD and localization, most rely on purely statistical features, extensive PMU coverage, or computationally intensive models, limiting practical applicability in real-time scenarios. The novelty of the suggested work lies in its integration of physically meaningful bus voltage features with an ANN classifier, which enhances interpretability, robustness, and accuracy. Unlike conventional approaches that require high sampling rates or complete system observability, our methodology achieves reliable FC and localization even when PMU deployment is limited. To further strengthen system observability under constrained conditions, we introduce a novel PMU placement strategy that identifies optimal bus locations, reducing hardware requirements without compromising detection performance. Extensive simulations on the IEEE 14-bus system demonstrate that the suggested framework not only outperforms existing ML as well as DL techniques in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, and False Discovery Rates (FDRs), but also significantly reduces the fault search area, enabling faster decision-making. This combination of physically informed feature selection, ANN-based classification, and optimized PMU placement represents a significant advancement over existing methodologies, offering a scalable and practical solution for modern transmission systems.

Proposed methodology

PMUs have been increasingly integrated into power grids to enhance monitoring capabilities, offering far greater accuracy and higher data rates than earlier SCADA systems. Although SCADA systems are widely employed for grid monitoring today, advancements in PMU technology and the precision of synchro-phasor measurements are poised to significantly transform the grid’s monitoring and control processes. This transformation promises increased accuracy and much faster response times, contributing to improved stability, reliability, and security of the power grid.

ML techniques are gaining traction in power system applications, particularly for classifying and localizing different transmission line faults. By utilizing the V and I data collected from PMUs, various key features can be extracted, laying the foundation for ML-based predictions of fault types, locations, and distances along the transmission line. This paper introduces a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) classifier28 designed to extract recursive deep features and time-frequency features using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). The data used for the analysis were based on the IEEE 14-bus system, with a model implementation conducted in MATLAB/Simulink. An ANN classifier29 is then fed these extracted features, correctly classifying faults according to their type, the transmission line that is impacted, and the position of the fault along the line. The outcomes show that great accuracy in FC is achieved by the suggested ML technique. Figure 1 displays a block schematic of the suggested model.

Feature extraction

Feature extraction is based on RNNs and STFT, which can be utilized together to extract meaningful features from time-series data such as those generated by PMUs in power systems.

Recurrent neural network

RNNs28 with an internal memory mechanism are especially helpful with sequential data. The ability of RNNs to use the past and present input to make a prediction is supported by this memory, which is created by the cyclical nature of the information flowing inside the network. This is essential to the analysis of those sequences whose order of the information matters. RNNs have an internal memory that enables them to store and use important features of former data thus they are suitable as PMU placement. This benefit makes RNNs the preferable option to tasks that need sequence of events like time series analysis. RNNs use available information in the past to analyze the present unlike the traditional neural networks which use the current input only. The inherent memory of RNNs makes available data of importance of the past input to the system, virtually giving access to multiple sources of information the data point at hand, and the existing environment created by the past items in the sequence. Using an RNN, the input data as well as target data of FC and localization are trained. The RNN consists of five different layers: the input, LSTM, FL, softmax, and classification layers.

Input layer

The input layer receives the input data, based on FC and localization, and can be time-series data from the Simulink model implemented with PMUs.

LSTM layer

The LSTM layer processes the input sequences to learn the temporal dependencies. It retains past information via its internal memory, which helps recognize fault patterns over time. This captures subtle time-dependent patterns that can help locate where a fault occurs within the system. The LSTM produces hidden states for each time step in the sequence. These hidden states can either be passed sequentially, or more commonly, the final hidden state is used to represent the entire sequence.

Fully connected layers

The most prevalent layer in the NN is the dense layer, which is characterized by its fully connected architecture, where every neuron is linked to all neurons in the preceding layer. Dense layers are commonly employed in the final phases of NNs to transform features into a more discriminative space, thereby facilitating classification tasks.

Softmax layer

An FL layer with the softmax Activation Function (AF), which converts raw outputs into a probability distribution across potential classes, is commonly used in neural networks. The layer’s output values indicate the probability that the input data belongs to each class, and it has an equal number of neurons as the classification categories. Artificial neural network

The suggested ANN-based framework uses several physically meaningful features extracted from bus voltage measurements to enhance FC and localization. These include voltage magnitude dips, which reflect sudden drops in voltage at a bus during a fault and indicate fault proximity and severity, and phase angle jumps, which capture abrupt changes in voltage angles caused by disturbances, aiding in distinguishing fault types. Additionally, sequence components: positive, negative, and zero are employed to separate unbalanced fault contributions, with negative and zero sequences highlighting asymmetrical faults. A change in voltage (dV/dt) is used to detect faults early, as it quantifies the change in voltage over time when a fault is initiated. The time the voltage stays below a given threshold is measured as the period of voltage sag, which provides information on fault persistence and system recovery. Lastly, the voltage recovery slope indicates the dynamic behavior of the system after the fault, revealing the network impedance and fault location. All these properties combine to represent both transient and steady-state faults, ensuring the ANN receives pertinent and physically interpretable inputs, which are crucial for improving classification accuracy and localization behavior, even in the presence of low observability.

Following fault-type identification, fault localization was achieved using an ANN-based approach. The ANN was capable of locating 15 different fault line numbers, as listed in Table 3. Identifying the fault type is the first step in the fault localization procedure. The fault-on voltages were retrieved based on the particular fault type using this information. For example, only the magnitudes of the phase L voltage are used for fault localization during the phase A to ground fault situations. An L-G fault’s input vector has dimension 1n, where 1 denotes a single phase and n is the number of buses having PMU meters. Conversely, for a three-phase fault, the input vector was 3n. The ANN generates a vector size m, where each element represents the probability of the fault being near the corresponding node. If a defect arises at node 702 in the IEEE-14 bus system, the output vector shows a high probability value for that node and negligible values for the remaining nodes. Likewise, in the event of a fault between nodes 702 and 703, the output vector shows high likelihood values for either node 702 or 703, contingent on the fault’s degree of proximity. Results and discussion

The suggested ANN was implemented and rigorously tested using MATLAB/Simulink for experimental validation. The simulations were conducted on the IEEE 14-bus system in MATLAB/Simulink environment. A total of four primary fault categories were modeled: LG, LL, LLG, and LLLG. Fault impedances were varied in the range of 0.01-50 Ω to replicate realistic fault severities, and fault inception angles were considered between 0°-90° with increments of 15° to capture system dynamics under diverse conditions. Load variations of ± 20% around the nominal operating point were also incorporated to reflect real-world load uncertainties.

From these configurations, we generated a total of 12,000 data samples, which were divided into 70% training (8,400 samples), 15% validation (1,800 samples), and 15% testing (1,800 samples). The data samples were bus voltage waveforms, and time-frequency characteristics (with the use of STFT) and temporal characteristics (with the use of RNN) were obtained to perform classification and localization activities. Such a configuration makes sure that there is adequate variation in operating conditions, severity of faults and disturbances in the system, such that the model works well under realistic conditions. This section penetrates into the simulated results which clearly confirm the excellence capability of the suggested solution in error classification and localisation relative to the conventional protection methodologies. This equals a significant decrease in the electrical and mechanical loads of the power grid. The suggested solution allows promptly detecting the faults and developing quicker intervention and mitigation schemes, which will eventually maintain the integrity of the power grid and improve its overall resiliency.

The phase angle at PMU-bus 1 reflects the angular difference between the voltage at that bus as well as the reference point, typically the nominal angle of the system. During a fault, significant shifts in the phase angles occur owing to the disturbance in the power flow, and these shifts vary depending on the fault type and its location relative to PMU-bus 1. By analyzing the phase angle graph, the specific patterns of angular displacement caused by different fault scenarios were identified. Discussion

The suggested ANN-based FC and localization framework demonstrates robust performance under various fault scenarios, limited PMU observability, and noisy measurements. Simulation results on the IEEE 14-bus system confirm that the approach achieves superior accuracy, sensitivity, and reduced FDRs compared with conventional ML and DL methods. The integration of physically meaningful voltage-based features ensures interpretability and enhances the classifier’s reliability, even when the system operates under dynamic or partially observable conditions.

Although this has some benefits, there are a number of shortcomings. The performance of the method is partly defined by the strategic position of PMUs; poor placement can lower the accuracy of classification. Also, the ANN is highly accurate, but it needs a substantial amount of historical data and computing power to train which may be difficult to achieve in real-time, especially in networks of huge scale.

In order to overcome these shortcomings, possible enhancements are the addition of adaptive feature selection to reduce computational complexity, dynamic PMU placement algorithms that adapt to topology variations in the system and the combination with streaming phasor data to provide real-time functionality. Moreover, future research with the possibility of extension of the framework to bigger and more complex power grids and preserving low latency and high accuracy is a promising direction.

Conclusion

This paper presents an innovative FC and FL strategy for transmission lines utilizing an ANN to address various line configurations and fault types. The suggested method demonstrated exceptional performance in FC and localization scenarios, exhibiting high generalizability. Specifically, RNN and ANN classifiers offer a powerful approach for accurately classifying and localizing transmission line faults in power grids. By leveraging the high-precision data provided by PMUs and extracting both recursive deep features and time-frequency characteristics through techniques such as STFT, the suggested method demonstrates superior FD and localization capabilities. The results, validated on the IEEE 14-bus system using MATLAB/Simulink, confirm that the combination of RNN and ANN can achieve high accuracy, thereby advancing the consistency and effectiveness of fault management in modern power systems. Adding 1% Gaussian noise to emulate realistic PMU measurements causes only a minor drop in accuracy, indicating that the model is resilient to measurement disturbances. The localization error stays minimal (1.7–2.1% of line length), confirming the method’s ability to accurately pinpoint fault locations. Future research directions include examining and categorizing various conflicts, which may necessitate additional PMUs or specialized features. PMU signals offer valuable insights into system steadiness, dependability, and power quality, providing a rich dataset for further exploration and optimization using ML techniques. To demonstrate broader applicability, future work will extend testing to larger and more complex networks (e.g., IEEE 39-bus, 57-bus, 118-bus systems), with diverse line impedances, lengths, and meshing configurations, ensuring the ANN-based FC and localization method remains robust, scalable, and unbiased across varied system topologies and operating conditions.
