Inflammation awakens dormant cancer cells by modulating the epithelial–mesenchymal phenotypic state

ABSTRACT
SignificanceBreast cancer often reveals its life-threatening effects after disseminated, clinically undetectable dormant cancer cells awaken, generating aggressively growing metastases. This narrative does not reveal the mechanism(s) provoking the awakening. As shown here, this profound shift can result from inflammation of the tissue microenvironment in which these carcinoma cells are embedded.

Dissemination of cells from primary breast cancer (BrCa) to distant tissue sites creates the metastases that represent the cause of almost all deaths from this disease. These metastases often appear months, years, and even decades after the corresponding primary breast tumors have been successfully treated. These delayed clinical relapses indicate the existence of disseminated BrCa cells lodged in distant tissues that enter into a clinically “dormant” state for extended periods of time until some of these are reactivated and acquire robust proliferative ability, resulting in the outgrowth of macroscopic, often life-threatening tumors (1, 2). Notably, given the limitations of current clinical imaging, it remains a matter of speculation whether these dormant cancer cells exist as individual disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) or only microscopic colonies (3). Prior to relapse, these dormant cancer cells are clinically undetectable and appear to be resistant to currently employed chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Hence, an understanding of how DTCs enter dormancy and how these cells are “awakened” will be critical for developing successful treatments of BrCa patients bearing disseminated but undetectable metastatic disease.

The dissemination of cancer cells from almost all primary breast carcinomas to distant sites can be explained mechanistically by the actions of the cell-biological program termed the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) (2, 4). Following extravasation into the parenchyma of a distant tissue, however, the subsequent behavior of the disseminated cells, which may form persistent micrometastases, is poorly understood. Recent several studies have demonstrated that the disseminated dormant cancer cells in the distant tissues exhibit certain mesenchymal features (5–7). These studies also showed that dormancy can be overcome by overexpressing an epithelial marker E-cadherin or suppressing an EMT-inducing transcriptional factor ZFP281 (5, 6). However, forced awakening by manipulating the expression of EMT-related factors fails to reflect the physiologic mechanism of this process (8).

Previous research in our laboratory and those of others (9–14) has established that the cells forming human BrCa cell lines can reside in multiple alternative epithelial/mesenchymal phenotypic states, including epithelial, quasi-mesenchymal, and highly mesenchymal states. Of special interest, residence in the quasi-mesenchymal state, which is orchestrated by partial expression of an EMT program, places epithelial cells in a phenotypic state in which they acquire certain mesenchymal markers while retaining certain epithelial traits. We and others have demonstrated that carcinoma cells exhibiting tumor-initiating stemness programs reside in this quasi-mesenchymal state, which also confers on them proliferative powers (9–12, 14–16). More complete expression of an EMT program, in contrast, yields cells that have lost tumorigenicity and have shed essentially all epithelial traits; we term such cells “highly mesenchymal.”

Previous work of others has demonstrated how DTCs enter into a state of dormancy after seeding in the parenchyma of the lungs (5–7, 17). Here, we report that the awakening of such dormant cells residing in the lung parenchyma is not triggered by cell-autonomous mechanisms operating within these cells but instead by inflammation occurring in the tissue microenvironment surrounding these cells. This shift in their microenvironment drives these cells from a highly mesenchymal to a quasi-mesenchymal phenotypic state, enabling them to stably acquire tumorigenicity and proliferative ability.

Results

Highly Mesenchymal Cancer Cells Enter Dormancy after Seeding in the Lungs.

In previous work, we demonstrated that cell-surface expression of integrin β4 (ITGB4) can be used to resolve triple-negative Sum159 human BrCa cells into highly mesenchymal ITGB4 low (Sum159low) and quasi-mesenchymal ITGB4 high (Sum159high) subpopulations (11). Previous finding from others demonstrated that mouse primary BrCa cells undergoing partial EMT contribute to the formation of metastases, whereas cells entering into the highly mesenchymal state after experiencing full EMT mostly fail to colonize in the lungs (18). This suggested that Sum159low cells could be used as a model to study the regulation of the EMT-induced phenotypic state operating during cancer dormancy, specifically the dormancy of DTCs after their entrance into the parenchyma of distant tissues.

Because carcinoma cells can often transit from one phenotypic state to another spontaneously due to epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity (19–22), we proceeded to isolate clones of phenotypically stable, highly mesenchymal (CD44 high, ITGB4 low) cells using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (Fig. 1A). As we found, the cells comprising 12 of these clones could stably reside in an ITGB4 low, highly mesenchymal state after being subjected to 20 serial passages in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We arbitrarily chose clone 1 (Sum159low-1) and clone 3 (Sum159low-3) for the work that followed. When viewed in a subconfluent state in two-dimensional culture, the Sum159low-1 and Sum159low-3 cells exhibited an elongated, fibroblast-like morphology, while corresponding quasi-mesenchymal Sum159high cells exhibited a polygonal, cobblestone-like shape (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Subsequent immunoblot analyses confirmed that the Sum159low-1 and Sum159low-3 cells exhibited elevated levels of the N-cadherin, fibronectin, and Zeb1 mesenchymal markers as well as low levels of the p63 and ITGB4 quasi-mesenchymal markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

After being orthotopically implanted in the mammary fat pad, both the highly mesenchymal Sum159low-1 and quasi-mesenchymal Sum159high cells were able to form tumors at this site (Fig. 1B). Consistent with our previous findings (11), Sum159high cells were found to be far more tumorigenic than Sum159low-1 cells (Fig. 1B). Additionally, metastatic colonization could be observed in the lungs of mice implanted orthotopically in the mammary fat pad with Sum159 high cells but not with Sum159low-1 cells (Fig. 1C). Although metastatic colonies could not be observed following implantation of these Sum159low-1 cells, immunofluorescence (IF) analyses showed that single disseminated Sum159low-1 cells persisted in the lung parenchyma, indicating that these cells were capable of disseminating from the site of orthotopic implantation but incapable, at least in the lungs, of the subsequent colonization that would yield macroscopic growths (Fig. 1D). These disseminated Sum159low-1 cells were found to be Ki67 negative, indicating that they were in a nonproliferative, dormant state (Fig. 1D). These results suggested that the weakly tumorigenic, highly mesenchymal Sum159low-1 cells possessed the ability to intravasate, disseminate, and extravasate into the lung parenchyma but lacked the ability to immediately form vigorously growing metastatic colonies. Further characterization revealed that these Ki67-negative, dormant Sum159low-1 cells lacked p63 nuclear staining and ITGB4 cell-surface staining, providing further indication that they resided in a highly mesenchymal state incompatible with tumor-initiating ability (Fig. 1D).

We considered the possibility that the failure of orthotopically implanted Sum159low-1 BrCa cells to form significant numbers of lung metastases was attributable to the slow growth of the primary tumors that they formed and resulting small numbers of cancer cells released into the general circulation. To circumvent this problem, we seeded cells directly into the lungs of mice via tail vein injection. Similar to their previously cited behavior, the Sum159high cells were able to outgrow rapidly to form metastatic colonies, whereas Sum159low-1 and low-3 cells were unable to do so (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). We observed that at day 7 post tail-vein injection, essentially all of the Sum159low-1 cells that had managed to seed the lung parenchyma remained in the highly mesenchymal (CD44 high, ITGB4 low) state (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). At day 42 post injection, single disseminated Ki67-negative Sum159low-1 cells could still be found in the lungs, indicating that they continued to reside in a dormant state (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Additionally, these cells lacked nuclear staining of p63 as well as ITGB4 and E-cadherin cell-surface staining, providing further indication that they resided in a highly mesenchymal state (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).

Wishing to extend these findings, we used two other experimentally generated, highly mesenchymal human BrCa cell lines termed NAMEC8R ITGB4 low (NAMEC8Rlow) and HMLER C1-sgKMT2D (11, 16, 22, 23). We found that the cells of both lines indeed exhibited a low efficiency in forming metastatic colonies in the lungs (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Additionally, disseminated Ki67-negative dormant NAMEC8Rlow cells lodged in the lungs were once again p63 negative (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Recognizing that these three experimental models of BrCa cells may not reflect the behavior of all BrCa cells, we suggest, nonetheless, that residence in a highly mesenchymal nonproliferative state is a frequent outcome of dormant BrCa cells that have become lodged in the lungs.

Lung Microenvironment Signals Awaken Dormant Cancer Cells.

Our earlier work demonstrated that the pro-inflammatory lipopolysaccharide (LPS) could elicit awakening of dormant BrCa cells lodged in the lungs of mice, albeit very inefficiently (24). In the present work, we have found that treatment of mice with the chemotherapeutic drug bleomycin, which is known to cause extensive lung damage, inflammation, and fibrosis, provides a far more effective strategy for inducing the awakening of disseminated Sum159low-1 cells in the lungs (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). Thus, large, actively growing metastatic colonies arose in the lungs of treated mice; such colonies were essentially absent in control mice that had not been treated with bleomycin. Similar results were also obtained by using the other two highly mesenchymal BrCa cell lines described above (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Hence, treatment with bleomycin, as described here, presents a highly useful experimental model of at least one type of metastatic awakening occurring in the lungs.

Importantly, these initial observations did not address the possibility that the bleomycin agent could also act directly on the dormant carcinoma cells rather than on their surrounding tissue microenvironment. Wishing to address this, we found that the Sum159low-1 cells treated with bleomycin in vitro for 4 d failed to form metastatic colonies in the lungs (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Moreover, since bleomycin is no longer active 3 d after being delivered to the exposed tissue (25), we also administered bleomycin to mice 3 d before Sum159low-1 cells were implanted into the lungs of mice. As we found, these Sum159low-1 cells could still be awakened (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F), indicating the importance of exposure of these carcinoma cells to the bleomycin-treated lung microenvironment rather than directly to the drug itself. To summarize, the above results suggested that the decisions by dormant disseminated BrCa cells in the lungs that lead to their awakening are triggered by non-cell-autonomous signals, specifically those arising in the tissue microenvironment in which these cells are lodged.

Awakened Cancer Cells Undergo a Highly Mesenchymal to Quasi-Mesenchymal Transition.

To investigate the phenotypic state of the awakened cells, we performed bulk RNA-seq of the awakened Sum159low-1 cells induced by bleomycin treatment. Principal component analysis of bulk RNA-seq data demonstrated that the transcriptome signatures of the awakened Sum159low-1 cells induced by bleomycin treatment resembled those of the quasi-mesenchymal Sum159high cells cited earlier (Fig. 2B). Pathway enrichment analysis of these data showed that the EMT pathway is significantly downregulated in these awakened cells (Fig. 2C). More specifically, as revealed by bulk RNA-seq datasets, levels of the mRNAs encoding the quasi-mesenchymal markers p63 and ITGB4 were increased in the awakened Sum159low-1 cells induced by bleomycin treatment, whereas the mRNA levels of the mesenchymal markers CDH2, FN1, and the Zeb1 EMT-inducing transcriptional factor were decreased (Fig. 2D). IF assays showed that essentially all of the awakened Sum159low-1 cells induced by bleomycin treatment displayed p63 nuclear staining and ITGB4 cell-surface staining (Fig. 2E). This could be confirmed by isolating the bleomycin-awakened Sum159low-1 cells from lungs and gauging ITGB4 expression through FACS analysis (Fig. 2F). To determine whether the observed highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition was generalizable to at least one other awakened cell line, we observed that almost all bleomycin-induced, awakened NAMEC8Rlow cells displayed p63 nuclear staining (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Additionally, ITGB4 protein level was increased in the awakened NAMEC8Rlow cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G).

To rule out the possibility that highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition is an idiosyncratic effect of bleomycin-induced awakening, we sought an alternative experimental approach to awaken disseminated dormant cancer cells and further examine epithelial–mesenchymal phenotypic changes during this process. We speculated that the recruited stromal microenvironment of an actively growing tumor in the lungs might strongly resemble that arising following bleomycin treatment. Indeed, we found that awakening could be achieved by coinjection of either Sum159low-1 or Sum159low-3 cells with cells of the MDA-MB-231 LM2 (LM2) human BrCa cell line; the latter BrCa cells represent a highly metastatic variant of the widely used MDA-MB-231 BrCa cell line (26) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Notably, these awakened Sum159low-1 and Sum159low-3 cells also underwent a transition from a highly mesenchymal to a quasi-mesenchymal state (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–F), indicating that acquisition of a quasi-mesenchymal, tumor-initiating phenotype represents a common endpoint of the awakened state, irrespective of the induction method.

Awakened Cells Reside Stably in a Quasi-Mesenchymal Tumorigenic State without ongoing Microenvironment-Derived Signals.

We sought to determine whether the ongoing influence of the microenvironment, which was responsible for initial awakening described above, was subsequently required to stably maintain residence of the previously highly mesenchymal cells in the quasi-mesenchymal bleomycin-induced state. To do so, we isolated the awakened Sum159low-1 cells from the bleomycin-induced lung metastases and propagated them for two subsequent months in vitro (Fig. 3A). The resulting established, awakened cell line was termed Sum159low-1 Bleo-LM (Fig. 3A). This awakened cell line was found to stably reside in the quasi-mesenchymal state, clearly doing so in the absence of ongoing microenvironment-derived signals. Both immunoblot and qRT-PCR analyses confirmed that the protein and mRNA levels of ITGB4 and p63 in Sum159low-1 Bleo-LM cells were strongly increased, while those of N-cadherin, fibronectin, and the EMT-inducing transcription factor Zeb1, which together served as representative mesenchymal markers, were decreased (Fig. 3 B and C). Taken together, these results confirmed that bleomycin-awakened cells entered stably into the more epithelial, quasi-mesenchymal state, doing so for an extended period of time in vitro without ongoing interactions with the lung microenvironment.

We proceeded to investigate whether the ex vivo propagated Sum159low-1 Bleo-LM cells (Fig. 3A) also exhibited the biological traits of awakened cells, specifically elevated stemness and tumor-initiating ability. Here, we drew on observations of others that the ability to form spheroids in mammosphere assay depends on the presence of self-renewing stem-like cells and therefore could be used, in principle, as a surrogate assay to predict the stemness and tumor-initiating ability in vivo (27–30). Accordingly, following 2 mo of ex vivo propagation, we introduced awakened Sum159low-1 Bleo-LM cells into mammosphere culture. Such cells were indeed found capable of forming numerous spheroids, while the control Sum159low-1 cells failed to do so (Fig. 3D). To further confirm this behavior, we reimplanted Sum159low-1 Bleo-LM cells in the mammary fat pads or the lungs of mice. We found that these quasi-mesenchymal cells indeed developed tumors rapidly when implanted in the mammary fat pads or metastatic colonies when seeded in the lungs (Fig. 3 E and F). These observations confirmed that, following awakening and extended passaging in vitro, such cells also continued to maintain tumor-initiating ability, doing so without ongoing signaling from the lung microenvironment.

Awakening of Dormancy Is Dependent on Highly Mesenchymal to Quasi-Mesenchymal Transition.

The results described above showed that the awakened cells undergo a highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition, as evidenced by upregulated levels of quasi-mesenchymal markers p63 and ITGB4 together with decreased levels of mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, fibronectin, and Zeb1. These findings provoked the question of whether this observed major phenotypic transition actually played an essential functional role in the observed awakening.

Others and our own earlier studies had demonstrated that the p63 transcription factor plays a critical role in maintaining epithelial stemness (31) and continued residence in the quasi-mesenchymal state (11, 16). Accordingly, we determined the contribution, if any, of p63 to the awakening process. Here, we found that knockout of the p63-encoding gene in the genome of the dormant Sum159low-1 cells completely prevented the awakening observed in vivo in response to bleomycin treatment (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). Additionally, we found that continuous overexpression of the Zeb1 EMT-inducing factor, which drives cells into a highly mesenchymal state, suppressed bleomycin-induced awakening (Fig. 3H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). Taken together, these observations converged on the conclusion that a highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition induced by bleomycin treatment was indeed functionally critical for awakening.

Macrophages Involved in the Stage of Lung Inflammation Are Essential for Awakening.

The sequence of lung inflammation and fibrosis following bleomycin treatment has been well documented. Thus, after bleomycin treatment, acute inflammation is switched on and lasts for ~7 d, followed by fibrogenic changes involving the deposition of extracellular matrix and resulting distortion of lung structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) (32). Consistent with this earlier report, in our own experiments, Ly6G+ neutrophils could be observed on day 1 post-bleomycin treatment but not on day 3 and thereafter (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). The number of F4/80+ macrophages increased at day 3 post-bleomycin treatment, and their abundance peaked at day 7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). α-SMA+ fibroblasts were observed at day 7 post-bleomycin treatment and were most enriched at day 14 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B).

In order to understand more precisely when during these steps the awakening of dormant disseminated cancer cells was initiated, we treated mice with bleomycin on the same day as implantation of Sum159low-1 cells or, alternatively, 7 or 14 d before Sum159low-1 carcinoma cell implantation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). We found that bleomycin treatment on the same day as dormant cell implantation yielded a far higher efficiency of awakening relative to bleomycin treatment undertaken 7 or 14 d before lung implantation. Indeed, concomitant lung implantation and bleomycin treatment was between 6- and 33-fold more efficient, as measured by bioluminescence flux analysis, in inducing awakening relative to bleomycin treatment at 7 or 14 d before implantation. This suggested that the initial, earlier phase of bleomycin-provoked lung inflammation, rather than the later, delayed response of induced lung fibrosis, played a more important role in the awakening of the dormant cancer cells.

To further confirm this notion, we performed and integrated single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) of the Sum159low-1 cells harvested from mice treated with or without bleomycin exposure at day 3 and day 7 after treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D and E). Sum159low-1 cells, harvested from mice treated with or without bleomycin, could be divided into 9 clusters as illustrated in the two-dimensional space using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). As judged by the transcriptomes of these cell populations, markers of the awakening of these cells could be clearly observed already at day 7 after bleomycin treatment, as evidenced by the appearance of Sum159 cluster 6 at this time, which includes transcriptomes encoding quasi-mesenchymal markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D–F). These multiple lines of evidence indicated that the early, prefibrosis stage (32) of the bleomycin-induced response triggered the awakening of dormant cancer cells, doing so by shifting them from the highly mesenchymal to the quasi-mesenchymal phenotypic state.

To understand the nature of the inflammatory cells in the pulmonary parenchyma that contributed to the observed awakening, we studied the scRNA-seq analyses of control, bleomycin-treated lung tissue. Here, we found that the proportions of recruited monocytes, M2 macrophages, and fibroblasts/myofibroblasts were increased in bleomycin-treated lungs, while that of alveolar macrophages was decreased (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Notably, among these cell types, M2 macrophages were only observed in the lungs of bleomycin-treated mice, being almost completely absent in the lungs of untreated ones (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). These M2 macrophages mainly expressed the M2 markers of CD204, Arg1, and CD206 (33–35) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Accordingly, we speculated that the appearance of these M2 macrophages in response to bleomycin treatment represented a major cause of awakening. Of relevance here are the recent, contrasting findings of others demonstrating that lung-resident alveolar macrophages induce dormancy of disseminated BrCa cells after they arrive in the lungs (7). In contrast, we focus here on the roles of recruited M2 macrophages induced by inflammation operating only after disseminated BrCa cells have entered into a dormant state in the lung parenchyma. Of note, we did not further pursue here the precise phenotypes of macrophages labeled here as belonging to the roughly classified M2 subtype.

We employed IF analysis in order to localize the M2 macrophages within the bleomycin-exposed lung tissue. Here, we found that F4/80+ macrophages were recruited locally to the microenvironment associated with awakened Sum159low-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), whose awakened state was indicated by their presence as cell clusters rather than individual cells. This could already be observed at day 7 and 14 after bleomycin treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Moreover, the recruited macrophages were largely if not entirely of the protumor M2-subtype, as judged by their expression of the CD204 marker (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B).

These observations were compatible with the notion that these bleomycin-induced macrophages contributed in important ways to the observed awakening. This notion was supported by further work demonstrating that depletion of macrophages resulted in strong attenuation of the awakening of dormant DTCs (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). This depletion was achieved experimentally by administration of a monoclonal antibody reactive with murine CSF-1, the factor that serves to recruit and then differentiate macrophages, which was applied together with clodronate liposomes, which serves to deplete phagocytic cells (36, 37). These observations provided direct support for the notion that macrophages played a causal role in the bleomycin-induced awakening.

M2 Macrophages Promote Highly Mesenchymal to Quasi-Mesenchymal Transition to Awaken Dormant Cancer Cells.

The above observations caused us to examine the direct effects of macrophages, if any, on the phenotypic state and stemness of Sum159low-1 cells. To do so, we treated Sum159low-1 cells in vitro with the conditioned medium (CM) produced by in vitro polarized M0, M1, or M2 macrophages. Relative to the CM of M0 or M1 macrophages, the CM of M2 macrophages was found to have the strongest effect of inducing Sum159low-1 cells to undergo a highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition as judged by their upregulating ITGB4 and downregulating N-cadherin expression levels (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the CM of M2 macrophages stimulated Sum159low-1 cells to form numerous spheroids in the mammosphere assay (Fig. 5B). We also injected Sum159low-1 cells together with either polarized M1 or M2 macrophages into the tail vein and thus the lungs of mice. We found that Sum159low-1 cells were awakened by commingling with M2 macrophages but not M1 macrophages prior to tail-vein injection (Fig. 5C). Taken together, these and earlier results provided evidence that M2-like macrophages could contribute in a critical way to inducing the highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition that promotes at least one form of bleomycin-induced awakening.

We further investigated whether the biological state of the Sum159low cells induced by commingling of M2 macrophages, as described above, was functionally equivalent to the results of exposing mice harboring dormant cells to bleomycin in vivo. To do so, we harvested macrophage-awakened cells from the lungs and propagated them in vitro for 2 mo (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Cells of the resulting awakened cell line, termed Sum159low-1 M2-LM were found to stably reside in a quasi-mesenchymal tumorigenic state in the absence of ongoing microenvironmental signals, echoing previous observations of bleomycin-awakened cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F).

Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) Ligands Contribute to the Highly Mesenchymal to Quasi-Mesenchymal Transition.

Macrophages, especially M2-like macrophages, are known to secrete ligands of the EGFR that proceed to activate EGFR displayed by nearby carcinoma cells (38–40). Confirming findings of others, our scRNA-seq data showed that M2 macrophages appearing in the bleomycin-treated lungs are a major source of the EGFR ligand AREG (Fig. 5D). Moreover, relative to macrophages harvested from control lungs, those from bleomycin-treated lungs secreted higher protein levels of EGFR ligands including AREG and transforming growth factor-alpha (TGF-α) (Fig. 5E). These observations led us to speculate that activated EGFR signaling triggered in the dormant DTCs by macrophage-derived EGFR ligands contributed to the observed bleomycin-induced awakening. Indeed, we found that the exposure to the EGFR pharmacologic inhibitor erlotinib blocked spheroid formation induced by the CM of M2 macrophages as gauged by the mammosphere assay (Fig. 5F). Pathway enrichment analysis derived from scRNA-seq data confirmed that the EGFR signaling pathway was already increased in Sum159low cells harvested from mice treated with bleomycin at day 7 timepoint (Fig. 5G). Additionally, knockout of the EGFR-encoding gene in the genome of the carcinoma cells or pharmacologic inhibition in vivo of the EGFR kinase activity by erlotinib treatment completely blocked bleomycin-induced awakening of Sum159low-1 cells (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A).

To investigate whether EGFR signaling also contributed to the postawakening expansion of metastatic colonies, we treated mice with erlotinib 14 d after bleomycin treatment. In this instance, we found that erlotinib treatment had no detectable effect on metastatic colonization efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Hence, inhibition of EGFR signaling did not affect tumor growth once dormant cells had acquired tumor-initiating ability and entered into an awakened state. Furthermore, the awakened cells propagated in vitro did not depend on EGFR signaling to sustain their tumorigenicity and awakened state, as erlotinib did not inhibit the metastatic colonization of Sum159low-1 Bleo-LM cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Taken together, these observations indicated that EGFR signaling, ostensibly activated by macrophage-derived EGFR ligands, was critical to the initial bleomycin-induced awakening but was no longer required to maintain the awakened state once the awakened state had been established by the ligand-activated EGFR.

These observations provoked us to examine whether EGFR activation, on its own, could trigger awakening of the Sum159low cells. Here, we found that Sum159low-1 cells propagated in monolayer culture and treated with recombinant EGFR ligand TGF-α in vitro, which previously had exhibited a fibroblast-like mesenchymal morphology, acquired a polygonal, cobblestone-like shape characteristic of epithelial cells (Fig. 7A). Moreover, immunoblot and qRT-PCR analyses showed that Sum159low-1 cells treated with TGF-α or forced to overexpress transforming growth factor alpha (TGFA) underwent a highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition, as evidenced by the upregulated expression level of ITGB4 and downregulated expression levels of N-cadherin, fibronectin (Fig. 7B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–D). Similar results were also achieved by administration of the alternative EGFR ligand, AREG (Fig. 7B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 E–G).

We proceeded to investigate the functional role of TGF-α in the dormant Sum159low-1 and low-3 cells lodged in the lungs. We found that the experimentally forced overexpression of TGF-α indeed awakened Sum159low-1 and low-3 cells in vivo (Fig. 7C). IF further showed that the awakened Sum159low-1 cells exhibited p63 nuclear staining and ITGB4 cell-surface staining, indicating that TGF-α induced awakened cells indeed resided in a quasi-mesenchymal state (Fig. 7D). To confirm that such awakening is achieved by the direct effect of TGF-α interacting with EGFR displayed by the dormant cancer cells rather than an unrelated effect of TGF-α, we exposed the Sum159low-1 EGFR knockout cells to the TGF-α ligand or overexpressed TGFA in these cells while being propagated in vitro. We found that knockout of EGFR completely abrogated the highly mesenchymal to quasi-mesenchymal transition induced by TGF-α ligand treatment or forced TGFA overexpression (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). Additionally, knockout of EGFR completely inhibited awakening induced by forced TGF-α overexpression in vivo (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C), indicating that such awakening depended on autocrine signaling driven by secreted TGF-α; this result, on its own, could not rule out additional effects of secreted TGF-α on the microenvironment.

To further investigate whether the awakening induced by TGF-α overexpression is as stable as that induced by bleomycin exposure or M2 macrophages, we harvested these awakened cells from lungs and propagated them in vitro for 2 mo (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). A resulting cell population, termed Sum159low-1 TGFA-LM, was found to stably reside in a quasi-mesenchymal stem-like state without being exposed to ongoing microenvironment-derived signals (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 B and C), providing further evidence that these cells were indeed functionally awakened. Such observations suggest, at the very least, that EGFR activation in vivo contributes in major ways to the awakening of previously dormant DTCs.

Discussion

Previous observations of others showed that DTCs end up in the parenchyma of the lungs and enter into dormancy (5–7, 17). The initial entrance into the dormant state, however it is achieved, is the starting point of the present work, which describes the subsequent behavior of already-dormant tumor cells in response to awakening signals. In principle, metastatic awakening might result from cell-autonomous, stochastic decisions undertaken spontaneously by disseminated carcinoma cells. Instead, as suggested by the evidence presented here, we conclude that, at least in the presently used experimental model, awakening is provoked by signals that the dormant disseminated cells receive from the surrounding tissue microenvironment.

At present, we cannot know how generalizable this lesson is. Thus, the choice of human Sum159 BrCa cells was an arbitrary one. Experimental logistics dictated that we begin our experiments with cells that resided stably in a highly mesenchymal, nonproliferating state, doing so in response to the previous findings of others (5–7) that such a state or a similar one typifies the phenotype of dormant disseminated cancer cells. More specifically, we cannot know from our own work whether the phenotypic state of the dormant ITGB4-low BrCa cells is identical to that described in the cited studies (5–7). We would note here that the work of others in this area described the entrance of cells into “mesenchymal” states without making a distinction between cells residing in multiple alternative phenotypic states that express multiple mesenchymal markers. Here, in contrast, we distinguish two distinct states, termed quasi-mesenchymal from highly mesenchymal cell states. In addition, it remains unclear whether there are multiple alternative, highly mesenchymal phenotypic states that differ from one another in their biological properties, most importantly in their respective responses to contextual signals leading to their awakening from a dormant state. In the present work, we find that induced inflammation of the pulmonary microenvironment in which dormant cells are lodged operates to efficiently awaken dormant cells; importantly, the awakening induced by bleomycin occurs in the initial inflammatory state induced by this agent rather than the delayed, profibrotic state.

We cannot know whether the behaviors observed in this experimental model can be generalized to all dormant human DTCs, to alternative mechanisms of induced awakening, or to alternative anatomical sites of dormancy and awakening. Thus, while we found similar awakening behavior of two other highly mesenchymal human BrCa cells, their responses as dormant disseminated cells may not typify those exhibited, as an example, by DTCs lodged in the tissues of human BrCa patients. In our hands, two very different mechanisms of awakening using LPS (24) and the presently used, vastly more effective treatment with bleomycin both implicate inflammation as the provoking agent of awakening. Extensive research will need to be undertaken in the future to determine how generalizable is the mechanism of inflammation-driven awakening and most importantly, whether it operates in the tissues of BrCa patients harboring DTCs.

We propose here that the observed metastatic awakening can most effectively be understood by invoking existing understanding of the EMT programs and the phenotypic states that they orchestrate. We note that the actions of EMT programs appear to be very similar in a diverse array of normal differentiated epithelial cell types and their neoplastic derivatives (42). This provides some hope that some of the conclusions drawn from the present work with BrCa cells will prove to be applicable to other types of disseminated, dormant carcinoma cells. Nevertheless, we recognize that specific mechanisms of awakening may be dictated by the differentiation programs borne by the DTCs, by their state of malignant progression, by the sites of dissemination, and by the mechanisms of tissue inflammation operating in those sites; the latter clearly operate in very different ways in different sites of dissemination.

In earlier work, we found that the EMT program interacts directly with the stem-cell programs of both normal and neoplastic mammary epithelial cells (11, 16, 30, 43). In particular, cells that exhibit elevated stemness (as gauged by their ability to seed normal differentiated tissue formation, to initiate primary tumor formation, or to seed metastatic colonies) all reside in a state that we term “quasi-mesenchymal.” In contrast, we find that cells residing in either highly epithelial or highly mesenchymal states lack such stemness. Moreover, we note that forced expression of the Zeb1 EMT-inducing transcription factor traps cells in the highly mesenchymal, non-stem-cell state and, at the same time, blocks awakening. This behavior supports the notion that partial expression of an EMT program in highly epithelial cells can drive such cells into a quasi-mesenchymal state, whereas more complete expression of an EMT program can convey such cells to the highly mesenchymal state that is incompatible with stemness and proliferation, at least in sites of dissemination. Interestingly, the quasi-mesenchymal marker p63 is also a well-known human basal cell marker (44), which may reveal a mechanism underlying long standing clinical observations that basal-like mammary tumors tend to relapse within 2 y, whereas luminal tumors can relapse at various times following clinical presentation, doing so with delays of up to 25 y (3, 45, 46).

Importantly, to date we lack evidence that this repeatedly observed shift into the quasi-mesenchymal state during awakening, as provoked by EGFR ligands, is sufficient to yield fully awakened cells on its own. Thus, it remains possible that at least one additional contextual signal released by the inflamed lung stroma, is required to fully awaken dormant Sum159 DTCs, as defined by the ability of awakened cells to gain stable residence in the quasi-mesenchymal, actively proliferating state. Moreover, a more precise categorization of the subtype of macrophages assigned here to the M2 class will be required in the future. Success in finding the full array of signals required for bona fide awakening should, in the future, allow us to examine whether comparable sets of signals are capable of evoking full-blown awakening in a diverse array of disseminated, dormant carcinoma cells.

Methods

Cell Culture and Treatment.

Sum159 cells, MDA-MB-231 LM2, HMLER-, and NAMEC-based cell lines were cultured using medium outlined in SI Appendix. All cell lines were maintained in subconfluent conditions and medium were replenished every 48 h. The in vitro treatment procedures for cancer cells with the indicated agents are described in SI Appendix.

Plasmids and Cloning.

Stable expression of td-luci or green fluorescent protein (GFP)-luci cell lines were generated using the pRRLSIN.cPPT.PGK-GFP.WPRE construct (Addgene plasmid #12252; RRID: Addgene_12252) (47). Human CRISPR/Cas9-GFP–expressing knockout plasmids were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Doxycycline-induced vectors for ZEB1 and TGFA were designed and manufactured by VectorBuilder and cotransduced with pLenti-CMV-rtta3-hygro (Addgene plasmid #26730; RRID: Addgene_26730).

Western Blotting, ELISA, and FACS Analysis.

The procedures and antibodies are described in SI Appendix.

RNA Preparation, Quantitative Real-Time PCR, and RNA-Seq.

RNA was extracted and purified with the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen). Complementary Deoxyribonucleic Acid (cDNA) was synthesized using the High-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted in a LightCycler 480 II (Roche) using the FsatStart Universal SYBR Green Master mix (Roche). The procedures and analysis methods of bulk and scRNA-seq are detailed in SI Appendix.

Animal Experiments.

Animals were maintained in compliance with the Animal Care and Use Committees at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Eight-week-old randomly assigned NRG mice were used. For experimental lung metastasis, tumor cells (5 × 105/100 μL Phosphate Buffered Saline) were injected via tail vein. For orthotopic transplantation, tumor cells (2 × 106/100 μL) were transplanted into the fourth mammary fat pads of female mice on both sides. To induce inflammation, we used bleomycin treatment and LM2 cell coinjection. Intratracheal bleomycin (1.25 mg/kg) instillation (48) was performed on day 3 after the tail vein injection of cancer cells. NRG mice were injected via tail vein after Sum159low and LM2 cells were mixed at 1:10 ratio. To investigate whether inhibition of EGFR signaling affects metastasis, we performed intraperitoneal injection of 25 mg/kg erlotinib every 2 d (49) on the day of bleomycin treatment. For macrophage depletion, Sum159low-1 cells were injected into mice. 2 d later, mice were treated with 0.5 mg anti-CSF-1 neutralizing antibody (Clone 5A1, BioXCell; RRID: AB_10950309) and 0.05 mg/g bodyweight Clodronate liposomes every 3 d (36, 37). Metastases were monitored weekly by bioluminescent reporter imaging, measured using Living Image 4.3.1 software (Caliper Life Sciences).

Mammosphere Formation Assay.

Cells were seeded at 20,000 cells/mL into 24-well plates containing 1 mL Ince BMI-T medium (United States Biological). At day 5, cells were imaged.

Macrophage Isolation, In Vitro Polarization, and Preparation of CM.

The procedures of macrophage isolation, in vitro polarization and preparation of CM are described in SI Appendix.

IF and Histological Analysis.

The procedure of IF is described in SI Appendix. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich). Antibodies are outlined in SI Appendix. Lung tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin to define tumor tissue areas in the lung. Specimens were analyzed with confocal fluorescence microscopy (Leica Tcs Sp5 II, Leica, Heidelberg, Germany).

Statistical Analysis.

Data are shown as means and SDs of the means. Statistical significance was calculated using two-tailed Student’s t test for direct comparison of two conditions and this was performed using the GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (RRID:SCR_002798). The cutoff for statistical significance was set at *P ≤ 0.05.
