Vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Addis Eyeberu, Tamirat Getachew, Getahun Tiruye, Bikila Balis, Dawit Tamiru, Habtamu Bekele, Mohommed Abdurke, Addisu Alemu, Yadeta Dessie, Kasiye Shiferaw, Adera Debela
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin A deficiency (VAD) during pregnancy is a public health challenge in low-income countries. There are inconsistent findings that can affect policy in planning appropriate intervention. This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to summarize the evidence in order to identify existing gaps and propose strategies to reduce VAD during pregnancy in Ethiopia.MethodsThis study included published and unpublished observational studies searched from different databases (PubMed, CINHAL [EBSCO], Embase, Google Scholar, Directory of Open Access Journals, Web of Sciences, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Google Search and MedNar). Statistical analysis was conducted using Stata version 14 software. Heterogeneity and publication bias were assessed. Forest plots were used to present the pooled prevalence using the random effects model.ResultsA total of 37 618 pregnant women from 15 studies were included. The overall pooled prevalence of VAD was 29% (95% confidence interval 21 to 36) with I2=99.67% and p<0.001. Socio-economic and sociodemographic factors were identified as affecting vitamin A deficiencies among pregnant women.ConclusionsNearly one-third of pregnant women in Ethiopia had VAD. Strengthening intervention modalities that aimed to increase the uptake of vitamin A–rich foods can avert VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia.Keywords: Ethiopia, meta-analysis, pregnant women, systematic review, vitamin A deficiency

Introduction

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble retinoid that has a role in a variety of body functions.1 It has a key function in maternal haemoglobin, embryonic growth and organ and system development in pregnant women.1,2 Because of the changed metabolism demand, vitamin A requirements increase during pregnancy and lactation.3 Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) during pregnancy is a worldwide public health issue, particularly in low-income countries.4,5

The existence and the amount of VAD are routinely diagnosed using clinical ocular and serum retinol assays.6,7 Night blindness, growth retardation and foetal/neonatal and maternal death have all been linked to low vitamin A levels during pregnancy.8,9 According to studies on vitamin A status, women who did not get enough vitamin A during pregnancy had more poor perinatal outcomes, including anaemia, pregnancy-induced hypertension, preterm delivery, intrauterine growth restriction, low birthweight and deformities.10–16

Inadequate vitamin A consumption is a major cause of VAD, with various underlying sociocultural and socio-economic factors.17 VAD is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a low tissue level of vitamin A that has unfavourable health effects despite the absence of clinical deficiency.18 Deficiency is deemed to be of public health significance if >20% of pregnant women have a serum retinol level of 0.70 μmol/l, if Bitot's spots are detected in >0.5% of pregnant women or if >5% of women have a history of night blindness in their most recent pregnancy.5

Night blindness affects 9.8 million pregnant women worldwide, while low serum retinol concentration (0.70 mol/l) affects an estimated 19.1 million pregnant women, corresponding to 7.8% and 15.3% of pregnant women in VAD-prone countries, respectively.5 According to regional estimates, night blindness affects a higher percentage of pregnant women in Africa (9.8%) and Southeast Asia (9.9%).5 VAD has long been recognized as a major public health concern in Ethiopia, particularly among pregnant women and young children.19–23 According to the WHO, >22% of pregnant women in Ethiopia were diagnosed with VAD between 1995 and 2005.5

Ethiopia's government is redoubling its efforts to meet the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).24 Reduced maternal mortality and improved newborn and child health are two critical health indicators in which optimum maternal nutrition plays a role. As such, Ethiopia has implemented the revised national nutritional program that aims to ensure pregnant and lactating women have access to micronutrient services and supplementation routines for multiple micronutrients.25 Furthermore, the WHO recommends that pregnant women should receive vitamin A supplementation, which should be continued for a minimum of 12 weeks until delivery in areas where there is a severe public health problem related to VAD.26 Although VAD is recognized as a public health problem in Ethiopia, current efforts have not reduced the burden of VAD.5 Identification of the pattern of VAD in low-income countries indicates the need to reduce the burden of the problem and its consequences.

Despite studies conducted on VAD among pregnant Ethiopian women, there is no cumulative evidence that illustrates the impact of VAD across the nation. Also, research on VAD among pregnant women has shown conflicting and inconclusive results.20,21,27 As a result, a comprehensive study is required that can aggregate past findings and be used to make decisions. Furthermore, a preliminary search of the literature revealed that no systematic reviews or meta-analyses demonstrating the risk of VAD among pregnant Ethiopian women have been conducted. Therefore this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to establish evidence-based nationwide data on VAD among pregnant women.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist.28 The review was registered by the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42021283936).

Eligibility criteria

We included cross-sectional, cohort and case–control studies that assessed pregnant women for VAD diagnosed clinically using night blindness or Bitot’s spots and/or plasma or serum retinol levels. Studies that were conducted in Ethiopia and measured VAD in pregnant women were eligible. Studies that measured/assessed maternal vitamin A levels among pregnant mothers and were published and accessible from 2001 to 4 October 2021 and written in English were eligible. We excluded studies that did not report pregnant vitamin A status, experimental studies, reviews, commentaries, editorials and case series/reports.

Information sources and search strategy

The articles were searched using the following databases (PubMed, Embase, CINHAL [EBSCO], POPLINE, Google Scholar, Directory of Open Access Journals, Web of Sciences, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Google Search and MedNar) using a combination of Boolean logic operators (AND, OR, NOT), Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords.

The search strategy included (‘vitamin a’ [MeSH Terms] OR ‘vitamin a’ [All Fields]) AND (‘deficiencies’ [All Fields] OR ‘deficiencies’ [All Fields] OR ‘deficiency’ [MeSH Subheading] OR ‘deficiency’ [All Fields] OR ‘deficient’ [All Fields] OR ‘deficients’ [All Fields]) AND (‘pregnant women’ [MeSH Terms] OR (‘pregnant’ [All Fields] AND ‘women’ [All Fields]) OR ‘pregnant women’ [All Fields]) AND (‘Ethiopia’ [MeSH Terms] OR ‘Ethiopia’ [All Fields] OR ‘Ethiopia s’ [All Fields]). Additionally, Vitamin AND A AND Deficiency AND Pregnant AND women AND Ethiopia was used with all fields, title/abstract. Non-electronic sources were used combined with a direct Google Search, Google Scholar and MedNar. The search was also made by combining the above search terms in Ethiopia. In addition, the investigators searched manually for grey literature and other relevant data sources such as e-mail and unpublished thesis/papers with planned dates of coverage. We followed the standards and techniques of searching articles in databases,29 then all identified keywords and index terms were checked across all databases.

Study selection

All search articles were exported to EndNote X8 citation manager (Clarivate, London, UK) and duplicate studies were excluded. Studies were then screened by a careful reading of the title and abstract. The authors (AE, TG, KS, DT and BB) screened the titles and abstracts of the identified studies by applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full-text articles available in English were further evaluated based on objectives, methods, population and key findings (prevalence/magnitude, associated factors/predictors/determinants of VAD). Any discrepancy was resolved through discussion or by asking a third reviewer when a consensus could not be reached. The exclusion criteria were duplication, title/abstract, the outcome of interest and the study population. The overall study selection process is presented using the PRISMA statement flow diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram shows the selection process of included articles for systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data extraction

After the selection of appropriate articles, the authors (AE, TG, KS, DT and BB) independently extracted the data. A pre-defined Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) format was used to extract the data from selected studies under the following headings: author and year, region, setting, study design, sample size, study subject, diagnostic tool, data collection methods, the primary outcome of interest and determinant factors. The accuracy of the data extraction was verified by comparing the results with the data extraction by the remaining investigators (AD, HB, GT and MA), who independently extracted the data in a randomly selected subset of papers (30% of the total). The quantitative data (the total sample size [N] and the frequency of the event [n]) and the effect size were extracted from the included articles and summarized using Excel 2016 for meta-analysis and synthesis.

Data item/criteria for VAD

The outcome variables of interest were maternal vitamin A status measured through clinical assessment and serum retinol level, i.e. a pregnant woman who has night blindness, Bitot’s spots or a serum retinol level >0.70 μmol/l.27,30–35 VAD can also be measured based on conjunctival impression cytology and serum levels of vitamin A,36 by serum retinol level,5,21,22,37–39 and by both clinical signs and symptoms and serum retinol level.20 Therefore this review included all studies that used either of the above definitions.

Pooled prevalence is a statistical technique for combining multiple prevalence studies.

Community setting refers to those studies conducted at the household level, while institutional setting refers to those studies conducted at a hospital.

Risk of bias and critical appraisal of studies

To minimize the risk of bias, comprehensive searches (electronic/database search and manual search) were used and also included published, unpublished, institutional and community-based studies. The cooperative work of the authors was also critical in reducing bias, setting a schedule for the selection of articles based on clear objectives and eligibility criteria, deciding the quality of the article, regularly evaluating the review process and extracting and compiling the data.

The methodological reputability and quality of the findings of the included studies were critically evaluated using the quality assessment tool for observational studies (cross-sectional, case–control and cohort studies) developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI)40 (see Supplementary File 1). The two groups of authors independently evaluated the quality of the studies. The mean score of the two groups of authors was used for a final decision. The differences in the inclusion of studies were resolved by consensus. The included studies were evaluated against each indicator of the tool and categorized as high, moderate and low quality. A high-quality score was >80%, moderate quality 60–80% and low quality <60%. Studies with a score ≥60% were included. This critical appraisal was conducted to assess the internal validity (systematic error) and external validity (generalizability) of studies and to reduce the risk of bias. Fifteen studies that passed the quality assessment were further considered for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data synthesis and statistical analysis were conducted using Stata 14 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Forest plots were used to show the prevalence of VAD in Ethiopia. The random effects model of analysis was used as a method of meta-analysis to reduce the heterogeneity of included studies. Subgroup analyses were also conducted by different study characteristics, such as study setting (community based or institution based), diagnostic tool and publication year. Moreover, meta-analysis regression was conducted to identify the sources of heterogeneity among studies.

A meta-analysis of observational studies was carried out based on the recommendations of the I2 statistic described by Higgins et al.41 (an I2 of ≥75% suggests considerable heterogeneity). The investigators checked for potential publication bias through visual inspection of a funnel plot and Egger's regression test. Publication bias was assumed for p-values <0.10. The results of the review were reported according to the PRISMA guidelines. The findings of the included studies were presented using a narrative synthesis followed by a meta-analysis chart.

Results

Description of review studies

A total of 132 articles were identified through the major medical and health electronic databases and other relevant sources. From all identified studies, 33 articles were removed due to duplication and 99 studies were reserved for further screening. Of these, 55 were excluded after being screened according to titles and abstracts. Of the 44 remaining articles, 29 were excluded because they did not present the outcome of interest and non-pregnant population. Finally, 15 studies that met the eligibility criteria were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The sample size of the included studies ranged from 159 in a study conducted in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ region (SNNP) of Ethiopia30 to 23 148 in a study conducted in all regions of the country.20 Overall, a total of 37 618 pregnant women were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. From the included studies, four were from the Amhara region,22,27,36,39 four studies were from the Sidama region,21,32,33,37 four were carried out countrywide,5,20,31,38 one was from the Oromia region,35 one was from the SNNP30 and one was from the Tigray region.34 Nearly two-thirds (9 [60%]) of the included studies were carried out in a community setting5,20,21,30–32,37–39 while the remaining six studies were carried out in an institutional setting.22,27,33–36 The majority (14 [93.3%]) of the included studies were cross-sectional,5,20–22,27,30–36,38,39 while one study was a cohort study.37 More than three-fourths (12 [80%]) of the included studies reported only the prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia,5,20,22,30–38 while 3 of the articles reported both prevalence and determinant factors of VAD in Ethiopia.21,27,39 The general characteristics of the studies selected for the meta-analysis are outlined in Table 1.

Prevalence of VAD

The overall pooled prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia was 29% (95% confidence interval [CI] 21 to 36; I2=99.67%; p<0.001) (Figure 2). The pooled prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia ranged from 3% to 73% in studies done by Demissie et al.20 and Wondwossen,35 respectively.

Pooled prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia, 2021.

Subgroup analyses

Based on subgroup analysis by the region where the studies were conducted, the lowest prevalence (14% [95% CI 4 to 25]; I2=99.81%; p<0.001) of VAD among pregnant women was observed among studies conducted in the countrywide while the highest prevalence (73% [95% CI 69 to 78]) of VAD among pregnant women was observed in a study conducted in the Oromia region (Figure 3).

Subgroup analysis of the pooled prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia based on region, 2021.

Based on subgroup analysis using the tool used to diagnose VAD, the highest prevalence (35% [95% CI 20 to 49]) of VAD among pregnant women was observed in the studies that utilized clinical signs and symptoms (Figure 4).

Subgroup analysis of the pooled prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia based on the diagnostic tool, 2021.

Based on subgroup analysis by the study setting, the highest prevalence (35% [95% CI 19 to 52]; I2= 99.7%; p<0.001) of VAD among pregnant women was observed in a study conducted at the institution level (Figure 5).

Subgroup analysis of the pooled prevalence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia based on study setting, 2021.

Publication bias

To observe publication bias, a visual inspection of the funnel plot was carried out (Figure 6). Egger's test was also performed.

Meta-regression to check heterogeneity

A meta-regression analysis was conducted since there was statistically significant heterogeneity and the I2 test statistics were <0.05. The purpose of the analysis was to identify the source of heterogeneity so that the correct interpretation of the findings is made. However, the meta-regression analysis found no significant variable that can explain the heterogeneity. There was no statistically significant study-level covariate (sample size, publication year and setting) in the included studies. Therefore the heterogeneity may be explained by other factors that these studies did not capture (Table 2).

Systematic review of factors

It was identified that age significantly influenced vitamin A status among pregnant women.21,27 In both studies, mothers 35–49 y of age were at higher risk for VAD. However, mothers 18–35 y of age were at lower risk for VAD.27

Socio-economic status, including household monthly income and having a private house, significantly influenced vitamin A status during pregnancy.27,39 Other socio-economic factors, including sharing a house with animals and having communal or private latrines, were associated with VAD.39

Education status, media exposure and no fasting significantly influenced vitamin A status among pregnant women,27 while grand multiparity was significantly associated with VAD among pregnant women.21

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to explore the evidence of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia. In this study we summarized the available data on VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia and the importance of supplementation, including current recommendations.

VAD during pregnancy remains a major public health concern in Ethiopia. In Africa, 9.8% (95% CI 8.4 to 11.1) of pregnant women face night blindness related to VAD. Similarly, 9.9% (95% CI 9.5 to 10.3) of pregnant women face night blindness related to VAD in Southeast Asia.42 We found a pooled prevalence of 29% (95% CI 21 to 36) in our current review. This might be due to women's low socio-economic status, high fertility, low dietary diversity scores, impaired health conditions and dietary intake.43–46 Vitamin A requirements are increased during pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester because of accelerated foetal development at this stage. The high prevalence of VAD shows that policymakers need to revise the existing guidelines and set new strategies to decrease the burden of VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

The WHO recommends vitamin A supplementation in areas where VAD is a severe public health problem.26 A daily intake of 10 000 IU or up to 25 000 IU/week of vitamin A through an oral liquid, oil-based preparation of retinyl palmitate or retinyl acetate was recommended as a safe level of vitamin A for pregnant women to prevent maternal or perinatal mortality, early delivery, low birthweight or congenital abnormalities.42,47,48 It should be given a minimum of 12 weeks until delivery. Other evidence also shows that vitamin A supplementation during pregnancy has many purposes, including reduction of the incidence of anaemia,49 maintenance of maternal night vision, foetal ocular health, prevention of maternal postpartum infections, development of foetal organs and maintenance of the foetal immune system.8,50–52 However, the WHO report and other publications show that vitamin A supplementation during pregnancy increases the risk of vertical transmission of human immunodeficiency virus, non-teratogenic developmental toxicity and infant mortality if a single dose of vitamin A exceeds 25 000 IU, particularly between days 15 and 60 following conception.53–55 Evidence indicates that thorough investigations are needed, especially in Ethiopia, to test the safety of vitamin A supplementation during pregnancy. Furthermore, it is important to conduct a nationwide study, stratified by region, to direct efforts towards the most critical regions, maximize the use of resources and implement supplementation intervention to control VAD among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

The findings of this study indicate that Ethiopia should adopt and implement WHO-recommended strategies for the prevention and control of VAD that include supplementation, fortification of foods and dietary diversification. Similar evidence also supports the diversification of diet as a means of preventing and controlling VAD.26

Vitamin A deficiency in the Oromia, Sidama and SNNP regions was relatively high (73% [95% CI 0.69 to 0.78], 39% [95% CI 0.37 to 0.41] and 35% [95% CI 0.28 to 0.43], respectively) compared with nationwide and Amhara and Tigray region studies. This may be due to sample size and socio-economic differences.43 According to a study done in the Tigray region, pregnant women >35 y of age were 4.5 times more likely to have night blindness related to vitamin A deficiency than those <25 y of age.34 This may be due to frequent pregnancies, as giving birth depletes the body's vitamin A store or there could be iron deficiency anaemia, which affects hepatic retinol metabolism.47,56

Similarly, pregnant women who cannot access media were more likely to have night blindness. It is recognized that media has a large role to play in raising awareness and communicating messages about good nutrition and healthy diets to the masses, but also in conducting advocacy efforts to ensure that nutrition remains high on the political agenda and that the right policies, governance structures and actions are put in place to foster accountability across stakeholders.57 In this review, socio-economic factors influenced vitamin A status during pregnancy. This was strengthened by evidence that showed VAD is prevalent in poor countries, regions and underserved families affected by inequalities in income, education, housing, access to health services and other related aspects.5 Moreover, evidence also shows that inequality of healthcare service and income affect the health of the population.58

Strengths and limitations

The investigators used extensive and comprehensive search strategies from multiple databases. Published and unpublished studies and grey literature were included. Studies were evaluated for methodological quality using a standardized tool. Although the literature search was systematic and assessed all related studies within the desired scope, it is possible that relevant publications (e.g. publications that were not in the English language) may have been missed. Studies with abstracts were the only ones included. This may affect the finding's inclusiveness.

Conclusions

VAD is a major public health problem in Ethiopia. Nearly one-third of pregnant women in Ethiopia have VAD. It is important to conduct a country-wide study to direct efforts toward the most critical regions and maximize the use of resources. An intervention that aims to increase the uptake of vitamin A–rich foods, vitamin A supplementation and food fortification that can avert vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women in Ethiopia should be part of policy designs and program implementation. Furthermore, adequate nutrition for pregnant women should be advocated, as improved nutrition during pregnancy will generate benefits beyond a reduction in vitamin A deficiency.
