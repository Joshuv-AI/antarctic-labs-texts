HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes among older men in Sichuan Province, Southwest China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhihua Ye, Yibei Song, Yuan Li, Shu Liang, Jin Peng, Jia He, Shuangru Li, Jiang Yuan, Ruixi Zhang, Bihui Yang, Xiaochun Zhang, Yi Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn China, men aged 50 years and older face elevated HIV infection risks due to ongoing sexual activity and limited prevention knowledge. HIV/AIDS-related stigma exacerbates these risks through delayed testing and reduced prevention engagement, yet stigma attitudes toward people living with HIV remain poorly understood in this population in China. This study examined the associations between HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes among older men in Sichuan.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 841 older men via multistage cluster sampling between July and August 2023. Validated questionnaires were used to assess HIV/AIDS knowledge, stigma attitudes, sociocultural factors (sexual attitudes, social support, and socioeconomic status), and the utilization of HIV/AIDS services. Multivariable logistic regression and propensity score matching (PSM) were employed to control for confounding variables and strengthen the association estimates.ResultsAwareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge was associated with lower stigma attitudes (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.84), with PSM analyses confirming stronger effects (AOR = 0.51–0.53, 95% CI 0.37–0.72). High education level (junior high school and above: AOR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.21–0.70) and liberal sexual attitudes (AOR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.37–0.71) showed stronger inverse associations with stigma attitudes than knowledge did.ConclusionsThis study identifies a low-knowledge–high-stigma pattern among older men in Sichuan, Southwest China, which may be influenced by historical fear-based health messaging and conservative sociocultural norms. Although awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge was associated with lower stigma attitudes, education level, and sexual attitudes demonstrated more potent effects. These findings underscore the need for integrated interventions that combine knowledge-based education with sociocultural approaches to mitigate stigma in this region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25165-y.Keywords: Older men, HIV, AIDS, Knowledge, Stigma, Propensity score matching, China

Background

In HIV/AIDS research, individuals aged 50 years and above are conventionally considered older adults [1]. Globally, the number of older people living with HIV (PLWH) has risen substantially in recent decades [1]. China follows a similar pattern: the proportion of PLWH aged 50 years and above grew consistently from 22% in 2011 to 48% in 2022 [2, 3]. Newly reported cases in this age group increased sharply from 11,751 in 2010 to 51,856 in 2022, far exceeding growth rates among younger populations [3], indicating that later life acquisition is a major driver of the epidemic. A meta-analysis further supports this vulnerability, showing a pooled HIV prevalence of 2.1% among older adults in China, compared with 0.05% in the general population [4]. Men constitute the majority of older PLWH in China, with commercial heterosexual contact being the primary transmission route [2]. Unlike women in this age group, men often remain sexually active beyond age 50 and report ongoing sexual needs [2]. However, they are frequently overlooked owing to social prejudice and discrimination against older sexuality [5]. Our previous study in Southwest China indicated substantial engagement in high-risk behaviors; 16.23% of rural older men reported such behaviors, and 11.07% paid for commercial sex [6]. Compounding these risks is a significant knowledge gap: only 31.41% possessed basic HIV/AIDS knowledge, and only 3.21% answered all core questions correctly [6]. Low condom use exacerbates this situation: 68.75% never used them during commercial sex, and none reported consistent use [6], leading to condomless high-risk practices [2, 6]. These behaviors not only increase personal infection risk but also may facilitate transmission to spouses, positioning older men as a potential “bridge population” between high-risk groups and the general population [7]. In response, China incorporated the global “90-90-90” targets into its national HIV/AIDS strategy in 2017 [8], explicitly prioritizing older adults [9]. Nonetheless, by 2022, 16% of PLWH remain undiagnosed [10], with older individuals disproportionately affected by late diagnosis [11].

HIV/AIDS-related stigma remains a significant obstacle to achieving global treatment and prevention targets [12]. Research consistently links stigma to delayed HIV testing, reduced engagement with prevention services, and hesitancy to disclose HIV status among high-risk populations [13–15]. HIV/AIDS-related stigma manifests through multiple mechanisms, commonly categorized as public stigma and self-stigma [16, 17]. Self-stigma includes experienced, anticipated, and internalized forms of stigma, each reflecting the perspectives of PLWH [16, 18]. In contrast, public stigma encompasses negative beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors directed toward PLWH by the general public [16, 17]. Our study focuses specifically on public stigma because evidence suggests that self-stigma frequently stems from public stigma, which is socially transmitted [16, 19]. Therefore, addressing public stigma may serve as a crucial initial step in mitigating the broader impact of HIV/AIDS-related stigma. This fear of public stigma discourages many older PLWH from seeking timely care or sharing their diagnosis [5], heightening mortality risks and increasing transmission potential [11]. Despite China’s significant strides in reducing stigma [20], public stigma against PLWH remains substantial, with a study reporting that nearly half of rural and over one-third of urban residents in certain regions still hold stigma attitudes [21]. Older men, in particular, exhibit disproportionately high levels of stigma attitudes toward PLWH [5, 22], which can be attributed to intersecting sociocultural vulnerabilities. Key contributing factors include (1) limited education and restricted access to HIV/AIDS information, which reduces the effectiveness of awareness campaigns [22]; (2) persistent adherence to traditional sexual taboos that inhibit open dialog about sexual health [5, 6]; and (3) internalized and fear rooted in late 20th-century fear-based HIV/AIDS propaganda [20]. These overlapping challenges underscore the need to critically examine how sociocultural context shapes stigma among older Chinese men, a step essential for designing targeted interventions that target gaps in current antistigma strategies.

Attitudinal assessment is a key measure for understanding stigma [23]. However, recent research analyzing public stigma attitudes toward PLWH in China remains limited [5, 17, 21, 24], with even fewer studies focusing on older men [5]. Domestic scholars often attribute stigma attitudes primarily to gaps in HIV/AIDS knowledge influenced by sociodemographic factors [5, 17, 21]. In contrast, international studies highlight that stigma attitudes persist even among populations with sufficient HIV/AIDS knowledge, with weak or nonsignificant negative associations [25–27]. This divergence stems from differing research priorities: international research emphasizes sociocultural factors, such as religion and socioeconomic position [25, 26], whereas Chinese studies focus on knowledge deficits [5, 17, 21]. Although promoting HIV/AIDS education is widely advocated in China as a strategy aimed at reducing stigma, this approach risks underestimating the profound influence of the sociocultural context, potentially undermining the effectiveness of interventions. This tension reflects a broader theoretical debate between attribution theory, which emphasizes individual cognition, and sociocultural theory, which criticizes such models for neglecting systemic societal influences on the formation of stigma [28, 29]. Early domestic research has begun addressing this gap by highlighting how traditional sociocultural norms shape stigma [30]. However, empirical evidence validating this model remains sparse [24]. Building on these sociocultural complexities and guided by frameworks that position stigma within broader cultural and structural contexts [29, 30], this study incorporates sexual attitudes, social support, and socioeconomic status as key sociocultural variables that are associated with stigma attitudes among older men.

To address these gaps, this study investigates a key question: Is awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge associated with lower stigma attitudes among older men within their specific sociocultural context in Southwest China? Using multivariable logistic regression and propensity score matching (PSM), we analyzed the associations between HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes in this population. Our findings aim to inform culturally adapted strategies to combat stigma attitudes.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study from July to August 2023 among older men in Sichuan Province, Southwest China, using onsite questionnaires to assess HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes. Sichuan Province has the highest number of PLWH in China, with some cities reporting that over 60% of PLWH are older adults [31]. In collaboration with the Sichuan Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and based on data from the National HIV/AIDS Comprehensive Response Information Management System, we selected three areas with high numbers of older PLWH, Chengdu city, Yibin city, and Liangshan prefecture, to target regions where the epidemic is most concentrated and where the need for public health intervention is greatest. Participants were recruited through primary healthcare institutions in these regions. A pilot survey in Chengdu refined the questionnaire, trained interviewers, and optimized the data collection procedures before the formal survey was systematically implemented across all institutions.

Participants

Participants were recruited via a three-stage cluster random sampling method. First, three townships, towns, or subdistricts were randomly selected separately from Chengdu’s County-level City A, Yibin’s District B, and Liangshan’s County C. Second, one village or community was randomly chosen separately from the selected townships, towns, or subdistricts. Third, one villager or resident group was randomly chosen separately from the selected village or community. The government staff assisted in inviting all eligible older men from these groups to participate in local primary healthcare institutions.

The inclusion criteria were (1) aged 50 years or older, (2) male, (3) local residency, and (4) confirmed HIV-negative status via HIV testing. The exclusion criteria included cognitive impairments, severe illness, or inability to communicate. Only participants who had heard of HIV/AIDS were included in the final analysis. After providing written informed consent, eligible participants completed a 30-minute one-on-one interview in private settings conducted by interviewers who were fluent in the local dialect. Our interviewers received standardized training to ensure accurate delivery of the questionnaire content without deviating from the original intent. All questionnaires were subsequently reviewed immediately for accuracy and completeness. All participants received 15 CNY (approximately 2 USD) compensation, regardless of whether they completed the interview.

HIV testing

HIV testing: In accordance with China’s National Guidelines for the Detection of HIV/AIDS, primary healthcare institutions have implemented parallel screening through the following methods: (1) rapid on-site testing via the Dot Immunocolloid Gold Rapid Test (InTec PRODUCTS, INC) at HIV testing sites or (2) venous blood collection for HIV antibody detection via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) at local HIV screening laboratories. Both methods constitute preliminary screening. Reactive results from either method triggered the collection of fresh venous blood samples, which were subsequently transferred to designated HIV confirmation laboratories for protein immunoblotting (Western Blot) confirmation. Participants with confirmed positive results were promptly referred to local CDC-designated HIV treatment facilities for counseling, care, and antiretroviral therapy initiation.

Variables and measurement

Scale development and refinement

The questionnaire incorporated both published and self-developed instruments. Its development involved a systematic process to ensure appropriateness for the study population. To increase the suitability for our study population, which may have low educational levels, reduce the response burden, and improve interview completion rates among older men, all scales underwent a standard process. For the self-developed scales (sexual attitudes and social support), an initial item pool was generated based on a literature review and consultations with experts in sociology, epidemiology, and public health. The process for all scales included (1) expert review for cultural relevance, clarity, and content validity; (2) pilot survey testing with 82 participants to evaluate comprehensibility, reliability, and construct validity; and (3) item analysis leading to the removal or revision of unclear items, showing low internal consistency or low factor loadings, or being culturally inappropriate. For the stigma scale, we adapted a Chinese version of Zelaya’s HIV/AIDS Stigma Scale [32, 33] through the same steps. All scales are provided in English in Supplementary Material 1.

Independent variable

HIV/AIDS knowledge was assessed via the validated HIV/AIDS Knowledge Questionnaire from the Chinese CDC [5, 6], a tool routinely used in national HIV/AIDS surveillance. It includes eight dichotomous items (“yes,” “no,” and “don’t know”). Each correct answer received one point, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 8. Consistent with China’s National HIV/AIDS Sentinel Surveillance Implementation Plan [34], participants scoring 6 or higher were categorized as “aware,” and those scoring less than 6 were categorized as “unaware.”

Dependent variable

HIV/AIDS stigma attitudes were measured via the above-mentioned refined 10-item scale. Factor analysis of the pilot survey data supported a three-dimensional structure: the original “fear of infection” and “shame and prejudice” domains merged into “fear and moral judgment” (2 items), whereas “personal support for discriminatory actions” (5 items) and “community support for discriminatory actions” (3 items) were retained. In the formal survey, factor analysis confirmed this structure with acceptable validity (KMO = 0.74; Bartlett’s test was significant; factor loadings ranged from 0.55 to 0.94; explaining 68.41% of the variance) and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.77).

The items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly agree”) to 5 (“strongly disagree”). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded. A total stigma score was calculated by averaging all the items (ranging from 1 to 5), with higher scores indicating stronger stigma attitudes. The participants were classified as “high stigma” (mean score 3 or higher) or “low stigma” (mean score less than 3). A cutoff of 3 was used, corresponding to a neutral response (“uncertain”); scores of 3 or higher indicated no rejection of stigma attitudes, accounting for potential underreporting due to social desirability bias.

Covariates

The covariates included in this study were organized into three categories. First, the demographic characteristics included eight variables: age, ethnicity, occupation, monthly income (incorporating child support), education level, household registration type, marital status, and living situation.

Second, utilization of HIV/AIDS prevention services was assessed by asking participants whether they had previously engaged in any comprehensive HIV/AIDS intervention activities (HIV voluntary counseling, testing, or condom distribution) or had participated in knowledge promotion activities. This composite measure aligns with the integrated strategy of China’s national HIV/AIDS program [35].

Third, sociocultural variables included sexual attitudes, social support, and socioeconomic status. Sexual attitudes were assessed via the above-mentioned self-developed six-item scale evaluating views on nonmarital sexual behavior. The items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating more liberal attitudes. A total score was calculated by averaging the items, and participants were categorized as having “liberal” sexual attitudes (mean score 3 or higher) or “conservative” sexual attitudes (mean score less than 3). The cutoff at the scale midpoint was selected because scores below 3 reflect explicit disagreement with liberal attitudes, thereby indicating a conservative stance. In the formal survey, the scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and acceptable construct validity (KMO = 0.77; Bartlett’s test was significant; factor loadings ranged from 0.60 to 0.92; explaining 75.66% of the variance). Social support was assessed via the above-mentioned self-developed two-item scale measuring perceived economic and emotional support from family, friends, or social networks. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating greater support. A mean score was computed, and participants were classified as having “high” social support (mean score 3 or higher) or “low” social support (mean score less than 3), where scores below 3 reflected explicit rejection of adequate support. In the formal survey, this concise scale showed good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) and acceptable construct validity (explaining 86.86% of the variance). Socioeconomic status was indicated by occupation, monthly income, and education level. The subgroup classifications for all the variables are presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Data management

After data collection, all completed questionnaires were reviewed. A database was created via EpiData 3.1 software to ensure completeness and accuracy, followed by double data entry and verification. No missing data were present in the final analytical sample.

Primary analysis

All analyses were performed in Stata 17.0 software. Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations for continuous variables; rates and proportions for categorical variables) were used to summarize participants’ basic information, HIV/AIDS knowledge, and stigma attitudes. Bivariate analyses via chi-square tests were conducted to examine the initial associations between HIV/AIDS stigma attitudes, HIV/AIDS knowledge, and all covariates. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression models were applied to identify variables associated with stigma attitudes. All prespecified variables were retained in the multivariable model regardless of their significance in preliminary tests to control for potential confounders and prevent the omission of relevant variables [36]. The results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Propensity score matching analysis

PSM was conducted via the psmatch2 package to further control for confounders and validate the logistic regression findings. This method was employed because research indicates that effect estimates from propensity scores (PS) methods are consistently closer to the actual value and exhibit lower systematic differences than those derived from logistic regression [37]. PS were estimated via a logistic regression model with HIV/AIDS knowledge (unaware vs. aware) as the outcome and all covariates as predictors. Three matching methods were applied: caliper matching (radius of 0.02), radius matching (radius of 0.02), and kernel matching (quadratic kernel, bandwidth of 0.06). The radius (maximum allowable PS difference for matching) was set to 20% of the pooled standard deviation of the PS between groups. Kernel matching calculates a weighted average of PS via a kernel function. All methods allowed replacement and restricted matched samples to the common support range of PS. No fixed matching ratio was imposed to optimize balance and minimize sample loss. However, the ratios did not exceed 1:4. The average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) was calculated to estimate differences in stigma attitudes between the “unaware” and “aware” groups. Standardized bias (S.B.) was used to evaluate covariate balance between groups, with S.B. values less than 0.1 indicating adequate balance. The common support range of the PS was analyzed to assess bias risk, where broader ranges indicated lower risk. PS distributions before and after matching were visualized via the two-way package to illustrate improvements in balance.

Sensitivity analysis

To further validate the robustness of our findings, we repeated the logistic regression analysis to assess the association between HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes, enabling direct comparison of the results before and after PSM. The ATT and AOR derived from different PSM methods were estimated via bootstrap sampling with 500 replications to ensure statistical reliability.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval NO. 2023KL-081). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Participant characteristics

Among the 1,038 initially surveyed participants, six were excluded because of incomplete interviews, resulting in a 99.42% completion rate. Three others were excluded based on HIV-positive testing results. Among the remaining 1,029 eligible participants, 841 who had heard of HIV/AIDS were included in the final analysis. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the 841 participants. The mean age was 62.38 years (SD = 8.39), with 97.03% of the participants identified as Han Chinese. Over half (52.79%) were farmers, 35.79% reported a monthly income of less than 1,000 CNY, and 12.72% were uneducated. Most participants held agricultural household registrations (87.99%), the majority were married (82.64%), and nearly half (46.49%) lived exclusively with their spouses. A majority (79.55%) had received HIV/AIDS prevention services (of which 19.74% received comprehensive HIV/AIDS intervention activities and 79.07% were exposed to knowledge promotion activities), 89.66% reported a high level of social support, and 61.36% held liberal sexual attitudes.

HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes

The mean HIV/AIDS knowledge score was 4.62 (SD = 2.19), with an awareness rate of 41.14%. The average stigma attitudes were 3.32 (SD = 0.57), and 64.80% of the respondents reported high stigma attitudes. Six participants (0.71%) exhibited an absence of stigma attitudes (scores less than or equal to 2 for all items). Among the stigma dimensions, “fear and moral judgment” scored highest (mean = 3.57, SD = 0.73), followed by “community support for discriminatory actions” (mean = 3.25, SD = 1.02) and “personal support for discriminatory actions” (mean = 2.88, SD = 0.83). Bivariate analysis

The results of bivariate analyses via chi-square tests between all variables and HIV/AIDS knowledge or stigma attitudes are presented in Table 1. HIV/AIDS knowledge was significantly associated with several demographic and sociocultural factors. Awareness was greater among younger participants (P < 0.001) and varied by occupation (P < 0.001), with workers and those in other occupations demonstrating greater awareness than farmers did. Higher monthly income (P < 0.001) and educational attainment (P < 0.001) were also positively associated with awareness. Married individuals had greater awareness than widowed participants did (P < 0.001), and those living with their spouse and family reported greater awareness than those living alone did (P < 0.01). Participants who had utilized HIV/AIDS prevention services (P < 0.001) or reported higher levels of social support (P < 0.001) also exhibited greater awareness. Interestingly, individuals with conservative sexual attitudes were more likely to be aware than those with liberal attitudes (P < 0.01).

HIV/AIDS stigma attitudes were also significantly associated with multiple factors. Farmers and workers exhibited higher stigma attitudes than those in other occupations did (P < 0.05). Higher education was correlated with lower stigma attitudes (P < 0.001), with participants who had completed junior high school or above showing the least stigma attitudes. Rural residence was associated with higher stigma attitudes than nonrural registration (P < 0.01). Those who had not used HIV/AIDS prevention services held higher stigma attitudes than those who had (P < 0.01). Additionally, participants with conservative sexual attitudes reported higher stigma attitudes than those with liberal views did (P < 0.001). HIV/AIDS knowledge was also significantly associated with stigma attitudes (P < 0.001), with awareness of knowledge corresponding to lower stigma attitudes.

Factors associated with HIV/AIDS knowledge: Older age was negatively associated with knowledge (60–69 vs. 50–59 years: AOR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.42–0.91; ≥70 vs. 50–59 years: AOR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.25–0.66). Conversely, higher education levels (junior high school and above: AOR = 3.05, 95% CI 1.58–5.92) and utilization of HIV/AIDS prevention services (AOR = 5.42, 95% CI 3.17–9.27) were significantly associated with awareness of knowledge.

Factors associated with HIV/AIDS stigma attitudes: Higher stigma attitudes were negatively associated with education levels of junior high school and above (AOR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.21–0.70), liberal sexual attitudes (AOR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.37–0.71), and awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge (AOR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.84). Notably, while utilization of HIV/AIDS prevention services was strongly associated with awareness of knowledge, it was not significantly associated with lower stigma attitudes (AOR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.63–1.48).

Propensity score matching analysis

To confirm the robustness of the negative association between HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes identified in the multivariable model, PSM was employed. All the PSM methods (caliper, radius, and kernel matching) satisfied the balance and common support requirements (see Supplementary Material 2). The ATT and AOR demonstrated consistent estimates across all methods. The PSM analysis reaffirmed that HIV/AIDS knowledge was significantly negatively associated with stigma attitudes (ATT = −0.12 to −0.13, P < 0.01), with the effect size being even stronger than the prematching estimate (AOR = 0.51–0.53, 95% CI 0.37–0.72). Discussions

Our cross-sectional study conducted among older men in Sichuan Province, Southwest China, confirmed that awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge was associated with lower stigma attitudes, even after we adjusted for sociocultural factors. PSM analyses reinforced the robustness of this association, with an effect size greater than that obtained from conventional logistic regression. However, further analysis revealed that higher education levels (junior high school education and above) and liberal sexual attitudes demonstrated substantially stronger inverse associations with stigma attitudes. Despite this, HIV/AIDS knowledge retained a modest but consistent independent association with lower stigma attitudes.

These findings bridge theoretical and cultural perspectives on HIV/AIDS-related stigma. While supporting domestic evidence linking ignorance and misconceptions to heightened stigma attitudes [5, 17, 21], these findings contrast with those of international studies that emphasize sociocultural contexts. For example, a Malaysian national survey revealed that despite high levels of HIV/AIDS transmission knowledge (scores at the 70th percentile), stigma attitudes persist, with ethnicity and socioeconomic position (education and income), rather than knowledge gaps, being the strongest correlates of stigma [25]. This finding is consistent with studies among health workers in other sociocultural contexts; for example, research with Indonesian nurses demonstrated that individual religiosity and institutional religious affiliation were significant predictors of stigma attitudes, whereas HIV/AIDS knowledge was not [26]. In Southwest China, however, we found that both knowledge gaps and sociocultural factors were independently associated with stigma attitudes, underscoring the specific landscape where traditional sexual values and historical fear-based health campaigns intersect to shape higher stigma attitudes among older men. This is evidenced by the high stigma attitudes we observed, particularly within the dimensions of “fear and moral judgment” and “community support for discriminatory actions,” a pattern that is consistent with previous studies in Southwest China [5, 22]. This pattern underscores that efforts to reduce stigma must address not only knowledge gaps but also the underlying moral judgments and discriminatory intentions that sustain stigma.

To better understand the role of key sociocultural factors, we further contextualize the associations of sexual attitudes and education level with stigma attitudes. China’s younger generations increasingly embrace liberal sexual attitudes, yet older men remain anchored to conservative values emphasizing prudence and responsibility [38]. The moral weight associated with HIV/AIDS in this context is profoundly compounded by the perception that infection is a consequence of immoral behaviors, such as drug use, commercial sex, or homosexuality [30, 39]. When HIV/AIDS is conflated with stigmatized behaviors, it creates a moral dichotomy: the disease is perceived not merely as a health threat but also as a violation of cultural norms [30]. This moralization process, whereby concerns of immorality can supersede those of infection itself [30], is reinforced by normative pressures to conform, as observed in Hong Kong, China, where conformity to norms was independently associated with HIV/AIDS-related stigma [24]. This process, compounded by limited HIV/AIDS knowledge, entrenches stigma attitudes within sociocultural attitudes toward sexuality. Education consistently mitigates stigma attitudes across cultural contexts [5, 17, 21, 24]. Higher education levels are associated with lower stigma attitudes, aligning with global studies that position education as a moderating factor against stigma attitudes fuelled by ignorance and misconceptions [5, 25]. This stability suggests that education operates not only through knowledge acquisition but also through fostering a critical appraisal of sociocultural norms.

The more modest association of HIV/AIDS knowledge, however, must be understood within its historical context of dissemination. Historically, fear-based campaigns framing HIV/AIDS as a “death sentence” exacerbated stigma attitudes through increased perceived threats [20]. According to fear-appeal theory, such messaging triggers defensive avoidance when individuals lack the knowledge to assess risks rationally [40, 41]. Early Chinese health initiatives inadvertently reinforced this cycle [20]: older men with limited HIV/AIDS knowledge interpreted catastrophic warnings as justification for hostility toward PLWH. Our data reveal that Sichuan’s older men remain trapped in this pattern, with extremely low awareness rates of HIV/AIDS knowledge, which is far below the national 90% target [35]. A meta-analysis further confirmed stagnant progress, showing that the HIV/AIDS knowledge of China’s older population rose marginally from 35.30% to 47.56% between 2015 and 2021 [42], confirming the low-knowledge-high-stigma pattern.

These findings reflect elements of both cognitive and sociocultural models of stigma attitudes. The observed association between HIV/AIDS knowledge and stigma attitudes is consistent with attribution theory, which emphasizes individual cognitive drivers [28]. Moreover, the stronger relationship of stigma attitudes with education level and sexual attitudes highlights the relevance of sociocultural frameworks, which focus on normative and structural influences [29]. Rather than treating these perspectives as opposed, we suggest an integrated approach for the Chinese context, in which HIV/AIDS stigma attitudes are linked to both individual cognition (e.g., knowledge gaps) and sociocultural systems (e.g., moral norms). This model incorporates cognitive and sociocultural dimensions, providing a theoretical foundation for anti-stigma interventions that address the pattern of low-knowledge-high-stigma observed among older men in this region of China and similar areas.

These findings collectively suggest that awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge is associated with lower stigma attitudes, yet addressing educational disparities and sociocultural norms is critical for sustainable progress. Our findings caution against overreliance on knowledge-centric interventions to combat deeply entrenched stigma attitudes. While educational programs can mitigate stigma attitudes stemming from ignorance, they may fail to address moral judgments, leading to stagnation in efforts to reduce stigma [43]. Targeted education remains vital for populations with low baseline knowledge, particularly older men with limited education and no prior engagement with HIV/AIDS prevention services, as these groups presented the lowest knowledge levels in our study. Current initiatives should prioritize tailored campaigns for these demographics to disrupt the low-knowledge-high-stigma cycle observed in this study and others in China, a priority echoed by domestic scholars [5, 21].

In China, older adults typically access HIV/AIDS education through traditional media (television, radio) and healthcare institutions via bulletin boards, brochures, and lectures [6, 22], with prevention services such as free HIV testing and counseling predominantly delivered through primary healthcare institutions. Our findings in Sichuan suggest that while educational outreach achieves broad coverage, its association with deepened comprehensive knowledge and reduced stigma remains limited. This limitation is critically underscored by our specific finding that the utilization of prevention services was strongly associated with awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge but not with lower stigma attitudes, highlighting that the current service-based approach effectively conveys factual information but fails to address the deeper sociocultural and moral dimensions of stigma. This disparity reflects systemic limitations in current educational approaches, including the use of outdated formats and a lack of tailored, age-appropriate content for older adults [22]. Three targeted measures could address these gaps by combining knowledge enhancement with sociocultural norm shifting. First, integrating antistigma narratives and scientifically accurate information into health campaigns, for example, by sharing PLWH’s real-life resilience stories alongside explanations of transmission, could foster empathy and counter moral judgments while improving factual understanding. Second, modernizing educational delivery via accessible microfilms and interactive discussions at senior centers, depicting daily scenarios to clarify transmission risks (e.g., misconceptions about casual contact), could not only improve knowledge but also engage participants in reflecting on prevailing norms, building on successful community trials that have shown high acceptability [22]. Third, embedding HIV/AIDS education and testing into routine healthcare services, such as annual health checkups, under the National Basic Public Health Service (NBPHS), could normalize discussions through proactive “opt-out” notifications. While China currently combines HIV testing with elderly health checkups, passive methods such as facility-posted announcements contrast with proactive provider-led approaches used elsewhere [44, 45]. The interviews revealed that older adults seldom refused convenient, nontargeted HIV testing, suggesting that proactive notifications could reduce stigma despite potential resistance. Collectively, these measures demonstrate a dual approach that integrates knowledge enhancement with efforts to shift sociocultural norms. By leveraging existing public service media and healthcare networks in China, such integrated interventions could more effectively disrupt the low-knowledge-high-stigma cycle in affected regions.

Implementation faces practical challenges. China’s free elderly health checkups under the NBPHS target individuals aged 65 and older, excluding sexually active men aged 50–64 years. Our results within the sampled areas of Sichuan further demonstrate that the reach of more direct, actionable services, such as HIV testing and condom distribution, lags far behind that of general knowledge promotion. Addressing these gaps requires integrating actionable HIV/AIDS prevention services into broader elderly care programs to normalize discussions and reach underserved populations.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Second, self-reported data may underrepresent stigma attitudes due to social desirability bias. Third, the regional focus on Sichuan Province, particularly the selection of high-HIV-burden areas, may limit the generalizability of our findings to regions with lower HIV prevalence or different sociocultural contexts. Fourth, sexual behaviors and identities were not directly assessed, limiting our ability to empirically validate the positioning of older men as an at-risk group within this specific sample. Fifth, we did not assess whether participants were exposed to specific antistigma content, which limits our understanding of how such messaging may be associated with stigma attitudes. Sixth, we focused specifically on public stigma and did not measure self-stigma among PLWH. Consequently, we cannot empirically examine how the public stigma held by the community might propagate and contribute to self-stigma among PLWH. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to track the evolution of stigma, expand sampling to diverse regions to validate the proposed integrated stigma model, investigate the role of antistigma content in stigma reduction, and explore the associations between public stigma and self-stigma within aging populations.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study revealed that although awareness of HIV/AIDS knowledge was associated with lower stigma attitudes among older men in Sichuan, Southwest China, deeply rooted sociocultural contexts, such as conservative sexual norms and historical public health campaigns emphasizing fear, exert a more substantial influence on sustaining stigma attitudes. Notably, higher education levels and liberal sexual attitudes were more strongly linked to lower stigma attitudes than was HIV/AIDS knowledge. These findings underscore the limitations of interventions focused solely on increasing HIV/AIDS knowledge and highlight the necessity of addressing structural barriers and moralized perceptions that frame HIV/AIDS as a cultural failure rather than a medical condition. Public health strategies in this region should incorporate sociocultural awareness by pairing targeted education with updated, stigma-sensitive communication approaches to strengthen anti-stigma efforts. Integrating HIV/AIDS services into standard elderly care programs could further normalize access and reduce stigma. However, sustainable progress requires addressing systemic factors perpetuating the interplay between low-knowledge-high-stigma, collectively reinforcing stigma among older men in this region.
