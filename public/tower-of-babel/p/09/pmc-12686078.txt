Statistical Shape and Fibre Orientation Model for Muscle Architecture Characterisation of the Medial Gastrocnemius
Authors: Salim Bin Ghouth, Thor Besier, Geoffrey Handsfield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeCerebral palsy (CP) is a heterogeneous, neurodevelopmental disorder arising from a static brain lesion and leading to progressive muscle degeneration. Quantitative understanding of 3D muscle morphology and fibre architecture effects of CP are lacking. Here, we present a novel imaging and computational method to investigate morphology and 3D fibre orientations of skeletal muscles in adolescents with CP.MethodsPrincipal component analysis was performed on data derived from conventional MRI and diffusion tensor MRI; principal components (PCs) described dominant variations in each cohort. We applied this method to the medial gastrocnemius muscles of seven young people with CP and eight typically developing controls to quantitatively assess deviations in muscle structure associated with CP.ResultsMorphology and architecture models in each cohort had similar variance of dominant features, with size being the predominant mode of variation in both cohorts. The first three PCs accounted for a cumulative variance of CP and TD cohort of 96 and 97%, and 95 and 97% for shape models and shape and fibre orientation models, respectively. Size differences were greatest in the proximal and distal regions, rather than middle, of the muscle. Models revealed localised variation in 3D fibre orientations between the two cohorts in medial-distal region of the muscle. Local variations were observed in the medial- distal regions of the mean muscle of about 10° in the TD model compared to the CP model. PCs of architecture models indicated that greater localised angular differences in the 3D fibre orientations were associated with larger-than-average muscles.ConclusionThis work offers insights into both muscle morphology and architecture of the medial gastrocnemius in individuals with CP, including variations of overall size and localised muscle fibre orientations. The quantitative descriptions of muscle architecture we present here may contribute to greater understanding of muscle function and dysfunction and may motivate imaging-informed perspectives on therapeutic interventions for CP.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a complex, heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder that originates from a static brain lesion at an early age leading to worsening musculoskeletal conditions and gait impairments [1]. Musculoskeletal conditions may not be apparent in neonates upon their birth [1]. However, considering the rapid developmental changes in the paediatric phase, the musculoskeletal conditions progress as the affected children and adolescents grow. The complexity of the musculoskeletal condition associated with the disorder compels novel methods of probing and interpreting the musculoskeletal effects of CP. Handsfield et al. (2022) provided a narrative review of the effect of CP on muscle architecture and growth at a micro- and macro-scale [2], suggesting that understanding the progression of muscular impairments in CP requires examination of muscle growth and the nature of the condition around adolescence. Investigating the muscle architectural changes in the affected individuals at this early stage of life will lend insightful knowledge on common architectural1 patterns of lower limb muscles leading to the disability and may help in the design of targeted clinical interventions in children and adolescents with CP.

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) is a Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) modality becoming widely used for in vivo assessment of muscle architecture and outperforming many limitations of 2D ultrasound imaging [3, 4]. DTI takes advantage of the anisotropic diffusion properties of water molecules along microscale fibres and has been used increasingly for investigating 3D architecture of skeletal muscles [5–7]. Using DTI tractography, muscles can be reconstructed, allowing for a robust analysis and quantification of 3D fibre orientations. Even though DTI has shown reliability in studying group-wise differences in 3D architecture such as fascicle length and pennation angle, measurements of fascicle length and pennation angle alone may not provide sufficient information to clearly identify localised differences in participants’ muscles [8]. Therefore, a quantitative analysis of 3D muscle architecture is required to explore differences between children and adolescents with disorders like CP and typically developing counterparts [8].

Architectural properties of lower limb skeletal muscles like fascicle length and pennation angle of fibre arrangements play a role in muscle function [9]. For instance, fascicle length determines maximum contraction velocity, while pennation angle is associated with fibre packing and direction of muscle force production [10]. Generally, lower limb muscles in children and adolescents with CP tend to be smaller in volume and PCSA and, consequently, weaker [11, 12]. Research investigating muscle architecture of the medial gastrocnemius muscle has revealed differences in fascicle length and 2D pennation angle between CP and TD cohorts [13–16]. However, quantitative comparisons of 3D muscle architecture are limited, leaving a compelling knowledge gap in understanding how architecture varies quantitatively in 3D space between children and adolescents with disorders and typically developing counterparts. In this work, we propose a framework for quantitative and statistical description of the 3D architecture of the medial gastrocnemius muscle, an essential muscle for ambulation and often implicated in children and adolescents with CP [11].

To explore the variation in muscle architecture and morphology, statistical shape modelling was used. Shape modelling extracts dominant features, known as principal components (PCs), of anatomical geometries by reducing the high-dimensional shape descriptors to a small number of parameters [17]. Shape models have been used to investigate the variation of anatomical structures in bones and muscles [18–26]. Herein, the conventional shape modelling framework was extended to combine DTI with anatomical MRI for capturing the morphology (size and shape) and architecture (fibre arrangement) of the medial gastrocnemius muscle in a cohort of children and adolescents with CP and a typically developing cohort. To our knowledge, the combined shape and fibre SSM approach presented here has not been used previously, and offers new avenues to explore distinct features in skeletal muscle architecture between two populations of interest.

The objectives of this study were to (1) construct a shape model of morphology and architecture describing the 3D fibre orientations of the medial gastrocnemius muscle in children and adolescents with CP and typically developing children, and (2) determine the variability of the medial gastrocnemius muscle within each cohort and any distinguishable dominant features between the two cohorts. We hypothesised that children and adolescents with CP present different variations of size and pennation angle in 3D muscle architecture compared to typically developing children and adolescents.

Materials and Methods

Participant Characteristics

DTI and MR images of the lower limbs of seven (n = 7) children and adolescents with CP, and eight (n = 8) typically developing (TD) children and adolescents were obtained from a previous study [12]. This study was approved by the University of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee in which parents/gradians were consented and participants were assented. Note that from the original study [12], DTI data of two participants with CP and TD participants were excluded due to DTI quality issues. Children and adolescents with CP were ambulatory, falling within the range of Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) levels I to III (GMFCS I = 2, GMFCS II = 4, GMFCS III = 1). Six (out of seven) children and adolescents with CP presented with spasticity. Image Acquisition and Processing

MRI data sets were collected with the following parameters: sequence: high-resolution 3D T1 VIBE Dixon, TE/TR: 5.22/10.4 (ms/ms), FOV: 128 × 228 × 384 (mm × mm × mm), spatial resolution: 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 (mm × mm × mm). In addition, two stacks of DTI data sets were collected after the MRI with the following parameters: sequence: echo planar, TE/TR: 74.0/4400 (ms/ms), b-value: 500 (mm/s2), FOV: 200 × 200 × 5 (mm × mm × mm), spatial resolution: 1.64 × 1.64 (mm × mm), and with slice thickness of 5 mm. Participants underwent scanning in a feet-first supine position, their knees flexed at 10° and their ankles in a neutral position. To maintain a neutral ankle orientation, a foam block and a pneumatic bean bag were used. The MRI and DTI data sets were used to construct statistical models of the morphology and architecture of the medial gastrocnemius muscle.

The medial gastrocnemius muscles were manually segmented from MRI data sets by research investigators using ITK-Snap [27]. Segmentations of the medial gastrocnemius muscle were truncated at the medial meniscus as an anatomical landmark to create a consistent proximal anatomical landmark across all datasets. After truncation, 90 ± 5% of the muscle volume remained for both cohorts. The segmentations of the MR images were imported to MeshLab and surface meshes with an edge length of 2 mm were exported as stereolithography (STL) files [28]. All segmentations were processed and inspected for consistency. For this study, the most affected limbs of participants with diplegic CP, and muscles from the paretic limbs of participants with hemiplegic CP were selected. Left-leg segmentations were reflected against the sagittal plane so all 3D surface meshes were of the right limb muscles and reflected left limb muscles. DTI data sets were denoised in the PCA domain, and Gibbs ringing artefacts were removed using MRTrix [29]. MRI data sets were registered to the DTI data sets and the registration matrices were applied on the binary masks of the medial gastrocnemius muscles using FSL software [30]. A deterministic tractography algorithm was used to reconstruct 3D fibre orientations of the medial gastrocnemius muscle based on the eigenvectors and eigenvalues estimated from the diffusion tensors in MRTrix [31]. The following tractography parameters were used in this study: minimum length: 5 (mm), maximum length 200 (mm), angular threshold 40 (degrees), fractional anisotropy (FA): 0.1 < FA < 0.5 (mean 0.31), mean diffusivity (MD): 1 × 10-3 < MD < 2 × 10-3 (mm2/s) (mean 1.51 × 10-3 mm2/s). The generated streamlines were inspected visually to verify they were consistent with muscle anatomy from literature.

Generation and Fitting of 3D Volumetric Muscles

Surface meshes were fitted using radial basis functions (Gias2 python library, [21]), where correspondence was established between vertices in every mesh against the reference mesh, and where the reference for the first iteration was the population-average-size muscle. A mean shape was then generated for the fitted meshes at this iteration, and this fitting process was repeated iteratively for optimization; thus, a mean shape was generated at each iteration and used as a reference for the following iteration until a minimal fitting error was obtained by calculating the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of Euclidean distance. The desired fitting error is within the resolution of the image datasets. Then, a volumetric mesh form the generated mean surface mesh was generated. The mean volumetric mesh was used as a reference to generate fitted and aligned volumetric meshes of each cohort using a host-mesh fitting method with a Sobolev smoothing constraint, and the host-mesh fitting errors were reported [32].

Streamlines obtained from DTI tractography were used to generate fibre tracts using MRTrix [29]. Using home-built code written in Matlab (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), we computed vector fields to represent the 3D fibre orientations of the medial gastrocnemius muscle of each participant in each cohort. Points along each fibre tract were taken, and the vector between each point and the previous point along the tract was computed through subtraction and normalization, yielding a normalized vector, which was set to the midpoint location between the two original points. Anti-parallel vectors in the computed vector fields were flipped along the proximal-distal axis. Using home-built Python code, the generated vector fields were then fitted to the generated volumetric meshes of the medial gastrocnemius muscles using a nearest-neighbour algorithm [33]. The vectors within each mesh element were averaged, and an average normalized vector was assigned to the centroid of each element of the muscle volumetric mesh. The resulting vector fields comprised 52,389 vectors in each muscle.

Generation of the Models

Variations of high-dimensional muscle morphology and architecture in a cohort of children and adolescents with CP and typically developing children cohort were developed using the statistical shape modelling framework developed by Zhang et al. [21]. Each muscle in the data set was represented by a correspondent volumetric mesh and a correspondent 3D vector field across the two cohorts. For instance, each centroid of an element in the volumetric mesh is described by x, y, and z coordinates representing its location, and u, v, and w representing the orientation of the average vector assigned to the element centroid.

The fitted surface meshes were aligned using rigid registration based on their centre of mass to remove translation and rotation misalignment. Then, a principal component analysis (PCA) was applied independently to (1) the surface meshes to investigate the morphology variations (hereafter, also referred to as a model of morphology), and (2) the correspondent vector fields to investigate the 3D fibre orientations (hereafter, referred to as a model of architecture). Each statistical model of morphology and architecture is described by a mean model and a covariance matrix representing each muscle in the data set. The eigen analysis of the PCA yielded a combination of a mean shape, eigenvectors, and corresponding eigenvalues, that can approximate each morphology and 3D fibre orientations in the data set. Three PCs were sufficient to describe at least 95% of the cumulative variance of the statistical models of morphology and architecture. Thus, each muscle is represented by a numerical value – weight - along each PC. To assess the fidelity of the shape model, the generated weights were used to reconstruct muscle structure and architecture, and the reconstructed muscles were compared against the actual muscle in terms of the location and orientation of each vector in each participant’s dataset. Morphology models were generated as a baseline to compare their variance and captured features to the one acquired using architecture models. Morphology models have previously been used to study shape variations of skeletal muscles [26]. Additionally, morphology models were used as a comparison reference for predicting muscles using Partial Least Square Regression (PLSR) and muscle architecture models. Validation and Quantification of the Models

Statistical models of morphology and architecture were validated using Partial Least Square Regression (PLSR). PLSR was selected based on an analysis test of the actual muscles in comparison with the predicted muscles. PLSR minimises the least-squares distance between actual and predicted muscles based on some anthropometric and qualitative measures like sex, age, height, mass, muscle surface area, and muscle volume. Additionally, the required number of components to use for the PLSR were analysed and selected based on R-scores.

The angular deviations between each correspondent element of an actual muscle model and its prediction were calculated. A statistical test was used to assess the difference in angular deviations between each actual muscle and its prediction. Additionally, median pennation angles of actual and predicted muscle models were computed and the statistical difference was assessed as well. To calculate pennation angles, a line of action of the medial gastrocnemius muscle was defined as it passes through the minimum and maximum coordinate values along the proximal-distal axis. The minimum angle between each vector in a volumetric mesh element and the line of action of the muscle of interest was determined and the median angle of an entire muscle was considered as a muscle pennation angle (hereafter referred to as pennation angle).

The outputs of the statistical models of morphology and architecture were mean ± 2 standard deviations along each PC of the model of surface mesh and 3D fibre orientations. The PCs of the 3D fibre orientation model were visually compared using colour-coded maps of the mean and ± 2 standard deviations representing the angular deviations between the two cohorts. The angular deviations were calculated as the minimum angle between any two vectors using the cosine similarity method.

Results

The statistical models of the medial gastrocnemius morphology (i.e., surface mesh) produced a compact model in which three PCs accounted for a cumulative variance of 95% and 97% for the CP and TD cohorts, respectively. Likewise, the statistical models of architecture produced a compact model of the 3D fibre orientations in which three PCs accounted for a cumulative variance of 96% and 97% for the CP and TD cohorts, respectively (See Fig. 2). The fidelity assessment yielded an average distance difference – fibre location - of 2.9 × 10-6 ± 3.6 × 10-9 mm and 2.9 × 10-6 ± 4.2 × 10-9 mm, for CP and TD, respectively. Discussion

In this study, a framework for statistical modelling of morphology and architecture was presented to quantify differences in the medial gastrocnemius muscle between a cohort of children and adolescents with CP and typically developing children and adolescents. The iterative non-rigid registration of the surface meshes of the two cohorts yielded an RMS error of 0.4 mm, indicating an accurate representation of the muscle in the two cohorts. The non-rigid registration step is important for accurate registration of the volumetric meshes of the muscles. The framework generated correspondent 3D volumetric meshes of the medial gastrocnemius muscles, in which each element in the mesh is correspondent to an element in other meshes with a host-mesh error of 0.8 mm (RMS). When accurate correspondence is established, the framework can extract the dominant characteristics between the two cohorts using shape modelling methods.

The two cohorts, CP and TD, were quantitatively described by three principal components that captured the dominant characteristics, with a cumulative variance of between 95% and 97% for the statistical models of morphology and architecture. The cumulative variance implies high compactness of the generated statistical models of morphology and architecture. The size was included in the models for two fundamental reasons. The first reason is that size variations are dominant in both cohorts because the age range of the participants falls within the period of accelerated growth. The second reason is that 3D fibre orientations are likely related to the muscles’ morphology (shape and size) and were thus of interest. As such, the 3D coordinates (i.e., locations) of the vectors representing the 3D fibre orientations were included in the models. The elimination of either size variations or vectors’ three spatial coordinates resulted in low compactness of the generated models.

The models of morphology indicated the dominance of overall size variations within the two cohorts. Additionally, the mean of the CP cohort was smaller in the proximal and distal regions of the muscle (16 mm) with less difference in the posterior and anterior regions of the muscle (between 0 and 8 mm). However, there was a moderate difference in the variance of size variations between CP and TD cohorts. This might be indicative of a slower growth rate in the CP cohort compared to the TD cohort, where the size difference between the oldest and youngest participants of the TD cohort is greater compared to the CP cohort [2]. The dominant variance of size variation in both cohorts is consistent with the literature on the statistical shape models of lower limb muscles and bones in pediatric populations [25, 26]. The second and third PCs showed a comparable variation in the aspect-ratio of the muscle with a difference in the thickness increase pattern towards either the posterior or the anterior direction of the muscle. The variance of the second PC in the CP cohort is greater compared to the TD cohort indicating that muscle width and thickness changes are more dominant in the CP cohort. The third PC in both cohorts were similar.

Conversely, the architecture models demonstrated a minor difference in the pennation angle between the mean muscle of the two cohorts. The localised variations in certain medial regions of the muscle were around 10°. This is different from the findings by Bolsterlee et al. in which 4.1° differences in pennation angle and local variations of the 3D fibre orientations at the central and proximal regions were observed in the "group-averaged" medial gastrocnemius muscle of the more- affected side compared to a "group-average" of the less-affected side of children and adolescents with CP [8]. A difference of 4.1° is below the sensitivity of DTI; thus, it is unclear if this reflects a real difference between limbs or simply measurement uncertainty. It is worth noting that the study by Bolsterlee et al. compared the more-affected side to the less-affected side in young people with CP, while our study compared the more-affected side to TD controls. It is reasonable to expect greater pennation angle differences in the latter case. Our architecture models showed that the variations of the 3D fibre orientations are strongly coupled with morphology variations. With size variations, local variations of 3D fibre orientations were observed at specific regions of the muscle. The variations of 3D fibre orientations associated with the second and third PCs were identified in the medial and distal regions of the muscle. However, the magnitude of angular difference is greater with the second PC compared to the third PC. This may have functional implications on force generation in muscle, but the literature related to this is scarce.

The cross-validation demonstrated the prediction capabilities of the architecture models. The RMS of the difference between the distances of the predicted and actual centroids were noticeable. However, this is not surprising as it is challenging to predict the coordinates of the centroids inside the muscle using anthropometry and muscle volume data. The difference in pennation angles of the vectors assigned to these centroids between the predicted and actual muscles was higher than the sensitivity of the DTI, in which 90% of repeated DTI scans are within 10°difference when tractography is performed [34]. The reason could be the smaller sample size in this study. The inclusion of muscle volume as a predictor showed an improvement in the prediction accuracy, which indicates a relationship between muscle size (i.e., morphology) and 3D fibre orientations.

The results of this study supported our hypothesis that children and adolescents with CP demonstrated differences in their variations of muscle morphology and architecture compared to TD counterparts. This was demonstrated by both quantitative and qualitative results. For morphology, the TD cohort showed greater variance captured (89.8%, versus 82.1% for CP) by the first PC of morphology, which represents overall size. This implies that non-size-based variations of morphology have greater dominance in the CP cohort. For muscle architecture, we note that the variance captured by PCs was similar between cohorts. However, the qualitative local fibre directions differed between CP and TD cohorts, as evidenced by colormaps of vector field PCs from the CP cohort mapped onto the TD cohort. We conclude from these that the variations of morphology and architecture differed between the two cohorts, consistent with our hypothesis. Prior work noted that variance of muscle size was greater in a CP cohort compared to TD, irrespective of body size differences [11]. There are several limitations to be considered here, with the most obvious being the small sample size. In this work, MRI and DTI data sets of a cohort of seven children and adolescents with CP and eight typically developing children and adolescents were obtained. This limited number of data sets allowed for the development of the proposed framework and capturing characteristics of 3D fibre orientations; however, a greater sample size will provide a robust characterisation of different muscle morphology and architecture. Moreover, a challenging aspect of DTI stems from the fact that data of the diffusion tensor may be inaccurate in certain voxels due to several DTI artefacts. However, to reduce the DTI artefacts, DTI data sets were filtered and a deterministic tractography algorithm was used where several parameters can be specified like threshold for angular deviation and minimum/maximum acceptable fibre length. Furthermore, variation of ankle orientation introduces variations in the 3D fibre orientations. Nonetheless, participants in this study maintained a neutral ankle orientation using a foam block and a pneumatic bean bag throughout the scanning session to minimise differences in 3D fibre orientations due to different ankle positions. It has been assumed in this study that the morphology and architecture of the left legs are a reflection of the right legs. This assumption was necessary so as to correct for right-left differences on which the paretic or most affected limb of the CP cohorts was selected.

In summary, we have presented a framework to investigate cohort-based differences in morphology and 3D fibre orientations of the medial gastrocnemius muscle. The framework established a surface and volumetric correspondence across and between the two cohorts – one typical and one with a neuromusculoskeletal condition. The generated models extracted the dominant features in morphology and 3D fibre orientations between CP and TD cohorts of children and adolescents. The size and aspect-ratio of the muscle morphology were the dominant features accounting for 95% and 97% of variation. This is consistent with literature where the major difference between CP and TD cohorts is that muscles in CP cohorts are usually smaller in size [11, 12, 26, 35]. The pennation angle of the mean muscle of the two cohorts, CP and TD, differed by 2° with localised differences in certain regions of the muscle. This study introduced a novel approach to describe variation of 3D fibre orientations and investigate distinctions between a healthy and pathological cohort. 3D fibre orientations derived from Diffusion Tensor MRI represent muscle architecture, which is closely linked to the function and force generation properties of the skeletal muscle [10]. Quantitative assessments of muscle architecture differences, as have been demonstrated here, have the potential to highlight impairments in pathological muscles with a high degree of specificity. This may motivate new research, development of higher fidelity models of pathological muscle, and suggest clinical interventions that may target patient-specific impairments to muscle architecture. Extracted shape and fibre orientation models via principal components may have the potential to predict muscle force or classify participants into cohorts using machine learning methods. Future studies may take the approach presented here a step further by investigating the mechanical consequences of muscle shape and fibre orientation features on muscle health in cerebral palsy and other pathologies.
