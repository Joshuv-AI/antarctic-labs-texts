Artificial intelligence detection of endoscopic moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis: a novel tool to enhance clinical trial recruitment
Authors: Laurie B. Grossberg, Grace Geeganage, Aditya Mithal, Tina Deyhim, Andy Santisteban-Silva, Konstantinos Papamichael, Sami Elamin, Adam S. Cheifetz, Tyler M. Berzin, Loren G. Rabinowitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsArtificial intelligence (AI) may detect endoscopically active ulcerative colitis (UC) and streamline clinical trial enrollment. Here, we investigate the performance of a commercially available AI model, autoinflammatory bowel disease (AutoIBD)-UC (Virgo Surgical Video Solutions, Inc, San Francisco, Calif, USA), to detect moderate-to-severe UC in colonoscopy videos collected as part of routine clinical practice.MethodsAutoIBD-UC was applied to consecutive endoscopy videos between August 30, 2024, and October 17, 2024. Each video received an AutoIBD score (0-1); videos with scores above a predefined confidence threshold were flagged as possible moderate-to-severe UC. Flagged videos and medical records were reviewed to confirm UC diagnosis and grade Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES). Concurrently, research staff retrospectively reviewed all endoscopy reports to identify 3 cohorts: (1) 20 MES ≥2, (2) 20 non-UC inflammation, and (3) 20 screening colonoscopies. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated for all videos screened by AI. Median AutoIBD scores were compared by MES and disease extent.ResultsAutoIBD-UC was applied to 2273 endoscopy videos. Twenty-one videos were flagged, 10 of which had MES ≥2. AutoIBD-UC flagged 9 of 20 videos in cohort 1 and all MES 3 videos. More procedures with extensive (4 of 8) or left-sided disease (5 of 7) were flagged compared with those with proctitis (0 of 5). Median AutoIBD-UC scores differed between MES 2 (0.437; interquartile range [IQR], 0.270-0.571) and MES 3 (0.690; IQR, 0.607-0.765; P = .006), and by disease extent: proctitis (0.224; IQR, 0.193-0.270), left-sided (0.571; IQR, 0.554-0.603), and extensive (0.506; IQR, 0.408-0.598; P = .021). The sensitivity of AutoIBD-UC was 0.47, and the specificity was 0.99.ConclusionsAutoIBD-UC accurately detects moderate-to-severe UC with moderate sensitivity and high specificity. Larger studies are necessary to evaluate AutoIBD-UC's utility as a recruitment aid compared to standard practice.

Background

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD), are chronic, relapsing conditions that affect up to 2.4 million Americans and pose a growing burden on health care systems worldwide.1 Because of their complex and only partially understood etiology and pathogenesis, the management of UC and CD remains challenging.2,3 Currently, there is no cure for IBD, and more than half of patients do not achieve steroid-free remission, even with advanced therapies.4 Patients with UC face high rates of health care utilization, with approximately 65,000 hospitalizations annually, and about 10% of patients ultimately require colectomy.5 These realities underscore the urgent need for novel, safe, and effective medical treatments. Although significant advances have been made in IBD therapeutics, progress is limited by the difficulty of clinical trial recruitment.6,7 The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in endoscopy units to detect endoscopically active UC presents a novel approach to potentially streamline trial enrollment and expedite the approval of new treatments for patients with IBD.

Endoscopy serves as a cornerstone in diagnosing and monitoring IBD and is routinely used as an objective measure in IBD clinical trials.8 The Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES) is a commonly applied method to assess UC severity and response to treatment in both clinical practice and research.9,10 In order to mitigate interobserver variability, a video-recorded colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy reviewed by a central reader is a common prerequisite for patient enrollment in new drug trials.11 Perhaps not surprisingly, IBD trial recruitment remains low, with sites enrolling only 0.08 to 0.12 patients annually because of stringent criteria and inconsistent recruitment strategies. This inefficiency delays trial progress and impedes the development of urgently needed therapies.7,12

In response to recruitment challenges, AI and machine learning models are being explored as tools to support clinical trial enrollment across various specialties, including endocrinology, neurology, cardiology, and gastrointestinal oncology.13,14 In IBD, machine learning holds potential for accurate, automated endoscopic scoring, which could streamline trial recruitment.15 However, the potential for AI to identify eligible IBD trial candidates can be fully realized only after rigorous clinical research of these new technological advances. AutoIBD-UC is a proprietary computer vision–based AI tool developed by Virgo Surgical Video Solutions, Inc (San Francisco, Calif, USA) to automatically identify, classify, and longitudinally track cases of UC from colonoscopy video data. The algorithm is accompanied by a website designed for streamlined review of flagged videos by research staff.16 The model has yet to be tested outside validated test sets. Here, we investigate the performance of AutoIBD-UC in detecting moderate-to-severe UC and its correlation with AutoIBD-UC scores to MES in real-world clinical practice.

Methods

Virgo devices were installed in 16 endoscopy suites at a tertiary care academic medical center. In this prospective pilot study, AutoIBD-UC was applied to consecutive videos recorded as standard of care for patients undergoing endoscopy between August 30, 2024, and October 17, 2024. Each video was given an AutoIBD score (range, 0-1), and videos with scores above a predefined confidence threshold of 0.54 were automatically flagged as possible moderate-to-severe UC, defined as MES ≥2. Flagged videos and the corresponding medical records were then reviewed to confirm the presence or absence of a UC diagnosis and to grade disease activity using MES.

At the same time, research staff reviewed all endoscopy schedules to identify 60 procedures across 3 cohorts: cohort 1 consisted of 20 procedures with confirmed UC and MES ≥2, cohort 2 had 20 non-UC inflammation, and cohort 3 had 20 screening colonoscopies. Procedures were excluded from review if they were not colonoscopies or flexible sigmoidoscopies, if bowel preparation was inadequate, or if AutoIBD-UC was not applied to the procedure for technical reasons.

Sensitivity, specificity, and precision were calculated based on all videos screened by AutoIBD. Median AutoIBD scores were compared between MES and among disease extent using the Mann-Whitney U and the Kruskal-Wallis tests, respectively, for the videos in cohort 1.

Performance of AutoIBD-UC

AutoIBD-UC was applied to 2273 endoscopic videos between August 30, 2024, and October 17, 2024. A total of 21 procedures were flagged by the AI software, 10 of which had moderate-to-severe UC (sensitivity of 0.476, specificity of 0.99). All 10 true positives were identified by research staff using traditional prescreening practices, but one of these procedures was excluded from cohort 1 because of poor bowel preparation. The remaining 11 flagged videos included 3 colonic CD, 1 IBD unspecified, 2 radiation proctitis, 2 colon malignancies, and 1 ICI. Two of the videos flagged were not analyzed because 1 was an upper endoscopy and 1 was a pouchoscopy (Fig. 1). Precision was calculated to be 0.47 based on the 10 true positives and 11 false positives. When AutoIBD-UC was applied to the 60 procedures identified with traditional screening methods, it accurately detected 9 of the 20 patients (45%) with active UC in cohort 1 and none of the videos from cohort 3. The AI incorrectly flagged 4 of 20 (20%) non-UC inflammation (2 colonic CD, 1 ICI, and 1 colon malignancy). All patients with MES 3 and 27% with MES 2 were flagged by AI. More procedures with extensive (4 of 8) or left-sided disease (5 of 7) were identified compared with proctitis (0 of 5) (Table 1).

Association of the AutoIBD score with MES and disease extent

There was a statistically significant difference in median Virgo AutoIBD-UC scores between patients with MES of 2 (0.437; interquartile range [IQR], 0.270-0.571) versus MES 3 (0.690; IQR, 0.607-0.765; P = .006). Median AutoIBD-UC scores differed by disease extent: proctitis (0.224; IQR, 0.193-0.270), left-sided (0.571; IQR, 0.554-0.603), and extensive (0.506; IQR, 0.408-0.598; P = .021). In a subgroup analysis excluding patients with proctitis, the sensitivity for detection of MES ≥2 increased to 0.625, and the specificity remained 0.99.

Discussion

There is no cure for UC, and there remains an urgent need for better treatment options. Recruitment challenges for clinical trials continue to impede the development of new therapies.17 Centralized endoscopic reading aims to minimize variability in clinical trials; however, agreement between expert reviewers is imperfect, leading to the need for additional reads and algorithms for adjudication.18 Incorporating AI technology into endoscopy units as a means to streamline recruitment for clinical trials is a novel strategy that may expedite the availability of new treatments for patients with IBD by efficiently identifying eligible patients in a consistent and objective manner.19, 20, 21

In this prospective pilot study of over 2000 endoscopic procedures, we assessed the utility of a novel AI software to automatically identify patients with moderate-to-severe UC undergoing colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy. We found that AutoIBD-UC accurately detected 45% of the videos with MES ≥2 that were identified using traditional prescreening practices. AutoIBD-UC's performance increased with the extent of disease beyond the rectum and with a higher MES. Patients with the most severe disease (MES 3), however, were all accurately flagged by AI software. The sensitivity (0.47) of AutoIBD-UC limits its utility as a recruitment aid in settings where missed eligible patients are critical. Exploring alternative confidence thresholds lower than the predefined 0.54 may increase detection of MES 2 or 3 disease without compromising specificity, but this would need to be explored with Virgo in future studies. Of note, the sensitivity of the software increased from 0.47 to 0.625 in a subgroup analysis excluding proctitis. Many UC clinical trials exclude patients with disease limited to the rectum, so decreased detection of limited proctitis may be less consequential for trial recruitment. However, the inability of AutoIBD-UC to report the extent of disease in its current version is a limitation of the software that could be further investigated.

Although AI has not yet been incorporated into the standard-of-care practice to assess endoscopic disease severity in UC, it holds significant potential to increase trial efficiency, reduce study burden on gastroenterologists and research coordinators, and save costs, ultimately facilitating larger, more robust trials.22 To date, several AI models have been developed to score endoscopic disease severity in patients with UC. A recent meta-analysis including 12 studies showed an overall high sensitivity and specificity for AI endoscopic assessment of still images (sensitivity 91% and specificity 89%) and videos (sensitivity 86% and specificity 91%).23 In addition, the use of AI algorithms in clinical trials has been shown to perform well compared with human readers11; however, the role of AI as a tool for trial recruitment has not been well studied.

Although AI has shown promise in assessing endoscopic disease severity in patients with UC in this study and others, several limitations must be considered.11,23 One major concern is the potential for bias and variability in AI models, which can arise from training data sets that may not adequately represent diverse patient populations, leading to inaccurate assessments.24 In addition, AI-driven algorithms may struggle with ambiguous or borderline cases where human expertise remains crucial for nuanced interpretation,11 as our pilot data reflect. Another challenge is the dependence on high-quality endoscopic images; suboptimal lighting, bowel preparation, or motion artifacts can compromise AI accuracy and reproducibility.24 Furthermore, regulatory and ethical considerations, including data privacy and the need for robust validation across multiple clinical settings, pose significant barriers to widespread implementation for now.25 These limitations highlight the necessity of integrating AI with expert clinical judgment to optimize patient recruitment in UC clinical trials.

This single-center pilot project assesses 1 AI technology and is limited by the low number of true-positive UC cases, which limits generalizability. In addition, a few false-positive videos were identified, suggesting that the model is imperfect in identifying UC compared with other inflammatory conditions, confirming the ongoing need for manual review. Furthermore, reliance on MES as the reference standard is a limitation, given its known interobserver variability. Lastly, exclusion of procedures attributable to inadequate bowel preparation or technical nonapplication of the AI system may introduce selection bias. Larger prospective studies with incorporation of histologic activity or centralized endoscopic review are needed to assess the utility of AutoIBD-UC as a recruitment aid compared with standard recruiting practices. Incorporating AI to automatically analyze endoscopic images and assign a disease severity score, such as MES, can streamline patient evaluation, reducing the need for time-consuming manual assessments by clinician reviewers and decreasing the effort and support needed for prescreening by research staff. These benefits may outweigh the potential small number of missed cases by AI software.

In conclusion, AutoIBD-UC AI software accurately detects moderate-to-severe UC with high specificity. Further investigation with larger sample sizes is necessary to fully evaluate the software's accuracy, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness as a trial recruitment aid compared with standard practice. The use of AI models has the potential to increase efficiency in clinical trial recruitment, reduce bias, and expedite approval of groundbreaking treatments for patients with UC.
