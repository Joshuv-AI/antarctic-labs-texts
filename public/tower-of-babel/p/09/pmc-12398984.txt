A nomogram for individualized prediction of acute respiratory distress syndrome in patients with severe traumatic brain injury: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zixuan Wang, Yan Xiao, Min Zhu, Siyao Xu, Yuan Zhong, Xiaohong Liu, Jinqiang Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognosis of patients with a concomitance of severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is poor, and early identification of such patients can provide diagnostic and therapeutic assistance for clinical treatment. However, few studies have been conducted to identify the risk of ARDS in patients with sTBI. This study aimed to construct a risk prediction model for ARDS in patients with sTBI and evaluate its efficacy.MethodsFrom 2016 to 2023, 502 patients diagnosed with sTBI were selected from the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University. All participants were randomly allocated to either the training or validation group. Feature selection for constructing the prediction model and developing a nomogram was carried out using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariable logistic regression analysis. The effectiveness and clinical relevance of the model were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, the area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration curves, and the decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe study found that 32.9% of patients with sTBI developed ARDS. The model was established based on oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry (SpO2), pneumonia, and fluid volume in the first 24 h. The model showed good discriminative ability with AUC values of 0.841 for the training and 0.821 for the validation groups. Calibration curves demonstrated that the predicted results align well with the actual results. The DCA suggested that the nomogram could lead to clinically beneficial outcomes at a significant threshold.ConclusionsThe diagnostic nomogram for ARDS in sTBI patients demonstrated satisfactory predictive value, assisting clinicians in identifying high-risk patients for ARDS.Trail registrationChiCTR2400085916.Keywords: Severe traumatic brain injury, Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Prediction model, Nomogram

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a neurological disorder that can have severe consequences and ranks among the primary causes of traumatic fatalities globally [1]. An estimated 50–60 million individuals around the world experience TBI annually [2], with severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI) comprising approximately 10–15% of these cases [3]. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a frequent complication when individuals suffer from sTBI, with a morbidity rate of 20%−40% and a mortality rate of 30%−40% [4, 5]. The mortality rate is 1.5-2.0 times higher than that of patients with sTBI alone [6]. The prognosis of patients with a concomitance of sTBI and ARDS is poor, and it is crucial to diagnose and intervene promptly in the development of ARDS to improve clinical outcomes in patients with sTBI.

However, ARDS often goes undiagnosed or diagnosis is delayed in the clinical setting. During ICU monitoring, 40% of the ARDS patients were not diagnosed, and less than 30% of the ARDS patients were diagnosed on the day they met the diagnostic criteria [7]. Currently, clinicians mainly rely on lung imaging and arterial blood gas (ABG) to identify whether a patient is experiencing ARDS. Clinicians possess inadequate and inconsistent knowledge of imaging criteria, which can impact the diagnosis of ARDS [8]. Additionally, some patients with ARDS may not be identified immediately due to the absence of lung imaging and ABG testing in settings with limited resources [9]. Hence, there is an urgent need for a practical tool that is easy to use and understand for early identification of ARDS.

The use of clinical risk prediction models as quantitative tools for risk assessment is on the rise in the medical field. In recent years, researchers have also constructed risk prediction models to assist in diagnosing ARDS [10]. Nevertheless, the study populations were relatively homogeneous, with a focus on patients with sepsis [11], covid-19 [12], and acute pancreatitis [13], while neglecting studies on patients with sTBI. As a result, specific indicators related to the pathogenesis and treatment of sTBI were not included in the selection of influencing factors. These factors included the admission glasgow coma scale (GCS) score [14], pulmonary contusion [15, 16] and various types of shock [15], among others. Considering these associated influencing factors, this study aimed to construct a risk prediction model for ARDS in patients with sTBI and evaluate its efficacy.

Methods

Population

Our study collected data on patients with sTBI who were hospitalized in the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University from January 2016 to December 2023. Our study recruited patients who were: (1) ≥ 18 years old; (2) GCS score ≤ 8 after resuscitation or within 48 h of injury [17]. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with serious injuries such as drowning, burns, or poisoning, etc.; (2) patients who were discharged or died within the initial 48 h of admission; (3) patients already diagnosed with ARDS prior to admission; (4) cases where ARDS diagnosis was not possible due to incomplete data.

Data collection

The patient information and clinical data were extracted from institutional electronic medical records and anonymized prior to analysis. The clinical data of admission included demographic characteristics, comorbidities (hypertension, lung disease, diabetes), first vital signs and symptoms on admission, clinical scores, treatment details (transfusion, infusion, mechanical ventilation, etc.), and laboratory examinations (blood routine test, blood chemical examination, etc.). The results of the first laboratory and imaging tests after admission were recorded. The clinical scores were based on the worst result within 48 h of admission.

Sample size

According to the EventPerVariable (EPV) method of logistic regression, influencing factors included in the prediction model should have at least ten times the number of covariates in terms of outcome occurrence [18]. Previous studies have found that patients with sTBI have 3–7 independent risk factors for developing ARDS [19, 20], with ARDS occurring from 20%−40% [5, 21, 22]. Additionally, a 10% loss of follow-up rate should be considered. Poldrack et al. [23] suggested a model construction ratio of 70% and a validation ratio of 30%. Therefore, the sample sizes for the study were calculated as 119–276 patients. Our study enrolled 502 patients, meeting the sample size requirements.

Outcome and definition

The study’s outcome indicator was whether patients developed ARDS

The new global definition of ARDS published in 2023 was used to diagnose ARDS in this study [9]. The specific diagnostic criteria include: (1) acute onset or worsening of hypoxemic respiratory failure within a week of exposure to risk factors; (2) bilateral opacities on chest radiographs and CT scans, or bilateral B-lines and/or consolidation on ultrasound; (3) arterial oxygen pressure/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ≤ 300 mmHg or SpO2/FiO2 ≤ 315 (if SpO2 ≤ 97%) on high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) (≥ 30 L/min) or noninvasive ventilation/continuous positive airway pressure (NIV/CPAP) with at least 5 cmH2O positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP); (4) for intubated patients and resource-limited settings, PaO2/FiO2 ≤ 300 mmHg or SpO2/FiO2 ≤ 315mmHg (if SpO2 ≤ 97%).

The diagnostic criteria for pneumonia in this study refer to the “Diagnostic Criteria for Hospital Infections” by the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China. A patient can be diagnosed with pneumonia if either of the following two criteria. The patient presents with cough, tenacious sputum, and wet rales on lung auscultation, along with at least one of the following: (1) Fever; (2) Elevated white blood cell count and/or increased proportion of neutrophils; (3) Radiological evidence of inflammatory infiltration in the lungs; (4) Patients with chronic airway diseases who develop an acute infection during a stable phase, with a change in pathogen or new radiological lesions compared to admission.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Counting variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%).

All enrolled patients were randomly allocated in a 7:3 ratio to either the training group or the validation group. The variables were screened using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression within the training group, followed by a multifactor logistic regression analysis of the selected variables. Considering the analysis, a model was constructed and presented as a nomogram. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to evaluate the discriminative ability by calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC) values. The calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the accuracy of the models. The calibration curves are a visual representation of the results of the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. By comparing the predicted probabilities with the observed probabilities at different quantiles, they serve as a graphical tool for assessing the accuracy of the predicted probabilities. Essentially, the calibration curves display a scatter plot contrasting the predicted probabilities with the occurrence probabilities. The decision curve analysis (DCA) was plotted to measure how useful the model is for clinical purposes. We considered results to be statistically significant if P < 0.05. All the analyses were used R version 4.3.1.

Our study encompassed 502 patients with sTBI (Fig. 1), and the incidence of ARDS was 32.9% (165/502). The profiles of the patients were presented in Table 1. Feature selection

The study involved 54 variables, and LASSO regression was used to select the factors associated with ARDS in sTBI patients. The 54 variables were condensed to 9 potential predictors using the LASSO regression algorithm, as shown in the coefficient profiles (Fig. 2A/B). The LASSO model included nine independent predictors: SpO2, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score, abbreviated injury score of the chest (AIS chest), pneumonia, brain midline shift, transfusion in the first 12 h, fluid volume in the first 24 h, mechanical ventilation (MV) and albumin (Alb). Subsequent multifactorial logistic regression analysis results suggested that SpO2, pneumonia, and fluid volume in the first 24 h were the key factors in predicting ARDS in patients with sTBI (Table 3).

Construction and evaluation of the model

A nomogram model for predicting ARDS in sTBI patients was constructed using data on SpO2, pneumonia, and fluid volume in the first 24 h (Fig. 3). Each variable corresponds to a line segment marked with scales representing possible values. The longer the line segment is, the greater the impact of the variable on the outcome. The Points assigned for each variable are based on its value, and the Total Points are the sum of all the individual scores of the variables. Corresponding to the total score is the predictive probability of the occurrence of ARDS, which is shown at the bottom row of the nomogram, labeled as Pr(ARDS). This probability indicates the risk of a patient developing ARDS.

Nomogram predictive model of ARDS in patients with sTBI. ARDS, acute respiratory distress syndrome; sTBI, severe traumatic brain injury; SpO2, oxygen saturation as measured by pulse oximetry

In the training group, the AUC was 0.841 (95%CI: 0.8004–0.8826) (Fig. 4A), with a specificity of 77.49% and a sensitivity of 78.33%. In the validation group, the AUC was 0.821 (95%CI: 0.7545–0.8883) (Fig. 4B), with a specificity of 60.38% and a sensitivity of 91.11%.

Discussion

Our study found that 32.9% of patients with sTBI developed ARDS, consistent with the documented prevalence range of 20–40% [4, 5] in the literature. Compared to the Berlin definition, the updated definition of ARDS expands the diagnostic criteria to include patients using HFNO and in settings with limited resources [9], likely leading to an increase in the incidence of ARDS. However, our study was conducted in a comprehensive tertiary hospital, with most sTBI patients using MV within 48 h of admission. Consequently, there was almost no underdiagnosis due to the use of HFNO and resource limitations. As a result, the morbidity rate identified was consistent with previous research findings.

Based on LASSO and logistic regression, our study identified three main factors associated with ARDS in sTBI patients, including SpO2, pneumonia, and fluid volume in the first 24 h. Then, multiple indicators were employed to evaluate the performance of the nomogram model, which was constructed by the factors mentioned above. The model for ARDS in this study achieved an AUC of 0.841, with a validation AUC of 0.821, indicating satisfactory predictive performance for ARDS in patients with sTBI. The model may provide clinicians with a practical tool to evaluate the risk of ARDS in patients with sTBI, enabling early intervention in high-risk patients and lead to improve clinical outcomes.

Trauma can disrupt the blood-brain barrier and cause hemorrhagic shock, causing large fluctuations in cerebral perfusion pressure and body blood pressure, which can result in secondary brain damage [24]. Hence, it is critical to maintain cerebral perfusion and hemodynamic stability through fluid resuscitation for patients with sTBI in the early stages of injury [25]. However, our study demonstrated that large volumes of fluid infusion within 24 h of admission are risk factors for ARDS in patients with TBI, which aligned with prior research [26]. This may be attributed to the infusion of substantial quantities of fluid in a brief period, leading to a notable increase in pulmonary blood volume, heightened capillary hydrostatic pressure, increased vascular endothelial permeability, and fluid infiltration into the alveoli, thereby precipitating pulmonary edema and consequently elevating the risk of developing ARDS [27]. Consequently, the fluid management strategy for patients with sTBI should be dynamically adjusted based on hemodynamic and cerebral perfusion assessments to avoid the indiscriminate administration of fluids.

Our research found that patients with sTBI who developed ARDS had lower SpO2 levels upon admission, consistent with the findings of a systematic review by Taran et al. [14]. This might be due to the impact of hypoxia on the pulmonary immune status of sTBI patients, disrupting tissue barriers and increasing the risk of ARDS. ABG is considered the gold standard for diagnosing hypoxemia, but conducting timely ABG monitoring with limited resources can be challenging. Previous studies [28–30] have shown a significant correlation between SpO2/FiO2 and PaO2/FiO2 values in evaluating ARDS [31]. As a result, the revised global definition permitted the utilization of SpO2 as a substitute for PaO2 in diagnosing ARDS [9]. It also suggests that pulse oximetry might become an important tool for the prompt diagnosis of ARDS, particularly in prehospital settings where diagnostic resources are lacking.

Pneumonia was recognized as a risk factor for ARDS due to its potential to induce acute and diffuse inflammatory lung injury [9]. According to our study and previous research, approximately half of all ARDS cases were attributed to pneumonia [32]. In patients with sTBI, pneumonia occured for two main reasons: MV and chest trauma [33]. Patients with sTBI required MV to protect the airway and maintain respiratory function due to their critical condition. Nonetheless, using MV increased the susceptibility to ventilator-associated pneumonia [34]. In addition, previous studies revealed that 20% of TBI patients also experience chest trauma, and those with chest trauma have a three times higher risk of developing pneumonia compared to those with other traumas [35]. To reduce the incidence of pneumonia and the risk of ARDS, clinicians need to emphasize chest trauma and airway management in patients with sTBI.

Although our study successfully constructed a practical model for identifying the risk of ARDS among sTBI patients, there are still several limitations to address in future research. Firstly, our study was a single-center retrospective analysis. Therefore, it is essential to conduct multicenter prospective trials to further determine the accuracy of the model. Secondly, our study only performed internal validation of the model. External validation will be necessary to evaluate the generalizability of the model. Lastly, due to the retrospective design and missing data, not all relevant variables were included in our analysis. For example, regarding Injury Severity Score (ISS), we were unable to incorporate it into our study. ISS is a critical factor in assessing trauma severity and may influence outcomes such as ARDS prediction in patients with sTBI.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that SpO2, pneumonia, and fluid volume in the first 24 h were independent risk factors ARDS among sTBI patients. The nomogram model incorporating these factors demonstrated robust discrimination and high predictive accuracy. Therefore, the nomogram can provide ancillary assistance to clinicians in identifying ARDS in patients with sTBI as early as possible. Clinicians can adjust treatment planning and clinical decision-making to prevent ARDS during hospitalization.
