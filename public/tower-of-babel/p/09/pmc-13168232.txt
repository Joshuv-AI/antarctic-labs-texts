Overcoming multidrug resistance Acinetobacter baumanii via downregulated virulence factor genes by green fabricated silver Nanoparticles mediated Piper nigrum
Authors: Bassam Shaker Mahmood, Yasser Ali Hussein, Hind Majid Ahmed, Anwer Jaber Faisal, Majid S Jabir, Bakr Ahmed Taha, Suresh Ghotekar, Balamuralikrishnan Balasubramanian, Ayman A Swelum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcinetobacter baumannii is a well-known pathogen that causes both community-acquired infections and nosocomial infections, particularly pneumonia associated with the use of ventilators. Numerous virulence factors and strong genomic plasticity are reported to be present in this opportunistic pathogen, which help it succeed in the infectious process. We aimed to study the inhibition and anti-biofilm abilities of the green-synthesized silver nanoparticles against the strain. Silver nanoparticles were green synthesized using the extract from Piper nigrum. Together with SEM, NTA, FTIR, XRD, and UV spectral analysis, the production of AgNPs was fully described. According to SEM and NTA examination, the produced NPs were in the 30–60 nm range. AgNPs were functionalized with organics in the reaction mixture, as indicated by FTIR measurements. Strong antibacterial and antibiofilm properties against MDR Acinetobacter baumanii were demonstrated by green-produced AgNPs. AgNP-treated isolates exhibited elevated ROS generation and membrane permeability. Antibacterial resistance was demonstrated by passaging the isolates for 15 generations. The positive control showed resistance after 13 generations, whereas the AgNPs demonstrated remarkable antimicrobial susceptibility even after 14 generations. We found a significant downregulation of all biofilm gene members in the study compared to the positive control (P < 0.05). These results highlight the potential for designing AgNPs for targeted future biomedical applications.Keywords: AgNPs, MDR, Acinetobacter baumannii, Biofilm inhibition, Virulence factors genesSubject terms: Biophysics, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

A. baumannii, a Gram-negative coccobacillus that mainly infects critically ill patients and has become a global issue in healthcare settings1. The most common manifestation of A. baumannii infections is ventilator-associated pneumonia, followed by infections of the skin, soft tissues, and bloodstream. In less frequent cases, this bacterium also causes urinary tract infections associated with the use of catheters or following surgery. Many medications are no longer effective due to the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant A. baumannii (MDRAB), which also causes a high infection-related death rate2. In the early 1970s, it was discovered that clinical strains of A. baumannii, which had previously been considered benign, were highly susceptible to the majority of first-line antimicrobial agents. It has, however, started to exhibit discernible resistance to widely used antibiotics at hitherto unanticipated rates throughout the last 50 years3. One main reason could be the overuse of β-lactam antibiotics, which leads to the development of drug resistance among the strains. This might again be a cause of the rise in carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii strains, which reduced the antibacterial impact seen among the subjects. Colistin-resistant isolates have now been observed globally, raising concerns about the need to halt the spread of resistance4. Omps, also referred to as porins5, are believed to be essential for the virulence in A. baumannii. OmpA, CarO, Omp33, OprD, and OmpW are the Omps that have been identified and thoroughly investigated thus far6. A. baumannii might have switched to use two types of motilities, namely twitching and surface-associated motility, instead of the regular flagellar-based motility7. As is known, the flagellar-mediated process is based on the appendages, and this process is carried out by type IV pili8. Twitching motility, which is facilitated by type IV pili, allows A. baumannii to stretch and retract in order to continue moving on abiotic surfaces9. Additionally, type IV pili contribute to pathogenicity, biofilm formation, and DNA uptake10. Some isolates of A. baumannii have been shown to exhibit surface-associated movement in addition to twitching motility11.

The antibacterial properties of silver (Ag) have been recognized for ages. Ag has been utilized for diverse reasons, including as an antibacterial ingredient in patented medical-grade goods12. The use of Ag for antimicrobial purposes declined in the 1940s as new therapeutic options, such as antibiotics, became available. The potential uses of Ag have been significantly expanded by the latest developments in nanotechnology, which have led to the creation and characterization of AgNPs. The antibacterial properties of AgNPs against a range of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, including MRSA and erythromycin-resistant Streptococcus pyogenes, have been demonstrated in numerous studies13. It has long been recognized that medicinal plants and products derived from them play a crucial role in primary healthcare and in the treatment of diverse illnesses14. According to Khan and Ahmad15, bioactive plant extracts are a crucial component of traditional medicine, often exhibiting a range of biological activities, including antimutagenic and antioxidant properties. Black pepper, or Piper nigrum, is a spice cultivated in tropical countries such as Brazil, Indonesia, and India. Several traditional medical systems, including Ayurvedic and Unani, utilize P. nigrum and its active ingredients as stimulants, carminatives, diuretics, anticholera agents, sialagogues, anti-asthmatics, and more16. Using the tissue Culture Plate (TCP) method and the Congo Red Agar (CRA) method, Rahman et al.17 investigated the antibiofilm activity of black pepper extract on Acinetobacter baumannii. They found that the extract exhibits strong anti-biofilm activity, making it a viable non-antibiotic treatment option for Acinetobacter baumannii infections associated with biofilms. Numerous reports are available on the antibacterial efficacy of Pepper extract, and a few studies have been conducted specifically on A. baumannii. We report, for the first time, antibacterial studies against A. baumannii using silver nanoparticles synthesized from pepper extract. In this work, we assess the toxicity and effectiveness of green-synthesized AgNPs as a potential antibacterial agent against A. baumannii. We studied the antibacterial efficacy through anti-biofilm quantification and differential expression of biofilm and pili formation gene members by qPCR.

Methods and methods

Materials

The 99.5% pure silver nitrate (AgNO3) used in the preparation of the nanoparticles was purchased from Qualigens. We bought organic-grade Piper nigrum seeds from nearby retailers. Colistin sulphate, purchased from a nearby dispensary and sold as Colistop (12.5 mg/Indi Pharma), served as the study’s positive control18.

Extraction and green synthesis

A laboratory-scale food processor was used to grind the black pepper seeds, which had been dried for three days at 40 °C in a hot air oven. After that, the powder was utilized for extraction. In this investigation, 40 g of powdered pepper was extracted using the Soxhlet technique with 250 mL of methanol and ethyl alcohol19. At 80 °C, the extraction was carried out for 24 h, and the extract was placed in a rotary evaporator to concentrate the extracted materials. The green synthesis was then carried out using the extract.

Estimation and purification of total phenolic content

The Folin-Ciocalteu method was used to perform the total phenol test. The extractions of methanol and ethanol that were discussed in the previous section were both tested. 1.25 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu (10%) and 1.25 mL of Sodium Carbonate were added to around 50 µL of extract20. The spectrophotometer was used to measure the absorbance at λ = 760 nm. Based on the calibration curve created using different gallic acid concentrations, the total phenol content before and after column purification was computed and expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAEs) per gram of the dry sample.

Eight fractions were obtained by combining the eluted fractions according to their TLC pattern after the methanolic fraction was chromatographed using a flash column on a silica gel eluted with a chloroform–methanol step-gradient (beginning with 50:0 to 5:1)21. Methanol was used to elute the fractions after they had been further chromatographed on a Sephadex LH-20 column. The fraction with high absorbance at 760 nm was subjected to HPLC analysis (Shimadzu LC-20AT, SPD-20A) using Phenomenex Luna C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm particle size) as the stationary phase. Water:Acetonitrile (80:20%v/v) was used as mobile phase, and Gallic acid (10 mg/10 mL) was used as reference standard.

Green synthesis

Approximately 10 mL of the purified fraction was diluted to 50 mL and then added to an equivalent volume of 3 mM AgNO3. The mixture was placed on a magnetic stirrer at 50 °C for 2 to 3 days until a color shift was observed. After a discernible change in color from light brown to black, the contents were washed thrice with deionized water before being centrifuged for 10 min at 8000 rpm. After that, the pellet was added to a vial and dried in a hot air oven. Characterization and antibacterial research were conducted using the resulting powder.

Characterization

A UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, 1800) with a resolution of 1 nm between 200 and 800 nm was used to measure the UV–visible spectrum of an aqueous solution confirming the production of AgNPs22. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were collected using the KBr pellet method on a Perkin Elmer Spectrum One FTIR spectrophotometer (Bomem MB100) with a range of 3600–400 cm−1. The AgNPs’ atomic ratio and surface morphology were determined using FESEM-EDX (SEM; Philips XL30ESEM). In Kyoto, Japan, using a Horiba SZ-100 analyzer, the synthesized AgNPs’ hydrodynamic size (Z-average), zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined using the DLS method.

Antibacterial evaluation

Agar well diffusion

Briefly, after autoclaving, the agar medium was chilled to 52 °C, inoculated with a bacterial culture (5 × 104 CFU/mL) overnight, and 20 mL of the inoculated agar was evenly distributed throughout the solid media plates. After the agar had set, 20 μL of the sample was added to the wells (20 mm) that had been punched into the agar. As a positive control, 50 μg/mL of the antibiotic Colistin sulfate was employed. The plates were examined for the inhibitory zones following a 24-h incubation period at 37 °C. The equation was utilized to determine the ability to form the inhibition zone. The percentage of inhibition is calculated as the sample’s inhibition zone diameter, divided by the positive control’s inhibition zone diameter multiplied by 10023.

Broth dilution assay

Using the serial two-fold microdilution approach, the MIC was ascertained by modifying an earlier technique by Mouzaki et al.24.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of any agent is known as the lowest concentration at which planktonic cell growth is stopped or inhibited.

Approximately 10 μL of an overnight culture (1 × 105 CFU/mL) was added to 180 μL of Luria–Bertani (LB) broth in each well. About 10 µl of sterile distilled water were added to the negative control well. Positive control (50 μg/mL) was added to the well labelled PC. To the well-labelled treatment, around 10 μL of AgNPs at various concentrations (5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL) were added. Following an overnight incubation period at 37 °C, the IC50 was calculated.

Drug resistance study

The drug resistance of the strains caused by the antibacterial agents was investigated using approximately 15 cycles of MIC measurements. Approximately 100 μL of the bacterial suspension from the sub-MIC well was inoculated into 10 mL of fresh medium at 37 °C overnight. A concentration of approximately 105 CFU/mL was then achieved by diluting the overnight bacterial solution for the subsequent MIC test. This was the first method by which AgNPs were introduced to the bacteria. Approximately 100 μL of the bacterial suspension exposed to AgNPs from the ½ MIC well was then injected into 10 mL of fresh medium. To allow the bacteria to develop untreated (AgNPs), the culture was then incubated overnight at 37 °C and without being exposed to AgNPs, the bacteria that survived the initial passage’s AgNP exposure increased to approximately 105 CFU/mL throughout the following night. The leftover bacterial solution was then treated with AgNPs, diluted to approximately 105 CFU/mL, and used in the MIC assay in passage 2. Several MIC tests were performed on fifteen of these passages. To examine drug resistance following a relatively extended exposure to the antibacterial agent, 15 passes were carried out, each using surviving bacteria that had been exposed to AgNPs. Bacterial drug resistance would be indicated by any increase in MIC accompanied by an increase in passage number after many exposures to the antibacterial agent25. The same method was used to test the positive control.

Anti-biofilm study

The anti-biofilm activity of AgNPs was examined using the crystal violet staining assay26. To facilitate the production of biofilms, bacterial culture (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was first cultivated on a 96-well plate for 24 h. The treatment wells received roughly 10 μL of AgNPs at different concentrations (¼ MIC and ½ MIC). Similarly, a positive control was added at both the ¼ MIC and 1/2 MIC concentrations. Following the staining of the methanol-fixed biofilm with crystal violet (0.1%), the OD570 was measured using a microplate reader. The following formula was used to determine the AgNP biofilm inhibition rates. Inhibition rate % = (OD570 Control − OD570 Sample)/OD570 Control × 100.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation

The quantity of ROS emitted by the bacteria was measured using the method described by Choi et al.27, with slight modifications. In short, about 50 mL of the culture was added to a conical flask and treated with its respective treatments. About 1 mL of Piper nigrum extract, AgNPs, Colistin (positive control), and DMSO (negative control) were added to the culture and incubated in a shaker at 37 °C. Following incubation for 6 h, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting pellet was added to a 2% Nitro Blue Tetrazolium (NBT) mixture. The pellet was now incubated at ambient temperature in a dark environment, and the contents were centrifuged (8000 rpm, 10 min). The pellet was washed with PBS and centrifuged again for 5 min at 8000 rpm. About 2 mL of KOH (2 M) solution was added to the suspension to disrupt the cell membrane. The suspension was mixed with approximately 1 mL of DMSO (50%) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The contents were centrifuged, and 100 μL of the supernatant was added to 96-well plates. The plate was read for absorbance at 620 nm using an ELISA plate reader (Genetix).

Bacterial cell membrane leakage

The OD measurement method (Ibrahim et al., 2020) was employed to assess the cell membrane leakage induced by green-synthesized AgNPs. In brief, approximately 2 mL of an overnight bacterial culture (1 × 104 CFU/mL) was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was washed with PBS (pH 7.4) and then treated with AgNPs at varying concentrations (½MIC and MIC). Following a 4-h incubation, the contents were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was then measured optically at 280 nm and 260 nm, respectively. The bacterial cells that were not treated with AgNPs served as a negative control. The cells treated with Colistin served as a positive control.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Approximately 20 µL of the isolate was added to 50 mL of media, and 10 µL of NPs (1/2 MIC) was added to the tube designated as the treatment. To the tube designated as positive control, 10 µL of Colistin (1/2 MIC) was added. The untreated tube acts as the control. The contents are properly vortexed and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h. After incubation, the cultures were removed and used for RNA extraction. The RNA extraction procedure was carried out as per the instructions provided in the manual (RNeasy Kit). The cultures (< 1 × 106 bacteria) were utilized for extraction after treatment.

RNA quality was assessed with a UV spectrophotometer, and cDNA was synthesized using the RT PCR kit (SuperScript TMII Reverse Transcriptase; 200 U/μL (HiMedia). The concentration of RNA achieved was approximately 2.13 μg/μL; as such, the reaction utilized approximately 1.08 μL of RNA, random primers, and 1 μL of RT enzyme. The mixture was then incubated at 25 °C for 10 min after being thoroughly mixed. After 45 min of incubation at 68 °C, the resulting cDNA was kept until it was needed for gene expression analysis.

Real-time PCR

Real-time primers (Table 1) were designed using Primer3 software and procured from Sigma-Aldrich. The iQTM SYBR Green Supermix (HiMedia) was used to perform the real-time PCR assay as described by Malla et al.28. The PCR assay was performed with primers (600 nM) and 1μL of the RT products, resulting in a total reaction volume of 25 μL.

To screen the positive amplification, each reaction was done simultaneously with its corresponding negative control. The samples (both control and treatment) were quantified on a thermocycler using the Corbett Research cycler (Bio-Rad). The program was executed for approximately 40 cycles, consisting of 45 s at 91 °C, 55 s at 65 °C, and an elongation step at 72 °C for 50 s. Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data was represented as value ± SD23. Variance analysis was done using one-way ANOVA and validated with Tukey’s test30.

Results

Estimation of phenolic content

The phenolic content was found to be higher for the methanolic fraction than the ethanolic fraction. Methanolic fractions before and after purification were found to be 19 ± 0.35 and 36 ± 0.21 µg/gm/gm, respectively. The fraction confirmed for the phenolic fraction was used for the green synthesis. The retention times were found to be 5.34 and 5.28 for the reference and eluted fraction, respectively (Fig. 1). The content calculated from the area was found to be 95% and 83.45% respectively.

Green synthesis and characterization

The size distribution of the produced silver nanoparticles was determined using the DLS under controlled circumstances at 45 °C, pH 9, and AgNO3 (3 mM). The stability of the generated nanoparticle is confirmed by the measured zeta potential of – 1.6 mV.

Most of the AgNPs exhibited a spherical form and ranged in size from 40 to 80 nm. The majority of the particles were between 60 and 70 nm in size as indicated in (Fig. 2). From the UV spectroscopy, the absorbance maximum of the silver nanoparticles synthesized is observed at 432 nm for a 3 mM solution (Fig. 3).

FTIR spectra

Figure 4 shows the Piper nigrum extract FTIR spectrum, and Figure shows the AgNPs synthesized FTIR spectrum. At 3706.75, 3416, 2920, 2386, 1625, 1382, 1025, and 576.16 cm‒1, sharp bands were found. Between 3706 and 3421 cm‒1, the band represents the typical OH stretching mode. The C–H asymmetric and symmetric stretching modes are seen at a peak of 2920 cm‒1. The peak shows alkane stretching at 2386 cm‒1, while the C=O stretching is seen at 1625 cm‒1. The peak represents the nitro group at 1382 cm‒1, while the 1025 cm‒1 peak represents the C–C stretch and C–F stretch of aliphatic compounds. The C–I stretch is represented by the peak at 576 cm‒1. After adding silver nitrate, the OH stretching mode is observed at a peak of 3416 cm‒1. The C=O stretching is seen at 1648 cm‒1. In contrast to Piper nigrum extract, the AgNPs’ peaks at 2920 cm‒1 and 2386 cm‒1, which represent the C–N and aliphatic C–F stretching, respectively, were suppressed (Fig. 5). Hence, we verify that the bioreduction of silver nitrate to silver nanoparticles depends critically on the regulated transmittance percentage of ketone, fluoro, and amine groups31.

FTIR spectra of the NPs obtained with Piper nigrum extract and with silver nitrate.

XRD spectra analysis

The patterns of the XRD spectra are used to confirm the crystalline nature of the nanoparticles synthesized with P. nigrum extract. The XRD spectra unequivocally demonstrate that the silver nanoparticles created with the aforementioned extracts are crystalline. At 2θ values of 37.27°, 44.1°, 65.2°, and 77°, the Bragg reflections of silver nanoparticles are observed (Fig. 6A). They appear to match the lattice planes indexed for spherical particles, which are (112), (200), (216), and (311). The particles were also confirmed to be spherical by SEM analysis (Fig. 6B).

Antibacterial evaluation assays

Using the agar well diffusion method, we found that NPs exhibited significant inhibition zones (P < 0.05) compared to the positive control. Positive control and NPs exhibited inhibition zones with diameters of 10.5 mm and 6.5 mm, respectively (Fig. 7A). On the other hand, extract (E) showed a 1.5 mm inhibition zone. Our broth dilution assay results are consistent with those obtained using the agar well diffusion method. Positive control and NPs showed a reduction in growth to 0.01 and 0.16 at 160 μg/mL, compared to 1.23 (control). The negative control showed an inhibition of 0.74 from 1.23 at 160 μg/mL (P < 0.05). From the regression curve, we found the IC50 values to be 22.5, 45.3, and 154 μg/mL for the positive control, NPs, and extract, respectively (P < 0.05). MIC values were found to be 81.5 and 125 μg/mL, and extract MIC value was found to be > 250 μg/mL (Fig. 7B).

A Agar well diffusion showing the inhibition zones. B Broth dilution assay. The IC50 value is shown in the graph (embedded). P positive control, NP Nano particles, E Extract.

Bacterial drug resistance assay

Following exposure to AgNPs and respective positive control, the serial MICs of planktonic strains were determined for 15 passages. The MIC was found in the previous section to be 81.5 and 125 µg/mL, respectively, for positive control and NPs against A. baumannii. The ½ MIC value of positive control (40 µg/mL) changed to 54 µg/mL at 13th passage and 14th passage and changed to 56 µg/mL at 15th passage (P < 0.05). On the other hand, NPs maintained the resistance till the 14th generation (Fig. 7). The MIC value of NPs (60 µg/mL) changed to 72 at the 14th passage and remained at 72 for the 15th passage as well (P < 0.05). With this, we confirmed that the isolate remained susceptible to NPs up to the 14th generation, whereas the positive control remained susceptible up to the 13th generation (Fig. 8). This confirms the antibacterial nature of the green-synthesized AgNPs.

Bacterial drug resistance assay. MIC was measured from passages 0 to 15. Colistin was used as a positive control (POS) for A. baumanii.

Antibiofilm activity

Biofilm development is a highly intricate process that offers cells a stable environment for survival and growth. Hence, halting this process would enhance the antibacterial activity32. Varying concentrations of AgNPs were found to inhibit the biofilm formation after 24-h incubation. The load of biofilm formation significantly decreased with an increase in AgNP concentration from ¼ MIC to ½ MIC after 24 h of incubation. The percent inhibition was found to be 40% and 78%, respectively, for ¼ MIC and ½ MIC. On the other hand, the positive control showed 63% and 87% at ¼ MIC and ½ MIC, respectively (Fig. 9). Thus, the AgNPs can significantly hinder the formation of A. baumanii biofilms and subsequently impede the proliferation of A. baumannii.

Anti-biofilm activity: histogram showing the biofilm suppression following treatment with NPs and positive control (P) at ¼ MIC and ½ MIC concentrations after 24-h incubation.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation

The oxidation of AgNP results in the release of Ag+ ions, which are responsible for circulation within the living organism. Formation of ROS leads to excessive oxidative stress, which harms membranes, proteins, DNA/RNA, and lipids—this increases cytotoxic effects in prokaryotic cells. ROS production in this isolate was evaluated by treatment with plant extract, AgNP, and colistin to measure ROS levels against the negative control (DMSO). There was a remarkable increase in the production of ROS upon treatment with AgNPs at both concentrations compared to the control and positive control (P < 0.05). The production of ROS was found to be increased by 67% and 76% at ¼ MIC and ½ MIC, respectively, when treated with AgNPs (P < 0.05). On the other hand, the positive control showed 76 and 84% of ROS production at ¼ MIC and ½ MIC, respectively (Fig. 10). The extract did show production of 50% ROS at ½ MIC.

ROS production under the treatment with AgNPs. P positive control, NP nanoparticle, E extract, C Control.

Bacterial cell membrane leakage assay

The majority of the antibacterial mechanisms of NPs are known to depend on their binding to bacterial membranes, resulting in membrane rupture and the release of nucleic acid contents (Ibrahim et al., 2020). To screen for leakages in cell membrane integrity due to AgNP stress, we recorded the OD280 and OD260 of the supernatant in our study. This supernatant was primarily composed of nucleic acids and proteins released from intracellular components. We observed a significant increase in the OD260 and OD280 values following treatment with AgNPs, which was found to be duration-dependent. The OD260 of NPs increased to 0.32 ± 0.03 after 4 h of incubation, from 0.21 ± 0.03 at 2 h of incubation at ½ MIC (P < 0.05). At the same time, the positive control showed a rise to 0.47 ± 0.05 after 4 h incubation from 0.27 ± 0.05 at 2 h incubation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 11).

qRT-PCR of gene members

From the relative expression data obtained, we found that all the gene members were significantly downregulated after treatment (P < 0.05). A downregulation was observed with the ompA gene member, where the expression was found to be 57% and 45% respectively, for NPs and positive control (p < 0.05). Similarly, the omp33 gene member was found to be down-regulated, with expression levels of 81% and 67% for NPs and the positive control (p < 0.05), respectively. A downregulation was observed with the carO gene member, where the expression was found to be 85% and 67% respectively, for NPs and positive control (p < 0.05). A downregulation was observed with the ompW gene member, where the expression was found to be 57% and 48% respectively, for NPs and positive control (p < 0.05). A downregulation was observed with the bauA gene member, where the expression was found to be 81% and 78% respectively, for NPs and positive control (p < 0.05). The results were highly significant when compared to controls (the control was assumed to be 1 or 100%) (Figs. 12, 13, 14, 15, 16).

Discussion

In our current context, we found potent antibacterial activity against A. baumannii, which was confirmed by traditional biofilm inhibition studies as well as by the differential expression of biofilm gene members using qPCR. The size distribution of silver nanoparticles was determined using DLS under controlled conditions, with most spherical particles ranging from 40 to 80 nm. The stability was confirmed by a measured zeta potential of -126 mV. The synthesized silver nanoparticles exhibited an absorbance maximum at 432 nm in a 3 mM solution, as observed using UV spectroscopy. Surface plasmon resonance bands of colloidal silver synthesized using black pepper extracts were observed at 420–446 nm at different temperatures, with a strong broad peak at 441 nm confirming the formation of silver nanoparticles33. Research conducted by Bratovcic et al.34 further verified that AgNPs made using pepper extract exhibited unique peaks in the 380–480 nm range. According to da Silva Ferreira et al.35, the absorbance maxima of silver nanoparticles are found at 440 nm for a 1 mM solution, 442 nm for a 2 mM solution, 450 nm for a 3 mM solution, and 466 nm for a 4 mM solution.

Studies by Krishnan et al.33 also confirmed similar FTIR band patterns with Piper nigrum. We found similar patterns with our AgNPs assignments.

From the antibacterial studies, we found that NPs and extract (E) exhibited significant inhibition zones compared to the positive control, with zones of 10.5 mm and 6.5 mm in diameter, respectively. The results of our broth dilution assay are consistent with those obtaining using agar well diffusion method. The growth of NPs and positive control was reduced from 1.23 (control) to 0.01 and 0.16 at 160 μg/mL. The results showed that the MIC values were 81.5 and 125 μg/mL. The extract’s MIC value was > 250 μg/mL.

Studies on the pepper extracts against A. baumanii were very limited. Slman et al.36 reported a recent study that also confirmed the antibacterial role of silver NPs with pepper extract towards both S. aureus and E. coli. Rahman et al.17 confirmed the antibacterial activity of their studies on Acinetobacter baumannii biofilm formation. Biofilm production was significantly decreased after exposure to 10 mg/mL black pepper extract, with 94% showing reduced biofilm formation. Studies conduted by Alateeqi et al.37 confirmed the role of Piper nigrum extracts in combating various bacterial infections.

From this study, we report that after 24 h of incubation, the biofilm formation dramatically decreased when the concentration of AgNP rose from ¼ MIC to ½ MIC. For ¼ MIC and ½ MIC, the percentage of inhibition was 40% and 78%, respectively. However, for ¼ MIC and ½ MIC, the positive control revealed 63 and 87%, respectively. AgNPs can thereby considerably prevent A. baumanii biofilm development, which in turn prevents A. baumanii from proliferating. To further elucidate the long-term efficacy of green-synthesized AgNPs against A. baumanii, our study demonstrated sustained susceptibility up to the 14th passage, compared to the 13th passage for the positive control (Colistin) (Fig. 8). However, evaluating resistance development over extended passages (> 15) or under diverse selective pressures, such as varying AgNP concentrations, pH, or temperature, is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of resistance mechanisms. Previous studies suggest that AgNP resistance may involve the upregulation of efflux pump or membrane adaptations38.

AgNPs were screened to assess their ability to inhibit bacterial growth using agar-well diffusion and anti-biofilm studies. Among the strains tested, the inhibitory action of the formulation is hampered by biofilm formation. AgNPs caused an inhibition zone of 22 ± 5 mm in weak biofilm producers, which was more pronounced than the 17 ± 4 mm and 10.1 ± 4 mm observed in moderate and strong A. baumannii biofilm producers38.

According to our findings, AgNPs have a strong ability to suppress the robust biofilms produced by A. baumannii. Additionally, tiny to well-formed biofilms are partially removed after an overnight incubation period. AgNPs may be used as a biofilm-disrupting agent as a result39. We are in line with earlier research that demonstrated the effective inhibition of biofilms formed by several microbes by AgNPs32. Inhibiting biofilm formation by A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, E. coli, and K. pneumoniae at comparable concentrations has been assessed by various researchers40, 41, 42.

We found that all gene members in the study were significantly downregulated after treatment (P < 0.05), as indicated by the relative expression data collected. AgNP treatment resulted in a downregulation of the ompA, omp33, and carO gene members (p < 0.05). AgNP treatment also resulted in the downregulation of the ompW and bauA gene members (p < 0.05).

The current study is the first to report the use of green-synthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) mediated by Piper nigrum extract to downregulate key virulence and biofilm-related genes (ompA, omp33, carO, ompW, bauA) in multidrug-resistant A. baumannii biofilms, as demonstrated by qPCR analysis (Figs. 12, 13, 14, 15, 16). While previous studies, such as Din et al.43, have shown the anti-bacterial activity of Piper nigrum extract alone, and Hetta et al.38 have reported AgNP activity against A. baumannii biofilms, none have investigated the combined effect of Piper nigrum-mediated AgNPs on gene expression. This work is significant since it is the first to demonstrate that AgNPs may decrease the expression of many genes linked to biofilm formation and virulence (ompA, omp33, carO, ompW, and bauA). This adds to the existing mechanism by which silver inhibits microbial development. We found that gene expression related to biofilm formation was greatly reduced at ½ MIC concentrations of AgNPs.

Green extracts, NPs, green-synthesized NPs, and biosynthesized NPs were all screened via qPCR to confirm the expression patterns of biofilm and other virulence gene members. Abirami et al.44 reported that pyrogallol treatment significantly reduced the expression levels of several important proteins in A. baumannii, including outer membrane proteins and members of antioxidant gene families. These proteins are involved in the antioxidant defense system, stress responses, virulence, and biofilm formation via bacterial adhesion and capsule formation. One important non-specific channel that aids different bacteria in preserving the integrity of their membranes is OmpA45. The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of some antibiotics like chloramphenicol, trimethoprim, imipenem, and colistin got reduced in an ompA mutant of A. baumannii when compared to the wild type (WT) strain46. At some point, we concluded that one of the primary strategies for lowering A. baumannii biofilm activity and thereby lowering antibiotic resistance mechanisms is the use of AgNPs to decrease the expression of OmpA, omp33, ompW, carO, and bauA. This study is limited by the absence of in vivo investigations to corroborate the in vitro efficacy of AgNPs against Acinetobacter baumannii. The lack of in vivo evidence limits understanding of their therapeutic potential, toxicity, biodistribution, and clearance. In vitro experiments were prioritized due to resource limitations. Subsequent research will incorporate animal models to fill these gaps.

Conclusion

Our knowledge of the traits of clinical isolates of A. baumannii has improved as a result of this investigation. We confirmed the potent role of AgNPs in inhibiting biofilm formation, and we also found that AgNPs are equally effective in managing antibacterial resistance. Additionally, applying AgNPs to the strain dramatically stopped their growth and multiplication. The extent to which a biofilm is generated has a great impact on the activity of AgNPs. This, in turn, decides the level of inhibition in the biofilm, ROS production, and membrane leakage. Important genes that aid in biofilm formation have their transcription levels downregulated by these AgNPs. Our results represent a further step in understanding how AgNPs can impede the persistence and proliferation of microorganisms. Through qPCR analysis, we confidently report the discovery as the first of its kind, in disclosing the mechanism by which green-synthesized AgNPs modify A. baumannii pathogenesis.
