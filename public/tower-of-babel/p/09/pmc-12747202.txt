Functional and transcriptional responses of chicken macrophages to hypoxia
Authors: Duanduan Li, Liang Zhao, Lin Zhang, Feng Gao, Tong Xing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of wooden breast (WB) myopathy, marked by palpable hardness and excessive fibrosis in the affected pectoralis major muscle of broilers, has greatly affected poultry industry. Hypoxia-triggered dysfunction of macrophages play a crucial role in inflammation-driven tissue fibrosis. Nevertheless, how hypoxia-induced changes in chicken macrophages contribute to WB development remain poorly understood. In this study, we established an in vitro hypoxia model using chicken macrophages HD11 cells and characterized their functional and transcriptional alterations. The results demonstrated that hypoxia enhanced macrophage proliferation while suppressing their migration. RNA-seq analysis revealed 418, 1,462, and 2,670 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h post-hypoxia exposure, respectively. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network indicates that DEG-enriched modules were primarily involved in immune response, cytoskeletal regulation, and metabolism. Key hub genes, including MMP2, MMP9, CCL4, CCL5, and CXCL12, were identified across different time points. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that prolonged hypoxia first induced acute stress in HD11 cells, which then progressively transitioned toward an immune-activation phenotype. This transition was evidenced by enrichment of DEGs in lysosomal, cytokine and immune-regulatory pathways, concomitant with up-regulated glycolysis and down-regulated antioxidant pathways. Furthermore, sustained hypoxia augmented the extracellular matrix (ECM)-remodeling capacity of HD11 cells, as reflected by elevated expression of ECM-associated genes and pathways, accompanied by functional reprogramming of cytoskeletal and membrane proteins. This research elucidates functional and transcriptional changes of chicken macrophages in response to hypoxia, demonstrating that hypoxia enhances the immune response and ECM-remodeling capacity of macrophages, which can provide theoretical insights for targeting interventions to control WB myopathy via macrophage mechanisms.Keywords: Wooden breast, Macrophages, Hypoxia, Inflammatory response, ECM remodeling

Introduction

Wooden breast (WB) is a prevalent myopathy in broilers that severely affects meat quality. Compared to the normal pectoralis major (PM) muscle, WB is characterized by higher pH, lower water-holding capacity, reduced protein content, and increased coarse fiber levels. These attributes collectively compromise the overall quality of poultry products (Bowker et al., 2018; Livingston et al., 2019; Bian et al., 2024).

Histological features of WB myopathy, such as inflammatory cell infiltration into muscle fibers and phlebitis, are accompanied by elevated levels of inflammatory factors. This indicates a more severe inflammatory response compared to normal broiler PM muscle (Sihvo et al., 2014; Xing et al., 2021). Studies have shown that inflammatory cells infiltrate venous vessel walls and spread along venous pathways as the disease progresses, eventually reaching degenerated muscle fibers in advanced lesions (Papah et al., 2017), strongly suggesting a crucial role of the inflammatory response in WB development.

Macrophages are pivotal effector cells within the immune system, critically regulating both the initiation of inflammatory responses and the process of tissue repair (Yasutaka and Ruslan, 2016). Following tissue injury, they are rapidly recruited to damaged sites by inflammatory signals and chemokines. Upon localization, these cells often polarize into the M1 phenotype, which exhibits potent phagocytic and microbicidal activity to clear pathogens and cellular debris. M1 macrophages further amplify inflammation by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and, via surface co-stimulatory molecules, engaging T cells to orchestrate adaptive immunity (Ogle et al., 2016; Hesketh et al., 2017). In the resolution phase of tissue repair, macrophages switch from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors that suppress inflammation, facilitate tissue regeneration, and restore homeostasis (Kiss et al., 2018). They also contribute to tissue remodeling by regulating the synthesis and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) (Madsen and Bugge, 2013). The role of macrophages in WB myopathy is gaining attention. Spatial transcriptomic analyses have revealed that macrophages in WB muscle exhibit pronounced co-localization with areas of aberrant lipid metabolism and active muscle-fiber regeneration (Wang et al., 2024). The observed spatial link strongly implies that macrophages play an active and significant role in the development of this disease. While our current knowledge of macrophage functions is largely based on mammalian models, the avian immune system exhibits critical distinctions. Theses encompass the presence of the bursa of fabricius, the absence of lymph nodes, and the dominance of IgY over IgG as the primary serum antibody (Warr et al., 1995). Given these fundamental disparities, targeted investigations into the specific biology and functions of poultry macrophages are essential.

Hypoxia is a critical regulator in diverse physiological and pathological processes, especially those related to inflammation and tissue repair. As key immune cells, macrophages undergo profound functional and phenotypic shifts under hypoxic conditions, which subsequently shape immune responses and disease progression (Raggi et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2025). Hypoxia enhances the secretion of inflammatory factors via activation of the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, the resulting accumulation of these factors recruits additional immune cells, thereby amplifying the local inflammatory response (Taylor and Colgan, 2017). The impact of hypoxia on macrophage polarization has yielded conflicting results. Research in mouse models shows that hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α (HIF-1α) can promote M1 polarization. However, contrasting observations indicate that acute hypoxia may also enhance M2 macrophage activity (Wang et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2025). These divergent findings suggest that the polarizing outcome is likely dependent on specific cellular context and the nature of the hypoxic stimulus. Furthermore, HIF-1α upregulates pro-fibrotic factors such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), which directly stimulate the proliferation and activation of fibroblasts and promote their differentiation into myofibroblasts, thereby enhancing ECM synthesis and deposition (Zhang et al., 2021). Hypoxia also induces macrophages to secrete multiple cytokines that act directly on the ECM, further promoting fibrotic remodeling (Tan et al., 2013; Cheng et al., 2018; Domoko et al., 2023). In summary, hypoxia-driven macrophage dysfunction is a critical contributor to tissue fibrosis. Current understanding of WB myopathy pathogenesis has primarily focused on mitochondrial and metabolic aspects, whereas the potential of hypoxia-induced dysfunction in macrophage remains largely unexplored and merits dedicated research.

In this study, we established an in vitro hypoxia model in chicken macrophages (HD11 cells) to investigate their functional reprogramming and transcriptional alterations under hypoxic conditions, and to identify key regulatory genes and signaling pathways involved during the hypoxic response. The findings of this study can provide theoretical insights of how hypoxia modulates the immune response and pro-fibrotic functions of macrophages, which advances the development of interventions to control WB myopathy via macrophage mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and hypoxia treatment

The chicken macrophages cell line HD11 was obtained from NuoLai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HD11 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (P/S). At 70–80 % confluence, cells were detached with 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) and subcultured into fresh plates. Cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO₂ and 95 % air. For hypoxic treatment (HYP group), after the cells had fully attached, the medium was replaced with hypoxia-preconditioned DMEM, and the plates were transferred to a tri-gas incubator (1 % O₂, 5 % CO₂, 94 % N₂) at 37°C for 6, 12, 24, and 48 h.Control (CON) group cells were cultured under normoxic conditions with regular DMEM culture medium in the standard CO2 incubator for the corresponding time periods. Following the completion of treatment, samples were collected according to the specific requirements of each experimental method.

Western blot analysis

Macrophages treated with hypoxia for 0, 6, 12 and 24 h were collected using protein extraction solution (RIPA lysis buffer: protease/phosphatase inhibitors: PMSF = 100:1:1). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and normalized to 1.2 μg/μL. Subsequently, 1/4 volume of 5 × loading buffer was added to the samples, which were then boiled for 10 minutes to denature the proteins. Equal amounts of protein samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis using a 6 % gel and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Cytiva, Utah, USA) at 200 mA under wet transfer conditions. The membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk for 1.5 h. The PVDF membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-HIF-1α antibody (1:1000, Abcam) and anti-Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase antibody (GAPDH, 1:5000, Proteintech). After washing five times with 1 × TBST, the membranes were incubated with Anti-rabbit IgG (1:3000, CST) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were visualized using Enhanced Chemiluminescence Reagent on a gel imaging system. The grayscale values of the proteins were quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell proliferation and migration activity assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). HD11 macrophages were subjected to hypoxic treatment for 0, 6, 12 and 24 h. At each time point, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added per well and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative cell viability was expressed as a percentage of the normoxic control.

Macrophages treated with hypoxia for 24 h were collected at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL (1 mL in total). The cells were fixed and stained using a cell cycle detection kit (KeyGEN, Nanjing, China) and then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD, New Jersey, USA) to determine the cell cycle distribution.

The migration ability of macrophages treated with hypoxia for 6, 12, and 24 h was evaluated by wound-healing assay. The method involved creating a scratch in the cell monolayer and monitoring the wound closure at fixed time points. Detailed procedures followed Ma et al. (2023), and statistical analysis was performed as described by Gao et al. (2018).

RNA isolation, library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from HD11 cells treated with hypoxia for 12, 24, and 48 h using Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The RNA quality was assessed using a bioanalyzer (Agilent, California, USA) and further verified by agarose gel electrophoresis using RNase-free reagents. Ribosomal RNA was depleted using the Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The retained mRNA was fragmented and reverse transcribed into cDNA using random primers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized with the aid of DNA polymerase I, RNase H, dNTPs (with dUTP replacing dTTP), and buffer. Subsequently, the cDNA fragments were purified using the QiaQuick polymerase chain reaction (PCR) purification kit. After end-repairing, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, the second strand was degraded using Uracil-N-Glycosylase (UNG). The digested products were size-selected via agarose gel electrophoresis and amplified by PCR to construct the sequencing library. High-throughput sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, California, USA). The obtained Raw Reads were quality-controlled using Fastp (v 18.0) to remove low-quality sequences, yielding Clean Reads. The screening criteria were based on the previous study (Li et al., 2025). The effective sequences were aligned to the chicken reference genome using HISAT2 software, with parameters set as “-rna-strandness RF” and other parameters kept at default values. The transcripts were assembled using StringTie (v 1.3.1). The expression abundance of each transcript was quantified by calculating the transcripts per million (TPM) value using the RSEM software.

Sample relationship analysis and screening of differentially expressed genes

Sample relationships were analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA). Differential gene expression between groups was analyzed using the DESeq2 software, with the criteria for differential expression set at the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and the absolute value of log2 fold change (|Log2FC|) ≥ 1. Heatmaps and volcano plots were generated using the R software package (v 3.6) to evaluate the relationships of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within and between groups.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted, primarily focusing on gene ontology (GO) analysis and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. These analyses were used to explore the changes in biological functions of DEGs and to identify key signaling transduction pathways and biosynthetic metabolic pathways that are significantly enriched. In these analyses, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA software and the MSigDB database to determine whether there were significant differences in gene sets within specific pathways between the two groups. The specific steps are as follows: Genes were ranked according to the Signal-to-noise metric, and their positions within a pathway were used to assign and normalize scores, resulting in the normalized enrichment score (NES). By examining and adjusting the NES values, the final FDR values were obtained.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network establishment

The PPI network of DEGs was constructed using the search tool for the retrieval of interacting genes (STRING) database and visualized using Cytoscape software. The MCODE plugin was employed to identify distinct modules within the PPI network, and the CytoHubba plugin was used to recognize the hub genes.

Validation of differentially expressed genes

We selected three genes associated with inflammatory response and immune defense, and quantified their relative expression levels using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Macrophages exposed to hypoxic conditions for 0, 12, 24, and 48 h were harvested, and total cellular mRNA was extracted. The mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiFiScript All-in-one RT Master Mix for qPCR (CWBIO, Taizhou, China). The mRNA expression levels were determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR), with 18S rRNA serving as the housekeeping gene. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The primers for mRNA amplification were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd.(Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using SPSS (v 20.0) and GraphPad Prism (v 8.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). For the cell cycle assay, eight replicates were used per group, while other cell-based experiments and omics sequencing were performed in triplicate. Differences between CON and HYP groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, differences between all-time points were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Changes in hypoxia signaling of HD11 macrophages

We validated the in vitro hypoxia model in HD11 macrophages by monitoring the stabilization of HIF-1α, the master transcriptional regulator of the hypoxic response. Results indicated a progressive activation of the hypoxia signaling, as HIF-1α protein level increased significantly after 6 h of hypoxia, continued to climb through 12 h, and peaked at 24 h compared with the CON group (Fig. 1A, B).

Hypoxia leads to functional reprogramming of HD11 macrophages

To dissect how hypoxia reshapes macrophages behavior, we monitored proliferation, cell cycle progression and migratory capacity(Supplementary Table 2). CCK-8 assays revealed a continuous rise in viability under both normoxia and hypoxia, yet at every time-point examined (6 h, 12 h, and 24 h) hypoxia conferred a marked proliferation advantage (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C). After 24 h of hypoxia, flow-cytometric profiling revealed a marked reduction in the G0/G1 fraction and an increase in the S-phase and G2/M populations compared to the CON group(Fig. 1D, E), indicating that hypoxia accelerated HD11 cell-cycle transit. In wound-healing assays, macrophages in response to hypoxia closed the scratch at a significantly slower rate at all intervals (6 h, 12 h, and 24 h; P < 0.01; Fig. 1F, G), suggesting that hypoxia suppressed HD11 migration.

Screening of differentially expressed genes

We performed RNA-seq on HD11 cells treated with hypoxia for 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h to investigate the genes and pathways that exhibited significant changes under hypoxic conditions. Quality control was performed on the raw reads, and after filtering out low-quality sequences, an average of 4,421,643 clean reads per sample was obtained. The average proportions of bases with Q20 and Q30 quality values were respectively 96.68 % and 91.11 % (Supplementary Table 3). Subsequently, the total reads, excluding ribosomal RNA, were aligned against the chicken reference genome (Gallus gallus). The average proportion of mapped reads reached 87.51 %, with more than 80 % of these reads aligning to the exon region (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Figure 1). Transcripts were reconstructed using StringTie and RSEM software and quantified at the gene level. In total, 17,596 genes were identified, including 17,007 known genes and 589 novel genes. PCA revealed distinct transcriptional differences among the four groups (Fig. 2A). DEGs were screened using the criteria FDR < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 1. Volcano plots and heatmaps demonstrated significant differences in DEG expression patterns between the control group and each treatment group. Additionally, as the duration of hypoxia treatment increased, both the numbers of upregulated and downregulated DEGs rose. Compared with the HYP0 group, 295 upregulated DEGs and 123 downregulated DEGs were identified at 12 h of hypoxia treatment, 960 upregulated DEGs and 502 downregulated DEGs at 24 h, and 1,493 upregulated DEGs and 1,177 downregulated DEGs at 48 h Supplementary Table 5; Fig. 2B-H).

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

To investigate the primary biological functions associated with DEGs, we performed a GO term enrichment analysis (Supplementary Table 6). In the HYP12 group, 342 significantly enriched GO terms (P < 0.01) were identified, comprising 278 biological process (BP) terms, 43 molecular function (MF) terms, and 21 cellular component (CC) terms. Similarly, the HYP24 group exhibited 546 significantly enriched GO terms (P < 0.01), including 445 BP terms, 63 MF terms, and 38 CC terms. The HYP48 group showed 639 significantly enriched GO terms (P < 0.01), consisting of 537 BP terms, 66 MF terms, and 36 CC terms. Across all treatment groups, the distribution of enriched GO terms was comparable, with significant enrichment predominantly observed in Biological Process categories such as Cellular process, Biological regulation, Regulation of biological process, Metabolic process, and Response to stimulus. Moreover, DEGs were notably enriched in the Binding and Catalytic activity subcategories under Molecular Function, as well as in Cellular anatomical entity and Protein-containing complex within the Cellular Component category (Fig. 3A).

KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on both up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs across the three groups to examine dynamic changes in signaling pathways over time (Supplementary Table 7). The up-regulated DEGs were significantly enriched in 17, 26, and 33 pathways at the three respective time points. The enrichment levels of up-regulated DEGs in pathways such as Focal Adhesion, MAPK signaling pathway, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, ECM-receptor interaction, Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, and Linoleic acid metabolism increased progressively with prolonged hypoxia treatment (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the up-regulated DEGs were significantly enriched in the Lysosome signaling pathway after 12 hours of hypoxia exposure, although this enrichment was not observed at the later time points. For the down-regulated DEGs, significant enrichment was detected in 13, 16, and 28 pathways at the three respective time points. After 12 hours of hypoxia treatment, these DEGs were predominantly enriched in the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway and Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction signaling pathway. In contrast, after 48 h of hypoxia treatment, the down-regulated DEGs were notably enriched in Metabolic pathways, Steroid biosynthesis, and Valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation signaling pathways (Fig. 3C).

The GSEA results provided complementary insights to the KEGG enrichment analysis. Upon 12 h of hypoxic treatment, the Lysosome pathway was significantly upregulated (Fig. 4A, G). After 24 h of hypoxic exposure, Linoleic acid metabolism, MAPK signaling pathway, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and ECM-receptor interaction were all significantly upregulated (Fig. 4B-F, H-K). Following 48 h of hypoxic treatment, Focal Adhesion, MAPK signaling pathway, Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, ECM-receptor interaction, and Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction were significantly upregulated, while Steroid biosynthesis was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5).

Analysis of protein-protein interaction networks

In order to investigate the functional modules and hub genes in transcriptome sequencing, we performed PPI network analyses among the DEGs identified in the three treatment groups (Supplementary Table 8). A total of 418 DEGs encoding proteins were selected by the HYP12 group for network construction and hub-gene identification. This network consisted of 180 nodes (genes) and 349 edges (interactions between genes). IL-1β and MMP9 occupied central positions with the highest connectivity (Fig. 6A). Through MCC topology analysis, we identified the top 10 genes as the hub genes of this network and constructed a sub-network graph based on their interaction relationships. These hub genes included members of the CC chemokine family (CCL4 and CCL5), the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family (CCR2, CXCR1, and CXCR4), as well as IL-1β, IL8L2, SRC, and HIF-1α (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Macrophages orchestrate tissue repair by secreting inflammatory mediators to modulate inflammation and by cytokines that directly or indirectly promote repair (Yasutaka and Ruslan, 2016). Previous studies have demonstrated that inadequate oxygen supply is a critical factor in WB myopathy, and multiple lines of evidence indicate that hypoxia significantly influences macrophages function (Raggi et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024a; Sun et al., 2025). Therefore, we established an in vitro hypoxic macrophage model and performed time-series transcriptome sequencing of HD11 cells to characterize their dynamic transcriptional responses to hypoxia.

Sequencing revealed 418, 1,462 and 2,670 DEGs in HD11 cells after 12 h, 24 h and 48 h of hypoxia, respectively. RNA sequencing identified 418, 1,462, and 2,670 DEGs in HD11 cells after 12 h, 24 h, and 48 hours of hypoxia, which demonstrates a time-dependent increase in transcriptional alterations. At 12 h of hypoxia, DEGs in HD11 cells were enriched in cytokine-related molecular functions, reflecting acute stress and early immune responses dominated by cytokine secretion. Lysosomes are cellular organelles responsible for intracellular catabolism and can eliminate extracellular metabolic waste, pathogens, and cellular debris through phagocytosis (Gros and Muller, 2023). Lysosome biogenesis in macrophages is essential for tissue homeostasis as well as antigen processing and presentation (Liu et al., 2024). The upregulation of the lysosomal pathway following 12 h of hypoxia suggests a heightened demand for cellular waste clearance. This is likely driven by the accumulation of metabolic byproducts under low oxygen conditions, which necessitates enhanced lysosomal activity. The exertion of macrophage phagocytic function will triggers reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and mitochondrial damage. This initiates sustained mitophagy and lysosome activation, which ultimately leads to lysosome destruction and promotes inflammation (Yin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Consistent with this, in this study, as the duration of hypoxia increased to 24 h and 48 h, more complex immune response regulatory mechanisms in HD11 cells replaced the initial lysosomal signaling pathway. This shift is characterized by the enrichment of DEGs in biological processes related to immune response, as well as the activation of an increasing number of immune-related signaling pathways. The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway is one of the important pathways significantly enriched by the up-regulated DEGs in this study. It contains three classical branches: extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and p38 MAPK (Wang et al., 2019). The activation of ERK pathway has been shown to promote RAW264.7 macrophages proliferation (Zhang et al., 2017). This study found that during the rearrangement period, genes within the ERK pathway or upstream regulators, such as receptor tyrosine kinase (RET), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 8 (MAP3K8), and Ras guanine nucleotide exchange factor 1 (RASGRF1), were all significantly upregulated. Additionally, cell cycle-regulating genes such as Hras (HRAS) were also upregulated, which is consistent with the experimental finding that hypoxia enhances the proliferative activity of HD11 cells. The significant down-regulation observed in the G1 phase and the corresponding up-regulation detected in the S phase further support the conclusion proposed by Lavoie et al. (2020), which states that ERK signaling in proliferating cells primarily facilitates cell proliferation by regulating the transition from the G1 to S phase of the cell cycle. The JNK and p38 MAPK pathways are key branches of the MAPK signaling family, primarily responsible for cellular responding to environmental stimuli. Their activation can promote pyroptosis via the NOD-like receptor family, pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response (Li et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Additionally, MAPK can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway by phosphorylating the inhibitor of κB (IκB) protein, which is a key signaling pathway in the inflammatory response. This study found that the expression of two key members of the NF-κB family—NF-κB1 and NF-κB2—was significantly upregulated in macrophages following hypoxia treatment for 24 and 48 h.

As hypoxia exposure was prolonged, the disruption of biosynthetic and metabolic functions in HD11 cells progressively worsened, as evidenced by the enrichment of down-regulated DEGs in progressively more metabolic pathways. Metabolic reprogramming in HD11 cells plays a critical role in regulating their cellular functions. Following 48 hours of hypoxia treatment, the downregulated DEGs in HD11 cells were significantly enriched in the glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism pathway. The marked suppression of this pathway may impair the normal functioning of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. In addition, key glycolytic genes—including lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), lactate dehydrogenase D (LDHD), phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1), triosephosphate isomerase 1 (TPI1) and GPI—were consistently up-regulated in HD11 cells at all three hypoxic time points. The above results are consistent with those previously obtained in mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages exposed to a 1 % oxygen environment (Sun et al., 2025), possibly indicating a shift of the cells towards a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Reduced glutathione (GSH) is a critical intracellular antioxidant that plays a key role in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining redox homeostasis within cells (Gaucher et al., 2018). The depletion of GSH impairs the cellular antioxidant system, thereby compromising the redox balance and triggering elevated oxidative stress (Kumar et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2023). Nicotinic acid and nicotinamide are precursors for coenzyme I (NAD⁺) and coenzyme II (NADP⁺), respectively. Declining NAD⁺ compromises mitochondrial respiration, curtailing ATP production and precipitating cellular energy stress. In neural and vascular tissues, NAD⁺ deficiency exacerbates oxidative damage and inflammatory responses (Lautrup et al., 2019; Qiu et al., 2023). NADPH provides the reducing equivalents required by glutathione reductase to convert GSSG back to GSH, thereby maintaining intracellular redox balance (Koju et al., 2022). We observed that glutathione metabolism and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism—two pathways associated with cellular antioxidant capacity—were significantly downregulated in macrophages following 48 h of hypoxia treatment. In contrast, no significant enrichment of these pathways was detected after 12 h or 24 h of hypoxia exposure. These findings indicate that prolonged hypoxia may lead to increased cellular oxidative stress over time, potentially contributing to the onset of chronic inflammatory responses.

The CC chemokine family is the largest subfamily of chemokines and directs immune-cell trafficking to inflammatory foci via cognate receptor binding (Ciechanowska and Mika, 2024; Guo et al., 2024). CXC chemokine receptors, which belong to the GPCR superfamily, bind CXC chemokines to orchestrate immune responses, cell migration and tissue repair (Baggiolini et al., 1989). In this study, we observed marked up-regulation of core CC-chemokine ligands and CXC-chemokine receptors. These results are consistent with the findings of Zhou et al. (2023), who reported elevated C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 3 (CXCL3), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CXCL5), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) and C—C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) under hypoxia. This suggests hypoxia may enhance the immune function of macrophages by promoting the recruitment of other immune cells.

Fibrosis is a pathological process characterized by excessive deposition and abnormal remodeling of ECM components. Matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9 degrade ECM proteins such as laminin, collagen and fibronectin, thereby playing crucial roles in ECM remodeling (Corbel et al., 2002; Zhang et al., 2024b). The tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases (TIMP) family is a group of proteins that inhibit the activity of MMPs and regulate ECM remodeling by modulating MMP expression levels (Alameddine and Morgan, 2016). In this study, MMP9 was identified as a core gene at both 12 h and 24 h of hypoxia, but the expression of MMP2 was down-regulated. Our findings align with those from a microglial hypoxia model (Patnaik et al., 2021). Although MMP2 and MMP9 are both gelatinases, their transcriptional responses to hypoxia are divergent, indicating distinct underlying regulatory mechanisms. In addition, the gene expression level of TIMP3, a member of the TIMP family, was elevated. These data indicate an MMP/TIMP imbalance in hypoxic HD11 cells that may promote fibrosis. The elevated expression of MMPs and TIMPs observed in WB myopathy by Xing et al. (2021) supports this hypothesis. The integrin family comprises transmembrane receptors formed by non-covalent α/β heterodimers, which are key mediators of cell–microenvironment interactions (Hynes, 2002). They mediate the recognition and adhesion of cells to ECM components such as fibronectin, laminin, and collagen, and modulate intracellular signalling via Focal Adhesion Kinase (FAK), Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase (PI3K) and MAPK pathways, thereby coordinating migration, proliferation, differentiation and survival (Pang et al., 2023; Chastney et al., 2025). Studies show that integrin subunit alpha 5(ITGA5), integrin subunit beta 2 (ITGB2) and integrin subunit beta 5 (ITGB5) act as key receptors of ECM receptors to trigger FAK phosphorylation, thereby promoting fibroblast phenotypic transition, endothelial cell migration and enhanced proliferation (Xu et al., 2021). This study found that the key TGF-β signaling receptors, TGFβR1 and TGFβR2, were upregulated. Members of the integrin family, including integrin subunit α3 (ITGA3), integrin subunit α8 (ITGA8), integrin subunit β3 (ITGB3), and integrin subunit β8 (ITGB8), as well as fibroblast growth factor 14 (FGF14) and fibroblast growth factor 22 (FGF22) in the FGF family, were all significantly upregulated. These results are consistent with the upregulation of the ECM-receptor interaction and focal adhesion signaling pathways identified by KEGG analysis and the enrichment of ECM-related functional changes in GO analysis.

Besides, we observed that the transcriptional levels of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and prolyl 4-hydroxylase beta subunit (P4HB) were significantly elevated after 24 and 48 hours of hypoxia treatment, which are both target genes of HIF-1α. VEGFA induces angiogenesis by binding to its receptors VEGFR1/2, thereby promoting endothelial proliferation, migration, and survival (Liu et al., 2023). Under hypoxia, VEGFA expression is markedly upregulated, which enhances angiogenesis to help meet tissue oxygen demands (Ni et al., 2022). Collagen synthesis requires the enzymatic conversion of proline to 4-hydroxyproline by prolyl 4-hydroxylase, a process in which P4HB serves as the essential β subunit and stabilizes the collagen structure (Hazari et al., 2023). Moreover, studies have demonstrated that under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α enhances collagen synthesis by upregulating P4HB expression, thereby aggravating the progression of fibrosis (Triantafyllou et al., 2019).

Our in vitro HD11 hypoxia model revealed that transcriptomic alterations in macrophages under varying hypoxic durations were primarily associated with immune response, biosynthesis and metabolism, and pro-fibrotic function. Elucidating how these changes drive the secretion of specific cytokines and contribute to WB fibrosis represents the primary objective of our subsequent investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we established an in vitro hypoxia model using chicken macrophages HD11 cells and found that hypoxia enhanced HD11 cell proliferation while inhibiting migration. Through high-throughput sequencing, we analyzed transcriptome changes in HD11 cells over increasing hypoxia duration. Initially, HD11 cells responded via cytokine secretion and lysosomal pathways to address acute hypoxia. As hypoxia duration increased, immune regulatory and signal transduction pathways (e.g., MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathway) activated, driving immune responses and adaption. Upregulated glycolytic pathways and downregulated antioxidant pathways exacerbated oxidative stress, promoting chronic inflammation. We also observed increased expression of TGF-β signaling pathway-related genes, ITG and FGF family genes, along with ECM-related pathways and altered cytoskeletal/membrane protein functions. These results suggest ECM remodeling in HD11 cells under hypoxia, potentially contributing to fibrosis. Future research could explore the functional impacts of these changes and develop therapeutic strategies to counteract hypoxia-induced fibrosis.
