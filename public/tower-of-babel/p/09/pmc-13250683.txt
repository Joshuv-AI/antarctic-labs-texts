Artificial intelligence-enabled histological analysis in pre-clinical respiratory disease models: a scoping review
Authors: Eva Kuhar, Junsoo Park, Forough Jahandideh, Majid Komeili, Ahmed Sadeknury, Navneet Kang, Prarthna Karunamurthy, Mohammad Reza Zarei, Amir Ebrahimi, Sean E. Gill, Patricia C. Liaw, Dean A. Fergusson, Duncan J. Stewart, Arvind Mer, Manoj M. Lalu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Histological analysis is a cornerstone of pre-clinical respiratory disease research. It enables assessment of pathology, therapeutic effects, and mechanisms. However, conventional approaches rely on manual scoring, which is subjective, time-consuming, and difficult to scale. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, offers potential to automate histology workflows. To date, its use in pre-clinical respiratory models has not been synthesised.We conducted a scoping review following the Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines. We searched MEDLINE and Embase (inception – January 2025) for pre-clinical studies using AI to analyse histology in respiratory disease models. Screening, full-text review, and data extraction were performed in duplicate.Of 6271 studies screened, 29 met inclusion criteria. Most used murine models (76%) and investigated lung cancer (28%), pulmonary fibrosis (24%), or tuberculosis (17%). Haematoxylin and eosin was the most common stain (48%), with others targeting collagen or immune markers. AI tasks included image classification (n=20), segmentation (n=10), and object detection (n=4), predominantly using convolutional neural networks (69%). Pre-processing methods (e.g. stain normalisation) were common, but annotation and training practices were inconsistently reported. AI model performance was generally high (accuracy ≥90%; seven studies); however, validation metrics varied, and external validation was absent. Most studies used “black box” models, with minimal application of explainability techniques. Reproducibility measures, such as sharing datasets or code were rarely reported.AI tools are poised to transform histological analysis in pre-clinical respiratory research. The field will be able to further harness AI to automate pre-clinical respiratory histological analysis by addressing gaps that we have identified in validation, transparency, and standardisation.

Introduction

In pre-clinical respiratory disease research, histological analysis is most commonly used to investigate structural abnormalities, inflammation and fibrosis in animal models [1, 2]. However, scoring histological features in pre-clinical animal models continues to be a significant challenge. These assessments are semiquantitative, time-intensive and prone to intersubject variability [3–5]. These limitations can slow down the evaluation of novel therapies and the translation of pre-clinical findings into the clinical setting.

Recent work is beginning to demonstrate how artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) tools can streamline many of the repetitive, time-consuming tasks that still dominate histological analysis in respiratory diseases [6–8]. AI-powered algorithms, particularly deep-learning approaches, demonstrate the potential to enable objective and high-throughput quantification of histological patterns. In pre-clinical research, AI and specifically convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have shown the potential to automate histological scoring of fibrotic lung disease in mice [9], detecting tumorous lesions on whole-slide images [10], and classifying tumour phenotypes [11]. These tools could improve the efficiency of histological scoring while also reducing the inherent variability in typical, human-led, manual assessments. Even with these promising preliminary results, some essential challenges remain, including the need for large and diverse histology datasets, as well as validation across distinct disease severities and animal models. Moreover, AI applications in respiratory disease research are constantly evolving, with novel technologies and methodologies being developed at an accelerated pace. Therefore, a synthesis of AI and machine learning is needed to inform the next steps in developing these technologies for pre-clinical respiratory histological analysis.

This scoping review consolidates the latest applications of AI-enabled histological analysis in pre-clinical respiratory disease models. We focus on animal studies since they allow researchers to control experimental conditions, decide precise timing for sample collection, and track disease progression and treatment effects at multiple time points. This level of control is rarely feasible in clinical research, where tissue is often only available during surgery, biopsy, or autopsy. Indeed, histological analysis remains the gold standard outcome measure for many pre-clinical respiratory disease models [12, 13], giving us a direct view of disease pathogenesis, therapeutic efficacy, and underlying mechanisms. By evaluating all AI applications to date, we can identify benefits, limitations, and critical knowledge gaps. This will help inform the development of future AI tools for pre-clinical respiratory research.

Methods

Protocol and registration

We conducted this scoping review in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines [14, 15] and with the methodological framework initially proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [16] and later updated by Levac et al. [17]. This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (a completed checklist is provided in the supplementary material S1). The protocol can be found at Open Science Framework [18].

Identifying the research question

Our research question was: “What AI/ML tools exist to assess histological features in pre-clinical models of respiratory disease?” We aimed to comprehensively synthesise pre-clinical studies that apply AI/ML methods to lung histology in animal models of respiratory disease.

Information sources and literature search

We developed a systematic search strategy (supplementary material S2) in collaboration with an information specialist (Risa Shorr, The Ottawa Hospital Learning Services, Ottawa, ON, Canada). Our search strategy was applied to PubMed (MEDLINE) and Embase (search last updated on 20 January 2025), and no date restrictions were applied to be as comprehensive as possible. Keywords used in our search included “respiratory tract disease”, “artificial intelligence”, and other relevant terms related to the disease, methodology and technology used in machine learning. For feasibility, we restricted inclusion to peer-reviewed manuscripts of pre-clinical primary studies. No language restrictions were applied; studies published in languages other than English were translated using DeepL to facilitate screening and eligibility assessment.

Eligibility criteria

We included experimental studies investigating the use of an AI/ML tool to assess histological evidence of lung pathology in animal models of human respiratory diseases. To ensure clarity in study selection, AI was defined as the study of intelligent systems that perform tasks typically requiring human intelligence [19]. Machine learning, which is a subfield of AI, refers to approaches in which intelligence emerges from learning patterns in annotated data [20]. We included all species and pre-clinical respiratory disease models. Algorithmic methods were included if they demonstrated sufficient learning or reasoning capabilities (i.e. characteristics of AI). For this review, pathohistological features were defined as histological structures that differentiate between healthy and diseased lung tissue. Studies were excluded if they 1) did not use AI/ML specifically for histopathological assessment, 2) primarily focused on nonhistological imaging modalities (e.g. radiography, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging) or 3) used histopathological analysis solely as a validation tool for another primary assessment method.

Study screening and selection

All citations retrieved through the search were uploaded into DistillerSR (Evidence Partners, Ottawa, ON, Canada), a cloud-based, audit-ready software that streamlines scoping review workflows, including title and abstract screening, full-text review, and duplicate removal. Two independent reviewers screened articles in duplicate by titles/abstracts and then by full text. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or by a senior author, if needed.

A standardised data extraction form was developed to systematically collect information on study characteristics, methodologies, AI-based tools, and results. Two independent reviewers extracted data in duplicate. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by a senior author, if needed. Data analysis and reporting

Extracted data were synthesised using descriptive statistics and qualitative synthesis. Study and animal model characteristics and categorical data were summarised in tabular format. For AI model performance, reported accuracy metrics were compared with human expert assessments where available. Qualitative findings, including study conclusions and limitations, were analysed to identify common patterns across studies. Figures and tables were generated to present key findings. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3, Windows UCRT build) using the packages tidyverse (2.0.0), readxl (1.4.5), circlize (0.4.16) and png (0.1.8).

Reporting assessment

To assess the reporting quality of the included studies, we applied the Must AI Criteria-10 (MAIC-10) checklist [21]. MAIC-10 consists of 10 criteria relevant to AI research in medical imaging including clinical need, study design, safety and privacy, data curation, data annotation, data partitioning, AI model description, robustness, explainability and transparency. As MAIC-10 was originally developed using clinically oriented terminology, we used pre-clinical-appropriate definitions. Specifically, “clinical need” was operationalised as pre-clinical need, and “safety and privacy” was evaluated by reporting of an ethical statement for animal use. For each study we recorded whether each criterion was explicitly reported. The proportion of studies meeting each MAIC-10 criterion was summarised descriptively.

Results

Overview of included studies

Our systematic search identified 8026 records, with 6430 unique records screened, and 29 studies meeting the inclusion criteria (supplementary figure S1). These studies were published between 2008 and 2025. 59% (n=17) of studies were published in the past 5 years.

The most frequently investigated respiratory conditions were lung cancer (n=8, 28%), pulmonary fibrosis (n=7, 24%) and tuberculosis (n=5, 17%) (table 1). Pulmonary haemorrhage (n=3, 10%,) and acute lung injury models were also investigated, with the latter specifically addressing severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) (n=2, 7%) and bacterial pneumonia (n=1, 3%). Other conditions studied included COPD (n=2, 7%), and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) (n=1, 3%; table 1, figure 1a).

Murine models were predominantly used (n=22, 76%, table 1), with commonly cited strains including C57BL/6, BALB/c, C3HeB/FeJ, diversity outbred mice, and A/J. Equine models comprised 10% (n=3) of the studies, all of which investigated pulmonary haemorrhage. Individual studies also used hamster (SARS-CoV-2), sheep (lung cancer), and rat (pulmonary fibrosis) models (each 3%; table 1, figure 1b).

Staining and imaging modalities

Haematoxylin and eosin was the most frequently used staining method (n=14, 48%; supplementary tables S1 and S2), while the remaining studies (n=15, 52%) employed stains tailored to particular histological features. Collagen was assessed using Masson's trichrome (n=5), picrosirius red (n=1), and second harmonic generation microscopy (a stain-free optical imaging method; n=1). Prussian blue and modified Turnbull's blue were used in three studies to quantify haemosiderin as a surrogate for pulmonary haemorrhage. Other specialised staining approaches included RNA in situ hybridisation targeting viral RNA and immune cell markers (such as TTF1, IBA1, MX1 (n=2)); modified Ziehl–Neelsen for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (carbol fuchsin, n=1), and modified Gill's stain for measuring chord length (n=1).

Digitisation of stained slides was performed using high-resolution, whole-slide imaging systems. Frequently reported scanners included Aperio ScanScope (Leica), NanoZoomer (Hamamatsu), and Zeiss Axioscan (supplementary tables S1 and S2). Image acquisition parameters were chosen based on downstream analytical needs. Lower magnifications (≤10×) provided broader architectural context, while higher magnifications (20× and 40×) were most often used for the complex classification and segmentation required for AI modelling. Seven studies utilised ultra-high magnification (≥200×), especially for tasks needing high cellular resolution, such as immunophenotyping [22, 41, 44].

Histological features and scoring systems

The histological features selected for AI-based evaluation varied according to the respiratory disease model and the primary histological focus (figure 1c). The most common applications included quantifying fibrotic lesions and collagen deposition (n=8, 28%), tumour detection or grading (n=6, 21%), alveolar architectural changes and emphysema severity (n=4, 14%), granuloma formation and necrosis in tuberculosis (n=4, 14%), immune cell infiltration (n=3, 10%), and hemosiderin accumulation or tissue damage metrics (n=2, 7%).

A variety of histological scoring strategies were used. Many studies (n=17, 59%) relied on direct feature detection or classification rather than established formal scoring systems (figure 1d). The majority of tumour-focused studies (n=5) used feature detection approaches. Formal grading systems were applied only in one study. Similar approaches (i.e. AI-based feature extraction over traditional scoring) were used to assess emphysema, alveolar structure, inflammatory cell assessment, and collagen or lung structure quantification. In the studies that did use formal scoring systems, the Ashcroft scale for fibrosis severity and composite lung injury scores were most commonly reported (n=4 each), followed by the total hemosiderin score in pulmonary haemorrhage studies (n=3).

Image processing workflows

Most studies (83%, n=24) implemented at least one standardised image pre-processing strategy. These were used to reduce variability from staining, illumination and imaging differences. The most common approach included artefact removal and exclusion of nondiagnostic tissue regions (i.e. to ensure analysis focused on biologically relevant areas (n=22)). Dataset augmentation through geometric or colour transformations was used in 19 (66%) studies to increase sample diversity and enhance model robustness. Stain normalisation methods (e.g. Macenko algorithm) were applied in 13 (35%) studies to minimise colour inconsistencies from sample preparation and scanning variations. Image contrast enhancement was also used in 13 studies to improve the identification of histological features and boundaries. Patch-based tiling was implemented in 13 studies. This approach, which divides whole slides into smaller tiles, enabled efficient gigapixel image processing. Patch sizes and overlap parameters were typically optimised based on model architectures and resolution requirements.

Technical parameters such as section thickness and scanner resolution were reported in seven studies, and they influenced tissue appearance and downstream analytic performance. For instance, variations in section thickness complicated image registration and alignment of histology with complementary methods (e.g. micro-computed tomography) [44].

Four studies used image processing pipelines without modern AI. These relied on manually engineered features or threshold-based rules. Additionally, two studies used hybrid workflows combining traditional processing with machine learning through manual or semi-automated region selection [22, 41].

AI task applications

Across the 29 included studies, we identified 34 distinct AI task applications, with several studies implementing multiple task types. The most common applications were image classification (n=20), image segmentation (n=10), and object detection (n=4) (figure 2a, supplementary figure S2). Image classification was broadly adopted across disease models, including fibrosis (n=6), cancer (n=5), tuberculosis (n=4), SARS-CoV-2 (n=1), pneumonia (n=1), BPD (n=1), pulmonary haemorrhage (n=1), and COPD (n=1) (figure 2a). Image segmentation (automated partitioning of images into distinct anatomical regions) was applied in cancer (n=3), fibrosis (n=2), COPD (n=2), SARS-CoV-2 (n=1), tuberculosis (n=1), and BPD (n=1). This approach enables more precise measurement of tissue structure, lesion boundaries, and cellular infiltration. Object detection (automated identification and localisation of discrete features) was primarily used for pulmonary haemorrhage (n=3), for identifying haemosiderin-containing macrophages in lung sections, and in one pneumonia study that quantified neutrophils and total counts of cells per area.

AI technologies, learning approaches and implementation

Diverse AI approaches were categorised by their learning mechanism (i.e. data-driven versus not) and model architecture. Data-driven models learn statistical relationships between pixel values and labels during training, whereas rule-based methods rely on predefined image-processing rules and do not require model training.

Most studies (n=27, 93%) employed data-driven learning approaches, primarily deep learning (n=20, 69%), which learn hierarchical features directly from raw images and reduce the need for handcrafted feature engineering. CNNs were used for image classification and feature extraction, with specialised architectures (e.g. Inception V3) [49] used to distinguish tissue regions such as tumours, necrosis, or fibrosis. U-Net [50] and DeepLabV3+ [51] were employed for pixel-level segmentation tasks that enabled identification of pathological regions [32, 47]. In some studies, AI-driven segmentation was followed by morphometric analysis, such as U-Net-based exclusion of nonalveolar structures for chord length measurement [34].

Object detection, such as RetinaNet (7%, n=2), extended beyond simple classification by automating localisation and quantification of features (e.g. metastatic foci, haemosiderin-laden macrophages). Attention-based models (including multiple instance learning frameworks) were used in two studies for whole-slide analysis, enabling the identification of regions that contribute most significantly to a diagnostic label. Other neural networks included radial basis function models (n=4, 14%) and Bayesian models (n=2, 7%) for tasks where pixel-level detail labels were unavailable.

Traditional machine learning methods were applied in seven studies. This requires domain experts to define specific measurable features (e.g. cell morphometry, texture descriptors, colour) from histological images, after which algorithms learn to make diagnostic decisions based on these pre-engineered features. In one study, support vector machines were integrated into multistep analysis workflows (e.g. initial segmentation of granulomas followed by support vector machine-assisted classification of necrotic regions) [41]. Two (7%) studies implemented rule-based algorithms without any data-driven learning component that relied on manually programmed instructions for pixel-level classification or feature extraction.

Approaches to AI implementation varied across studies. Several studies (31%, n=9) leveraged existing tools for feature extraction, segmentation, or classification rather than developing new algorithms (e.g. Orbit Image Analysis, Lesion Image Recognition and Analysis, HALO). In contrast, a smaller number of studies (10%, n=3) developed custom multistage AI pipelines. These pipelines combined pre-trained models with classical image processing steps, which were tailored to specific pre-clinical lung histopathology applications.

Annotation practices and slide volume

Annotation methodologies varied substantially across the studies. Manual annotation was most common (n=18, 62%) for creating pixel-level segmentations, bounding boxes, or tissue/region-level labels. Of these, 12 studies relied on a single annotator, most commonly a pathologist (n=7), followed by experienced staff or PhD students (n=3), while two studies did not report qualifications. The remaining six studies used multiple annotators, often with varying expertise, to assess inter-annotator variability.

Semi-automated annotation with human validation was employed in six (21%) studies. Three (10%) studies used fully automated systems or synthetic data without manual labelling; in two of these, pathologists selected relevant features for the model input but did not annotate the slides. Two studies did not provide a description of their annotation processes at all.

Most studies (n=22, 76%) reported the number of annotated whole-slide images, which is a proxy for dataset diversity, ranging from 10 to 464 (median 69). Reporting of annotated regions (often stated as “tiles” or “patches”), which indicates the extent of model training data, was inconsistent. Two studies provided exact tile counts in the hundreds (e.g. 324 tiles from six mice), and three in the tens of thousands (e.g. 35 115 tiles). Four studies used less precise descriptors (e.g. “thousands of micro-tiles”, “multiple fields per whole slide image”). The majority of studies (n=20) did not specify tile or patch counts. This variability limits direct comparison between datasets and may hinder assessments of generalisability and reproducibility.

Human integration and explainability

Seven studies reported structured integration of AI into human workflows (supplementary table S4). Of these studies, one described a formal human-in-the-loop approach, while the remaining six did not report operational details. Among the included studies, only one implemented an explainability framework to support the interpretation of the AI model.

Validation strategies

Most studies employed internal validation strategies (n=24, 83%). These included dividing available slides into training and testing sets and using cross-validation where subsets of the same data were used to evaluate the model in rotation. Notably, no studies reported external validation with fully independent datasets.

Overall performance

AI models demonstrated promising performance across a diverse range of tasks (table 2, figure 2b), with 79% (n=23) reporting at least one validation metric. Most studies reported a single outcome only (n=20, 69%) (e.g. accuracy for classification or Dice similarity for segmentation), while omitting complementary measures (e.g. sensitivity, specificity, confusion matrices) or more comprehensive metrics (e.g. F1 score, area under the curve). Performance varied by task complexity. For example, simple tasks (e.g. binary classification to distinguish tumour-positive and -negative regions) achieved the highest accuracy (95–99%). Performance was particularly high for these tasks when models were trained with supervised learning using well-annotated datasets. In contrast, more complex tasks demonstrated lower performance (e.g. 73–75% accuracy for multilabel classification, spatially resolved cell population analysis, cross-species prediction). Morphological overlap, cellular heterogeneity, and interspecies variability were reported as challenges that contributed to this lower performance.

Accuracy was the most frequently reported performance metric (n=12, 41%), particularly for classification and binary detection tasks (table 2, supplementary table S3). Seven studies reported accuracy >90%. Four studies extended metrics beyond accuracy. Among these studies, segmentation tasks demonstrated robust performance (e.g. Dice similarity coefficients typically >0.85 and reaching 0.98). Other spatial and probabilistic metrics were reported less frequently (e.g. Hausdorff distance [34], area under the curve [47] and mean average precision [31]). For continuous outcomes (e.g. iron content estimation), Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between AI and expert assessments were frequently >0.90.

A subset of studies reported interrater reliability for subjective or semi-structured scoring tasks. Reported metrics included Cohen's κ (0.782), Fleiss's κ (0.84) and Krippendorff's α (0.796 without AI, which increased to 0.910 with AI assistance) [29, 34, 42]. In the latter, agreement between pathologists improved from 87% to 94% with AI support (i.e. reduced interpretive variability).

Reported limitations

Across studies, three categories of limitations were identified (supplementary table S5). The first category related to technical challenges. These included challenges in detecting fine-grained histological features (n=7, 24%) and variability associated with staining quality (n=4, 14%). Additional technical factors reported included pre-analytic aspects such as tissue sectioning, slide thickness, scanner resolution, magnification and artefacts introduced during tissue processing. Several studies also noted resource-related considerations, such as computational costs (n=3) and the need for ongoing manual oversight (n=3). The second category related to data quality and annotation issues. Reported issues included inconsistent class assignment (n=5, 17%), absence of gold-standard annotations (n=3, 10%), variability among annotators, underrepresentation of specific classes, small dataset sizes, and the limited availability of publicly accessible datasets and code. The third category related to generalisability and interpretability. This included the lack of external validation, the use of single sections as proxies for whole-organ pathology, and the absence of model interpretability features.

Reproducibility practices and data sharing

12 (41%) studies reported using a code repository (e.g. GitHub), with six providing full access to relevant documentation. Most (n=22) used internal or proprietary datasets, and nine (31%) shared histological image datasets openly or with limited restrictions. Three studies used public datasets that could allow for cross-study comparison of models. None shared standardised protocols for dataset construction, annotation or reporting.

Reporting assessment (MAIC-10)

Overall, most MAIC-10 reporting criteria were addressed across the included studies (supplementary figure S3). Reporting of pre-clinical need was universal (100%), and study design as well as ethical statements for animal use were reported in most studies (both 86%). Core data-related criteria were also frequently reported, including data curation (93%) and data annotation (90%). In contrast, reporting of data partitioning strategies (e.g. training, validation and test splits) was less consistent, with 55% of studies providing this information. Description of the AI model was reported in 75% of studies, whereas robustness and transparency were reported in 66% and 65% of studies, respectively. As described earlier, one study implemented a formal explainability framework to support interpretation of AI outputs; however, explainability was reported in 48% of studies overall, most often through acknowledgement of the model's “black box” nature as a study limitation.

Discussion

Our review highlights the growing use of AI applications for histological analysis in pre-clinical respiratory research. Since human-led manual assessment can be labour-intensive and vulnerable to interobserver variability, implementation of AI could improve scalability and reproducibility of pre-clinical respiratory histology. However, in addition to these advantages, our scoping review identified several methodological and reporting gaps that must be resolved before more extensive adoption of AI methods in the pre-clinical setting.

Across the 29 included studies, substantial heterogeneity was observed in dataset diversity, disease models, staining protocols, and imaging parameters. This illustrates how rapidly the field is exploring AI applications across a wide spectrum of pre-clinical respiratory models. These applications could reliably identify morphologically distinct structures such as tumour regions and granulomas, especially when the histological workflows were carefully optimised (e.g. standardised staining, consistent section thickness, and uniform magnification). The majority of studies also improved model robustness by applying pre-processing strategies such as stain normalisation, patch-based tiling, and contrast enhancement before model training. In contrast, more subtle quantitative or semi-quantitative tasks such as fibrosis grading or inflammatory cell counts, proved far more challenging. Performance commonly declined when training datasets were limited in either size or diversity of disease severity.

Histological features were defined and quantified in different ways, with studies relying on binary annotations, unsupervised clustering, or direct measurement of features such as collagen density or nuclear area when training AI models. While these approaches make automation possible, they also reduce interpretability and make it hard to compare results between studies. The reporting of annotation and curation details were similarly uneven. Many papers did not describe their annotators expertise, whether a consensus process was used, or what quality control steps were taken, which hampers the confidence of ground truth labels. Performance metric use and reporting demonstrated similar inconsistencies. Accuracy and Dice coefficients were the most frequently reported performance measures, whereas metrics related to class imbalance, uncertainty, or failure modes appeared less often. Many studies also drew from datasets generated within a single laboratory, with comparatively few extending evaluation to independent data or alternative experimental conditions. These gaps are notable given how strongly histological readouts can be influenced by tissue handling, staining, imaging and design choices. These challenges are not new. The rapid advances in clinical digital pathology offer a clear blueprint for turning such principles into practice. In the clinic, AI has surged forward due to large, well-annotated public datasets such as CAMELYON, PANDA and ImageNet, which have enabled expert-level performance in tasks such as cancer diagnosis, subtyping and molecular profiling [53–55]. This success is the result of community efforts to make validation pipelines transparent, create consensus-based annotation practices, and address explainability. Landmark multicentre studies, such as those by Campanella et al. [56], have shown how interdisciplinary collaboration and rigour can accelerate the development of reliable AI models. Moreover, interpretability tools (e.g. attention maps) have helped evaluate performance and build end-user trust [57]. This focus on explainability is echoed across several methodological reviews and regulatory guidance documents, which stress that interpretable AI is a fundamental requirement for adoption in medical practice [58–61].

While this review outlines current AI applications in the histological image analysis of pre-clinical pulmonary disease models, it does have some limitations that should be noted. First, the search was limited to peer-reviewed publications, excluding grey literature such as preprints, and unpublished technical reports. We did not include grey literature, as the absence of formal peer review and limited reporting of methods makes it challenging to evaluate using the same criteria as peer-reviewed studies. Second, due to the rapid evolution of AI tools, recent advancements introduced after our literature search may not yet be included in this review.

The findings from this review outline both the achievements and shortcomings of current AI studies in pre-clinical histopathology. To move the field forward, researchers should focus on standardising AI methodologies and performance metrics, clearly reporting all protocols and annotation strategies, and assembling large, well-curated datasets. Additionally, performing rigorous validation that includes independent external data from multiple institutions and disease models will greatly improve AI model robustness and generalisability. In parallel, improving the explainability and interpretability of AI models will help build trust and support practical adoption in research. By addressing these challenges, AI has the potential to enable more efficient, objective, and generalisable histological assessment in pre-clinical studies.
