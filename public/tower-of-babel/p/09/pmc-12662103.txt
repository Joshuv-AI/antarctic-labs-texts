Emerging threat of antimicrobial resistance determinants and plasmid replicon types acquisition by Escherichia coli of poultry and other food-producing animal origin in China: local findings with global implications
Authors: Muhammad Shoaib, Muhammad Fazal Hameed, Amjad Islam Aqib, Weiwei Wang, Qing Wang, Shengyi Wang, Wanxia Pu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscherichia coli (E. coli) is a commensal and pathogenic bacterium responsible for harmless to severe infections in humans and food-producing animals inlcuding poultry. E. coli can significantly impact ecology and is a bioindicator of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) contamination. The global emergence of AMR is depleting the antimicrobial reserves for human use and highlights the need for antimicrobial stewardship. Generally, AMR emerges through the unjustified use of antibiotics in humans, food-producing animals, and agricultural settings. Animal species carry E. coli in their intestinal tract as a commensal organism; genetic flexibility and adaptability allow this bacterium to acquire diverse AMR determinants through selective pressure and horizontal gene transfer (HGT). HGT can be important in spreading the AMR determinants through the food chain and environmental exposure. Human exposure to this bacterium can occur through various sources, including meat contamination during animal slaughtering, animal waste, contamination of raw or processed animal milk, and the consumption of contaminated water, allowing E. coli carrying antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) to be transferred to humans. Animal waste can also be a potential contaminant of environmental sites and also facilitates the rapid dissemination of AMR determinants due to anthropogenic activities. There is an urgent need to establish proper guidelines for controlling the spread of AMR through E. coli from poultry and other food-producing animals to humans and the environment following the One Health approach. To meet this approach, potential knowledge about the recent AMR determinants acquired by E. coli and their dissemination drivers is needed. Therefore, this review concisely elaborates the E. coli epidemiology, phenotypic AMR, genotypic determinants acquired, and their dissemination driver.Keywords: Escherichia coli, Food-producing animals, Epidemiology, Antibiotic resistance genes, Plasmid replicon types

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a gram-negative, rod-shaped, and facultative anaerobic bacterium widely present in the natural environment. E. coli is a prevalent opportunistic and commensal bacterium in animals and humans, facilitating transmission between the hosts (Ramos et al., 2020). Moreover, E. coli is recognized as a significant foodborne enteric pathogen, causing severe human infections and impacting animal health (Qing et al., 2024). Besides this, the joint agricultural and environmental impact can be through the deposition of animal waste (Shoaib et al., 2023). Pathogenic E. coli causes infections in animals and humans through diverse virulent factors. It commonly infects dairy cows' mammary glands, leading to acute and localized mastitis (Cheng et al., 2019; Pakbin et al., 2021; Zaatout, 2022). Recently, E. coli outbreaks have been documented in multiple countries due to consumption of contaminated milk and other food products (Boyd et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2020b; Panel et al., 2020; Tack et al., 2021). Several studies reported that E. coli is responsible for a spectrum of intestinal and extra-intestinal infections in humans, including diarrhea, meningitis, peritonitis, sepsis, urinary tract infections, and pneumonia (Abdalla et al., 2022; Baloch, 2021; Sora et al., 2021). The classification of diarrheagenic E. coli differentiates into six pathotypes, each characterized by distinct pathogenic traits: enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), Shiga-toxin producing E. coli (STEC)/enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC), enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC), enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC), and diffusely-adherent E. coli (DAEC) (Fig. 1), responsible for intestinal infections (Abdalla et al., 2022). E. coli is a commensal bacterium of the gut in poultry, cattle, and pigs, which are commonly used for food production; the contamination of animal products by E. coli during slaughtering or other processes may present a potential risk for human transmission (Ramos et al., 2020). While meat-associated E. coli is mainly linked to EPEC and ETEC infections (Shen et al., 2022a). Another E. coli strain, extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC) originating from poultry has emerged and been linked to extra-intestinal human infections, including urinary tract infections (Borges et al., 2019; Nawaz et al., 2025). In humans, most ExPEC infections tend to be localized, such as simple urinary tract infections, while some can be fatal, such as bloodstream infections (Riley, 2020). E. coli cannot only adapt to various ecological niches but also exhibit resistance to multiple antimicrobials (Shoaib et al., 2024b). Inappropriate antimicrobial selection and unjustified use can facilitate the emergence of AMR in bacteria (Carvalho et al., 2019). Additionally, E. coli can exchange AMR genes through various mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as transposable elements (including transposons and insertion sequences) capable of moving to various DNA sites through excision and insertion mechanisms, integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) can move between the cells using conjugation machinery and capable of integrating into DNA through cite-specific recombination, phages/viruses, and conjugative plasmids can also move between cells via cell to cell contact using conjugation machinery (Khedkar et al., 2022). The dissemination of acquired AMR genes can extend to humans, animals, and the environment (Hao et al., 2024) via mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) such as transformation (no contact, through environment), conjugation (bacteria-bacteria contact), and transduction (virus-bacteria contact), with conjugation being the predominant means for the dissemination of AMR genes (Jian et al., 2021).

The classification of diarrheagenic E. coli differentiates into six pathotypes, each characterized by distinct pathogenic traits. Here, enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC), enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC), enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC), and diffuse adherent E. coli (DAEC). The accidental infection of APEC in humans may lead to Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) and neonatal meningitis E. coli (NMEC) infections.

The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) i.e. non-susceptible to at least one antimicrobial agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, extensively drug-resistant (XDR) i.e. non-susceptible to at least one antimicrobial agent in all but remain susceptible to two or fewer antimicrobial categories, and pandrug-resistant (PDR) i.e. non-susceptible to all antimicrobial agents in all antimicrobial categories bacteria can significantly undermine the treatment options in both human and animal settings (Magiorakos et al., 2012). Given the widespread use of antimicrobials in animal production, livestock-derived bacteria often resist a range of antimicrobials, including those commonly administered to humans (Wang et al., 2024). The epidemiology of AMR acquired by E. coli from food-producing animals is a crucial focal point in contemporary research and necessitates attention to devise appropriate action plans. While numerous researchers have documented AMR in Europe, a comprehensive assessment of the overall burden of AMR attributed to E. coli remains imperative in China. Consequently, this review focused on the epidemiology, encompassing phenotypic and genotypic AMR traits acquired by E. coli of poultry and other food-animal origin in China. Knowing the predominant AMR determinants in a particular geographical region not only improves the understanding of pathogen behavior but also enables the right decisions to deal with the growing AMR crisis.

Epidemiology and associated multidrug resistance of E. coli isolated from poultry and other food-producing animals

E. coli is a ubiquitous bacterium that can exist in various ecological niches, encompassing soil, food-producing animals, farmland, aquaculture, animal waste, contaminated food items, and underground water (Fig. 2a) (Shoaib et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2020). Research has extensively explored multiple facets of E. coli, including clinical epidemiology, potential sources, distribution, and global spread of drug resistance (Ghosh et al., 2019; Theobald et al., 2019). However, scant attention has been paid to the epidemiology and drug-resistant traits of E. coli from food-producing animals in China. The cornerstone of the global trade and Chinese food supply relies on food-producing animals, including poultry, cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats (Adesogan et al., 2020; Van Boeckel et al., 2015). E. coli prevalence has been noted at 18.5 % in mastitis-affected cow milk samples from various Chinese provinces, including Henan, Inner Mongolia, Beijing, Shandong, Guangdong, Liaoning, Hebei, and Heilongjiang (Cheng et al., 2019). Other studies noted 11.1 % from raw cow milk samples across Northeastern, Southern, Western, and Northern China (Yu et al., 2020) and 19 other provinces (Yang et al., 2018). Liu et al. (2021) isolated 34.4 % of E. coli strains from raw cow milk, while Su et al. (2016) noted a 13.8 % prevalence of E. coli from the milk of clinically and sub-clinically infected mastitis cows.

For swine, E. coli prevalence was reported to be 32.62 % in Shandong province (Wang et al., 2021) and 81.7 % in South China (Yuan et al., 2021). Meanwhile, commensal E. coli prevalence reached 83.6 % in food-producing animals (cattle, chicken, and pigs) (Huang et al., 2017) and 88.8 % in waterfowl (ducks, geese) in Hainan province (Zhang et al., 2021a). The wide distribution of E. coli across diverse animal species throughout China reveals varying prevalence linked to population, animal type, and detection methods. Furthermore, geographic boundaries and socioeconomic factors contribute to the varying epidemiology of E. coli (Qing et al., 2024). Foods of animal origin: Crucial components of the food pyramid and their role in AMR propagation

Food-producing animals occupy a pivotal position within the food pyramid, serving as fundamental sources of sustenance and nourishment for human populations. However, their significance extends beyond nutrition, encompassing a complex interplay with the escalating challenge of AMR (Kasimanickam et al., 2021). As vital contributors to the food supply chain, food animals exert a profound influence on both human health and the propagation of AMR. Animal food fulfills millions of people's protein needs worldwide (Council et al., 2015). In the stratification of the food pyramid, food-producing animals represent a cornerstone, supplying essential proteins and nutrients to meet the dietary requirements. Poultry, cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats, among others, contribute significantly to the availability of meat, dairy, and other essential food products (Adesogan et al., 2020; Van Boeckel et al., 2015). Poultry, a rapidly growing industry, is a vital source of lean protein, supplying meat and eggs to numerous countries, including China, the European Union, the USA, and Brazil, the largest poultry producers. The intensively reared poultry farms frequently use antimicrobials to control bacterial pathogens such as E. coli, Salmonella enterica, Campylobacter, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus spp (Abreu et al., 2023). Among the E. coli strains, avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC) exhibits high levels of AMR (Hedman et al., 2020) and high prevalence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing E. coli in poultry production is also increasing. A recent literature review conducted by Salman et al. (2024) reported 60.8 % ESBL-producing E. coli from chicken in Henan province (Yuan et al., 2009), 97.9 % in Northeast and South China (Tong et al., 2015), and 88.7 % in Shandong province (Li et al., 2016).

Dairy cattle, esteemed for contributing nutrient-rich milk offered to diverse age groups, have witnessed a surge in demand, reflecting the global proliferation of dairy farming, including China (Sagar et al., 2023). However, mastitis remains a critical challenge for dairy farmers, precipitating extensive antibiotic usage. Antibiotics in animal farming drive the emergence of MDR strains such as ESBL-producing E. coli, which can impact human health through antibiotic residues in milk and E. coli-contaminated meat at slaughterhouses (Oladeji et al., 2025). The swine industry similarly occupies a pivotal role in meeting the protein demands. China, the European Union, and the USA collectively account for half of the world's pig population (See, 2024). The escalated antibiotic use in pig farming intensifies the spectrum of AMR dissemination (Yuan et al., 2021). The surge in antimicrobial usage in food animal production directly correlates with intensified animal husbandry practices aimed at meeting escalating protein needs. This heightened reliance on antimicrobials has spawned a concurrent rise in AMR within food-producing animals, constituting a potential reservoir for amplifying and diffusing AMR within the human population (Van Boeckel et al., 2020). Recent reports from China noted increased prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli strains both in swine (80.8 %) and cattle (97.9 %) production systems (Yu et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2019).

The intricate web of the food chain interconnects all life forms within the environment. In this intricate network, AMR genes can traverse horizontally across consumers in the food pyramid. Various consumables such as vegetables, meat, milk, fruits, and processed foods serve as prolific sources of AMR bacteria. This phenomenon arises from the commonplace use of antibiotics in food-producing animals for infection prevention, treatment, and food preservation purposes (Sagar et al., 2023). Consuming these AMR bacteria-carrying foods can facilitate the horizontal transfer of AMR genes to consumers. Humans occupy a pivotal nexus within the food pyramid. Their interaction with AMR bacteria is twofold: it can occur through the food pyramid or direct infection by an AMR pathogen (Sagar et al., 2023). AMR genes may also spread to human populations via direct contact with bodily fluids harboring AMR pathogens, including blood, feces, and saliva (Samtiya et al., 2022). Nevertheless, contaminated edibles like milk, meat, eggs, seafood, and dairy products are principal sources of AMR gene dissemination (Ruiz and Alvarez-Ordóñez, 2017). Inadequate surveillance of antimicrobial resistance within the food chain, particularly in developing nations, imposes a safety hazard on farmers and food processors, who might inadvertently come into direct or indirect contact with potential AMR pathogens (Founou et al., 2016). To safeguard the entirety of the food chain, from farm to table, against the perils of antimicrobial resistance, vigilant monitoring becomes imperative. The vertical transfer of AMR genes from infected animals or humans to their offspring is another noteworthy avenue of transmission. For instance, a mother harboring AMR pathogens during pregnancy may transfer these genes to her children (Patangia et al., 2022). This vertical transmission underscores the need for comprehensive strategies to curtail AMR at its roots, ensuring the well-being of present and future generations.

The antibiotic resistance determinants carried by environmental E. coli can be transmitted across the food chain. This persistent presence constitutes a primary driver for disseminating AMR to humans, profoundly jeopardizing public health (Ben et al., 2019). Furthermore, agricultural soils, fertilizers, and wastewater have also been found as reservoirs for antibiotic-resistant bacteria carrying AMR genes (Lima et al., 2020). Alarmingly, the use of untreated animal and human waste as bio-fertilizers elevates the risk of horizontal transfer of AMR genes. The transmission of E. coli occurs through the fecal-oral route, including contaminated surfaces, edibles, underground water, interactions with soil, and direct human-animal contact (Lawal et al., 2022). While E. coli outbreaks in adults are infrequent, their causation can often be traced back to contaminated food or water. The environment, not to be underestimated, serves as a plausible wellspring of pathogenic E. coli impacting human health (Gomes et al., 2016; Shoaib et al., 2025). Besides, other food products such as raw meat, raw and pasteurized milk, vegetables/crops, and drinking water can be contaminated by E. coli through various sources and impact human health (Cho et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021; Luna-Guevara et al., 2019). The unjustified use of antibiotics within animal production systems engenders the proliferation of widespread AMR bacterial strains harboring antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs). These strains can potentially transfer to human populations through horizontal and vertical gene transfer mechanisms (Fig. 2b) (Dandachi et al., 2020; Huijbers et al., 2020). This multifaceted interplay between antibiotics, pathogens, and the environment underscores the need for comprehensive strategies to combat AMR and safeguard global health.

Antimicrobial resistance determinants acquired by E. coli of poultry and other food-producing animal origin

The utilization of antimicrobials to combat livestock diseases in the mid-1940s (Podolsky, 2018). Later, the addition of antimicrobial-growth promotors (AGPs) in livestock feeds, including cattle, pigs, and poultry, was started in the early 1950s to meet the global food security challenges (Podolsky, 2018). Immediately after the introduction of AGPs, scientific research on the possible effects of long-term antimicrobial use, particularly antibiotics, has gained more attention (Hassan et al., 2021). Following these investigations, the Member States of the European Union (EU) firmly prohibited the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in food-producing animals from January 1, 2006 (Anadón et al., 2006). This pivotal decision to curb the usage of antimicrobial agents, notably antibiotics as growth promoters, contributed significantly to mitigating the cumulative load of antibiotics used in food-producing animals that surpassed human consumption (Ma et al., 2021).

Many antibiotics employed as growth promoters in food-producing animals primarily target Gram-positive bacteria (Brown et al., 2017). At the same time, veterinary clinics commonly utilize broad-spectrum antibiotics that are effective against Gram-negative bacteria, including E. coli (Arbab et al., 2022). Antimicrobial agents used as AGPs cover several antimicrobial classes, including aminoglycosides, tetracycline’s, penicillin, cephalosporin’s, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and streptogramins. AGPs are normally administered at lower concentrations, ranging from 2.5 mg to 125 mg per kg, depending on antimicrobial agents and animal species (Brown et al., 2017). AGPs act through bacteriostatic or bactericidal mechanisms of action. According to the bacteria-centric hypothesis, AGPs induce changes in the bacterial community in the animal intestines, which enhance animal growth by modulating the gut microbiota (Brown et al., 2017). The administration of antimicrobials as AGPs and for treatment purposes leads to the emergence of AMR bacteria and their potential risk of transmission to human beings. Later, the World Health Organization (WHO) also highlighted AMR as a global public health menace, creating a substantial threat to health, development, and food security worldwide (WHO, 2021). By 2010, China accounted for a considerable share (23 %) of the global antimicrobial usage in food-producing animals, a proportion expected to rise to 30 % by 2030 (Mulchandani et al., 2023). The healthcare settings have not been spared either, grappling with escalating levels of AMR against clinically crucial antimicrobials within prevalent bacterial species (Zhu et al., 2022). This increase in healthcare-associated AMR could be traced back to the transfer of ARGs between the microbiota of food-producing animals, humans, and the environment, forming a web of transmission pathways. Besides, the emergence and propagation of MDR bacteria signified a pivotal public health dilemma globally. MDR strains are characterized by their resistance to at least one antimicrobial agent from three or more classes (Algammal et al., 2023). Yu et al. (2020) conducted a study that revealed 96.6 % of MDR E. coli strains isolated from raw milk samples across various provinces in China.

Similarly, Ma et al. (2022) identified 88.7 % MDR E. coli strains from chickens and pigs in Zhejiang, while Zhao et al. (2021) found 75.4 % MDR E. coli strains from sheep in Northwest China. Numerous other studies have also revealed MDR by E. coli strains originating from food-producing animals across different regions of China, as listed in Table 1 (Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022, 2021; Yang et al., 2018; Yuan et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2021). Extended spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs)

Beta-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillin and cephalosporins, are the most widely used in veterinary and human medicine (Leibovici and Paul, 2007). Regrettably, increasing resistance to these antibiotics has occurred (Apley, 2022). Recent studies have shed a sobering light on the surge of resistance, particularly ESBLs produced by Enterobacteriaceae, with a notable focus on E. coli from food-producing animals (Table 2). This huge increase in resistance is particularly apparent in the case of β-lactams, which constitute widely used and traditionally safe antibiotics. The drug resistance is primarily mediated by β-lactamases, which can hydrolyze most of the β-lactam antibiotics, including 3rd and 4th generation extended-spectrum cephalosporins (ESCs) and monobactam antibiotics (Hussain et al., 2021). Within this paradigm, ceftiofur and other ESCs are widely used in China for animal therapeutic applications (Jiang et al., 2021). ESBLs are primarily synthesized by Gram-negative bacteria, especially E. coli, and are considered an essential mechanism for ESCs resistance (Hussain et al., 2021). MDR is commonly seen in most ESBL-resistant strains with co-resistance to the most often used antibiotics like tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones, which makes these pathogens resistant to many antibiotics and limits treatment options (Dupouy et al., 2019; Rozwadowski and Gawel, 2022). The genes encoding ESBLs can be divided into the following main categories: blaTEM, blaCTX-M, blaOXA, blaNDM, and blaSHV. Among them, blaCTX-M was the most prevalent genotype in E. coli isolates from food-producing animals in China (Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2021). Furthermore, recent findings have also reported that E. coli strains carrying blaCMY, blaLAP-2, and blaOXA genes in food-producing animals in China (Ma et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2018; Yuan et al., 2021). An intricate interplay unfolds as food-producing animals as emerging hosts for ESBL-producing E. coli. This microbial relay can traverse from animals to humans via various direct and indirect routes, underscoring the potential for inter-species transmission (Subramanya et al., 2021). Shafiq et al. (2019) also detected the blaCTX-M-15 gene in E. coli isolated from pigs and highlighted the possible threat of transfer of these genes through conjugative plasmids from animals to humans. This nexus between food-producing animals and humans further underscores the implications of comprehensive strategies to mitigate the transmission of AMR genes and safeguard public health.

Carbapenems, a critical class of beta-lactam antimicrobial agents, serve as "last resort" antibiotics for severe bacterial infections (Gupta et al., 2017). Carbapenem resistance primarily arises from multiple factors, including the production of carbapenemases through genes such as blaNDM and blaKPC, loss of outer membrane porins in Gram-negative bacteria, and overexpression of efflux pumps (Alizadeh et al., 2020). Consequently, carbapenem-resistant bacteria (CRB) transmission among the One Health components poses a potential public health risk (Kyung and Yoo, 2024). The increasing prevalence of ESBL enzyme-producing Gram-negative enterobacterales has contributed to the emergence of carbapenem-resistant E. coli and their transmission between human and animal (Shen et al., 2022b). The improper utilization of antibiotics in food-producing animals, both for prophylactic and therapeutic purposes, can amplify the spread of resistant strains. The presence of resistant strains in animal feces and poultry droppings can contaminate the broader environmental settings, including water, food, and food products, which act as sources for disseminating ARGs across diverse species (Dandachi et al., 2020; Huijbers et al., 2020). In addition to clonal dissemination, carbapenemase-producing genes are often carried by mobile genetic elements (MGEs) like plasmids and integrons, facilitating the transfer of these genes among various genera of Enterobacteriaceae (Ramsamy et al., 2022). Clinical carbapenem-resistant strains have also emerged in human settings due to the increased use of carbapenems in medical practice (Hameed et al., 2021). Various carbapenem resistance genes, including blaVIM, blaOXA, blaNDM, and blaKPC, have been detected in E. coli strains from diverse food-producing animals in China (Ma et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2021) and other countries (Al et al., 2020; Diaconu et al., 2020; Murugan et al., 2019; Tello et al., 2022). The presence of carbapenem-resistance genes in E. coli underscores the potential threat of gene transfer to humans, posing an emerging risk to public health. Given the comprehensive literature review discussing the status of AMR and acquired ARGs in E. coli isolated from food-producing animals, urgent measures are needed to implement public health interventions to curb the spread of AMR genes from food-producing animals to humans across broader geographical regions in China. Such interventions should address all levels of the food production chain to mitigate the growing threat of AMR.

Tetracycline

The landscape of tetracycline resistance often pivots on acquiring resistance genes rather than mutations, underpinning this phenomenon. This domain has unraveled two notable mechanisms: efflux pumps and ribosomal protection (Sheykhsaran et al., 2019). Among the 33 tetracycline-resistance genes (tet), 23 genes encode efflux pumps that allow bacteria to transport antibiotics out of the cell actively. The remaining ten genes encode for ribosome defense proteins (Roberts, 2005). E. coli predominantly manifests tetracycline resistance through active efflux pumps driven by seven specific tet genes (Goetz et al., 2022). Wang et al. (2021) documented a striking 97.2 % phenotypic tetracycline resistance, with tetW and tetC emerging as the predominant genotypic determinants in E. coli isolated from swine in Shandong, China. Similarly, Li et al. (2022) observed a 30.0 % phenotypic resistance rate, with tetA, tetB, and tetC playing pivotal roles as the dominant genotypic determinants in E. coli strains derived from fecal samples of cattle, pigs, and chickens in Sichuan, China. Further insights into this intricate mechanism were reported by Hu et al. (2022) who unveiled a diverse aggregate of acquired genes—namely, tetA, emrY, emrK, tolC, mdfA, marA, acrS, acrA, acrB, evgS, and evgA in E. coli from raw meat. This intricate interplay of resistance genes underscores the multifaceted nature of tetracycline resistance mechanisms in E. coli from various sources, advocating for comprehensive strategies to address this emergent challenge.

Glycyclines

Glycyclines, a type of antibiotic derived from tetracyclines, known as tetracycline analogs, were ingeniously designed to overcome tetracycline resistance, particularly from efflux pumps and ribosomal protection mechanisms. This modification introduced an N, N-dimethylglycylamido side chain at position 9 of the minocycline (Moffa and Brook, 2015). Like tetracyclines, these agents bind to the 30S ribosomal subunit, inhibiting protein synthesis and impeding the binding of amino-acyl tRNA to the A ribosomal site (Bergeron et al., 1996). This persistent effort led to the development of the first glycylcycline, i.e., tigecycline, which gained FDA approval for clinical use on May 27, 2005 (Zhou et al., 2023). The World Health Organization classified this antimicrobial agent as a critical antibiotic of defense against MDR bacteria, especially in life-threatening infections (Herzog and Uhl, 2014). Until 2019, resistance to tigecycline primarily occurred through mutational and regulatory alterations with limited horizontal transmission. However, a pivotal discovery in China unveiled the plasmid-mediated horizontal transfer of tet(X) genes among isolates from both human and animal sources (He et al., 2019). Subsequently, various tet(X) variants like tet(X3), tet(X4), tet(X5), and tet(X6) emerged in E. coli isolated from animals, humans, and environment in China and globally (He et al., 2019; Li et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2020). Despite tigecycline not being approved for animal use, observations from food-producing animals, food products, and their environments suggest that they might serve as a substantial reservoir for tigecycline resistance genes, posing an emerging risk of gene transmission to humans.

Phosphonic

In response to the emergence of highly resistant E. coli strains, including MDR and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) variants, the reevaluation of fosfomycin, an old antibiotic from the phosphonic class, has been reintroduced in clinical environments. This revival positions fosfomycin as a primary treatment choice for uncomplicated urinary tract infections within hospital settings (Michalopoulos et al., 2011). However, the widespread distribution of fosA gene variants (fosA1-10) has posed a substantial challenge to the continued effectiveness of fosfomycin as a therapeutic option (Yang et al., 2019). Notably, fosA3 is the predominant plasmid-associated resistance gene among these genes, significantly influencing fosfomycin resistance (Yang et al., 2019).

Currently, fosA3 has gained prominence within E. coli strains across diverse regions, encompassing Europe, Asia, China, and the USA (Alrowais et al., 2015; Benzerara et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2012; Sorlozano-Puerto et al., 2020). Consequently, the global dissemination of E. coli strains harboring the fosA3 gene has become a significant global health concern (Zurfluh et al., 2020). Recently, a novel Fosfomycin resistance gene fosA10 has been identified on conjugative plasmid IncFII type which is playing a pivotal role in its rapid dissemination (Huang et al., 2020b). Despite the prohibition of fosfomycin use in the feed of food-producing animals in China, there are several reports of E. coli carrying the fosA3 gene in pigs, chickens, and cattle within the country (Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Zou et al., 2021). This underscores the persistent challenge of limiting the spread of fosfomycin resistance within animal populations.

Quinolones/fluoroquinolones

Quinolones and fluoroquinolones, potent antibacterial agents employed in veterinary medicine, have been utilized to combat various E. coli infections in food-producing animals. Regrettably, the extensive application of quinolones has contributed to the rise of quinolone-resistant E. coli strains in livestock in China (Lu et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2021). These drugs target enzymes crucial to bacterial function, including DNA topoisomerase IV encoded by parC and parE genes and DNA gyrase encoded by gyrA and gyrB genes (Bush et al., 2020). Resistance mechanisms in Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli to fluoroquinolones predominantly involve two pathways, among others: impeding the action of bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, and diminishing drug entry due to changes in the outer membrane and increased drug excretion from bacterial cells (Majalekar and Shirote, 2020). These enzymes are essential for bacterial DNA replication. Mutations within genes encoding these enzymes in the bacterial DNA reduce their susceptibility to quinolones. In clinical bacterial strains, such as E. coli, the chief mechanism for elevated resistance is the occurrence of multiple mutations in amino acid sequences known as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the quinolone resistance-determining region (QRDR), which modify the quinolone resistance domain of the target enzyme (Correia et al., 2017).

Unlike chromosomal mutations, transferable plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes in bacterial strains lead to the increased resistance levels. Nonetheless, inter-bacterial species transmission of PMQR genes results in faster and more widespread mutations than chromosomal mutations and other forms of antibiotic resistance (Maris et al., 2021). Additionally, a standard route for co-resistance to quinolones and β-lactam drugs is facilitated by the presence of β-lactamases and QRDR substitutions, whether in the presence or absence of PMQR genes (Basu and Mukherjee, 2018). The PMQR genes identified in E. coli isolates from food-producing animals include qnr, oqxAB, qepA, and aac-(6′)-Ib-cr genes (Lu et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2021). The qnr genes encode pentapeptides that shield bacterial enzymes (gyrase and topoisomerase IV) from quinolone effects (Lin et al., 2020). On the other hand, the qepA and oqxAB genes encode transporter proteins functioning as efflux pumps, expelling antibiotics from bacterial cells (Brandis et al., 2021; Li et al., 2019). The aac-(6′)-Ib-cr gene, which encodes aminoglycoside acetyltransferase, acetylates multiple fluoroquinolones, rendering them ineffective (Wang et al., 2020a). In an extensive study, Hu et al. (2022) identified an array of diverse fluoroquinolones-resistant genes (marA, acrS, H—NS, acrF, acrE, acrA, acrB, emrR, emrA, emrB, mdtH, qnrS1, CRP, mdtE, mdtF, gadW, gadX, evgS, evgA) within 22 Shiga-toxin producing E. coli (STEC) strains isolated from raw beef and mutton meat samples in Jinan city, Shandong, China. The presence of these genes implies acquiring additional antibiotic resistance via HGT.

Polymyxins

The prophylactic use of colistin within animal feed remained a significant practice in the past and now banned throughout the world. Colistin stands as critical antibiotic which means that this antibiotic is the sole, irreplaceable, and one of the limited available therapies for treating the life-threatening human infections and resistance to this antibiotic can be transferred to humans through non-human use such as food-producing animals. Further, animals, food products, or the environment can transfer the resistance to humans through bacteria or acquisition of resistance genes which could have devastating consequences for human health (Collignon et al., 2016). Following the colistin ban, the prevalence of colistin resistance in bacteria isolated from food-producing animals has been relatively low in numerous countries (Bertelloni et al., 2022; Kempf et al., 2016). Initially, polymyxin resistance was attributed to gene mutations controlling 4-deoxyaminoarabinose (Lara4N) synthesis (Gogry et al., 2021). However, recent findings have unveiled the emergence of the mcr-1 gene, a plasmid-borne colistin resistance gene in various species within the Enterobacteriaceae family from diverse sources (Huang et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021). Colistin had been extensively employed in China's food animal sector, particularly in pigs and poultry. However, the first report on the emergence of mcr-1-carrying E. coli strains isolated from pigs at a Guangzhou slaughterhouse raised concerns about potential human health threats (Liu et al., 2016). Subsequently, the mcr-1 gene harboring E. coli was also detected in other food-producing animals, food products, and human populations worldwide (Brilhante et al., 2019; Duggett et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Oh et al., 2020; Trung et al., 2017; Vounba et al., 2019). Until now, ten mcr variants (mcr-1 to mcr-10) confer resistance to colistin have been identified in various bacteria across different countries and regions (Borowiak et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2018; Liassine et al., 2016; Rebelo et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018). In a study by Zhao et al. (2020) isolated a significant proportion of poultry-origin E. coli strains (15.3 %) harboring mcr-1 gene from 2017 to 2018 in Shandong province. Another study reported that 8.8 % of E. coli strains isolated from healthy meat ducks were found to carry the mcr-1 gene (Liu et al., 2020a). Although, the colistin has been banned but still due to the persistence and transfer of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes is still a challenge and need to be addressed immediately to address the global issue of AMR.

Aminoglycosides

Aminoglycoside resistance primarily occurs from enzymatic modification of the drug by enzymes produced by bacteria and decreased antibiotic affinity for ribosomal RNA (Partridge, 2015; Ramirez and Tolmasky, 2010). This alteration decreases the antibiotic's effectiveness due to modification in the targeting site (Partridge, 2015). Within the Enterobacteriaceae family, aacC genes have been identified as the predominant genes encoding aminoglycoside nucleotide transferase (Ho et al., 2010; Jian et al., 2021). (Li et al., 2022) revealed the presence of aacC2 and aacC4 genes in E. coli strains from chicken, pigs, and cattle, contributing to aminoglycoside resistance (Li et al., 2022). Moreover, a cluster of aph genes has been discovered in E. coli isolates from various food-producing animals in China, conferring aminoglycoside resistance by producing aminoglycoside phosphotransferase enzymes (Ma et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021a). Besides, str genes like strA and strB, encoding enzymes that inactivate streptomycin (an aminoglycoside), were identified in E. coli strains isolated from chicken, pigs, and cattle, contributing to streptomycin resistance (Li et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2022). Recent investigations by Liu et al. (2022) and Ma et al. (2022) unveiled the presence of the rmtB gene, which encodes 16S rRNA methyltransferases in E. coli strains isolated from ducks, chickens, and pigs, thus conferring resistance to aminoglycosides. Additionally, multiple subtypes of aadA genes have been identified in E. coli isolates from food-producing animals, conferring resistance to spectinomycin and streptomycin through the action of adenylyltransferase enzymes (Hu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2021). These findings underscore the diverse genetic mechanisms driving aminoglycoside resistance in E. coli from food-producing animals.

Sulfonamides/trimethoprim

Combining sulfonamides and trimethoprim is widely used in veterinary medicine for therapeutic and prophylactic purposes (de Faria et al., 2021). Sulfonamide resistance genes, namely sul1, sul2, and sul3, are frequently carried by plasmids and are extensively prevalent among Enterobacteriaceae, including E. coli in China's food-producing animals (Li et al., 2022). The sul1 and sul2 genes are commonly found in E. coli isolates from diverse regions of China (Yu et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2021) and other countries (Alawneh et al., 2020; Freitag et al., 2017). Recent research has also highlighted that mutations in the folP gene can contribute to sulfonamide resistance in E. coli. Point mutations within the folP gene result in dihydropteroate synthase alterations, hindering sulfonamide action (Sánchez-Osuna et al., 2019). Numerous sub-variants of dfr genes have been identified within Enterobacteriaceae, conferring trimethoprim resistance by modifying the target dihydrofolate reductase enzyme. These variants are integrated with gene cassettes, often associated with class 1 or 2 integrons (Brolund et al., 2010; Domínguez et al., 2019). Among E. coli strains isolated from various food-producing animals in China and globally, several dfr genes have been commonly observed, including dfr5, dfr12, dfr14 (Ma et al., 2022), dfrA1, dfrA5 (Yu et al., 2020), dfrA7, dfrA12, dfrA15, dfrA16 (Freitag et al., 2017; Majumder et al., 2021), and dfrA17 (Hu et al., 2022). These findings underscore the diversity of dfr gene variants contributing to trimethoprim resistance within E. coli populations from various sources.

Macrolide, lincosamide, and streptogramin (MLS)

Resistance to lincosamides, macrolides, and streptogramin B antibiotics often occurs in conjunction due to shared mechanisms of action among the genes conferring resistance to different antibacterial agents. The expression of erm genes leads to modification of the rRNA target site, preventing these antibiotics from binding to the ribosomal subunit. Common erm genes identified in E. coli strains extracted from dairy milk include erm(A), erm(B), and erm(C) genes (Liu et al., 2021). These genes belong to the erm family and encode methylases that confer resistance to streptogramin B (Efthymia and Constantinos, 2018), macrolides (Feßler et al., 2018b), and lincosamides (Feßler et al., 2018a). Further studies have unveiled additional gene families associated with resistance. Ma et al. (2022) identified mph genes, specifically mphA, in E. coli strains from chickens and pigs in Zhejiang province, China. These genes encode phosphotransferases, conferring resistance to macrolides (Chajęcka-Wierzchowska et al., 2023). Moreover, other macrolide resistance genes, such as msrA and msrB, identified in E. coli strains from dairy cattle, code for transporter proteins and confer resistance to streptogramin B and macrolides (Yang et al., 2015). Recent findings by Ma et al. (2022) also revealed the presence of lnu and mef genes, including lnuF and mefB, in E. coli isolates from China. These genes encode nucleotidyl transferases, conferring resistance to lincosamides and macrolides. Hu et al. (2022) identified multiple efflux pump genes (evgA, evgS, gadW, gadX, mdtE, mdtF, CRP, H—NS) in E. coli that contribute to macrolide resistance. The E. coli is known to be intrinsically resistant to macrolides due to protective outer membrane and broad-spectrum efflux pumps such as AcrAB-TolC system (Shoaib et al., 2025). However, streptogramin antibiotics are not typically employed for E. coli infection control; identifying resistance genes underscores the horizontal acquisition of genes from various sources, including the environment, which could potentially propagate these genes to streptogramin-sensitive pathogens.

Multidrug efflux pumps

The emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial pathogens, including strains of E. coli, presents a rapidly escalating global health crisis, with many infections reaching the point of being incurable. The roots of drug resistance are multifaceted, but a fundamental component is the action of intrinsic efflux pumps, which actively export antimicrobial compounds from the cell (Du et al., 2018). Resistant phenotypes frequently evolve when this baseline efflux capability is amplified. Such amplification can occur through the overexpression of transporter genes, the uneven distribution of pumps during bacterial division, or genetic mutations. In fact, upon exposure to a harmful agent, the swift deployment of efflux mechanisms often constitutes the bacterium's most rapid and effective countermeasure (Bergmiller et al., 2017). This is evidenced by complex phenotypic shifts in organisms like Escherichia coli, where antibiotic exposure triggers widespread changes in the expression profiles of numerous genes responsible for transport (Li et al., 2015). Bacterial pathogens employ a diverse array of drug efflux transporters, which can function cooperatively or provide backup for one another, creating robust multidrug resistance (Lee et al., 2000). Currently, six major families of these efflux pumps are recognized. Among them, the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters derive their energy directly from ATP hydrolysis. The remaining five families operate as secondary active transporters, harnessing the energy from transmembrane ion gradients. These include the major facilitator superfamily (MFS), the multidrug and toxin extrusion (MATE) family, the small multidrug resistance (SMR) family, the resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND) superfamily, and the proteobacterial antimicrobial compound efflux (PACE) family (Huang et al., 2022a). A recent study by Hu et al. (2022) identified multiple MDR efflux pump genes such as evgA, evgS, gadW, gadX, mdtE, mdtF, CRP, H—NS belong to different super families in E. coli isolated from raw meat. Shoaib et al., 2024a conducted a recent study in Gansu province of China and identified diverse MDR efflux pumps in E. coli of dairy farm origin. Among the MDR efflux pumps, rsmA, acrEF, acrAB-tolC, oqxAB, CRP, and acrS belong to RND superfamily, kpnE and kpnF belong to SMR superfamily while mdtE, mdtF, mdtM, and H—NS belong to MFS superfamily were identified through whole genome sequence analysis. Similarly, another study conducted by Shoaib et al. (2025) in Xinjiang province of China identified MDR efflux pumps mentioned earlier with the addition of marA belong to RND superfamily and general bacterial porin with reduced permeability to beta-lactams and soxS and soxR which play role of multiple efflux pump super families including RND, ABC, and MFS.

Diversity in plasmid replicon types and associated AMR determinants

The escalation of antimicrobial resistance poses a significant threat to human and animal health globally. Recent investigations have underscored the efficacy of plasmids as vectors for disseminating ARGs through horizontal gene transfer mechanisms, particularly conjugation, more frequently than previously recognized (Villa and Carattoli, 2020). Within the Enterobacteriaceae family, 27 distinct plasmid incompatibility (Inc) groups have been characterized. Notably, plasmid replicon types, such as the IncF, IncHI2, IncA/C, IncN, IncI, and IncL/M, play an important role in facilitating the spread of antibiotic resistance genes within and between the bacterial species (Rozwandowicz et al., 2018). A smaller number of specific plasmid families, including IncX, IncY, and IncP, also play a pivotal role in disseminating resistance genes in Enterobacteriaceae. For instance, the IncX plasmid, belonging to a relatively rare family, has been primarily associated with the propagation of PMQR genes (Nohejl et al., 2022). However, the relatively lower prevalence of IncX plasmids might also be attributed to the limitations of the standard PCR-based replicon typing protocol used for plasmid family identification (Villa and Carattoli, 2020). The subsequent discussion elaborates on various plasmid families acquired by E. coli of food-animal origin: The IncF plasmid family displays heterogeneity in size (ranging from 50 to 200 kb) and replicon types (Karami et al., 2021), belonging to the IncF incompatibility group, are notably abundant and have been extensively identified across diverse food-producing animals in various regions of China (Table 3). The plasmid multi-locus sequence typing (pMLST) database encompasses many plasmid families, with a considerable proportion attributed to the IncF group isolated from E. coli. These plasmids are further categorized into subgroups based on the FII, FIA, and FIB(FAB) allele loci (PubMLST, 2023). The IncF plasmid possesses self-replication mechanisms and often encodes different addiction systems contingent on toxin-antitoxin determinants. These systems regulate copy numbers and ensure widespread inheritance during cell division (Vrancianu et al., 2020).

Previous investigations have highlighted the potential role of IncF plasmids to harbor a diversity of ARGs conferring resistance to critical classes of antimicrobial agents, including quinolones, β-lactams, chloramphenicol, aminoglycosides, and tetracyclines (del Carmen Rocha-Gracia et al., 2022; Delgado-Blas et al., 2022; Vinué et al., 2020). Notably, ESBLs of the CTX-M class frequently co-occur with aminoglycoside and PMQR resistance genes (Bodendoerfer et al., 2020; Habeeb et al., 2014). Disseminating these MDR plasmids among Enterobacteriaceae could significantly impact clinical disease management. Furthermore, potentially transmitting these plasmids from animals to humans presents a heightened risk to public health (Afema et al., 2018; Aguirre et al., 2020). For example, IncF plasmids have been identified in diverse animal species, such as swine, chickens, and ducks, which closely correlate with human E. coli isolates (Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a). It has also been observed that IncF plasmids carry multiple ARGs, including blaCTX-M, rmtB, oqxB, qnrS, qnrB, and qepA, within E. coli strains isolated from food-producing and companion animals in Guangdong, China (Yang et al., 2015). Considering these insights, comprehending the prevalence and subtypes of IncF plasmids is crucial in exploring innovative approaches for targeting plasmids in the fight against drug-resistant bacteria.

In the pre-antibiotic era, IncX plasmids were initially identified in Salmonella and Klebsiella strains (Jones et al., 1993), however, they have also been detected in E. coli (Guo et al., 2019). The extensively studied IncX plasmid, R6K, is a self-transporting, iteron-containing multi-copy plasmid whose replication is an action-controlled molecular interaction of two minimal replicon components, which are γoriV and Rep (π protein) (Grudniak et al., 2007). IncX family plasmids were initially categorized into two subgroups, IncX1 and IncX2, based on restriction analysis (Jones et al., 1993). Later, based on conserved region polymorphism of 18 IncX plasmids, the IncX plasmid family was further classified into IncX3 and IncX4 subgroups (Johnson et al., 2012). Recent investigations have unveiled that IncX plasmids can harbor multiple ARGs, including PMQR genes (qnrS1, oqxA, oqxB, mdfA, gyrA), ESBL genes (blaTEM-1, blaCTX-M-123, blaCTX-M-14, blaCTX-M-24, blaCTX-M-64, blaCTX-M-224, blaCTX-M-65), and carbapenemase genes (blaNDM-1, blaKPC-2) (Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2021). These findings underscore the vast diversity within the IncX plasmid family and highlight their overlooked role in mediating the transfer of antibiotic-resistance genes within Enterobacteriaceae.

The IncH incompatibility group (IncHI), particularly the IncHI2 subgroup, is widely prevalent within the Enterobacteriaceae family. Members of the IncH group often harbor various resistance genes, including the metallo-β-lactamase-encoding blaNDM gene (Li et al., 2020a). IncHI2 plasmids, a subset of IncHI, are frequently encountered among Enterobacteriaceae, with a substantial presence in E. coli isolated from humans (Zhang et al., 2022b), chickens (Zhang et al., 2022a), and sporadic occurrences in pigs (Ma et al., 2022). Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin, enrofloxacin, and cephalosporins like ceftiofur are extensively used in veterinary medicine (Caneschi et al., 2023). IncHI plasmids can carry a diversity of resistance genes, including fluoroquinolone resistance genes (qnrS, qnrB, oqxAB, qnrD), β-lactam resistance genes (blaTEM, blaCMY, blaCTX, blaSHV), aminoglycosides resistance genes (aph(3)-I, aph(3)-II, aac(3)-II, aadA, aac(3)-lia, aac(3)-lid, aac(3)-IV, aph(3`)-la, aph(3″)-lb), fosfomycin (fosA3), and florfenicol (floR) resistance genes (Yang et al., 2022; Yasir et al., 2021; Zurfluh et al., 2020). Recent reports have highlighted that ESBLs and PMQR resistance determinants co-localize on the same IncH plasmid (Fang et al., 2016; Juraschek, 2022).

Certain plasmid families, including IncN and IncP1, are recognized as diverse host-range plasmids. They play a significant role in disseminating ARGs through their ability to disseminate across diverse clinically relevant bacterial species and environments (González-Plaza et al., 2019; Lima et al., 2020; Shintani et al., 2023). Notably, IncN and IncP1 plasmid families detected in E. coli of swine and duck origin were found to carry antibiotic resistance determinants of critically important antibiotics, including ESBLs like blaTEM, blaCMY, blaCTX-M, and quinolones resistance genes such as qnrS, qnrA, and qnrB (Liu et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2021). Recent observations identified IncN plasmids in E. coli strains to harbor blaKPC genes, conferring resistance to carbapenems (Wang et al., 2020b).

IncR plasmids, introduced as a novel plasmid Inc group in 2009 (García-Fernández et al., 2009), often coexist with replicon types like IncFII, IncC, IncH, and IncN. The plasmid pEFER was the initial sequence with a single IncR replicon (Compain et al., 2014). In clinical enterobacteria, particularly E. coli strains from diverse geographical areas, IncR plasmids were identified as carriers of multiple drug resistance genes, including blaVIM-1, blaNDM-1, blaKPC-2, blaDHA-1, armA, and qnrS1 (Juraschek et al., 2021; Kohler et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021b). Notably, the array of resistance genes harbored by IncR plasmids can disseminate to other mobile plasmids through transposition or recombination events, enhancing the adaptability to MDR plasmids (Qian et al., 2020). IncR plasmids harboring MDR genes are increasingly found among Enterobacteriaceae isolates (Jing et al., 2019). Ma et al. (2022) reported IncR plasmid replicon types in E. coli strains isolated from pigs and chickens that may harbor multiple ARGs mentioned in Table 3. IncY plasmids are also prevalent among the Enterobacteriaceae family members, including E. coli, Salmonella, and K. pneumoniae in China (Huang et al., 2020a). Notably, the prevailing ARG carried by IncY plasmids is the ESBL gene, blaCTX-M-15 (Saidani et al., 2019). Additionally, these plasmids were found to have the mcr-1 gene conferring colistin resistance, indicating their wide distribution and high transmissibility (Yuan et al., 2021). Zhang et al. (2022a) also noted the presence of IncY plasmid replicons in E. coli strains isolated from chickens in central China.

IncI incompatibility complex plasmids (IncI) are characterized by their ability to produce type I pili. Based on the structural resemblance of these pili, IncI plasmids are categorized into at least seven distinct replicon types: IncI1, IncI2, IncZ, IncIγ, IncK1/2, and IncB/O (Zhang et al., 2019a) exhibit lower replication rates and conjugative capabilities, and they possess a host range that varies in size from 50 to 250 kb. The core structure of IncI plasmids consists of distinct regions responsible for replication, maintenance, transfer initiation, and binding during conjugation (Sampei et al., 2010). The replication region encompasses two protein genes, repZ, and repY, along with an RNA regulatory gene, inc, and four functional DNA sequences, including itrons, CIS, ter, and oriV, which orchestrate the regulation of plasmid replication (Sampei et al., 2010). Notably, the cleaved genes, parA, and parB, in the supportive region play a role in actively distributing replicated DNA to daughter cells throughout the cell cycle. Within the binding transfer region, two gene clusters, tra, and pil, encode thick and robust pili that play a crucial role in conjugation and thin and flexible pili that enhance binding efficiency in liquid media, respectively (Zhang et al., 2019b). The IncI plasmid family is widely prevalent among E. coli isolates obtained from various food-producing animals across diverse regions of China (Table 3).

The diverse range of plasmid replicon types identified within E. coli strains underscores the potential for transferring ARGs from food-producing animals to humans through plasmid-mediated mechanisms. Considering the comprehensive analysis of plasmid-mediated ARGs in E. coli from food-producing animals, it is evident that plasmids may serve as important vectors for disseminating AMR across diverse bacterial species. The diversity of plasmid incompatibility groups, such as IncF, IncHI, IncN, IncP1, IncR, and IncY, underscores their role in spreading a wide array of ARGs, including ESBLs, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, and other classes. The adaptability of these plasmids to different hosts and environments emphasizes the potential for inter-species transfer, posing a significant threat to both animal and human health. Therefore, a multifaceted approach is imperative, including strict antibiotic stewardship, improved animal management, and innovative interventions targeting plasmid-mediated resistance to curb the alarming rise of AMR at the interface of food-producing animals and humans.

Antimicrobial resistance in E. coli from Chinese poultry and other food-producing animals: local findings with global implications

The findings of this review, which provided detail on specific AMR determinants and PRTs in E. coli from poultry and other food-producing animals in China, are not an isolated phenomenon but highlights a critical node in the global AMR network. The AMR is a increasing threat to global population in One Health settings, excavate the efficacy of life-saving therapeutics and pushing population at heightened threat. From the last two decades, the emergence and dissemination of MDR organisms in human and animal settings have been increased at an unprecedented rate, leading to a critical global health crises (Marino et al., 2025). In the current review, the high prevalence of MDR E. coli of poultry and other food producing animal origin in China also aligns with the World Health Organization (WHO) recent AMR report, which noted increasing antibiotic resistance trend in South-East Asia region (WHO, 2025a). Our data from China, a leading global poultry and livestock producer, provide the international community with important early warning signals and emphasize the need to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship in poultry and livestock farming to slow the global rise of AMR. A recent literature review conducted by Marino et al. (2025) also noted high global burden of MDR pathogens including E. coli in clinical settings which may indicates the global spread of MDR pathogens in One Health settings. This trigger the strong multi-sectoral collaboration across all regions within One Health framework to enhance collaborative surveillance and policy action. The WHO has also introduced One Health approach to identify intimate connection between human, animal, and environment and implement solutions in this framework (WHO, 2023). The WHO also called governments to begin with the development of individual National Action Plans (NAP) based on One Health approach on AMR. In response to this, 120 of 194 member states including China, have developed NAPs. The WHO also developed Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance Systems (GLASS) program explicitly aims to construct a global overview of AMR pathogens by supporting countries in establishing their surveillance facilities (WHO, 2025b).

The emergence of AMR in bacterial pathogens including E. coli can occur through diverse resistance mechanisms such as enzymatic degradation, decrease membrane permeability, target site replacement, efflux pump activation, biofilm formation, and adaptive responses (Smith et al., 2023). These mechanisms were found to co-exist in E. coli isolates from poultry and other food-producing animals in China leading to multi-dimensional profiles that complicate the diagnosis and treatment of such pathogens. Moreover, the identification of diverse PRTs in our E. coli isolates, especially, IncF and IncI is of international concern, as such plasmids play an important role in dissemination of ARGs by acting as ‘shuttles’ between different bacterial species and One Health settings (Castañeda-Barba et al., 2024). Their presence in Chinese poultry and livestock production systems indicates a potential reservoir for ARGs that can lead to their entrance in global food chain, environment, and transfer to human pathogens. A study conducted by Cao et al. (2022) has shown that a subset of plasmids often shuttles ARGs between the bacteria in different environments (e.g. from food animal production to humans) and can dominate across vast geographical zones highlights the potential role of HGT in driving AMR pandemics. Further, the emergence of critical resistance genes (e.g. mcr-1, tet(X4), and blaNDM-5) in E. coli from Chinese poultry and livestock production systems is of particular concern. As, these genes are plasmid-associated and confer resistance to colistin, tigecycline, and carbapenems respectively, which are the last-resort antibiotics in human medicine and can be shuttled to humans through direct and indirect routes regionally and globally.

China as a global leader in poultry production and AMR determinants acquired by E. coli which is a major food-borne pathogen could have direct and indirect impacts on international food safety and trade (Tauxe et al., 2008). Also, the globalization of the livestock industry necessitates a deep knowledge of regional AMR profiles of bacterial pathogens (Torres et al., 2021). Our review provides a critical bench mark for international partners and veterinarians operating in or trading with China to inform decisions on antibiotic usage and animal health management that have a global impact. Moreover, under the One Health framework, our findings from animal sector provide critical data for understanding and alleviating the cross-border dissemination of resistant strains, a challenge that has also been prioritized by nations like the United States and European Union in their monitoring guidelines to combat AMR (Himanshu et al., 2022). AMR is also a global challenge for achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), identifying the particular resistance determinants and their transmission drivers regionally or locally provides solid data that can inform the national and international action plans, similar to China and other countries are implementing (Gurung et al., 2024). Thereby, the curb of AMR in China’s food-production systems is not just a national issue but an important component of global health protection because it’s directly effecting the international food chain and use of critical antimicrobials globally. International cooperation such as Sino-UK collaboration on bacterial resistance which focus on studying the bacterial resistance in One Health spectrum, pathogen resistance mechanisms, microbial data sharing, developing new diagnostic tools, and exploring new antibiotic alternative therapies are the keys to control the growing threat of AMR globally (https://www.gov.uk/government/news/tackling-drug-resistance-uk-china-funding-competition-opens, accessed on 18 October 2025).

Conclusion

The prevalence of E. coli and MDR E. coli strains remains elevated among food-producing animals in China. It may pose a significant public health risk due to the horizontal transmission of AMR genes facilitated by diverse plasmid replicon types. To address this pressing issue, a collaborative effort is essential to safeguard human, animal, and environmental well-being. This necessitates continuous monitoring and research to enhance our understanding with concrete evidence. To take effective action, it is recommended that the Chinese Government establish a comprehensive national AMR monitoring program encompassing zoonotic and indicator bacteria like E. coli in healthy livestock such as cattle, poultry, and pigs. Additionally, the harmonized monitoring of AMR in veterinary clinical isolates should be integrated into the national action plan. A unified "One Health Action Plan" should be developed, outlining effective strategies to combat AMR across all levels. It is crucial to regulate antimicrobial use in livestock, reserving certain antibiotics exclusively for human medical purposes. These combined efforts may play a vital role in mitigating the growing threat of AMR in China.
