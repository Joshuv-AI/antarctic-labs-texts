“I feel like I’m marinating in the boat…and it just makes me feel gross”: a sociocultural exploration of female rowers' experiences of the menstrual cycle
Authors: Ciara Everard, Clíodhna McHugh, Kathryn Ackerman, Pauline Cullen, Jane Thornton, Kellie Wilkie, Gráinne M Donnelly, Lari Trease, Geraldine Foley, Sarah J Mcdonnell, Kirsty Elliott-Sale, Fiona Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to explore how socio-cultural norms and values influence female rowers’ experiences and perceptions of the menstrual cycle in sport. Individual semistructured interviews were conducted with 18 menstruating female rowers (29.2±11 years) from Ireland and the USA. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using a reflexive thematic analysis.Five themes were identified: (1) normalisation of abnormal periods: the majority of athletes described experiencing menstrual cycle irregularities. These irregularities were viewed as ‘normal’ or even valorised within rowing subcultures, precluding athletes from seeking help; (2) ‘Is she faking it?’ The delegitimisation of menstrual cycle pain and symptoms: pain was viewed as part and parcel of rowing culture, leading to athletes silencing or suppressing their pain as well as seeking medical interventions to ‘manage away’ their symptoms; (3) stigma and fear of leakages: athletes expressed a profound fear of leakages, which was amplified by patriarchal ideals and stigmatising notions surrounding bleeding bodies; (4) gendered norms and issues of non-disclosure: the interpersonal dynamics surrounding gendered norms create and perpetuate cultures of non-disclosure; (5) period power: emergence of alternative sociocultural norms that challenge dominant ideals and expand perceptions of how the menstrual cycle is experienced and managed within sport settings. Heeding calls for more research on the menstrual cycle within sports medicine, this study extends the literature by highlighting how sociocultural influences can perpetuate harmful discourses and practices that have implications for athletes’ menstrual health and well-being. Moreover, the findings illustrate how these sociocultural norms create contexts of concealment and taboo, ultimately limiting progression towards best practice management for female rowers.Keywords: Qualitative Research, Sports medicine, Dysmenorrhea

Practical implications

The findings provide real-world insights into how athletes experience and manage their menstrual cycle and amplify the need for increased awareness, destigmatisation and evidence-based research surrounding female menstrual health.

The findings add to understanding the gendered and embodied inequalities that shape female athletes’ performance and experiences and highlight how female athletes’ voices and embodied understandings are central to progressing management of menstrual cycle pain and person-centred care.

The findings underscore the need to bring coaches, athletes and clinicians together to help dismantle hierarchical structures and address gendered norms that perpetuate cultures of concealment and non-disclosure.

Introduction

The menstrual cycle (MC) impacts sporting participation, performance and health across multiple physiological and psychological parameters. For example, up to 51% of athletes perceive it as impacting their training and performance due to both adverse symptoms and by creating a distraction.1,3 Given the individual variation in the MC, researchers have highlighted the need to explore athletes’ perceptions and lived experiences, as opposed to group averages, to help optimise athlete support.2 Accordingly, a small but growing body of qualitative research2,9 has deepened our understanding of the profound and variable impact of the MC for female athletes. For example, researchers have documented the physical and mood disturbances that athletes experience during their MC and highlighted how although MC dysfunction is quite prevalent among athletes, it is often normalised within sporting circles.2 3 Research further illustrates how shame, taboo and stigma concerning MC experiences can build contexts of concealment precluding athletes from seeking help.2,8 A ‘gender barrier’ in discussing the MC with male coaches and clinicians also exists,8 10 which is of particular concern given that only 10% of Olympic coaches are women.11 While several initiatives have been implemented within elite sport to address MC health and its impact on female athletes,12,14 there is clearly a need for further research that can generate contextualised insights into athletes' lived experiences and the sociocultural milieu in which they are situated. Such insights could help explain the persistence of communication barriers, secrecy and stigma,2,9 while simultaneously identifying and highlighting where positive changes have been made to help promote further progress.

Menstruating female athletes are situated within a unique context. They are influenced by complex and intersecting sociocultural values, including performance narratives,15 gendered sporting norms16 as well as wider discourses concerning feminine ideals and menstruation.17 To expand, performance sport is said to be dominated by an ‘all in’ attitude,18 whereby athletes are socialised into believing that pain, sacrifice and possibly injury are necessary in the quest for success.15 19 This performance culture is further compounded by the dominance of masculine ideals and gendered hierarchies within athlete subcultures, which can act to position women as being inferior to males within the sporting arena.16 Such gendered and sporting expectations can lead female athletes to suppress pain and/or modify emotions and behaviours to fulfil cultural stereotypes. There is no doubt that these norms, coupled with wider cultural discourses surrounding menstruation that portray it as a dirty, deviant and polluted endeavour,20 coalesce to suppress and silence female athletes’ experiences of the MC. Moreover, these circulating and inter-related forces can act to shape and frame female athletes’ perceptions of what it means to be a menstruating female (or not) in sport. Recently, there has been greater attention and focus on this topic in efforts to support female athletes12,14; however, the persistent influence of broader sociocultural factors on female athletes’ perceptions, experiences and management of the MC warrants further consideration.

Against this backdrop, this study aimed to explore female rowers' lived experiences of the MC. In particular, we aimed to analyse the salience of the sociocultural context for rowers’ experiences and management of the MC. The sport of rowing was chosen due to the limited research within this sport. World Rowing has been committed to increasing female participation, with the 2021 Tokyo Olympics marking full gender equality.21 That said, recent research highlights how gender biases persist within rowing subcultures due to ‘legacy issues’, as rowing has historically been a male-dominated sport.22 23 Such gender issues include female athletes and coaches feeling uncomfortable giving feedback and being perceived as being less capable or ambitious than their male counterparts.16 23 These macrolevel influences may implicate how female rowers experience and manage their MC. As part of a larger study, 827 female rowers conducted a survey,24 from which 67% reported experiencing irregular MCs, while 69% reported fears of leaking into their unisuit. Most (78%, n=629) believed their MC negatively impacts performance, with decreased motivation to compete during menstruation due to menstrual symptoms.24 However, despite this impact, half of the sample reported that they would not discuss their MC with their coach. Given these results, we decided to interview 18 female rowers to further explore their experiences. Based on the findings and given the sociocultural context surrounding the MC, we aimed to analyse how this context may have shaped their experiences.

Methods

Design and philosophical positioning

The study is underpinned by an interpretivist paradigm, rooted in the tenets of a relativist ontology and a constructivist epistemology.25 Ontologically, we subscribe to the belief that no single, mind-independent, external reality exists; rather, reality is multiple, fluid and mind-dependent. Aligned with this view, we chose to use semistructured interviews to generate contextualised insights into participants' lived experiences as opposed to trying to objectify or generalise the findings.25,27 Epistemologically, we assume knowledge is subjective and constructed through interaction between the researcher and the participants. The authors’ positioning, therefore, will have shaped both the data co-construction and the interpretations offered throughout.25,27 The research team comprised 13 female researchers. Six were physiotherapists specialising in sports medicine and women’s health. Three were exercise scientists who focused on female athlete research and three were physicians specialising in sports medicine. Two authors were former international rowers and five had been international rowing team clinicians. Data collection was conducted by the second and final author, while data analysis was primarily conducted by the first, second, fourth and final author with input from the rest of the research team. The second and final authors were former elite athletes (Gaelic football and rowing, respectively), with extensive experience in qualitative research and sports and exercise medicine. The fourth author has expertise in both qualitative research and sociological theory. The first author has expertise in qualitative analysis, is a former elite athlete, but would be considered a cultural ‘outsider’28 to rowing.

Patient and public involvement

Two elite rowers and one rowing coach (all female) provided feedback on the interview guide and minor modifications were made accordingly to improve clarity.

Participants

Following ethical approval, convenience sampling29 was used to recruit menstruating female rowers registered with Rowing Ireland and US Rowing, over the age of 18. A weblink to the study information was sent to these members via Microsoft Forms and information was also displayed on Rowing Ireland and US Rowing’s websites and promoted through their social media channels. As part of a larger study, participants were first invited to take part in an online survey, based on that by Schoep et al.30 At the end of the survey, participants were invited to contact the researchers if they were willing to be interviewed. In total, 827 participants completed the online survey,24 and from here, 18 menstruating female rowers volunteered to take part in an online interview. Participants’ rowing experience ranged from club to international level, and their mean age was 29.2±11 years (see online supplemental file for demographics and background information). Data collection was conducted between April and November 2023.

Data collection

All participants provided informed consent prior to the online interview. The development of the semistructured interview guide (see online supplemental file) was guided by the questions asked in the survey and developed further from discussions within the research team and the PPI representatives. The overarching aim of the study was to explore participants’ experiences of the MC, including pain and symptoms, and questions were aligned with this remit. However, preliminary analysis showed that participants focused mainly on stigma, gendered bias in sport and the normalisation of absent periods. Consequently, the research focus was refined to examine how sociocultural factors shaped these experiences.

The second and final author conducted the first 10 interviews together, with the authors taking it in turns to ask questions, while the other recorded field notes. The second author conducted the final eight interviews alone. Cointerviewing was used as the second author was less experienced with qualitative interviewing.31 As a recognised training tool, cointerviewing can facilitate rapport and create a more dynamic conversation allowing for richer data generation. While power imbalances can be an issue with cointerviewing, some researchers argue that cointerviewing can redistribute power away from a single researcher by enhancing the coconstruction of knowledge.31 Interviews were conducted via Zoom32 and lasted between 37 and 64 min (average: 50 min). All interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim and pseudonymised to remove identifying information. Postinterview field notes were completed, and transcripts were sent to participants for accuracy confirmation.

Data analysis and rigour

A reflexive thematic analysis33 34 was performed, as it aligned with our philosophical positioning and allowed for the data to be analysed both inductively (ie, grounded in participants’ experiences) and deductively (ie, using theoretical constructs) to address the research topic. First, familiarisation of the data took place, which involved reading and rereading of the transcripts and was carried out by the first, second and final author. Second, an inductive analysis was conducted, where initial codes were formed by the first, second and final authors. A further inductive and deductive analysis (third step) was conducted by the first author to create themes that aligned with the refined research topic (ie, how sociocultural norms influenced participants’ experiences). Fourth, codes were clustered together around a central organising concept to form overarching themes, which were then discussed and further refined through critical friends’ discussions26 and external reflections.35 36 These themes were then reviewed against the transcripts and the entire data set. To assist with this stage, the first author composed an overall story for each transcript and then compared it to the overall story of the themes present.37 Fifth, the themes were named to capture interpretative stories of the data, and, finally, they were written up.

In line with our overarching philosophy (ie, interpretivism), a relativist position was used to enhance the methodological rigour and trustworthiness of the study.27 Trustworthiness of the data was addressed by using ‘thick descriptions’ and illustrative quotations to support interpretative findings and enhance the naturalistic generalisability of the study.35 36 Analytical rigour was further attended to through self-reflexivity,36 38 39 including multiple authors in both data collection and analysis, critical friend discussions and the incorporation of external reflections from all team members.26 35 36 The findings of the study were reported using the standards for reporting qualitative research guidelines.40

To expand, the first author engaged in self-reflexivity through reflexive journaling, whereby she reflected on her biases and assumptions and how they may have influenced the data analysis.38 39 For example, she reflected on her own experiences of menstruation in sport to help identify the potential or actual effects of personal, contextual and circumstantial aspects on the findings of the study.38 39 These reflections were shared, and initial interpretations of the data were discussed with the second and final author, who had completed the data analysis as well as the fourth author. These critical friend discussions helped the first author revisit and deepen her interpretations, by posing challenging questions, offering a theoretical sounding board and providing alternative perspectives.26 Following initial theme development, the findings were shared with the wider research team for external review. Feedback suggested that the themes primarily reflected adverse experiences of the MC in sport and did not fully capture recent advances that some members had observed in practice. In response, the research team not only revisited the data but also reflected on the voluntary nature of participation, which may have attracted individuals with more challenging experiences, and the fact that recent developments—particularly at the elite level—may not yet have reached subelite contexts. Considering this feedback, the final theme was further developed with signposting provided to recent initiatives to help present a more balanced perspective on the MC in sport. However, the authors aimed to remain faithful to the lived experiences of participants and used quotations to illustrate their views.

Normalisation of abnormal periods

Sixteen of the 18 participants reported either currently or previously experiencing missed, delayed or lighter menstruation. Athletes viewed these menstrual abnormalities as normal or even beneficial (due to the symptoms and ‘inconvenience’ of menstruation). In some instances, this normalisation of absent menstruation was supported by their healthcare provider. For example, P12 described, “I think I've assumed that it is [absent menstruation] fairly normal for athletes and that’s been corroborated by my health care providers, who sort of write it off as like, OK, you're an athlete. You don't have a period. That’s what happens.”

Rather than viewing menstruation as a vital biological sign, this normalisation of amenorrhoea by some medical personnel illustrates the pervasiveness of this cultural narrative. By using this narrative to ‘explain away’ athletes’ symptoms, some medical professionals can preclude athletes from receiving adequate support as the following participant (P12) demonstrates, “It was always used to explain it, like it’s normal, you’re training a lot… but there was never a next step or a solution for how to get my period back”.

In other instances, absent menstruation was not only normalised but also valorised by other athletes and coaches. Subscribing to a ‘slim to win’ ideology, an absent period was conflated with the notion of training hard and improved performance. P8 shared, “We talk about stretching to prevent low back pain, but we never talk about fuelling to prevent energy deficiency-related disorders. And so that meant that athletes are getting their advice from each other, which isn't always good advice, like people thought that losing your period meant that you were doing a good job of training hard, or that you were about to get a personal record… or that that was necessary to achieve success… and so, these 14, 15, 16-year-olds, who don't know any better think that they will get faster by losing weight… and our coach almost encourages that”.

As indicated by the above participants, the lack of education and foregrounding of female athlete health within sporting circles, can lead to misinformation, poor MC literacy and the creation and perpetuation of harmful discourses and practices.

Is she faking it? The delegitimisation of MC pain and symptoms

This theme relates to participants’ perspectives on how pain is viewed as ‘part and parcel’ of rowing culture, with MC pain viewed as part of the broader performance narrative and often given less legitimacy than other types of injury or illness. This outlook aligns with an ‘all-in’ performance ethos, whereby pain, dedication and sacrifice are deemed necessary in the quest for success.15 P3 described, “I feel like there is no option… I mean if Olympic swimmers can set their PRs [personal records] on their period, then why can’t we do the same… you need to just push through it”. Nevertheless, this resignation to a totalitarian view ignores the realities of menstrual discomfort and the variability of period pain and symptoms. For example, while menstruation was “minimally disruptive” for some participants, for others, it was a time of severe physical discomfort, with “period pain so intense that they[I]can't get off the floor (P11)”. Those with symptoms of dysmenorrhoea discussed how the pervasive attitude of pushing through it led to their silencing and suppressing of pain or lying to avoid the stigma associated with deviating from culturally accepted norms. For example, P14 described, “I would never tell my coach that I can’t train because of my period pain, so I often just lie and say I have a cold”. Indeed, participants highlighted that while it may be acceptable to take time off due to illness or injury, MC pain and symptoms were not given the same accommodations. This perception points to wider discourses regarding the delegitimisation of dysmenorrhoea, which is often denied the status of ‘real pain’,17 as P5 corroborated, “Sometimes you'd be like, is she really in pain, or she just like faking it’ because it’s a bad workout?”

Consequently, this delegitimisation of MC pain within the rowing community led to a foreclosure of discourse on tailoring training to accommodate MC symptoms. Moreover, to mitigate the stigma of MC pain and conform to the sporting ideal of a ‘disciplined body,41 one that defines itself in actions of self-regimentation and control, several participants described engaging in strategies to suppress, control or manage away their symptoms. For example, P14 shared, “To put it bluntly, I didn’t have time for periods… I didn’t want any of the performance variation that comes with it… so, I was given this injection that stopped everything”. However, P14 later described feeling misinformed when left to navigate the longer term consequences of her decision. “Where I think they dropped the ball was explaining how it would affect me long term… it was only recently that an OB-GYN [Obstetrician-gynaecologist] was like you’ve been on this way too long, you know bone density issues come with this injection and there could be lasting side effects with your fertility… I’ve been off it two years now, and I still don’t have regular periods”. Indeed, the suppression of the MC deemed necessary for athletic supremacy disconnected the menstruating athlete from their bodies, leaving them feeling disembodied and disempowered.

Stigma and fear of leakages

Across interviews, participants expressed a profound fear of leakages, and the associated stigma attached to bleeding bodies reproducing a concealment imperative.42 43 To help mitigate against this anticipated stigma, athletes described engaging in self-surveillance tactics,44 45 continuously monitoring and policing their bodies to avoid potential mishaps. For example, participants described doubling up on sanitary products and packing additional unisuits in anticipation of a potential leak. This hypervigilance appeared to be rooted in the long-standing notion that MC blood, as opposed to other forms of blood, is dirty and unsanitary and thus necessitates containment,42 43 as P8 described, “I don't want to smell like blood in front of my teammates, and I also like don't want them to be exposed to my blood, because I don't think that’s sanitary. And so, I try to prevent that whenever possible”.

Athletes’ internalisation of these stigmatising notions was further amplified by patriarchal ideals of what constitutes an attractive female body, tethering them to the ‘culture of concealment’.43 For example, P2 conformed to the patriarchal notion that menstruation is the antithesis of a sexually desirable feminine body,44 and engaged in impression management tactics,45 to render her MC invisible to the male gaze, as she describes “ I always wear a tampon when I'm rowing… it just looks better. If you see padding or bulking, you think ‘she’s on her period’. But a tampon is less noticeable… If it were another girl, you wouldn't mind. But if some boy looked at you and they could see that you're wearing a pad, I think you'd be a bit mortified… I feel like it’s just not the most attractive. It looks like you're wearing a nappy”.

Ultimately, athletes’ internalisation of taboos and stigmas surrounding menstruation impacted their performance (ie, by creating a distraction) and well-being by inciting feelings of disgust and shame. P6 described, “The bleeding component makes me feel really gross and disgusting. When I'm sitting in the boat, I feel like I'm marinating… I'm always so worried about bleeding through my unisuit as it’s happened before… when you get out of the boat and you see a little puddle of blood; it’s just dread and also grossness”.

Overall, management of the embodied realities of the MC placed affective burdens on menstruating rowers with implications for both comfort and performance.

Gendered norms and issues of non-disclosure

Participants highlighted a ‘gender barrier’ in discussing MC pain and symptoms with male coaches. The barriers to disclosure included their perception that male coaches would be unreceptive to discussing the MC; ‘not wanting to make them feel uncomfortable’ by disclosing MC issues; or being fearful that male coaches lack understanding and thus would negate their training altogether. Such barriers created emotional burdens for female rowers as they had to navigate these discomforts and the potential of being underestimated. For example, P18 described, “Some male coaches are just so bad at it [discussing MC], they don’t want to talk about it, and they don’t understand periods, so if you do say anything it’s just like ‘you don’t have to do this, don’t ask me anymore questions,’ that kind of a thing”.

These findings support the wider literature on gendered norms in sport that illustrate how male coaches may adjust their practices to the detriment of female athletes based on erroneous and gender-stereotypical assumptions about women’s own expectations and capacities for athletic excellence.16 Such misconceptions are internalised and/or consciously navigated by menstruating athletes who deny their MC as part of their embodiment. This internalisation further perpetuates a culture of non-disclosure, as some participants highlighted how they aimed to resist such gender stereotypes by actively choosing not to discuss their MC with male coaches. P13 shared, “Most of my coaches have been predominantly male and so I don’t want to add to that bias with a male coach, I don’t want to be treated differently because of that [period]… there are already a lot of stereotypes around it and that women are inferior or incapable because of that, or that you can’t do X,Y,Z because you’re out of commission or whatever”. This statement was further corroborated by other participants who reflected the idea that to achieve parity with male athletes, female athletes should not let menstruation impact them.

Overall, this theme illustrates the interrelated nature of how gendered norms are perceived and enacted on within rowing communities. Rather than cultures of non-disclosure being created exclusively by male coaches being unreceptive to discussing MC pain and symptoms, these cultures emerge in a more dynamic and reciprocal manner. Anticipating potential gender bias, female athletes chose not to discuss the MC with their male coaches for fear of being viewed as ‘weaker’, or to avoid the affective cost of being the first to break the silence. P16 described, “I feel like it would be an awkward conversation, and we both might be uncomfortable… I would be worried that they might see you a bit differently or think that there’s a weakness… I don’t think he [coach] would be too closed off, I just think it’s not really encouraged, and other people haven’t done it, so you don’t want to be the first one to go up and make that commotion”.

Period power

In contrast to the previous themes, this theme outlines the emergence of alternative sociocultural norms that challenge dominant ideals, thus expanding perceptions of how the MC is experienced and managed within sport settings. For example, several participants challenged the notion that the MC resulted in decreased performance, and instead emphatically depicted that ‘they perform better on their period’. Moreover, such participants outlined how their embodied understandings of the MC positively impacted their training and performance, as they aimed to harness the benefits of their biology. Such views align with increasing research and understanding around how to optimise training in line with the MC46 as well as contemporary narratives that promote the notion of ‘period power’.47

Consistent with these embodied understandings, some athletes described pushing back against the idea that they need to control or suppress their MC, instead opting for more ‘naturalistic’ methods of management. P11 shared, “My primary care practitioner recommended the oral contraceptive pill, but personally I didn’t want hormones pumping around my system, and even though I don’t like the symptoms I have, I don’t actually want my period to stop, I like the fact that I have it, which may seem strange”.

Some participants highlighted how the MC was openly discussed within rowing communities, a culture that was promoted and facilitated by male coaches. For example, some participants discussed how male coaches were interested to learn about the MC, were open and empathetic around MC discussions and actively tried to address stigma by bringing menstrual products to regattas. Finally, some participants aimed to challenge the taboo and stigma around menstruation by aiming to normalise the corporeal reality of leaking bodies. P7 described, “I feel very strongly about normalising menstruation as part of my experience and like sometimes, you know, leaks happen. And you know we get blood and pus and gross stuff on the oars anyway. You know it’s just something else to clean up”.

Such comments from participants reflect an effort by menstruating rowers to reclaim and reframe the MC as normative and capacitating rather than incapacitating of athletic excellence.

Discussion

Heeding calls for more research on the MC within sports science and sports medicine,1,5 this study extends the literature by highlighting how female rowers understand and experience the MC. The findings suggest that menstrual norms that include stigma and concealment maintain power over athlete menstruators and are amplified by the gendered norms of female athletic embodiment. While some individual coaches and athletes acted to defy such stereotypes, adopting a positive menstrual disposition was largely contingent on individual coaches and athletes with less evidence of collective approaches. Fundamentally, the stigma and concealment surrounding the MC created affective, emotional and physical burdens for female rowers, which ultimately can limit progression towards best practice management strategies.

Aligned with previous research, our analysis indicates that menstruation continues to be considered a hindrance to excellence in sport, and as such MC dysfunction is welcomed and normalised, with consequences that include a reluctance of menstruating athletes to raise it as a concern.3 48 Some medical professionals are complicit in perpetuating MC dysfunction as normative and using it to explain away amenorrhea, which can pose serious consequences to athletes’ long-term health.49 Rowing as such aligns with gendered analysis of other elite sports.2 3 48 However, our findings extend the literature by highlighting how MC dysfunction was not only normalised but also valorised within rowing subcultures, due to a commitment to ’slim to win’ ideologies. Conforming to the notion of a disciplined and predictable body, athletes also described wanting to ‘shut off’ or suppress their MC to minimise performance variability.3 This desire to control or regulate their reproductive health finds female athletes seeking to engage in practices that serve to alienate themselves from their bodies (eg, taking injections to stop everything). However, the danger of these disembodied practices is that it leaves female athletes vulnerable to misinformation (eg, it is normal not to have a period), harmful ideologies (eg, slim to win) and the uncritical acceptance of medical professionals’ opinions regarding the management of their MC.

Alongside aiming to minimise performance variability, part of the reason that the MC is considered to be a hindrance to athletic performance, we argue, is due to the affective, emotional and physical burdens that menstrual management places on female rowers. Such burdens are created due to the long-standing taboos and discourses that depict menstruation as unsanitary and in need of containment.42 43 Aligned with this view, any visible signs of menstruation become emblematic of a female’s contamination, stigmatising women as being unsanitary, unattractive and lacking bodily control.50 These norms were internalised by female rowers and observed in the way they engaged in self-objectification and self-policing strategies, viewing themselves from a third person perspective, and trying to anticipate and control how others, specifically males, viewed them.50 For example, female rowers describe choosing to wear tampons instead of sanitary pads to conceal their menstruation from the male gaze as well as ‘doubling up’ on sanitary products and packing additional unisuits to maintain the concealment imperative. Unique to the context of rowing, the physical demands of the sport (eg, long hours on the boat), lack of facilities, spatial proximity to other athletes and aesthetic clothing amplified female athletes’ concerns, resulting in hypervigilance and anticipatory actions. Although some athletes aimed to defy stigmatising notions of menstruation, normalising leaks by placing them in the same category as other bodily fluids, these views were not reflective of the norm. For others, failure to maintain menstrual secrecy was viewed as a failure in disciplined attempts to avoid the public spectacle of bleeding feminity,42 and thus created affective burdens for female rowers (eg, “when you get out of the boat and you see a little puddle of blood; it’s just dread and also grossness”).

These affective burdens were further observed in how the MC was discussed, negotiated and managed within interpersonal encounters between female rowers with male coaches. To expand, our findings align with previous research by highlighting how gender barriers with male coaches may limit MC discussion, due to assumptions around social discomfort and a lack of knowledge.8 That said, our findings extend previous research by articulating how a wider internalisation of gendered norms enacted within sporting communities maintains menstrual stigma. For example, in anticipation of stereotypes around female athletes being less capable than males,16 female rowers can become complicit in creating cultures of non-disclosure by subscribing to a form of neoliberal self-management, whereby the individual is seen as responsible for pushing back against these societal norms, by ‘not letting menstruation impact them’.51 Although positioned as acts of defiance, these practices can serve to perpetuate cultures of non-disclosure and stigma further by placing the onus on female athletes to ‘manage away’ the material realities of menstruating bodies, recasting gender inequity as a personal rather than a systemic issue.52 That said, while menstrual norms in general require adherence in societal terms to menstrual etiquette (eg, silence, concealment), there is evidence that some individual coaches and menstruators are resisting and reconfiguring expectations of how the MC is experienced, discussed and managed within sport settings. For example, some female athletes described how male coaches can act as allies, engaging in gender-responsive coaching, ensuring menstrual products are readily available, and normalising and promoting discussions around the MC. These findings highlight how menstrual normativity is a constantly negotiated achievement within sport settings, with potential for alternative perspectives and progressive management strategies to emerge. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that responsibility to adopt a positive menstrual disposition was largely contingent on individual coaches and female athletes, emphasising the need for more collective and systemic approaches.

Practical implications, limitations and future research directions

First, the findings amplify the need for increased awareness, education and signposting to evidence-based research and initiatives surrounding female reproductive health,12,14 to help counteract the reproduction of problematic narratives that compromise athletes’ long-term well-being. To date, there have been several initiatives within sport that aim to demystify and normalise conversations around the MC by enhancing access to educational resources and promoting collaboration between female athletes and sporting partners.12,14 While these initiatives represent progress in supporting female athletes, their integration into practice requires ongoing evaluation, as cultural change is often difficult to embed, and gaps may persist between policy initiatives and lived realities.

Second, central to progressing management of MC pain and person-centred care are female athletes’ voices and embodied understandings.20 However, our findings highlight how, in order to maintain menstrual etiquette (eg, concealment) and adhere to sporting norms, female rowers engaged in disembodied practice. To counteract the silencing and suppression of MC symptoms, future research could look towards more arts-based methodologies or body-mapping as a reflective tool that can help centralise athletes’ embodied understandings of the MC. Foregrounding bodily knowledge can help progress MC management by empowering female athletes to make informed decisions about their bodies in relation to training and performance.

Third, given the inter-related nature of how gendered norms are enacted within sport settings and how they act to maintain menstrual stigma and silence, offering opportunities for coaches and athletes to come together through collaborative and codesigned approaches are essential to fostering open dialogue around MC issues.53 Such collaborative approaches can help dismantle the inherent hierarchy between athletes and coaches and encourage communal discussions that generate solutions to the gendered norms perpetuating environments of non-disclosure and mismanagement of MC symptoms in sport.

Finally, our study was limited to rowers’ experiences and a predominantly white middle-class sample, which lacked cultural and ethnic diversity; future research could explore how sociocultural norms may differ across other sporting contexts and cultures. The trustworthiness and robustness of the findings could be improved by employing multiple, life-story or focus group interviews to enrich our understanding of how athletes’ experiences may change over time.

This study extends the literature by highlighting how sociocultural influences can perpetuate harmful discourses and practices that implicate athletes’ menstrual health and ultimately limit progression towards best practice management for female rowers. Overall, our research calls for the need to eliminate secrecy and stigma around the MC; improve communication between athletes and their entourage around MC effects and foreground female athletes’ embodied understandings to improve athlete health and performance.
