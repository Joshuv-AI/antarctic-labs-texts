Integrating single-cell transcriptomics and epigenetics in a multi-omics MR framework identifies PARK7 as a causal gene in streptococcal septicemia
Authors: Hanghang Han, Enhao Huang, Dan Liu, Yingying Zhang, Siyu Zhou, Lizhen Huang, Xueke Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStreptococcal septicemia is associated with high mortality and morbidity, yet the underlying epigenetic mechanisms, particularly those involving mitochondrial genes, remain poorly understood. This study employed a multi-omics Mendelian randomization (MR) framework to investigate the causal role of mitochondrial gene regulation in streptococcal septicemia, with an emphasis on epigenetic influences.MethodsWe utilized genetic instruments—including cis-methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs), cis-expression QTLs (eQTLs), and cis-protein QTLs (pQTLs)—for 1,136 mitochondrial-related genes from MitoCarta3.0. These were analyzed for causal associations with streptococcal septicaemia (FinnGen R12: 3,239 cases, 439,048 controls). Epigenetic and transcriptomic data were integrated with MR analyses of 731 immune cell traits. Single-cell RNA sequencing (GSE175453: 4 sepsis patients, 5 controls) and bulk transcriptomic data (GSE57065: 28 septic shock patients, 25 controls) were used for validation. Additionally, single-cell eQTLs for PARK7 across 14 immune cell types were applied in MR to evaluate cell-type-specific causality.ResultsMR analyses identified 280 mQTLs, 79 eQTLs, and 29 pQTLs with causal links to streptococcal septicemia. Integrative multi-omics analysis revealed PARK7 as the only gene consistently implicated across epigenetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic levels. Epigenetic regulation via mQTLs was notably prominent. Single-cell RNA sequencing demonstrated significant PARK7 upregulation in T and NK cells from sepsis patients, corroborated by elevated PARK7 expression in septic shock patients across multiple timepoints. Temporal correlation analyses indicated that PARK7 levels inversely correlated with depleted CD8 + T cells, naive CD4 + T cells, and resting NK cells. Critically, sc-eQTL MR confirmed that genetically predicted PARK7 upregulation in two immune subsets—CD4 + KLRB1+ T cells and CD4 + KLRB1- T cells—causally increased septicemia risk.ConclusionOur multi-omics genetic approach establishes PARK7 as a causal gene in streptococcal septicemia, mediated in part by epigenetic mechanisms. PARK7 upregulation in specific T and NK cell subsets contributes to susceptibility, linking mitochondrial epigenetic regulation to immune dysfunction and underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-026-00673-2.

Introduction

Streptococcal septicemia is a life-threatening systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by infection, with conditions such as Group B Streptococcus (GBS) sepsis exhibiting a 7% mortality rate and a high incidence of severe sequelae in survivors [1, 2]. While genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered several genetic susceptibility loci, the definitive causal genes and their regulatory mechanisms—particularly the epigenetic pathways influencing immune cell dysfunction—remain largely obscure [3–5]. Mitochondria, central to cellular energy and signaling, are critical regulators of immune responses [6–8]. Nevertheless, a systematic causal investigation into how mitochondrial genes, and specifically their epigenetic regulation, contribute to septicemia is still lacking.

Critical gaps persist in this field. First, GWAS findings are often unable to pinpoint the true causal genes due to linkage disequilibrium, and they typically do not incorporate functional epigenetic layers such as DNA methylation [9, 10]. Second, while genetic effects can operate across multiple molecular tiers, there is a notable scarcity of studies that integrate epigenomic data (e.g., from methylation quantitative trait loci, mQTLs) with transcriptomic and proteomic information to identify core regulators with consistent causal effects across these levels [11, 12]. Furthermore, the cell type-specific epigenetic mechanisms through which key genes mediate susceptibility within the immune landscape are yet to be defined [13, 14].

To address these questions, we applied an integrated multi-omics Mendelian randomization (MR) framework, with a specific emphasis on epigenetic regulation. We systematically utilized cis-methylation quantitative trait loci (cis-mQTLs), alongside cis-expression QTLs (cis-eQTLs) and cis-protein QTLs (cis-pQTLs) of mitochondria-related genes, as genetic instruments to infer causality with streptococcal septicemia. This approach aimed to identify key genes whose influence is consistent from epigenetics to protein levels. Furthermore, we integrated MR analyses of 731 immune traits, single-cell RNA sequencing from septic patients, and single-cell eQTL (sc-eQTL) mapping to validate candidate genes and delineate their cell-type-specific causal functions within the immune system.

This study not only seeks to identify core genetic and epigenetic regulators of streptococcal septicemia but also to elucidate their pathogenic roles within specific immune cell subsets. Our findings are expected to provide novel insights into the epigenetics of sepsis immunopathology and may inform the development of future targeted therapies.

Methods

Ethics

No ethical approval was required for this study as all analyses were based on publicly available summary-level statistics with no access to individual-level data.

Research workflow

The research workflow of this study was conducted according to Fig. 1. Based on the outlined workflow, this comprehensive study was designed to systematically investigate the genetic and epigenetic underpinnings of streptococcal septicemia through an integrated multi-omics approach. Initially, we selected instruments for 1,136 mitochondrial-related genes from human MitoCarta3.0, using cis-eQTLs, cis-pQTLs, and - with a specific focus on epigenetic regulation - cis-mQTLs—all derived from European populations—to perform MR analyses against streptococcal septicemia. We aimed to identify key genes showing consistent statistical significance across all three molecular levels, with particular interest in those regulated at the epigenetic level, which led to the prioritization of PARK7. In parallel, we examined 731 immune phenotypes as exposures in MR to delineate alterations in immune and blood cell profiles associated with streptococcal septicemia, thereby informing which cell types may mediate PARK7’s role.

Subsequently, single-cell sequencing data from 5 healthy controls and 4 sepsis patients, along with transcriptomic data from 25 healthy controls and 28 septic shock patients—sampled at 30 min, 24 h, and 48 h post-onset—were utilized to validate MR findings and elucidate dynamic changes in PARK7 expression across 22 immune cell types. Finally, single-cell eQTL data for PARK7 from 14 immune cell types were applied in a follow-up MR analysis to confirm its critical and cell-type-specific role in streptococcal septicemia, thereby closing the loop from genetic and epigenetic association to functional validation.

Exposure variables and selection of instrumental variables

The exposure variable in this study was a gene set of human mitochondria-related genes (Human MitoCarta3.0, comprising 1,136 genes; source: https://www.broadinstitute.org/) [15]. Genetic instrumental variables (IVs) associated with this gene set were selected for analysis: Instrumental variables for gene epigenetic regulation (DNA methylation levels, cis-mQTLs) obtained from the GoDMC database (http://mqtldb.godmc.org.uk/downloads) [16]. To ensure the relevance of epigenetic instruments to the target genes, CpG sites related to the target genes were extracted from the Illumina HumanMethylation450k chip annotation database. Potential confounding variants were excluded to ensure specificity of the association between the IVs and the exposure. Instrumental variables for gene expression levels (cis-eQTLs) were sourced from the eQTLGen database (https://www.eqtlgen.org/cis-eqtls.html; European population) [11], based on eQTL data significantly associated with the target genes. Instrumental variables for protein expression levels (cis-pQTLs) were derived from a combination of the deCODE database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/downloads/summary-statistics) [17] and the UKB-Pharma Proteomics project (UKB-PPP) [18] (https://www.synapse.org/Synapse:syn51364943/wiki/622119). Additionally, genetic IVs for 731 immune phenotypes (GCST90001391–GCST90002121) were extracted from the OpenGWAS project [19], using a significance threshold of P < 1 × 10-5 and a clumping r² < 0.001 . Unless otherwise indicated, all two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses adhered to the following principles. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with the selected loci were further screened as IVs. In conducting Mendelian randomization analysis, it is essential to select genetic variants that serve as strong and valid instrumental variables. To ensure robustness and minimize bias, a stringent genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10− 8 is applied for selecting single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the exposure. Furthermore, to avoid confounding due to linkage disequilibrium, a clumping procedure is performed with an r² threshold of less than 0.1 [20], thereby ensuring that the selected instruments are largely independent of each other. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analytical approach. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The F-statistic was computed to evaluate IV strength (only SNPs with F > 10 were retained). Given the unique characteristics of the single-cell eQTL data for the 14 immune cell types, the conventional genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10− 8 was not applicable for filtering genetic instruments [21]. Instead, for the exposure variable—PARK7 expression levels in these 14 immune cell types—we selected SNPs based on a threshold of P < 0.05 and an r² < 0.3, and an F-statistic > 10 to perform the Mendelian randomization analysis.

Outcome data sources

Data for streptococcal septicaemia were obtained from the FinnGen project (release R12), including 3,239 cases and 439,048 controls.

Mendelian randomization analysis

This study employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) framework to assess the causal relationship between exposures and outcomes. The IVW method served as the primary analytical approach, supplemented by MR-Egger regression, the weighted median method, and MR-PRESSO for additional validation and bias assessment. To ensure the robustness of the results, comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted, including: MR-PRESSO to detect and remove outlier instrumental variables (global test P-value > 0.05); Cochran’s Q test to evaluate heterogeneity (P-value > 0.05); MR-Egger intercept test to assess horizontal pleiotropy (P-value > 0.05).

Construction of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

The genes implicated by the significant mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL loci were submitted to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to query known and predicted protein-protein interactions [22]. The resulting interaction network was downloaded and imported into Cytoscape software for further analysis. Hub nodes within the network were identified using the cytoHubba plugin, applying the Degree algorithm to rank proteins by their connectivity. The final PPI network visualization was generated using R software.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

This study analyzed the public dataset GSE175453, which includes single-cell RNA-seq data of peripheral blood circulating immune cells from 4 sepsis patients and 5 healthy controls [23]. Immune cell subsets—including Monocyte, T cells, B cell, HSC_G-CSF, Platelets, CMP, and NK cell—were annotated and subjected to differential expression analysis to identify key genes significantly altered in sepsis.

Transcriptomic data analysis

To further validate dynamic expression changes of key genes, this study analyzed the transcriptomic dataset GSE57065 [24], which contains gene expression data from 28 patients with septic shock and 25 healthy controls. Whole-blood transcriptomic profiles at three time points—30 min, 24 h, and 48 h after shock—were specifically examined. Immune cell composition (22 subsets) was estimated to reveal temporal expression trajectories of key genes associated with disease progression and immune response. Furthermore, we selected an additional dataset for validation. The GSE185263 dataset comprises whole-blood RNA sequencing data generated using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (GPL16791). The dataset comprises a total of 392 human samples, collected from 348 sepsis patients and 44 healthy control subjects. This dataset was utilized in the present study to perform an independent validation of PARK7 expression levels.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses in this study—including Mendelian randomization, single-cell data preprocessing (cell clustering, annotation, and differential expression), transcriptomic analysis, and sensitivity tests—were performed using R software (version 4.5.1).

Results

MR identifies 280 mQTLs with causal effects on streptococcal septicemia

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed to investigate the causal relationship between epigenetic regulation, as proxied by DNA methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs), and susceptibility to streptococcal septicemia. We identified 280 statistically significant mQTL loci causally associated with the disease (Fig. 2, Table S1). Among these, high methylation levels at 116 loci conferred a protective effect against streptococcal septicemia, whereas increased methylation at the remaining 164 loci was associated with an elevated risk of developing the disease. Given that methylation primarily functions to regulate the expression of proximal genes, we mapped the significant methylation sites to their corresponding genes. This mapping revealed that 100 methylation sites were uniquely associated with individual genes, while 180 sites corresponded to 60 genes. Notably, the methylation probe cg10385390, associated with the PARK7 gene, exhibited the most significant MR association (P = 2.95 × 10⁻⁰⁸), with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.665–0.823). This suggests that epigenetic silencing via hypermethylation at cg10385390 may reduce an individual’s susceptibility to streptococcal septicemia, potentially by suppressing the expression of the PARK7 gene. This finding positions PARK7 as a prime candidate whose disease-associated effect is likely mediated, at least in part, through an epigenetic mechanism.

MR identifies 79 eQTLs with causal effects on streptococcal septicemia

To explore the causal effects of gene expression on streptococcal septicemia, we utilized blood-derived eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium (Fig. 3). Our analysis identified 79 significant eQTLs. Increased expression of 45 genes was protective against the disease, while higher expression of 34 genes was associated with increased susceptibility. The most significant associations were observed for CMC2 (P = 4.39 × 10⁻⁰⁷, OR: 1.239, 95% CI: 1.14–1.346) and PARK7 (P = 1.09 × 10⁻⁰⁵, OR: 1.213, 95% CI: 1.113–1.322), indicating that elevated expression of these genes heightens susceptibility to streptococcal septicemia.

MR identifies 29 pQTLs with causal effects on streptococcal septicemia

To further investigate causal effects at the protein level, we performed MR analysis using protein QTLs (pQTLs) from two sources: the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) and the deCODE database. Analyses identified 10 and 22 pQTLs from UKB-PPP and deCODE, respectively, that were causally linked to streptococcal septicemia. After removing duplicates, a total of 29 unique pQTLs remained (Fig. 4A, B). Two proteins, PARK7 and ACADSB, showed consistent effects across both databases, with increased plasma levels associated with higher disease susceptibility. To integrate the findings from the mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL analyses, a PPI network, comprising 224 nodes and 1388 interactions, was constructed to elucidate the functional relationships among the proteins encoded by genes identified in the QTL analyses (Fig. 5). Nodes are color-coded: orange for mQTLs, green for deCODE pQTLs, blue for UKB-PPP pQTLs, and purple for eQTLs; nodes identified across multiple QTL types are represented with mixed colors. Notably, the node with the highest degree centrality was SOD2 (Fig. S1), a key gene at the core of the network. Previous studies have demonstrated that SOD2, carried by neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicles, alleviates septic coagulopathy by scavenging endothelial reactive oxygen species [25]. Li et al. [26]. also found that SOD2, upregulated via SIRT1/FOXO1a, clears mitochondrial ROS to alleviate sepsis-induced liver injury.

Integrative analysis of mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL MR identifies PARK7 as a key gene

Our multi-omics MR approach systematically investigated 1136 mitochondrial genes, with a foundational layer of analysis focusing on epigenetic regulation via DNA methylation. From an initial set of 6670 CpG sites (step1), 4727 passed quality control and were included in the two-sample MR analysis (step2), ultimately identifying 280 causal mQTLs (step3). Similarly, we identified 79 causal eQTLs, 10 pQTLs from UKB-PPP, and 22 pQTLs from the deCODE database (Fig. 6A). These 32 causal signals corresponded to 29 unique pQTLs after removing three duplicates. Integration of the genes corresponding to these four sets of significant QTLs revealed a single gene, PARK7, that was consistently implicated across all molecular layers (mQTL, eQTL, pQTL-PPP, and pQTL-deCODE) (Fig. 6B). The data suggest a complex epigenetic and transcriptional regulatory role for PARK7: hypermethylation at the cg10385390 site appears to be protective, potentially by reducing PARK7 expression (Fig. 6C). Conversely, other methylation sites (cg12809925, cg00042356), indicating that the epigenetic influence on PARK7 is not uniform, alongside the identified eQTLs and pQTLs, suggest that increased PARK7 gene and protein expression promotes disease susceptibility (Fig. 6C). These opposing epigenetic effects, likely dependent on specific genomic context of the CpG sites, highlight the intricate nature of epigenetic regulation and warrant further investigation.

MR identifies 34 immune cell types with causal effects on streptococcal septicemia

Given the close relationship between mitochondria and immune function, we extended our MR analysis to 731 immune cell traits to identify those causally influencing streptococcal septicemia. This analysis identified 34 immune cell types or states with significant causal effects (Fig. 7). Strikingly, the most frequently implicated cell type was T-regulatory (Treg) cells, with 14 distinct Treg traits showing significant associations, suggesting a potentially important role for Tregs in the pathogenesis of streptococcal septicemia.

Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals elevated PARK7 expression in T cells and NK cells of sepsis patients

To validate and contextualize our genetic findings, we analyzed a public single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE175453) comprising peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 4 sepsis patients and 5 healthy controls. Unsupervised clustering identified major immune cell lineages, including Monocytes, T cells, B cells, Hematopoietic Stem/Progenitor Cells (HSC_G-CSF), Platelets, Common Myeloid Progenitors (CMP), and Natural Killer (NK) cells (Fig. S2A-D). We visualized the proportions of immune cell subsets in the two sample groups using stacked bar charts. The results revealed that, compared with the Control group, the Sepsis group exhibited elevated levels of Monocytes and decreased levels of Platelets (Fig. S3). To visually illustrate the expression distribution characteristics of the PARK7 gene across different groups, we performed UMAP dimensionality reduction and visualization analysis on the Control and Sepsis groups, respectively (Fig. 8A). Differential expression analysis of PARK7 across these cell types revealed a significant upregulation of PARK7 specifically in T cells and NK cells from sepsis patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 8B, C). No significant differences in PARK7 expression were observed in Monocytes, B cells, Platelets, CMP, or HSC_G-CSF (Fig. 8D-H).

PARK7 transcript levels are elevated in the blood of septic shock patients

Analysis of the GSE57065 dataset, which includes blood samples from 28 septic shock patients and 25 healthy volunteers, confirmed the translational relevance of our findings. PARK7 transcript levels were significantly elevated in the blood of septic shock patients at 30 min, 24 h, and 48 h after shock onset compared to controls (Fig. 9A-C). A summary plot visualizing PARK7 levels across all time points further illustrates this sustained increase (Fig. 9D). Furthermore, we selected an additional dataset for validation. The GSE185263 dataset is a comprehensive multi-center transcriptomic study comprising whole-blood RNA-seq data from 392 human samples, collected from 348 sepsis patients and 44 healthy control subjects. Differential expression analysis demonstrated that PARK7 was among the genes showing both significant upregulation and statistical significance in the sepsis group (Fig. S4). These findings from a rigorously designed international cohort provide corroborating evidence that PARK7 expression is increased in sepsis, consistent with our initial findings.

Septic shock is associated with depletion of T cells CD8, T cells CD4 naive, and NK cells resting

The immune landscape undergoes dynamic changes during sepsis progression. A bar chart illustrates the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes at different time points in septic shock (Fig. 10A). Compared to healthy controls, septic shock patients at the 30-minute time point exhibited significant increases in Plasma cells, Monocytes, Macrophages M0, Macrophages M1, Eosinophils, and Neutrophils, alongside significant decreases in naive B cells, memory B cells, CD8 + T cells, naive CD4 + T cells, and resting NK cells (Fig. 10B). At 24 h, resting Mast cells were significantly increased, while activated CD4 + memory T cells and activated Dendritic cells were decreased (Fig. 10B). By 48 h, the significant differences for memory B cells and M1 Macrophages were no longer observed (Fig. 10B). Critically, the levels of CD8 + T cells, naive CD4 + T cells, and resting NK cells remained persistently and significantly lower in patients across all three time points compared to controls (Fig. 10B), suggesting that the depletion of these cell populations may be a key feature influencing sepsis progression.

We performed a correlation analysis between PARK7 levels and the abundances of 22 immune cell types across the different time points in septic shock (30 min, 24 h, 48 h) compared to the control state. The results, visualized in a lollipop chart (Fig. 11), revealed that PARK7 levels were significantly positively correlated with Macrophages M0, Monocytes, and Plasma cells. Mendelian randomization using PARK7 sc-eQTL identifies a causal effect of its expression on streptococcal septicemia

Finally, sc-eQTL analysis was employed to investigate the causal effect of cell-type-specific PARK7 expression (PARK7 eQTLs) on disease risk. This analysis identified two specific immune cell subtypes for which increased PARK7 eQTL levels were causally associated with higher susceptibility to streptococcal septicemia (Fig. 12): CD4 + KLRB1+ T cells and CD4 + KLRB1- T cells. Therefore, PARK7 expression in specific immune cells causally increases septicemia risk.

Discussion

Although previous studies have revealed aberrant DNA methylation and mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with sepsis compared to healthy individuals [27–29], the causal relationship between these epigenetic alterations, particularly those in mitochondrial genes, and streptococcal septicemia remains largely unknown. In this study, we employed Mendelian randomization (MR) to systematically integrate large-scale epigenetic (mQTL) data of mitochondria-related genes to elucidate their causal association with streptococcal septicemia. By further integrating transcriptional (eQTL) and translational (pQTL) data, we identified PARK7 as the only mitochondria-related gene consistently implicated from the epigenetic to the proteomic level, specifically at the mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL levels. The causal role of PARK7 was further substantiated by its cell-type-specific upregulation in sepsis patients and scMR analysis confirming that genetically predicted higher PARK7 expression in specific immune subsets causally increases disease risk. These findings collectively position PARK7 as a pivotal player in the immunopathogenesis of streptococcal septicemia, with epigenetic regulation being a key component of its causal pathway.

First and foremost, our cross-omics convergence on PARK7 robustly addresses a key limitation of conventional GWAS—the difficulty in pinpointing causal genes from linkage disequilibrium blocks. The consistent causal associations across mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL data provide strong genetic evidence for PARK7’s involvement, transcending the correlation-based inferences typical of observational studies. Crucially, the inclusion of mQTL data provides a direct link to epigenetic mechanisms. The direction of effects reveals a complex epigenetic regulatory paradigm: hypermethylation at the cg10385390 site was protective, likely through transcriptional repression of PARK7, whereas other genetic instruments for increased PARK7 expression and protein levels consistently elevated disease risk. This suggests that fine-tuned epigenetic and transcriptional regulation of PARK7 is critical, where its overexpression is detrimental, a hypothesis strongly supported by our validation analyses.

It is noteworthy that the role of PARK7 (DJ-1) in sepsis appears complex and context-dependent. Elevated serum PARK7 levels are associated with an increased risk of acute lung injury in patients with severe sepsis [30], suggesting its potential as a biomarker for adverse outcomes. In contrast, experimental studies indicate that PARK7 protects against sepsis by interacting with p47phox to promote NADPH oxidase-dependent ROS production, thereby modulating inflammation and improving survival in murine models [31]. However, other research shows that PARK7 deficiency can enhance bacterial clearance and reduce organ injury [32–34], highlighting its multifaceted functions in sepsis pathogenesis that require further elucidation. These apparent discrepancies may be explained by the dualistic functions of PARK7 in different cell types, disease stages, or its differential epigenetic regulation, highlighting the need for context-specific investigations.

The relevance of our genetic findings is strongly corroborated by the transcriptional and protein-level data from sepsis patients. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis pinpointed the specific immune contexts where PARK7 is dysregulated, revealing significant upregulation in T cells and NK cells from patients. This cell-type-specific expression pattern provides a plausible mechanistic link to our parallel MR analysis of immune traits, which identified T-regulatory (Treg) cells as the most frequently implicated cell type. The sustained elevation of PARK7 protein in the blood of septic shock patients over 48 h further underscores its potential role not just in initiation but also in the perpetuation of the dysregulated immune response. The temporal correlation analysis, showing PARK7 levels were negatively associated with the abundances of CD8 + T cells, naive CD4 + T cells, and resting NK cells—cell types we found to be persistently depleted in patients—suggests that PARK7 may be involved in the lymphopenia and NK cell dysfunction that characterize sepsis immunopathology.

Recent studies highlight key immune cell alterations in sepsis pathogenesis. PD-L1 + plasma cells suppress T lymphocyte proliferation and IFNɣ secretion, correlating with increased mortality in patients and murine models [35]. Additionally, a unique CD4 + Icos + T cell subset in lymph fluid differentiates into regulatory T cells, reducing inflammation and organ injury in early sepsis [36]. Therapeutic approaches such as melatonin inhibit CD4 + T cell apoptosis via the Bcl-2/BAX pathway, improving survival in septic mice [37]. Therefore, our current research reveals significant alterations in key immune cells within the blood of septic patients, thereby underscoring the importance of these findings for promising strategic directions in sepsis therapy.

A key innovation of our study lies in extending the Mendelian randomization framework to single-cell resolution expression quantitative trait loci (sc-eQTLs), enabling direct testing of the causal effect of gene expression within specific immune cell subtypes. While previous studies have linked bulk tissue eQTLs from blood to disease, such analyses cannot resolve the specific cellular context from which genetic effects originate, substantially limiting biological interpretation. We addressed this gap by integrating sc-eQTL data across 14 immune cell types and performing sc-eQTL analysis. This approach allowed us to causally demonstrate, for the first time, that genetically elevated PARK7 expression in two specific immune cell subtypes—CD4 + KLRB1+ and KLRB1 − T cells—directly increases the risk of streptococcal septicemia. This finding not only elevates the role of PARK7 from a general association to a cell-context-dependent causal driver but also reveals that its pathogenic mechanism is highly context-specific, providing novel genetic evidence for understanding its cell-type-specific functions in sepsis immunopathology.

Despite the strengths of our multi-omics design and validation, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the primary GWAS and QTL data are derived from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Second, while MR strongly suggests causality, the exact molecular mechanisms by which PARK7 influences immune cell function and sepsis susceptibility remain to be fully elucidated through experimental studies. For instance, the opposing effects of different methylation sites on PARK7 represent a specific knowledge gap in understanding the context-dependent epigenetic regulation of this gene and require further investigation.

In conclusion, through a comprehensive genetic and translational approach that prioritized epigenetic interrogation, we establish PARK7 as a key causal gene in streptococcal septicemia. Our study delineates a pathway from genetic variation and epigenetic regulation to multi-omics alterations, culminating in cell-type-specific immune dysregulation. The consistent signal across molecular layers, with a foundational epigenetic component, and the validation in patient samples make PARK7 a compelling candidate for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations. Future research should focus on elucidating the precise role of PARK7 in T cell and NK cell biology during infection, the dynamics of its epigenetic regulation in sepsis, and on evaluating its potential as a biomarker for patient stratification or as a novel therapeutic target in sepsis.

Conclusion

Integrated multi-omics MR establishes PARK7 as a causal gene in streptococcal septicemia, mediated by epigenetic regulation and causally increasing risk via its upregulation in specific T and NK cell subsets.
