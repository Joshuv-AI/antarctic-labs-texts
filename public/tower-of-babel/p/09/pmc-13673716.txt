Chinese sacbrood virus destroys AcPPAN-copper homeostasis to evade phenoloxidase-based antiviral immunity in Apis cerana
Authors: Yaxi Guo, Huitong Qiu, Yang Qi, Hua Bai, Yingshuo Huang, Ting An, Yueyu Ma, Ming Li, Li Sun, Dongliang Fei, Mingxiao Ma, Yuming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Chinese sacbrood virus (CSBV) poses a serious threat to the Apis cerana, a major plant and crop pollinator. CSBV primarily infects larvae early in their development causing liquefaction of the larval epidermis preventing normal pupation. Bee larvae can resist viral infection through immune responses, with phenoloxidase (PO) being a key link between host cellular and humoral immunity. It is unknown whether host genes can modulate the PO cascade to affect viral replication. Herein, we performed RNA-Seq on CSBV-infected Apis cerana larvae and identify expression of the gene Peter Pan (AcPPAN), the gene had no significant effect on the development of larvae.ResultsAfter silencing AcPPAN, we found that the immune response of the larvae is suppressed and promoted CSBV replication. Subsequently, we fluorescently labeled copper ions in the larval hemolymph and found that AcPPAN responds to CSBV infection and is involved in limiting virus-induced copper accumulation within the hemolymph.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that CSBV promotes viral replication in larvae by suppressing AcPPAN expression, elevating hemolymph copper ion concentration, and inhibiting phenoloxidase-mediated antiviral immune responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05736-w.Keywords: CSBV, AcPPAN, Apis cerana, Phenoloxidase, Antiviral immune response

Introduction

Honey bees are social insects that play a crucial role in the pollination of plants, including crops important for human survival [1]. Currently, Apis mellifera and Apis cerana are the main cultivated bee species. However, bee viruses constantly threaten the health of these colonies, causing significant economic losses to global beekeeping and agricultural production [2]. Among these, the Chinese sacbrood virus (CSBV) is particularly harmful to Apis cerana, as its infection can lead to colony collapse [3, 4]. CSBV is derived from the Sacbrood virus (SBV), an iflavirus consisting of positive-strand RNA that has evolved to a variety of geographical distributions [5]. The genome is 8.8 kb long, encoding a large open reading frame, with structural genes located at the 5’ end encoding four capsid proteins: VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4 [6]. CSBV has two primary modes of larval transmission: it can infect larvae horizontally when adult worker bees feed them, or it can be transmitted vertically from mother to offspring [7]. It mainly infects young larvae, causing their heads to grow upwards and darken, form blisters, and ultimately fail to pupate normally, leading to death [8]. Although infected adult bees show less severe symptoms than larvae, they exhibit lethargic behavior, reduced lifespan, and eventually abandon the colony [9]. In previous studies, we found that CSBV was more susceptible to 4-day-old larvae and determined that 48 h of infection (when a large number of copies of the virus could be detected in the larvae) was the optimal time for detection. Here, we investigate the relationship between CSBV infection and the host bee immune response, aiming to understand its underlying pathogenic mechanisms and identify effective antiviral targets.

Like other arthropods, bees possess a relatively complete immune defense system against pathogenic microorganisms [10]that can be divided into cellular immunity and humoral immunity. Cellular immunity primarily involves the aggregation of blood cells, such as plasma cells, to eliminate invading pathogens through phagocytosis and encapsulation [11]. Toll and Imd signaling pathways, which are responsible for generating anti-infection immune effectors [12]. Immune effectors mainly include melanin, antimicrobial peptides, and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which directly participate in pathogen elimination. In addition to effector proteins, the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway represents an important and well-documented antiviral immune mechanism in insects [13–17].

Cellular immunity and humoral immunity are closely linked, both working together to recognize and eliminate pathogens [18]. In insects, a key component connecting cellular immunity and humoral immunity is the phenoloxidase (PO) system. Following pathogen recognition, blood cells promote the cleavage of serine proteases, activating prophenoloxidase (ProPO) to form active PO, which catalyzes the oxidation of phenolic substances into quinonoid compounds. These compounds polymerize to form melanin, which encapsulates and eliminates pathogens through a process known as melanization [19]. The quinonoid compounds produced in the PO cascade, along with ROS, have a direct lethal effect on pathogens [20]. ROS also further activate humoral immune signaling pathways (such as Imd and Toll) to inhibit viral infection [21, 22].

PO is a copper-binding enzyme, and the availability of copper ions (Cu+) is crucial for its catalytic oxidation and activity regulation [23, 24]. Studies have shown that low concentrations of Cu+ can transform PO into its enzymatically active form, while high concentrations of Cu+ significantly inhibit the enzyme’s activity [25, 26]. Research suggests that rice resists viral infections by inducing copper transport genes HMA5 and COPT to promote copper accumulation in the stem [27]. In a copper-dependent process with micromolar affinity, SARS-CoV-2 Nsp1 protein suppresses the host immune response by binding to the human 40 S ribosome [28]. In addition to their impact on viruses, Cu+ also play a regulatory role in bacterial infections. Copper enhances the immune response mediated by ALPK1 kinase, activating cells to inhibit bacterial infections [29]. Therefore, an exploration of how Cu+ regulate bee immune responses through PO activity is needed.

Peter Pan protein (AcPPAN) is a conserved member of the Brix protein family, serving as a ribosome biogenesis factor that directly interacts with rRNA to mediate the processing of rRNA precursors [30]. It protects mitochondrial structure and participates in mitochondrial apoptosis to regulate normal mitosis in cells, but it is not essential for general biosynthesis or DNA replication [31]. The absence or overexpression of AcPPAN in fruit flies disrupts normal development in adult flies [32]. This indicates that AcPPAN plays an indispensable role in cell growth, but its function in regulating immune responses remains unknown.

In this study, we report a novel instance and function of AcPPAN in Apis cerana, which plays a role in CSBV infection. The study found that CSBV infection inhibits AcPPAN expression, affects Cu⁺ accumulation in the larval hemolymph, and suppresses PO activity, ultimately inhibiting the PO cascade reaction including melanization, thereby reducing the production of ROS and other antiviral chemical substances. Collectively, these cascading effects ultimately promote CSBV replication.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The study involves Apis cerana, which is neither an endangered species nor a protected species. Bee samples were collected from experimental colonies maintained by the College of Animal Science and Veterinary Medicine at Jinzhou Medical University (JZMU) in Liaoning, China. The apiary is owned by the College of Animal Science and Veterinary Medicine and is not privately owned or protected in any way. The aforementioned research does not require specific permissions.

Raising Apis cerana larvae and CSBV infection

Three-day-old larvae were selected from healthy colonies and cultured in a 48-well plate at 32 °C and 75% humidity in an incubator. The larvae were fed with artificially formulated larval feed, with the feed replaced daily at noon, and any deceased larvae were also removed at this time. After 24 h of cultivation, the virus was administered orally: the larvae were transferred to a small cup, and then 1 ml of larval food containing 1 µl of 1 × 105 copies/µl CSBV was also added to the cup. The cups were then weighed. The experimental group of larvae was then transferred to a 6-ml cup and returned to the incubator for 6.5 h. Afterward, the larvae were removed and washed twice with ultrapure water, then placed into a new 48-well plate. The small cup was weighed again, and the among fed was calculated as the difference between the two weigh-ins; this accounted for the evaporation amount and the weight carried by the larvae when removed.

RNA sequencing and gene enrichment analysis

Apis cerana larvae (control/infect), 4 days old, following the treatment, whole bodies were homogenized in 1 mL of TRIzol (Invitrogen), and RNA was extracted with the phenol–chloroform method following the manufacturer’s instruction. The RNA was finally resuspended in 30 µL of nuclease-free water. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the Total RNA-Seq Kit version 2 (Takara/Clontech) was used for library preparation. All libraries were analyzed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and quantified via real-time PCR prior to sequencing. Trimmomatic (version 0.38) was applied to trim low-quality bases. StringTie software, with default parameters, was utilized to calculate gene expression. Gene expression profiling was based on the number of reads. To estimate expression values and transcript levels, TPM (Transcripts Per Million mapped reads) values were adopted. DESeq2 was chosen for the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes with an adjusted Pvalue (padj) < 0.05 and abs (log2(fold change)) > 1 were assigned as DEGs. The term enriched Gene ontology (GO) is used in honeybee. Fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster) were used as the closest species in the database, and REVIGO clusters with moderate similarity were used. GO enrichment analysis was statistically evaluated using the hypergeometric test, and the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to correct for multiple testing in order to control the false discovery rate (FDR). GO terms with an adjusted P value (padj) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

RNA extraction and PCR amplification

Following the manufacturer’s guidelines, total RNA was isolated from the larvae using Total RNA Isolation Reagent (Biosharp, Beijing, China). A reverse transcription kit (RevertAid RT, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to synthesize cDNA (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). PCR amplification was carried out following the method of Mingxiao M et al. [3]for the detection of CSBV” to avoid unnecessary repetition.

Analysis of gene expression

For gene expression analysis, the collected larvae had their RNA extracted using the Total RNA Isolation Reagent (Biosharp), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sample concentration and purity were quantified using a Nanodrop ONE instrument (Thermo Fisher). A reverse transcription kit was used to synthesize cDNA (RevertAid RT, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Gene relative expression levels were determined via qPCR, utilizing the QuantStudio 3 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR detection system (Thermo Fisher). The qPCR reaction system contained a total volume of 20 µl, comprising 0.8 µl primer, 10 µl SYBR, 0.4 µl ROX, 1 µl cDNA template, and 7.8 µl water. The temperature profile consisted of denaturation at 50 °C for 2 min, followed by a second denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, then 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and combined annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. A melting curve was generated after each run (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 20 s, increasing by 0.3 °C until reaching 95 °C for 15 s), followed by qPCR data analysis. The β-actin gene served as the internal control, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each sample was tested in triplicate on an 8-well plate. Reactions using water without RNA as a template were used as a negative control. (in Table 1)

CSBV purification

First, CSBV-infected larvae were ground, and an equal volume of ultrapure water was then added to them. The mixture was then subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles and centrifuged at 4℃ at 1100 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was retained and subsequently centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min, still at 4℃, after which this supernatant was also retained. Finally, the retained supernatant was centrifuged again, at 8000 rpm and 4℃, for 30 min. The virus copy concentration of the final supernatant was then measured using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and stored at − 80℃ .

Determination of CSBV Copy Levels

The concentration of CSBV was determined using absolute quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) with a standard curve method. A 161 bp fragment of CSBV was amplified using forward and reverse primers. qPCR was carried out with a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Thermo Fisher) [33, 34]. The 20 µL qPCR reaction mixture consisted of 0.5 µL (each) forward and reverse primers, 0.3 µL CSBV probe, 10 µL PerfectStart II Probe qPCR SuperMix (Transgen, Beijing, China), 1.5 µL cDNA template, and 7.2 µL water. The PCR reaction started with one cycle at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 5 s at 94 °C and 30 s at 59 °C. The amplified CSBV PCR products were purified and inserted into the plasmid vector pMD-18T (pMD™18-T Vector Cloning Kit, Takara, Japan) to generate recombinant plasmid DNA. A standard curve was established by plotting the CT values against the logarithm of genomic copy concentrations for a dilution series of recombinant CSBV plasmid DNA ranging from 100 to 1 × 109 genomic copies. Hemolymph extraction

Three fresh larvae from either the control group or the experimental group were simultaneously processed. Using sterilized autoclaved tweezers, a small wound was created on the body of the larvae. As hemolymph oozed out from the wound, 50 µl of the transparent fluid was collected from each larva into a pre-cooled 1.5 ml Eppendorf (Lengrui, Jiangsu, China) tube containing 1 µl of anticoagulant (phenylthiourea, PTU). The collected samples were then stored at − 80℃.

Detection of Cu+ in hemolymph

Following collection, 50 µl of hemolymph was directly added to the center of a 20 mm glass bottom culture dish, followed by the addition of 200 µl of 4% paraformaldehyde, and the sample was fixed at room temperature for 30 min. The fixative was discarded, and 200 µl of 0.2% Triton-X-100-PBS was added and samples incubated at room temperature for 5 min.Following the removal of the Triton-X-100-PBS solution, 200 µL of Coppersensor-3 (CS3, 2µM, CymitQuimica, Barcelona, Spain) was added to the wells under cover of darkness, and incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Then the CS3 was discarded, and the wells were washed twice with PBS (3 min/wash), PBS was discarded, and 200 µl of DAPI-containing anti-fluorescence quenching agent was added to the center of the culture dish. Wells were imaged with a Leica Microsystems confocal microscope (Deerfield, IL, USA) (λx = 538 nm, λm = 548 nm for CS3; λx = 359 nm, λm = 457 nm for DAPI). The average fluorescence intensity of the three images was analyzed using Image J software, and GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (Dotmatics) was then used to produce graphs and further analyze the data.

Determination of PO activity

We used an Insect Phenol Oxidase (POX) Elisa Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, H247-1-2) to detect the PO activity in the larvae. As mentioned previously, 50 µL of freshly harvested hemolymph was quickly mixed with 50 µL of the biotin working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing the plate 5 times, 50 µL of HRP streptavidin working solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for another 30 min. Following another five washes, 50 µL each of color developing solutions A and B were added, with incubation proceeding at 37 °C for 10 min. Finally, 50 µL of stop solution was added, and the mixture was subsequently placed in a SYNERGY H1 microplate reader (Biotek, USA) to read the PO activity at 450 nm.

Fluorescent indication of ROS generation

Detection of ROS in larvae was performed by diluting DCFH-DA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in PBS at a ratio of 1:1000 to achieve a final concentration of 10 µmol/L. Hemolymph fixed in a glass-bottom dish was incubated with the diluted DCFH-DA under dark conditions for 30 min. The samples were then washed twice with PBS. After adding DAPI, imaging of the hemolymph was conducted. (λ = 488 nm for ROS; λx = 359 nm, λm = 457 nm for DAPI)

Structural characteristics and phylogenetic analysis of AcPPAN

To further confirm the cloning of AcPPAN (GenBank: No.XM_017060463.1), we performed functional annotation and phylogenetic analysis on the AcPPAN amino acid sequence obtained from NCBI. We used BLASTP (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), SignalP-6.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?SignalP) and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/smart/ set_mode.cgi)to analyze the conserved domains of AcPPAN. The AcPPAN sequence was submitted to MEGA6 software for schematic drawing.

Survival and pupation rate statistics

Bee larvae belonging to the experimental and control groups were evaluated for signs of viability and Pupation rate at 24 h intervals. The procedure for larval examination was as follows. Larvae capable of normal peristalsis were considered to be viable. Larvae that were soft and mushy, lacked the ability to curl up, had immobile mouthparts and heads, exhibited no feeding behavior, and were entirely black were confirmed to be dead. Pupation is considered successful when a clear head and abdomen are developed. The data were recorded by dedicated, professionally-trained personnel.

Statistical analysis

Each group consisted of five larvae (mixed uniformly) and three biological replicates were performed. Gene expression abundances were quantified via the 2−ΔΔCt approach, with data normalization implemented appropriately. The t-test was used to compare viral abundance and gene expression levels. Data from more than two independent samples were compared using one-way ANOVA. All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9 (Dotmatics) and Excel. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was defined as statistically significant, with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

RNA-Seq identified downregulated expression of AcPPAN in honey bee larvae in response to CSBV infection

CSBV infects Apis cerana larvae and inhibits further pupation development (Fig. 1A). To screen for functional genes regulating growth, development and CSBV replication, we selected 4-day-old Apis cerana larvae belonging to 3 infection and control groups (n = 10 larvae per group) for transcriptomic sequencing (PRJNA1068023). After screening, principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the gene expression of CSBV infected larvae was significantly different from that of the uninfected group (Fig. 1B). Then, Through Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, we identified PPAN as the sole gene exhibiting significant differential expression concurrently in two biological processes: host reproductive biosynthesis (GO:0000003) and host response to external stimuli (GO:0009605) post-CSBV infection (Fig. 1C). Using qPCR, we compared AcPPAN expression levels at 0, 24, and 48 h post-CSBV infection, revealing that LOC108000235 (AcPPAN) was downregulated during the early stage of viral infection (Fig. 1D). Therefore, we chose to further explore the role of AcPPAN in regulating viral replication.

Spatial and temporal expression, protein function prediction, and genetic evolution analysis of AcPPAN in Apis cerana

Before validating the functionality of AcPPAN, it was essential to understand the basic information about this gene. First, qRT-PCR detected the expression levels of AcPPAN across different tissues and developmental stages. The expression level of AcPPAN was highest during the 6-day-old larvae and relatively lower during the adult stage, with the highest expression in the abdomen (AD) and the lowest in the gut (Fig. 2A and B). SMART online software predicted the functional domains of AcPPAN protein, revealing the presence of both Brix and coiled coil functional domains (Fig. 2C). Finally, a NCBI-constructed phylogenetic tree revealed that AcPPAN is most closely related to the protein in Apis mellifera, and is relatively close in relation to other bee species such as Apis florea, However, its evolutionary distance from human, mouse, Drosophila and other species is relatively far (Fig. 2D).

Next, we explored whether AcPPAN affects the growth and development of Apis cerana larvae. To this end, host AcPPAN expression was suppressed via continuous oral administration of dsRNA (dsGFP/dsAcPPAN) to larvae, and the efficiency of gene knockdown was assessed at multiple time points post-feeding (72, 84, 96, 108, and 120 h). The results showed that AcPPAN decreased significantly from 108 h. (Fig. 3A and B).

At the same time, we selected a fixed time point (24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, 120 h, 144 h, 168 h) to detect the pupation and survival rate of larvae (dsGFP/dsAcPPAN). The results showed that in contrast to the dsGFP group, the pupation rate and survival rate of the larvae increased as the knockdown efficiency of AcPPAN gradually increased, but the difference is not significant (Fig. 3C and D). AcPPAN regulation of larval immunity

To evaluate the regulatory role of AcPPAN in the host’s innate immune response, we evaluated major antiviral immune pathways in bees and examined the transcription levels of key regulatory genes in each pathway. We found that AcPPAN significantly upregulated the RNAi, Toll, Imd, and PO signaling pathways (Fig. 4A).

Next, we experimentally infected larvae with CSBV after feeding dsRNA to determine whether AcPPAN expression has a regulatory function on viral replication. Infections with CSBV began 72 h after feeding dsRNA, and samples were collected for detection 48 h later (Fig. 4B). Following AcPPAN knockdown, the number of viral copies significantly increased compared to the control group, proving that AcPPAN activity has a significant antiviral effect against CSBV replication (Fig. 4C and D).

To investigate the influence of AcPPAN on the innate immune response during viral infection, we measured the expression levels of key genes in various immune pathways at the same time point (48 hpi). We observed that after knocking down AcPPAN expression, compared to the dsGFP-fed control group, the expression levels of key genes in immune pathways such as RNAi, IMD, and Toll were significantly downregulated (Fig. 4E). This suggests that AcPPAN may serve as an important immune regulatory gene in honeybee larvae.

AcPPAN responds to CSBV infection by regulating the accumulation of Cu+ in the hemolymph

Given the extensive regulatory role of AcPPAN in immunity and nutrition, as well as its coiled-coil structural cavity that can accommodate and bind metal ions, we next explored the impact of AcPPAN expression on PO activity. The host response to pathogen invasion transforms inactive ProPO in the hemolymph into active PO, initiating melanization and other PO cascade reactions, ultimately activating multiple immune response pathways. The conversion of ProPO to active PO relies on the redox reaction between Cu2+ and Cu1+ at the enzyme’s active site (Fig. 5A).

To investigate whether AcPPAN expression affects the activation of PO by regulating the concentration of Cu+ in the hemolymph, we fed 3-day-old larvae dsRNA (dsGFP, dsAcPPAN) or PBS (dsGFP, PBS), and 5 days later extracted the hemolymph. After labeling the unstable Cu+ with CS3 (Coppersensor-3), we measured the Cu+ concentration using a laser confocal microscope. After downregulating AcPPAN expression, the concentration of Cu+ in the hemolymph was significantly increased compared to the control group (dsGFP, PBS), indicating that AcPPAN limits Cu+ accumulation in uninfected larvae (Fig. 5B). Then, 3 days after feeding dsRNA, we infected the larvae with CSBV and measured the Cu+ levels in the hemolymph at 48 hpi. We discovered that even during active viral infection, AcPPAN knockdown still significantly enhanced the concentration of Cu+ in the hemolymph (Fig. 5C).

Knockdown of AcPPAN inhibits PO activation and ROS generation

The activation of PO relies on the synergistic effect and availability of Cu+; however, excessive Cu+ can inhibit the transformation of ProPO into PO. The activation of PO induces melanization, producing ROS and stimulating downstream immune responses. In particular, ROS increase the expression of the genes Dorsal and Relish, further promoting the upregulation of Nox and Duox to generate more ROS to clear viral particles (Fig. 6A).

To verify the regulatory effect of AcPPAN on PO and downstream immune signaling pathways, we first extracted the hemolymph from CSBV-infected larvae fed with dsGFP and dsAcPPAN, and found that AcPPAN knockdown significantly reduced PO activity in the hemolymph (Fig. 6B). We also measured the expression of Duox and Nox genes, and discovered that compared to dsGFP-fed larvae, the AcPPAN knockdown significantly reduced the expression of both genes in the hemolymph regardless of whether the virus was present (Fig. 6C and D).

Finally, we labeled ROS in the hemolymph of CSBV-infected larvae using a fluorescent probe (DCFH-DA) and evaluated ROS levels using a laser confocal microscope (ImageJ software was used to quantitatively analyze the fluorescence intensity of the two groups of four completely different fields). Compared to the control group (dsGFP), the AcPPAN knockdown caused a significant reduction in ROS concentration in the hemolymph (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

Bees have recently been affected by Colony Collapse Disorder (CCD), for which pathogenic viruses, bacteria, and parasitic mites are major causes [35]. There is currently no stable honey bee-derived cell line available for viral infection studies, which limits research to the use of larval infection models. In our preliminary work, we identified 4-day-old larvae as the optimal age for CSBV infection and collected 48 hpi samples for subsequent testing [36]. We originally attempted to inject virus solution into the larvae using a microinjection device. While this method ensured consistent delivery of viral copies, the microinjection caused trauma to the larvae, leading to physiological stress responses that could compromise the integrity of the results. In nature, CSBV primarily spreads horizontally through worker bees feeding larvae and via vertical transmission from queens to their offspring [37]. Thus, we ultimately decided to mimic the natural infection method of CSBV (oral feeding) as closely as possible in a laboratory environment. This approach (detailed in the Materials and Methods) not only mirrors the natural CSBV infection method, but it also ensures that each larva in the experimental group ingests the same viral load. We infected 4-day-old Apis cerana larvae with CSBV using this feeding method and compared them to an uninfected group, screening for differentially expressed genes, and further batch-validated these potential functional genes.

Through GO analysis and comparison with reported functions of mammalian PPAN proteins, we predicted that PPAN might influence gene expression, biosynthesis, and potentially regulate reproductive functions. For AcPPAN knockdown, continuous feeding of dsRNA (every 24 h) was required to achieve a good knockdown effect after 96 h. Combined with the spatiotemporal expression analysis of PPAN (the expression level of PPAN in larvae decreased significantly from the 9th day of age, but maintained at a high expression level until the 9th day of age, Fig. 2B), we hypothesized that in addition to the existence of dsRNase in the larval midgut, it may also be related to the self-response and repair of PPAN in the low larval age. At the same time, combined with phylogenetic analysis of AcPPAN (which revealed greater evolutionary distance from mammalian or drosophila PPAN homologs compared to other bee species), we predict that insect proteins often evolve distinct functions despite conserved domains, particularly in social species with complex environmental interactions. Based on this, we infer that PPAN may have potential broad-spectrum antiviral activity against common viruses of the Apis mellifera.

AcPPAN is an evolutionarily conserved protein; functional predictive analysis shows that AcPPAN possesses both Brix and coiled coil domains. This suggested to us that AcPPAN protein could exert an inhibitory effect on viral replication, possibly through regulating the expression of host immune-related genes. In this study, significant knockdown of AcPPAN occurred only after 108 h (see Fig. 3A), whereas viral infection began at 72 h after dsRNA feeding. Therefore, AcPPAN was not effectively silenced during the early stage of infection (0–48 hpi), potentially leading to an underestimation of its antiviral role. In the future, we will attempt to rear younger larvae and initiate RNAi earlier to achieve stable knockdown of AcPPAN prior to viral infection, thereby enabling a more accurate assessment of its function during the early stage of infection. Meanwhile, we found that AcPPAN can influence the expression of key genes in multiple immune response pathways, including the IMD, Toll, and RNAi pathways. The coiled coil is a domain comprising a hollow center supported by surrounding coils; the stability conferred by this structure allows it to carry and encapsulate small molecular substances, making it highly applicable for drug delivery systems [38]. This suggests that this structure may play a role in binding copper ions, eventually inhibiting the copper ion concentration and activating a phenoloxidase cascade.

Upon discovering the extensive regulatory capacity of AcPPAN over the larval immune system, we considered whether its function extended to regulating the PO cascade, which influences multiple immune response pathways. PO is a copper-binding enzyme, and the availability of copper ions (Cu+) is crucial for its catalytic oxidation and activity regulation [23, 24]. Studies have shown that low concentrations of Cu+ can transform PO into its enzymatically active form, while high concentrations of Cu+ significantly inhibit the enzyme’s activity [25, 26]. Our findings indicate that knockdown of AcPPAN in larvae leads to Cu⁺ accumulation, accompanied by a cascade of decreased PO activity, ultimately resulting in the production of large amounts of ROS and other antiviral products (such as products associated with the melanization pathway). In the future, we plan to use transcriptomics combined with RNA interference (RNAi) technology to screen and identify specific targets within the melanization pathway that affect viral replication.

Studies have demonstrated that Cu+ exert anti-pathogenic effects by regulating the activity of immune response-related enzymes [29]. PO is derived from the Cu+-dependent transformation of ProPO; however, excessively high concentrations of Cu+ strongly inhibit the activity of PO [25, 26]. In this study, knockdown of AcPPAN significantly increased the concentration of Cu⁺ in the hemolymph of Apis cerana larvae (Fig. 5B and C), indicating that AcPPAN is involved in maintaining copper ion homeostasis in the hemolymph and plays an important role in limiting excessive Cu⁺ accumulation. However, the current data cannot distinguish whether AcPPAN directly binds copper ions or indirectly affects copper accumulation by regulating the expression or activity of copper transporters (e.g., ATP7 or COPT family proteins).

However, this study also has some limitations. First, we did not delve into the specific molecular mechanisms by which CSBV infection inhibits PPAN expression. And we also did not elaborate the specific molecular mechanism by which PPAN inhibits the activity of copper ion-collecting phenol oxidase, and whether PPAN regulates the expression of Toll and other genes by directly regulating transcription or indirectly by affecting PO activity. In addition, whether there is a direct relationship between the production of ROS and CSBV infection, and the balance mechanism between antioxidant protection and antiviral immunity will be the focus of our further research.

In summary, this study identified a novel instance of the gene, AcPPAN, in Apis cerana, and elucidated its role in response to CSBV infection(Fig. 7). In the future, we can focus on screening natural products (such as plant-derived immune enhancers) that upregulate AcPPAN expression and adding them to bee feed to enhance the antiviral capacity of honey bees, thereby providing new and feasible methods for the prevention and control of CSBV.
