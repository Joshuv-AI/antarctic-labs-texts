Agreement, polarization, or both? Americans’ views of universities
Authors: Brian Powell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Responses to university policies and practices point to both consensus and polarization in the American public.

The American higher education system has been described as the envy of the world. For decades American universities have been a hub of technological and scientific innovation and, in turn, an engine for economic growth (1). On a more personal level, universities have been pivotal in shaping individuals’ life chances. Holding or not holding a college degree is a key determinant of occupational opportunities, income and wealth accumulation, physical and mental health, marital quality, and life satisfaction, among others (2). A college degree also translates into greater civic engagement, such as voting and volunteering (3). Despite growing concerns about the skyrocketing costs of attending college, most Americans see value in a college degree, young adults want to earn a college degree, and parents share this aspiration for their children (4).

In this issue of Science Advances, Belot and colleagues (5) take a more expansive look at American universities, going beyond their educational and research productivity. Like corporations and other organizations, universities can be criticized or praised not only for their output (e.g., graduation rate at universities and profits at corporations) but also for their policies and practices. These policies may include the degree to which universities encourage or discourage traditional values, freedom of expression, diversity in hiring and the classroom, environmental sustainability, and political activities by students and faculty, among others. Policies can both reflect and affect societal values, some of which are at odds with each other.

Given their linkage to societal values, higher education policies have been forcefully discussed and debated by political and media elites. However, conspicuously missing from these conversations is the American public. This gap is perplexing. Social scientists repeatedly have documented the potentially powerful link between public opinion and social change. Despite some notable exceptions, public opinion both shapes and is shaped by public policy (6). Yet, the voice of the public as it pertains to higher education policies regrettably has been ignored.

Where does the public stand on university policies? What does the public believe universities should and should not emphasize? Do these beliefs dovetail with or conflict with beliefs about other organizations, such as corporations? How extensive is agreement among the public regarding the role of universities and to what degree is the public polarized?

Belot and colleagues answer these timely questions using a two-pronged approach. They first report the patterns from a national survey that asked Americans their opinion regarding whether “major US universities” should engage in multiple “initiatives beyond their core mission.” These initiatives include diversity and inclusion, environmental sustainability, political engagement, speech and expression policies, free speech and open dialogue, traditional values, health and well-being programs, global perspectives, patriotism, and veterans. To provide more information and greater clarity, each initiative is supplemented with a brief description. To gauge if views regarding universities align with or diverge from views regarding the corporate world, a subsample of Americans also was asked parallel questions regarding comparable initiatives at “for-profit major US corporations.”

Accompanying this survey is a clever second survey in the form of an incentivized survey experiment. Americans were presented with sets of two universities and asked how they would allocate funds between the two universities. These universities vary in their rankings in the areas of academic performance, environmental sustainability, free speech, and diversity, equity, and inclusion. By varying these rankings, the authors determined which areas are most central to what Americans value in universities.

Relying on the responses from the first survey, Belot and associates find moderate to strong support by Americans for all but one of the initiatives. Americans collectively believe that universities should engage in programs intended to increase diversity, encourage environmental sustainability, support traditional values and patriotism, boost health and well-being, foster global understanding, aid veterans, and promote free speech while also maintaining policies regarding acceptable speech and expression. In other words, Americans see universities’ roles as going beyond their teaching and research functions. The one exception is political engagement. Americans reject the idea that universities should participate in or encourage programs “serving political ideologies.” This exception also applies to Americans’ views regarding corporations. Otherwise, despite some differences in views regarding universities and corporations, Americans endorse both university and corporate involvement in a wide range of activities beyond their core missions.

Overall support, however, does not mean the absence of disagreement. Looking only at average scores can obscure possible polarization in reactions to certain policies. To that end, Belot and associates compare responses by gender, education, and political ideology. Some disagreements between men and women and between Americans with and without a college degree emerge, especially regarding diversity and inclusion programs. Even with these differences, though, the overall pattern is one of agreement regardless of gender and education. In stark contrast, conservatives and liberals express diametric views regarding the mission of universities and, to a lesser degree, of corporations.

The patterns from the survey are mostly corroborated in but also complicated by the incentivized survey experiment. The experiment indicates that Americans would invest more in universities that outrank other universities in the areas of academic performance, environmental sustainability, and free speech. Group differences may be strong. For example, men and conservatives reward schools less for their sustainability efforts than women and liberals do. Yet all groups positively reward universities for their success in these three areas. The one outlier is the response to diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives. Americans on average neither reward nor penalize universities that rank higher on these initiatives. The differences between groups, though, are sizable. Universities incur a large penalty by men and an even larger one by conservatives but, in a mirror image, gain a financial edge among women and liberals.

The survey and survey experiment combination offers persuasive evidence that Americans share an inclusive vision of what universities can and should do. At the same time, though, Americans diverge in their preferences regarding some university initiatives, especially ones focusing on diversity, equity, and inclusion. In other words, we see both agreement and polarization in public views of universities.

The research by Belot and colleagues not only highlights noteworthy patterns but is generative. In addition to credibly answering the questions it asks, it raises critical questions. Among these is the question of timing. This study was fielded in December 2024. Most social scientists would see this as a very recent study. But would the responses to the survey and experiment be the same today? Changes in public opinion typically are slow, even glacial. Two notable exceptions are the rapid increase in support for marital equality and marijuana legalization (7). Public opinion regarding universities might be another outlier. In a very short period, the landscape of higher education has changed rapidly. We do not know whether the snapshot of the patterns reported here is stable or subject to change. This would require recurring surveys that gauge Americans’ opinions. The need to assess stability and change in public views, along with stability and change in agreement and polarization, is among the reasons that national surveys like the General Social Survey and the American National Election Studies are indispensable (8).

A second question this study raises is one of interpretation. The study details what was asked but cannot specify how Americans interpreted what they were asked. Consider, for example, initiatives in favor of “free speech and open dialogue.” Free speech for whom? For some Americans it could mean free speech for everyone, but for other Americans, it could mean free speech for certain groups. And for some Americans, the meaning of free speech may have dramatically shifted since the study was fielded last year.

“Traditional values” also might be subject to competing interpretations. At face value, this might appear a conservative policy. However, following the label “traditional values” is a description that refers to, among other programs (e.g., “support traditional structures and values”), “parental leave policies,” which are more enthusiastically endorsed by liberals than by conservatives (9). Some people answering the survey could be torn between the seeming contradiction between “traditional values” and “parental leave policies.” Are Americans responding to the label “traditional values” or the description?

Others have documented the power of labels in molding public opinion (10). In this study, its power might be most impactful when asking about diversity programs. Do the labels “diversity and inclusion initiatives” (asked in the survey) and “diversity, equity, and inclusion” (asked in the experiment) overshadow the actual description of the programs? Are the slight differences in the labels responsible for the greater polarization found in the experiment? Would the polarization be heightened if the label “DEI” were used? This research speaks to the possibility that labels are driving reactions regardless of their actual meaning.
