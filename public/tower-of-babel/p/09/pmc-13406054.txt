Trends and disparities in ovarian cancer incidence and mortality in the United States, 1999 to 2023: A population-based ecological trend analysis
Authors: Shengchun Huang, Qiaoxuan Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically analyzes the incidence, mortality, age distribution, racial disparities, and geographic trends of ovarian cancer in the United States from 1999 to 2023 using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surveillance data; evaluates changes in disease burden; and identifies health imbalances. This population-based ecological trend analysis used data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Wide-ranging ONline Data for Epidemiologic Research database. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR, 1999/2018–2023) and age-adjusted incidence rates (1999/2017–2022) were extracted at national, state, racial (White, Black, American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, and Hispanic), and regional (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) levels. Joinpoint regression was used to calculate the annual percent change (APC) with 95% confidence intervals for trend analysis. Epidemiological characteristics were compared across the demographic and geographic strata. From 1999 to 2022/2023, a significant decreasing trend was observed in both age-adjusted incidence rate (14.1–9.9 per 100,000) and AAMR (8.8–5.5 per 100,000). The substantial gap between incidence rank (14th among all cancers) and mortality rank (seventh among all cancers) highlights the high lethality of ovarian cancer relative to its incidence, reflecting the poor prognosis associated with this malignancy. Markedly divergent trends have been observed across racial and regional groups. Racial analysis revealed statistically significant declines in AAMR for both White and Black populations. Geographically, the Midwest and South showed significant improvement (APC = −2.7% and −2.5%, respectively), while the West exhibited a nonsignificant decline (APC = −1.7%, P = .0904). The age distribution demonstrated a sharp increase in mortality burden after age 40, peaking in the 65 to 74 age group. Overall progress in ovarian cancer control has been achieved in the United States. However, the disease burden remained substantial and uneven. Significant racial and geographic disparities have led to profound health inequities. Future strategies should focus on monitoring disparities, precise prevention of high-risk groups, and ensuring equitable access to medical advancements.

1. Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the leading cause of mortality among gynecological malignancies.[1–3] Often termed the “silent killer” due to its asymptomatic early stages and lack of effective screening methods, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages,[4] leading to poor prognosis and high mortality rates, posing a serious threat to women’s health.[5–9] Significant geographic and racial variations in ovarian cancer incidence and mortality exist globally.[10]

Recent advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of ovarian cancer pathogenesis –including the roles of homologous recombination repair deficiency, the tumor microenvironment, and cancer stem cell biology – have opened new avenues for targeted therapy, yet population-level progress in reducing disease burden remains uneven across demographic subgroups.[11–13]

National surveillance data from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) database provide authoritative evidence for assessing epidemiological trends and health equity related to ovarian cancer. This study aimed to conduct an in-depth analysis of the latest CDC data, systematically describing mortality and incidence trends from 1999 to 2023 across national and subgroup levels. It seeks to monitor the evolving disease burden of ovarian cancer in the United State across different racial, geographic, and age groups and to identify persistent disparities, thereby providing empirical evidence for informing future public health strategies aimed at optimizing prevention and reducing health inequities.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source

All data were publicly sourced from the US CDC WONDER database (https://wonder.cdc.gov/), including age-adjusted incidence rates (AAIR, 1999–2022)[14] and age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR, 1999–2023)[15] for ovarian cancer. The original data were standardized (standard population: 2000 US Standard. million), ensuring comparability across time and populations. AAIR were available for the period 1999 to 2022, while AAMR were available for the period 1999 to 2023. This 1-year discrepancy reflects the different data release schedules of the CDC WONDER database. Both datasets represent the most current data available at the time of analysis.

2.2. Study design

This is a population-based ecological observational study designed to describe macro-level epidemiological patterns of ovarian cancer.

2.1. Study variables

The primary indicators: AAIR, per 100,000 and AAMR, per 100,000.

Stratification variables: Consistent with the US statistical systems distinction between race and ethnicity and referring to previous studies utilizing US population data,[16,17] race was stratified into the following categories: White, Black, American Indian and Alaska Native, Asian, and Hispanic (ethnicity). Geographic region was stratified into Northeast, Midwest, South, and West according to US Census Bureau definitions. We also stratified variables by individual state and age group (25–29 to 85 years).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Trend analysis employed the Joinpoint Regression Program 5.4.0 (produced by Statistical Research and Applications Branch Surveillance Research Program, Division of Cancer Control and Population Sciences National Cancer Institute Information Management Services, Inc., Calverton)[18] to calculate the annual percent change (APC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), assessing temporal trends in incidence and mortality. We allowed for a maximum of 3 joinpoints for the overall 1999 to 2023 period, ensuring a minimum of 4 data points between joinpoints. No autocorrelation adjustment was applied. For shorter analysis windows (e.g., 2018–2023), the model often identified zero joinpoints, in which case the analysis defaulted to a single linear trend, and the resulting APC represents the average change over the entire specified period. Statistical significance was set at P-value <.05; APCs marked with “*” indicate statistically significant trends.[19] Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the distribution of absolute death counts across age groups.

3. Results

3.1. Overall trends and disease burden characteristics

From 1999 to 2022/2023, the US ovarian cancer AAIR decreased from 14.1 to 9.9 per 100,000, and the AAMR decreased from 8.8 to 5.5 per 100,000, representing a significant decline (Table 1). Between 2018 and 2022, the average annual incidence was 10.0 per 100,000 (ranked 14th among all cancers). Between 2019 and 2023, the average annual mortality was 5.9 per 100,000 (ranked 7th among cancers).[20] The substantial gap between incidence and mortality (crude case-fatality rate ≈ 59%) and their rank mismatch highlight the high lethality of ovarian cancer (Fig. 1).

3.2. Racial and regional disparities

3.2.1. Racial disparity

Trends from 2017 to 2022 (AAIRs) and 2018 to 2023 (AAMRs) revealed significant differences across racial and regional groups (Table 2). These trends are in line with the previous research.[21–23] The national age-adjusted mortality rate for ovarian cancer has declined significantly from 6.3 per 100,000 women in 2018 to 5.5 per 100,000 in 2023. This trend was characterized by an APC of −2.3%, which was statistically significant (P <.0001), indicating a consistent annual decline in mortality. The White women experienced higher incidence and mortality rates of ovarian cancer compared with women of other racial groups. Non-Hispanic White women experienced statistically significant declines in AAMR (APC = –2.4%, P <.0001). The decline in AAIR for this group was not statistically significant (APC = −1.8%, P = .0592). Although the Black population experienced the most rapid decline in mortality, its incidence rate remained stable, and no significant changes were observed in either the incidence or mortality rates among the Hispanic population. Notably, Hispanic women exhibited one of the highest incidence rates (~10.3 per 100,000), comparable to that of White women, yet a substantially lower mortality rate (~4.7 per 100,000). Asian women had the lowest mortality rate alongside a moderately low incidence rate, with both trends remaining stable over the period. Datas for American Indian and Alaska Native women were characterized by wide confidence intervals, reflecting substantial uncertainty due to small population numbers, precluding definitive conclusions about trends.

3.2.2. Regional disparity

All 4 US regions showed declining ovarian cancer mortality, albeit uneven. The Midwest and South regions demonstrated the most significant improvements. Conversely, the West’s decline was statistically nonsignificant (APC = −1.7%, P = .0904). None of the regions showed a statistically significant decreasing trend (all P-values > .05).

3.3. State-level variations

Most states exhibited declining trends in AAMR. Significant reductions were observed in California (APC = −1.8, P <.0001), Georgia (APC = −5.7%, P = .0012), Louisiana (APC = −4.7, P = .0068), Minnesota (APC = −3.4, P <.0001), New York (APC = −3.4, P <.0001), and Oregon (APC = −4.8, P = .0008). Georgia exhibited the largest magnitude of decline among all states; however, it should be noted that no correction for multiple comparisons was applied across the 50 states, and these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Several Southern states (Florida, Georgia, and Louisiana) have shown notable progress. Oregon was the only Western state with a significant improvement, while California performed well among the populous states. Alaska, Wyoming, and the District of Columbia showed nonstatistically significant trends (all P >.05), indicating no clear evidence of improvement; however, definitive conclusions about increasing trends cannot be drawn from these data (Table 3).

3.4. Age-specific mortality burden distribution

Age-specific death data from 2018 to 2023 indicated a strong correlation between ovarian cancer mortality burden and age (Table 4, Fig. 2). Deaths among women under 40 years of age are rare (<110 deaths per year). Risk escalated rapidly in the 40 to 44 age group (~180 deaths/year), peaked in the 55 to 64 age group (~1500–1700 deaths/yr), reached the absolute peak in the 65 to 74 age group (~1900–2100 deaths/yr) and then declined slowly and remained high in the 85 + age group.

4. Discussion

This study systematically reveals the “duality” of the US ovarian cancer epidemiological landscape over the past 2 decades: significant national progress coexists with persistent local health inequities.

The decline in the overall incidence and mortality coincides with major advancements in public health and clinical medicine over this period, including broader risk factor awareness, improvements in surgical quality, and the introduction of novel therapies such as poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors. However, the persistently high mortality-to-incidence ratio and the rank mismatch between mortality and incidence rankings continue to underscore the severity of ovarian cancer as a “silent killer”. Ovarian cancer is characterized by a generally poor prognosis, primarily because most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage.[24–26] The lack of effective early screening methods remains a primary bottleneck in improving outcomes.[27–29]

Ovarian cancer incidence and mortality rates differ between racial groups.[30,31] The incidence and mortality rates of ovarian cancer are elevated among White women relative to other racial groups.[32] The comprehensive improvements observed in the White population stand in sharp contrast to the stagnant trends among minority groups, including Hispanic, Asian, and American Indian/Alaska Native women. This pattern suggests profound disparities in access to ovarian cancer prevention, screening, and novel therapeutics.[33–35] The rapid mortality decline in the Black population represents a positive signal; however, the persistently high incidence rate constitutes a critical public health concern.[36]

The data reveal regional disparities in ovarian cancer in the US, and the existing literature has noted this observation.[37] Despite variations across the US, ovarian cancer mortality rates decreased in all 4 regions. The widespread, significant declines in mortality across all geographic regions may reflect the nationwide dissemination of treatment advancements over time. Conversely, the absence of significant breakthroughs in incidence control in any region indicates the shared national challenges in primary prevention and early diagnosis.

A core finding involves divergent trends in incidence and mortality over the past 5 years. The lagging improvement in the Western US and challenges in rural states may be associated with factors such as population diversity, the uneven distribution of healthcare resources, and poor geographic access, although individual-level data would be required to confirm these relationships.

The mortality burden of ovarian cancer is directly associated with increasing age.[38] Beyond chronological age, emerging evidence suggests that biological aging − reflected in epigenetic modifications, telomere attrition, and cellular senescence − may influence cancer susceptibility and progression.[39,40] Age distribution data from population surveillance can be used to guide precise prevention strategies, particularly by identifying high-risk age windows for targeted interventions. Focusing on health education and clinical vigilance in women aged over 40 years, particularly around perimenopause and early postmenopause, holds significant practical importance.[41] For younger women with a family history or high genetic risk, enhanced genetic counseling and risk management are essential.[42]

The strengths of this study include the use of authoritative, national surveillance data, a long coverage period, and multidimensional stratified analysis. Limitations include its ecological design, precluding causal inference at the individual level, and lack of data on tumor stage, histology, treatment details, and specific risk factors.

In summary, although substantial progress has been made in reducing the overall burden of ovarian cancer in the United States, this success remains unevenly distributed. Key findings from this study include; a significant national decline in both incidence and mortality over the past 2 decades, persistent high lethality as evidenced by the rank mismatch between incidence and mortality, significant racial disparities, with White women experiencing the most favorable trends while minority groups show stagnant patterns, geographic disparities, with the Midwest and South showing the most improvement while the West lags behind, and a heavy mortality burden concentrated in women aged 55 to 74 years. These findings have several implications for clinical practice and public health policy. First, the age-specific burden data can guide targeted screening and early detection efforts toward high-risk age groups. Second, the persistent racial and geographic disparities highlight the need for equitable access to advances in ovarian cancer treatment, including poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors and other targeted therapies. Third, the stagnant incidence trends in minority populations suggest that primary prevention strategies and risk factor modification have not been equally effective across all groups. Future control strategies should evolve from a “universal” approach to emphasizing “precision” and ‘equity,’- ensuring that all racial and regional groups, particularly underserved populations, have equal access to high-quality medical services across the prevention to end-of-life care continuum.
