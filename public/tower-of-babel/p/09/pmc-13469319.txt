Medical comorbidities of patients receiving amyloid‐targeting treatments in a memory clinic
Authors: William J. B. Powell, Suzanne E. Schindler, Martí Jiménez‐Mausbach, Inez Y. Oh, Anna Hofmann, Barbara Joy Snider, Madeline Paczynski, Nupur Ghoshal, Philip R. O. Payne, Albert M. Lai, Mackenzie R Hofford, Aditi Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONTrials of amyloid‐targeting treatments (ATTs) for early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease (AD) enrolled selected patients with minimal medical comorbidities. We investigated whether real‐world memory clinic patients who received ATTs also had fewer comorbidities than the broader clinic population.METHODSWe extracted data on demographics, comorbidities, and medications from patients who were seen at the Washington University Memory Diagnostic Center from July 1, 2023, through July 1, 2025. We compared the characteristics of patients by biomarker testing status and ATT treatment status.RESULTSOf 9938 unique patients, 1948 underwent AD biomarker testing and 335 received ATT infusions (304 lecanemab, 31 donanemab). The patients who underwent biomarker testing or received ATTs were more likely to be White individuals and to have fewer comorbidities than patients who did not undergo testing or receive ATTs.DISCUSSIONReal‐world ATT patients were healthier than the general clinic population and more similar to clinical trial participants.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a common neurological disease that affects an estimated 6.9 million Americans aged 65 or older, with the number estimated to grow to 13.8 million by 2060. 1 AD is defined pathologically by amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles 2 and is characterized clinically by slowly progressive cognitive impairment leading to functional dependence and complications that often result in death. 3 While there is currently no cure for AD, the FDA‐approved amyloid‐targeting treatments (ATTs) lecanemab 4 , 5 and donanemab 6 , 7 slow dementia progression in patients with early symptomatic AD. Evidence of amyloid pathology is required to initiate ATTs 5 , 7 and can be obtained via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests, 8 amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) scans, 9 or blood tests. 10 , 11 ATTs appear to be safest and most effective in patients with very early symptoms and milder pathology. 6 , 12 Appropriate use recommendations for both lecanemab and donanemab exclude patients who have unstable medical conditions or who have conditions besides AD that may be contributing to cognitive impairment. 5 , 7

Notably, AD biomarker tests and ATTs were developed and tested in research cohorts that were typically less diverse and had a lower rate of medical comorbidities than the general population. 13 There has been concern that real‐world performance of biomarker tests and ATTs might vary in more diverse cohorts with a higher prevalence of medical comorbidities. 14 , 15 Currently, relatively little is known about the characteristics of patients undergoing biomarker testing and receiving ATTs as part of clinical care. 16 A study in Sweden showed that most of their memory clinic patients would not be eligible for ATTs based on appropriate use recommendations, 17 and US studies found that White patients are more likely to be eligible for ATTs based on these criteria. 18 , 19 , 20 Data are needed to understand who is receiving these biomarkers and treatments in real‐world clinics.

The present study seeks to evaluate the real‐world use of AD biomarker testing and ATTs in a large specialty memory clinic in the United States. As the faculty clinical practice of Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center, the Washington University Memory Diagnostic Center (WashU MDC) sees approximately 4000 unique patients annually and performs a high volume of biomarker testing. The WashU MDC is also a major prescriber of ATTs. 12 Previous studies examined the demographic and cognitive characteristics of WashU MDC patients using electronic health record (EHR) data. 21 In this study, we used EHR‐derived data to understand how WashU MDC patients undergoing biomarker testing and receiving ATTs compared to the broader clinic population in terms of demographics and medical comorbidities.

METHODS

Inclusion criteria and consent

Data collected from patients with at least one WashU MDC encounter between July 1, 2023, and July 1, 2025, were extracted from the WashU Epic EHR (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI). This study was approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board (#201905161) and was granted a waiver of Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Authorization for the use of Protected Health Information (PHI); hence, participant consent was not required.

Variables

Demographic information included sex, race, and age at first visit. Complete demographic variable specifications are available in Table S1. The Clinical Dementia Rating® (CDR) was extracted from a structured Epic flowsheet.

The presence of comorbidities and conditions relevant to AD documented at any time were extracted. These included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), liver cirrhosis, and polyneuropathy. Comorbid conditions were grouped together using International Classification of Diseases Version 10 (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes. A full list of conditions and their associated ICD‐10 codes is available in the Table S2.

Medications documented at any time were identified using a string search within medication orders in the Epic data. Common and relevant medications were grouped for analysis, such as anti‐coagulants, anti‐hypertensives, and anti‐platelets. Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) were tracked given emerging evidence of potential neuroprotective effects and their growing use for type 2 diabetes and obesity, conditions relevant to dementia risk. The list of medications included in these groups is available in Table S3.

To account for comorbidity burden, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated for each patient using diagnosis codes extracted from the EHR. The CCI assigns weighted scores to 17 conditions based on their associated mortality risk, producing a composite score that has been widely validated as a predictor of 10‐year mortality and used extensively as a covariate in observational studies. 22

Additionally, information on whether AD biomarker testing was performed was extracted from the EHR. AD biomarker tests examined were CSF tests, amyloid PET scans, and blood tests. Tests ordered by either WashU MDC and outside clinicians were included. Clinicians decided whether to perform biomarker testing and which modality to order based on discussions with patients. Which clinician ordered the tests and the specific rationale for these choices was not recorded. Specifically, whether AD biomarker testing was performed to assess eligibility for ATTs and the role of comorbidities in these decisions were not recorded.

Sub‐cohort categorization

Patients from the WashU MDC were split into three sub‐cohorts: biomarker tested, biomarker tested with mild impairment, and ATT‐treated. First, patients were categorized by whether they received an AD biomarker test or not, regardless of the test result (Table S4). Second, the subset of patients with biomarker testing who had mild impairment (mild cognitive impairment/very mild dementia or mild dementia, defined here as a CDR score of 0.5 or 1, respectively) at any visit were identified (Table S5). Third, the subset of patients who received ATTs, either lecanemab or donanemab, were identified (Table S6).

Statistical analyses and coding

Analyses were performed to compare these groups. Chi‐squared tests 23 (or Pearson's chi‐squared) were used for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using t‐tests 24 , with standardized mean differences (SMD) reported as a measure of effect size. Logistic regression models evaluated the effects of variables on patient categorization with adjusted odds ratios. Data cleaning and statistical analyses were conducted using the R programming language, 25 and analyzed with Python 3.12, Numpy 1.26.4, and Pandas 2.2.3. 26

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

The CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) diagram for study inclusion is shown in Figure 1. Of 13,115 patients with Epic data who were seen in the MDC before July 1, 2025, 9938 patients seen after July 1, 2023, were included—this is the period during which ATTs were FDA‐approved and clinically available. Of these, the majority were female (N = 5716; 57.5%) and White (N = 8284; 83.4%) individuals (Table 1). Patients had a high rate of hypertension (69.4%) and hyperlipidemia (64.7%). Patients in the overall cohort had a mean body mass index (BMI) of 28.1 (standard deviation [SD] 6.1). Common medications included anti‐hypertensives (77.2%), antidepressants (71.2%), statins (70.2%), anti‐platelets (58.0%), and anti‐coagulants (18.9%). GLP‐1 RAs were used by 8.9% of patients.

Characteristics of patients undergoing biomarker testing

A total of 1948 patients (19.6%) underwent at least one AD biomarker test (Table 1), with 813 (41.7%) undergoing CSF testing, 595 (30.5%) undergoing AD blood tests, and 669 (34.3%) undergoing amyloid PET scans (some individuals had multiple tests). While the clinic overall had more females (57.5%) and the majority of biomarker tested patients were female (52.0%), females were less likely to undergo AD biomarker testing (odds ratio [OR] 0.76, 95% confidence interval [CI]: [0.68‐0.84], p < 0.001). Black individuals constituted 12.5% of the clinic population, but only 7.6% of patients undergoing AD biomarker testing were Black individuals, demonstrating that Black individuals were less likely to undergo biomarker testing (OR 0.51, 95% CI: [0.43‐0.61], p < 0.001).

The sub‐cohort who underwent AD biomarker testing generally had lower rates of comorbidities and higher use of some medications. The biomarker tested sub‐cohort had slightly lower rates of hypertension (66.4% vs 70.2%, p = 0.001), cerebrovascular disease (23.4% vs 29.6%, p < 0.001), and CKD (14.8% vs 20.3%, p < 0.001). In contrast, the biomarker tested sub‐cohort had higher use of antidepressants (74.7% vs 70.4%, p < 0.001), statins (72.5% vs 69.7%, p = 0.016), and acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (61.6% vs 40.2%, p < 0.001). We performed multivariate logistic regression 27 modeling to estimate adjusted ORs for biomarker testing (Figure 2). We included demographics, comorbidities, and medications as defined earlier in the analysis. After adjusting for these factors, higher adjusted odds of biomarker testing were associated with male sex and use of antidepressants and statins; lower odds were associated with cerebrovascular disease and CKD (Table S7).

Characteristics of patients with biomarker testing and mild impairment

We next identified the sub‐cohort of 1580 individuals who underwent biomarker testing and also had mild impairment documented at one or more visits (CDR 0.5 or 1; includes mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia, respectively) (Table 2). These patients could potentially be eligible for ATTs, although they may be excluded from treatment by a negative biomarker result, medical comorbidities, or other factors. Of 5943 patients who had a recorded CDR of 0.5 or 1, 4363 (73.4%) did not undergo biomarker testing. A total of 368 patients underwent biomarker testing but did not have a CDR score indicating mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (Table 2). The sub‐cohort who underwent biomarker testing and also had mild impairment documented was more likely to be male compared to the remainder of the WashU MDC cohort (49.0% vs 41.2%, OR 1.37, 95% CI: [1.23, 1.53], p < 0.001) and less likely to be Black individuals (6.3% vs 13.7%, OR 0.43, 95% CI: [0.34, 0.53], p < 0.001).

Compared to the remainder of the MDC cohort, the biomarker tested with mild impairment sub‐cohort had a lower rate of comorbidities including hypertension (65.6% vs 70.2%, p = 0.001), type 2 diabetes (21.5% vs 27.4%, p < 0.001), and cerebrovascular disease (21.1% vs 29.5%, p < 0.001). However, this group had higher usage of some medications in comparison to the remainder of the MDC cohort, including antidepressants (74.3% vs 70.4%, p < 0.001) and statins (72.8% vs 69.7%, p = 0.038), which may be indicators of healthcare utilization. In a logistic regression analysis that adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, and medication use, higher adjusted odds of biomarker testing with mild impairment were associated with male sex, hyperlipidemia, and use of antidepressants and statins; lower odds were associated with cerebrovascular disease, CKD, polyneuropathy, myocardial infarction, type 2 diabetes, and use of anti‐coagulants (Figure 2, Table S8).

Characteristics of patients receiving ATTs

Next, we examined the characteristics of ATT‐treated patients (Table 3). Cohorts were stratified by individuals receiving lecanemab (n = 304), donanemab (n = 31), either medication (n = 335), or neither medication (n = 9603). No patients received both lecanemab and donanemab. Of the patients receiving ATTs, 53.4% were female vs 57.7% in non‐treated group (p = 0.598) and 97.0% were White individuals vs 82.9% in non‐treated group (p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

The objective of this study was to characterize patients who underwent AD biomarker testing and received ATTs at a large memory clinic in the United States, and to understand how these patients compare to the broader clinic population. Patients who underwent biomarker testing and treatment were more likely to be White individuals and to have fewer medical comorbidities, including lower rates of hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, and CKD. The substantially lower burden of cerebrovascular disease compared to the broader clinic population is consistent with the appropriate use recommendations. 5 , 7 While anti‐coagulant use was lower in the treated population, 9.0% use (which may have occurred prior to ATT initiation) is still significant. Overall, our findings suggest that the population receiving ATTs in clinical practice resembles the relatively healthy, less‐diverse cohorts enrolled in the pivotal clinical trials of lecanemab and donanemab, raising questions about equitable access to these therapies.

Patients who underwent AD biomarker testing differed from the broader clinic population in several notable ways, including that women and Black patients were under‐represented compared to the broader clinic population. Multiple factors could account for under‐testing in these groups, including socioeconomic barriers to testing or ATT therapy. These barriers may include financial considerations, transportation challenges, and healthcare preferences. These disparities at the biomarker testing stage are particularly consequential because biomarker confirmation of amyloid pathology is a prerequisite for ATT initiation and may predict future cognitive decline, 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 meaning that inequities in testing directly constrain downstream access to treatment. Notably, the biomarker tested cohort had substantially higher rates of acetylcholinesterase inhibitor use (61.6% vs. 40.2%), suggesting this group was enriched for patients with suspected or confirmed AD who were already receiving standard‐of‐care pharmacotherapy. These patterns are consistent with prior literature demonstrating that White patients and those with fewer comorbidities are more likely to access AD diagnostic services and newer therapeutics. 21

Among patients with documented mild impairment (CDR 0.5 or 1) who also underwent biomarker testing, the demographic and clinical trends observed in the biomarker tested cohort were further amplified. Black patients constituted only 6.3% of this subgroup compared to 13.7% of the remainder of the cohort, and the prevalence of comorbidities including type 2 diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, and CKD was lower. This progressive narrowing of the patient population at each step of the care pathway—from clinic attendance to biomarker testing to treatment—illustrates a filtering effect whereby medically complex patients and patients from underrepresented racial groups are progressively less likely to reach the threshold for treatment consideration. Medical complexity operates largely as a downstream filter: patients may advance through earlier stages of evaluation only to be excluded from treatment by medical comorbidities (potentially related to appropriate use recommendations), a pattern echoed in a Swedish cohort study where most memory clinic patients failed to meet criteria for anti‐amyloid therapies. 17 By contrast, disparities affecting patients from underrepresented racial groups manifest earlier, at the threshold of biomarker testing itself. As Lewis et al. have shown, 21 these patients frequently present at later disease stages when the treatment eligibility window has already closed, diminishing the clinical impetus to pursue amyloid status workup; higher comorbidity burden and greater prevalence of non‐AD cognitive etiologies within these populations may further suppress testing rates.

Among the 335 patients who received ATTs, the divergence from the broader clinic population was most pronounced. Most patients in this study received lecanemab, because it was approved and became clinically available earlier. Once both treatments became available, patients were allowed to select their therapy. Key factors informing patient choice include donanemab's once‐monthly infusion schedule and finite treatment course, vs lecanemab's twice‐monthly infusion schedule with the option for indefinite maintenance therapy. ATT‐treated patients had lower mean CCI, and rates of key comorbidities were substantively lower: hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, CKD, and type 2 diabetes. White patients comprised 97.0% of ATT recipients compared to 82.9% of untreated patients. The magnitude of these differences suggests that real‐world ATT recipients may not be representative of the broader population of patients with early AD who could potentially benefit from these therapies. As evidence continues to emerge regarding the safety and efficacy of ATTs in more medically complex populations, it will be important to re‐assess whether current prescribing patterns are overly restrictive.

This study has several strengths. The WashU MDC is a major prescriber of ATTs, providing a relatively well‐powered sample of 335 treated patients for comparison against a large clinic population of nearly 10,000 individuals. The use of EHR‐extracted data allowed for a comprehensive assessment of available comorbidities and medication use for patient histories documented within the WashU system, and the three‐tier analytic framework (biomarker tested, biomarker tested with mild symptoms, and ATT‐treated) provided a stepwise view of how patient characteristics shift along the care pathway. However, several limitations should be noted. This study was conducted at a single academic medical center that serves as the clinical practice of a major AD Research Center, and the patient population may not be generalizable to community‐based memory clinics or other practice settings. Additionally, data were automatically extracted and did not include the biomarker result, which would further inform understanding of the treatment pipeline. Third, data on APOE genotype were not captured by the EHR extraction, precluding analysis of how APOE genotype—a key factor predicting risk for adverse events from ATTs—influences the prescribing patterns and demographic composition of the ATT‐treated cohort. Fourth, the study period (July 2023–July 2025) encompasses a period of rapidly evolving clinical practice, potentially shifting prescribing patterns over the study window. Fifth, we cannot determine from EHR alone whether proportional underrepresentation of Black patients and women at the biomarker testing stage reflects patient‐level hesitancy, clinician referral patterns, structural access barriers, or some combination thereof.

This study demonstrates that patients who underwent biomarker testing or who received ATTs were more likely to be White individuals and to have fewer medical comorbidities (hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, and CKD) than patients who did not undergo testing or receive ATTs. These characteristics mirror those of clinical trial participants rather than the real‐world population of patients with memory concerns, raising questions about diagnostic and treatment equity in AD. Future research should monitor outcomes in ATT‐treated patients to identify characteristics that predict both lower risks and greater therapeutic benefit, and should investigate strategies to reduce barriers to biomarker testing and treatment access for women, racial and ethnic minorities, and patients with medical comorbidities. Improved access to biomarker testing and cognitive assessments may enable earlier initiation of ATT therapies, potentially enhancing their efficacy in treating Alzheimer disease.
