Depressive symptoms mediate the longitudinal link between sleep duration and subjective well-being in middle-aged and older Chinese adults
Authors: Jieling Luo, Yuxuan Li, Liqing Wang, Yan Wang, Jie Shi, Xueting Zhu, Guiyun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence on how sleep duration affects subjective well-being and depressive symptoms in older Chinese adults remains limited. Data from 10,706 participants aged 45 years and above in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were analyzed. Descriptive statistics, restricted cubic spline models, and subgroup analyses were conducted. Mediation analysis with 1,000 bootstrap iterations assessed the statistical role of depressive symptoms. Sleep duration was significantly associated with depression, self-rated health, life satisfaction, and life expectancy. Restricted cubic spline models revealed a non-linear pattern: approximately 6.5 h of sleep was linked to more favorable levels of depressive symptoms and subjective well-being, with the associations plateauing beyond this point. Gender subgroup analyses showed consistent patterns. Mediation analysis suggested that depressive symptoms partially statistically accounted for the association between sleep duration and subjective well-being; however, because the mediator and outcomes were assessed concurrently (both in 2018), these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Adequate sleep duration, with an observed non-linear pattern around ~ 6.5 h, is associated with lower depressive symptom levels and greater subjective well-being in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Early screening for sleep and mental health issues in high-risk groups may help promote healthy aging.

Introduction

The aging population poses a pressing challenge for both developed and developing countries worldwide1. By the end of 2023, China’s population aged 60 and above is expected to reach 297 million, accounting for 21.1% of the total population2,3. Although there is no universally accepted definition of successful aging, many researchers emphasize its subjective nature and advocate incorporating older individuals’ self-perceptions into its definition4. The 14th Five-Year Plan for Healthy Aging calls for a comprehensive health service system for older adults, with interventions addressing multiple determinants of health. Therefore, it is crucial to prioritize not only material needs and physical health, but also older individuals’ psychosocial needs, particularly the enhancement of subjective well-being5.

Subjective well-being refers to an individual’s overall emotional and cognitive evaluation of life quality based on their subjective experience of daily life6. It is considered a key indicator of successful aging and is central to responding to population aging. As a core measure of quality of life in older adults, subjective well-being has received increasing academic attention7. Evidence suggests that subjective well-being is closely related to cognitive function and psychological status. Sato et al.8 used MRI to examine brain regions associated with subjective well-being and reported significant correlations with cognitive and psychological states. Studies from Western countries further indicate that individuals with higher subjective well-being and emotional vitality have a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and stroke9,10. Subjective well-being has also been linked to the economic context of the region of residence, with developing countries often reporting lower levels than developed countries11. Low subjective well-being may lead to serious consequences, including deterioration of physical and mental health, frequent illness, and even suicide12.

Despite its importance, the evaluation of subjective well-being remains unstandardized, and assessment tools vary considerably across studies. Fangfang Wen et al.13 assessed subjective well-being in young people using the Life Satisfaction Scale developed by Huebner and the Positive and Negative Affect Scale developed by Zhang et al. Sunwoo et al.14 assessed subjective well-being among older adults in Europe using an instrument covering life satisfaction, well-being, and self-rated general health. In a study of community-dwelling older adults, Lanshuang Chen et al.15 measured subjective well-being using life satisfaction, positive and negative affect, and depression assessed by the CES-D scale. To address limitations related to small sample sizes, Yifan Zhou et al.16 used the CHARLS database to examine the association between visual impairment and subjective well-being in older adults, operationalizing subjective well-being with life satisfaction, subjective life expectancy, and self-rated health. Building on this approach, the present study assesses subjective well-being across three dimensions, including life satisfaction, subjective life expectancy, and self-rated health. Depressive symptoms are also incorporated as a key outcome to enable a more comprehensive evaluation of these domains.

Sleep is crucial for both physical and mental well-being, and its duration and quality directly affect health in older adults17. Sleep disorders disrupt physiological balance and may trigger systemic responses that contribute to adverse outcomes. Sleep problems are common in the general population and are associated with chronic diseases, cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment, and increased cancer mortality risk. Persistent sleep problems have also been linked to a higher risk of both physical and mental illnesses, contributing to overall health decline. Prior studies have reported significant associations between sleep quality18, sleep duration19, and emotional states such as well-being, positive mood, and vitality. Sleep disorders may reduce well-being, while higher well-being may support better sleep quality. Adequate and high-quality sleep is associated with higher life satisfaction and better self-rated health, whereas sleep deprivation and sleep disorders are associated with lower life satisfaction and shorter subjective life expectancy20,21.

Depression is a common mental health problem affecting a substantial proportion of middle-aged and older adults22. Evidence indicates a strong link between depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances23, and depression may contribute to insomnia and early morning awakening. In parallel, studies have confirmed an association between subjective well-being and depression24. The biopsychosocial model emphasizes that biological, psychological, and social factors interact to shape health outcomes25. Within this framework, sleep as a biological factor may influence social functioning and subjective well-being through psychological processes reflected in depressive symptoms. Sleep-related disruptions may impair neurotransmitter balance and mood regulation, increasing depression risk and thereby reducing subjective well-being.

Building on the evidence that sleep and depression are closely related and that both are linked to subjective well-being, limitations remain in the current literature. The dose–response shape of these associations is still not well understood. In this study, “dose–response” refers to the continuous, non-linear relationship between sleep duration (exposure) and outcomes, rather than cumulative exposure over time. Linear models assume a constant change in well-being per hour of sleep, which may be overly simplistic; physiological and psychological evidence suggests that both very short and very long sleep may have diminishing or even adverse effects, supporting the need for non-linear (e.g., spline) models. It remains unclear whether nonlinear or threshold effects exist and whether a clear inflection or plateau region can be identified. In addition, subjective well-being has been operationalized inconsistently across studies, which limits comparability. To address these gaps, this study conducts a longitudinal analysis using CHARLS data to examine the associations between nighttime sleep duration, depressive symptoms, and subjective well-being. We explore potential nonlinear dose–response relationships using restricted cubic spline models. Guided by the biopsychosocial framework, we further test whether depressive symptoms statistically account for the association between sleep duration and subjective well-being across three domains, namely life satisfaction, self-rated health, and subjective life expectancy19,26. Our findings aim to provide clearer evidence to inform early screening and targeted interventions for sleep and mental health to support healthy aging in China.

Methods

Background of the study

The data for this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey conducted by the National School of Development at Peking University. The CHARLS project, surveyed 150 counties, 450 communities (villages), and 12,073 households across 28 provinces (including autonomous regions and municipalities directly under the central government) using a probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method. This method is considered representative of the entire population. In the present study, we selected the 2015 and 2018 waves because they best aligned with our research aim and ensured the availability of key variables across time. Specifically, the 2015 wave provided the exposure (nighttime sleep duration) and comprehensive covariates/physical measures, whereas the 2018 wave provided the main outcomes, including depressive symptoms (CES-D-10) and subjective well-being indicators; the approximately three-year interval also allowed assessment of temporal associations while maintaining measurement comparability. Wave 2020 was not included due to potential period effects related to the COVID-19 context. Detailed descriptions of the CHARLS design and data collection procedures can be found in previous publications and on the official website27,28.

Study sample

This study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2015 and 2018. This is a two-wave longitudinal analysis, with 2015 serving as the baseline and 2018 as the follow-up wave. Based on the 2015 survey, 19,450 respondents aged ≥ 45 years were identified as the baseline cohort and were linked to the 2018 follow-up dataset using the unique CHARLS individual identifier. The exposure (nighttime sleep duration) and baseline covariates were obtained from 2015, whereas depressive symptoms (CES-D-10) and subjective well-being outcomes (life satisfaction, self-rated health, and subjective life expectancy) were obtained from 2018. Inclusion criteria were1: age ≥ 45 years at baseline; and2 successful linkage to the 2018 dataset with available CES-D-10 and subjective well-being outcome measures. The exclusion criterion was missing data in any variables required for analysis. After screening, 8,744 respondents were excluded due to missing sleep duration (n = 1,428), grip strength (n = 3,482), BMI (n = 132), or subjective well-being outcomes (n = 3,702). The final analytic sample comprised 10,706 participants (Fig. 1).

Measures

Measurement of depression

Depression was assessed using the Chinese version of the Center for Epidemiological Survey Depression Scale (CES-D-10). The CES-D-10 employs a 4-point Likert scale with the following response options: “None,” “Rarely (1–2 days),” “Sometimes (3–4 days),” and “Always (5–7 days).” Two items were reverse-scored, while the remaining items were scored positively, with individual item scores ranging from 0 to 3. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms29,30. A score of ≥ 10 was used to identify depression. In the present study, the CES-D-10 showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.789). The CES-D-10 has been validated in Chinese middle-aged and older populations, demonstrating satisfactory reliability and validity31,32.

Measurement of sleep duration

Sleep duration was assessed using a self-reported question asking participants about their average nightly sleep duration over the past month. Participants provided their responses in hours and minutes. Based on the participants’ answers, sleep duration was categorized into three groups for descriptive comparisons: short sleep duration (< 7 h), medium sleep duration (7–9 h), and long sleep duration (> 9 h)33,34. The cutoffs of 7 and 9 h follow the National Sleep Foundation recommendations for older adults33. For dose–response analyses, sleep duration was additionally treated as a continuous variable and modeled using restricted cubic splines to examine potential nonlinear associations.

Measurement of self-assessed health

Self-rated health was assessed using the question: “How would you rate your health?” with five response options: “Very good,” “Good,” “Fair,” “Poor,” and “Very poor.” The original 5-point ordinal scores (1 to 5, with higher scores indicating poorer health) were used as a continuous outcome in all regression analyses (including restricted cubic spline models and multivariable linear regressions). For descriptive purposes only (Table 2), responses were dichotomized as “satisfied” (combining “Very good,” “Good,” and “Fair”) versus “dissatisfied” (combining “Poor” and “Very poor”).

Measurement of subjective life expectancy

Subjective life expectancy was assessed based on the respondents’ answers to the following question: “How likely do you think it is that you will be alive for the next 15 years or more?” Response options: “Almost impossible,” “Unlikely,” “Maybe,” “Likely,” “Almost certain.” The original 5-point ordinal scores (1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater subjective life expectancy) were used as a continuous outcome in all regression analyses. For descriptive comparisons (Table 2), responses were dichotomized as “yes” (“Likely” or “Almost certain”) versus “no” (the other three options).

Measurement of life satisfaction

Life satisfaction was assessed based on the respondents’ answers to the following question: “Please consider your life in its totality. Would you rate your satisfaction as: Completely satisfied, Very satisfied, Somewhat satisfied, Not very satisfied, Not at all satisfied?” The original 5-point ordinal scores (1 to 5, with higher scores indicating lower satisfaction) were used as a continuous outcome in all regression analyses. For descriptive purposes, responses were dichotomized as “satisfied” (“Completely satisfied,” “Very satisfied,” “Somewhat satisfied”) versus “dissatisfied” (the remaining two options).

Control variables

The study included covariates such as sociodemographic factors, BMI, grip strength, health behaviors, comorbidity count, and sensory function. Sociodemographic factors encompassed age, education level (classified into four categories: illiterate, primary school, middle school, and high school or above), and marital status (married or unmarried). Health behaviors included social activity (Yes/No), smoking status (Yes/No) and alcohol consumption (Yes/No). Vision was assessed by asking participants about their regular use of corrective lenses and their ability to recognize distant objects, such as identifying a friend across the street, with glasses if applicable. Similarly, hearing was evaluated through questions about participants’ overall hearing status and the impact of hearing aids, if used. Participants rated their vision and hearing on a scale ranging from “excellent” to “poor.” Vision was categorized as “good” for ratings of “excellent,” “very good,” or “good”; “moderate” for “fair”; and “poor” for “poor.” The same classification criteria were applied to hearing35. Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2), with a significance level of α = 0.05. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the associations between subjective well-being, depressive symptoms, grip strength, BMI, comorbidity count, and sleep duration. Multivariable linear regression with forward stepwise selection was used to identify potential influences on subjective well-being. Variables were entered and removed based on thresholds set at α_in = 0.05 and α_out = 0.10, respectively. Candidate covariates were pre-specified based on prior literature and clinical plausibility, and conceptually overlapping predictors were avoided to reduce redundancy. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics; all VIF values were < 10 and tolerance values were > 0.10, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Forward stepwise selection was applied as an exploratory parsimony strategy to derive a more compact set of predictors from this pre-specified pool. Sensitivity analyses primarily comprised linear regression analyses based on gender subgroups. The “forestplot” package in R was used to visualize the results of the regression analysis.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were used to explore the non-linear relationship between sleep duration (continuous) and each outcome. For these RCS models, the original 5-point ordinal scores of life satisfaction, self-rated health, and subjective life expectancy were treated as continuous outcomes. Given the large sample size, the linear regression assumptions were sufficiently robust; formal normality testing of the raw outcome distributions was not a prerequisite for these analyses.

To assess whether depressive symptoms statistically account for the association between sleep duration and subjective well-being, we performed mediation analyses using the “mediation” package in R. We first used linear regression to estimate the total effect of sleep duration (2015) on each subjective well-being outcome (2018). We then added depressive symptoms (2018) as a mediator to estimate the direct and indirect effects. The significance of the indirect effect was tested using the bootstrap method with 1,000 resampled iterations36.

Results

General characteristics of the participants

This study involved 10,706 participants: 5,449 in the middle-aged group (45–59 years) and 5,257 in the older adults group (60 years and older). The age distribution was generally balanced. Regarding gender distribution, 5,520 participants (51.6%) were female, and 5,186 participants (48.4%) were male, reflecting a nearly equal gender ratio. Of the middle-aged and older adult population, 5,484 (51.2%) reported insufficient sleep, 4,702 (43.9%) had normal sleep duration, and 514 (4.8%) reported sleeping more than 9 h. All group differences were statistically significant, except for those related to education, smoking, drinking, and life satisfaction. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Correlation between sleep duration, BMI, grip and outcome variables

The Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant associations between sleep duration and several health-related factors among middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, sleep duration was negatively correlated with depression (r = − 0.19, P < 0.001), self-rated health (r = − 0.12, P < 0.001), life satisfaction (r = − 0.10, P < 0.001), and positively correlated with life expectancy (r = 0.08, P < 0.001). Sleep duration was also negatively associated with comorbidity count (r = − 0.14, P < 0.01) and positively with grip strength (r = 0.11, P < 0.01). No significant correlation was found between BMI and self-rated health. Detailed results are presented in Table 3.

Results of the multivariate linear and nonlinear regression analysis examining the relationship between sleep duration and depression

The study found a significant relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms, supported by both linear and nonlinear models. Linear regression analysis showed a significant negative correlation between sleep duration and depressive symptoms (P < 0.001), suggesting that longer sleep duration is linked to fewer depressive symptoms (Fig. 2A). Nonlinear regression analysis revealed significant nonlinear effects (P for non-linearity < 0.001). Specifically, depressive symptom levels decreased as nighttime sleep duration increased, reaching a minimum and stabilizing around 6.5 h of sleep. Detailed analysis results are presented in Fig. 5A. Further analysis of gender-specific subgroups and regression analyses of the overall population revealed key factors influencing depression scores: co-morbidity count, sleep duration, social activity participation, grip strength, age, visual and auditory health, and marital status (see Figs. 2A and 3A, and 4A).

Results of the multivariate linear and nonlinear regression analysis examining the relationship between sleep duration and subjective well-being

The study found significant longitudinal associations between sleep duration and subjective well-being, including self-rated health, life satisfaction, and subjective life expectancy, supported by both linear and nonlinear models. Linear regression analysis revealed significant negative correlations between sleep duration and self-rated health (P < 0.001), and life satisfaction (P < 0.001), suggesting that longer sleep duration is associated with higher self-rated health and greater life satisfaction (Fig. 2B and C). In contrast, sleep duration was positively correlated with subjective life expectancy (P < 0.001), indicating that longer sleep duration is associated with higher subjective life expectancy (Fig. 2D). Nonlinear regression analysis further revealed significant nonlinear effects in all three relationships (P for non-linearity < 0.001). Specifically, the levels of self-rated health and life satisfaction became more favorable with longer nighttime sleep, reaching their highest around 6.5 h and stabilizing thereafter (Fig. 5). Similarly, subjective life expectancy increased with longer sleep duration. Gender subgroup analysis and regression analyses of the overall population identified key predictors of these outcomes, including co-morbidity count, sleep duration, age, visual and auditory acuity, and marital status (Figs. 3 and 4).

The results of regression and mediation analyses

The control variables in the linear regression analysis included demographic characteristics such as age, education, residence, and marital status, as well as lifestyle and health factors including drinking, smoking, social activity, hearing, vision, BMI, grip strength, and comorbidity count. The results from linear regression Models 1, 4, and 7 (Table 4) showed that sleep duration was significantly associated with key outcomes, including life satisfaction, self-rated health, and subjective life expectancy among middle-aged and older adults (β = −0.031, − 0.036, and 0.020; all P < 0.001). When depressive symptoms were additionally included in the models (Models 3, 6, and 9), the associations between sleep duration and each subjective well-being outcome remained statistically significant (Table 4).

For life satisfaction and self-rated health, the direct effect of sleep duration remained significant after adding depressive symptoms, indicating partial mediation. For subjective life expectancy, the direct effect became non-significant (β = 0.000, p = 0.99), suggesting that the association between sleep duration and life expectancy was fully statistically accounted for by depressive symptoms (Table 5). Mediation analyses suggested a non-zero indirect association of sleep duration with life satisfaction, self-rated health, and subjective life expectancy through depressive symptoms, supported by bootstrap estimates (Table 5).

Discussion

This study used data from the 2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to analyze the current state of nighttime sleep in middle-aged and older adults populations in China. The study found that in 2015, 51.0% of individuals experienced inadequate nighttime sleep, significantly higher than the 5.1% prevalence of long sleep duration. This suggests that inadequate sleep duration has become the primary sleep issue among middle-aged and older adult populations in China. This figure is notably higher than the 48.3% reported by Chen et al.37 using the 2011 CHARLS data, indicating a possible link to China’s economic and social development and increasing life stress. This finding indicates that the problem of insufficient sleep has become more pronounced in recent years. A longitudinal study has shown that individuals with chronic sleep deprivation are more likely to develop cardiomyopathy and vascular metabolic diseases38. Given the high prevalence and serious health consequences of sleep deprivation, public health agencies should implement more effective measures to screen for and manage sleep disorders.

Regarding gender differences, this study found that middle-aged and older women had a significantly higher prevalence of insufficient sleep than men, likely due to the greater family and caregiving responsibilities that shorten their nighttime sleep. Jiang et al. also found that individuals with insufficient nighttime sleep were more likely to be female, older, and suffer from more chronic illnesses34, consistent with this study’s findings, highlighting the need for special attention to these individuals. Correlation analysis revealed a significant association between sleep duration, grip strength, and BMI. Grip strength serves as a validated marker of musculoskeletal health and functional status in middle-aged and older adults, and has been consistently linked to sleep quality in previous studies39. Inadequate sleep contributes to muscle atrophy and dysregulation of metabolic and appetite-related pathways, leading to both decreased grip strength and increased adiposity. Conversely, obesity and metabolic disturbances impair sleep quality and duration, while reduced muscle strength limits physical activity and disrupts circadian homeostasis, further exacerbating sleep impairment40. Therefore, greater attention should be directed toward the nutritional and metabolic health of individuals with insufficient sleep duration, with particular emphasis on monitoring BMI and grip strength. Early screening and intervention targeting high-risk groups—such as those with obesity or sarcopenia—are essential to prevent the onset of physiological and psychological chain reactions.

This study examined the relationship between nighttime sleep duration, depression, and subjective well-being in middle-aged and older adults. The findings showed that baseline sleep duration was significantly associated with depression levels and subjective well-being, consistent with the results reported by Zhu et al.41 The study’s longitudinal design helps support the temporal ordering among variables, but the interpretation should remain cautious. Nevertheless, reverse causation remains possible, given evidence of bidirectional associations between sleep and depressive symptoms42. Subgroup sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the main findings, further supporting their robustness. Adequate sleep may help regulate emotions and stabilize mood and may be associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression43. In turn, this may be linked to higher subjective well-being. However, there is currently no standardized quantitative definition of sleep deprivation. Some scholars define sleep deprivation as ≤ 6 h of nighttime sleep44,45, whereas the National Sleep Foundation (NSF) recommends a threshold of < 7 h33. This study adopted < 7 h per night as a pre-specified categorical definition of insufficient sleep, consistent with the NSF recommendation and prior work by Bingxin Jiang et al.34, and used it for descriptive and subgroup comparisons. To better capture the trend in perceived well-being across varying sleep durations, sleep duration was also treated as a continuous variable and modeled using restricted cubic splines to examine its dose–response relationship with depressive symptoms and subjective well-being.

The spline analyses revealed a non-linear pattern: as sleep duration increased up to approximately 6.5 h, depressive symptom levels decreased and subjective well-being improved, after which the associations tended to plateau. This value describes the shape of the dose–response curve in our sample and is not intended as a universal clinical threshold. It is slightly lower than the 7–9 h recommended by the National Sleep Foundation for older adults, which may be explained by several factors. First, we assessed only nighttime sleep; daytime napping, which is common among Chinese older adults, was not included. Second, our outcomes focused on subjective well-being and depressive symptoms, whereas clinical sleep recommendations often prioritize physical morbidity and mortality. The most robust finding is that very short sleep (< 5 h) is consistently associated with poorer outcomes, whereas the additional benefit beyond 6.5 h is less pronounced. Therefore, maintaining a sufficient nighttime sleep duration (i.e., avoiding very short sleep) may be beneficial for supporting both psychological and physiological homeostasis in middle-aged and older adults46.

This study found that increased sleep duration was linked to improved self-rated health and life satisfaction, with depression playing a partial mediating role. Ouyang identified a bidirectional relationship between depression and sleep duration47, noting that depression is often associated with abnormal neurotransmitter levels (5-hydroxytryptamine and dopamine)48, both of which regulate sleep49. Accordingly, insufficient sleep duration may be related to dysregulation of these neurobiological pathways, potentially intensifying depressive symptoms. These depressive symptoms, in turn, may be associated with lower levels of satisfaction24. Together, these findings are consistent with a partial mediating role of depression in the association between sleep duration and life satisfaction, suggesting that addressing sleep problems alongside depressive symptoms may be relevant for improving overall well-being.

For subjective life expectancy, the association with sleep duration was fully accounted for by depressive symptoms (the direct effect became non-significant). Subjective life expectancy refers to an individual’s psychological estimate of their remaining years of life, shaped by their judgment and perception of their own health, aging, and death. A Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) survey showed that subjective life expectancy is associated with physical health and perceptions of aging, disease, and death50. In our study, the significant association between sleep duration and subjective life expectancy disappeared after adding depressive symptoms, indicating that depressive mood may be the key pathway linking sleep to life expectancy. Sleep, as a basic physiological need, is vital for maintaining optimal health. Adequate sleep is key to restoring physical strength and strengthening immunity. Additionally, adequate sleep supports mental health, which in turn shapes an individual’s expectations of future longevity51. Therefore, longer sleep duration may indirectly increase subjective life expectancy by reducing depressive symptoms. Overall, longer sleep duration was associated with fewer depressive symptoms and better well-being and life satisfaction.

Our study extends prior evidence on the sleep–depression–well-being relationship in middle-aged and older adults by jointly modeling linear and non-linear patterns and by assessing subjective well-being across multiple domains. By characterizing the dose–response shape and identifying an inflection/plateau region, our findings add nuance to largely categorical and heterogeneous prior literature and provide a clearer empirical basis for future confirmatory research and intervention development.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, nighttime sleep duration, depressive symptoms, and subjective well-being outcomes were self-reported, which may introduce recall and reporting bias. Second, depressive symptoms were assessed using a screening scale rather than a clinical diagnosis. Third, CHARLS captured sleep duration but did not include objective sleep metrics, sleep quality indicators, or specific sleep disorders; thus, interpretation is confined to reported nighttime sleep duration. Fourth, although we adjusted for a range of sociodemographic and health-related covariates, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, and unmeasured factors (e.g., medication use, pain, stress, or napping habits) may influence both sleep duration and well-being. Fifth, the observational design limits causal inference, and bidirectional relationships—particularly between sleep duration and depressive symptoms—remain possible. Sixth, because depressive symptoms and subjective well-being were both measured in the 2018 wave, the mediation analysis cannot establish temporal precedence between the mediator and outcomes; the indirect effects should be interpreted as exploratory rather than causal. Seventh, we did not adjust for baseline (2015) levels of depressive symptoms or subjective well-being, which limits inference about within-person change. Eighth, approximately 45% of the original cohort was excluded due to missing data; although comparisons between included and excluded participants showed only small differences, selection bias cannot be fully ruled out, and generalizability to the most frail or ill individuals may be limited. Finally, stepwise selection may introduce model-selection uncertainty; future studies should incorporate theory-driven confirmatory modeling with external validation.

Conclusion

This study provides population-based evidence of significant associations between nighttime sleep duration, depressive symptoms, and subjective well-being among Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Using both linear models and restricted cubic spline analyses, we observed a non-linear dose–response pattern: sleep duration around ~ 6.5 h was associated with lower depressive symptom levels and higher subjective well-being across three domains (life satisfaction, self-rated health, and subjective life expectancy), with the associations tending to plateau thereafter. These relationships were further supported by multivariable analyses identifying subgroups more likely to exhibit poorer outcomes, including older and unmarried individuals and those with comorbidities, sensory impairments, short sleep, weak grip strength, obesity, or related functional vulnerabilities. Depressive symptoms played a mediating role in these associations: partial mediation for life satisfaction and self-rated health, and full mediation for subjective life expectancy. Taken together, these findings suggest that nighttime sleep duration and depressive symptoms may help inform future risk stratification research in healthy aging. Future studies should incorporate objective sleep measures and confirmatory longitudinal or intervention designs to validate these patterns and to provide stronger evidence to support mental health and subjective well-being in aging populations.
