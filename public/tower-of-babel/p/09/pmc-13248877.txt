Trends in the Use of Atypical Antipsychotics Among Children and Adolescents With Bipolar Spectrum Disorders in Korea
Authors: Kee Jeong Park, Ye‐Jee Kim, Eunji Jung, Ju Yeon Kim, Hyo‐Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAtypical antipsychotics (AAPs) are increasingly prescribed for bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and trends of AAP prescriptions among Korean children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Korean National Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service to assess AAP use among Koreans aged 0–18 years with bipolar spectrum disorders between 2010 and 2022. This population‐based study used an annual cross‐sectional assessment to evaluate trends in AAP prescription.ResultsThe prevalence of AAP prescriptions for children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders increased over the 13‐year study period, rising from 0.14 in 2010 up to 1.23 per 1000 persons in 2022. During this time, the dominant AAP shifted from risperidone to aripiprazole. Data from 2022 revealed that the most prescribed AAP for children was aripiprazole, followed by risperidone, quetiapine, and olanzapine. Children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders and psychiatric comorbidities, particularly ADHD, were prescribed AAPs more frequently (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe prescription of AAPs in Korean children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders has increased over the last decade. This trend was particularly pronounced among individuals with psychiatric comorbidities, especially ADHD. Future research is needed to develop more evidence‐based AAP treatments for bipolar spectrum disorders in this population.Keywords: adolescents, atypical antipsychotics, bipolar spectrum disorders, children, comorbidities, longitudinal trends

Introduction

The worldwide prevalence of bipolar spectrum disorders is reported to be 2% to 3% in children and adolescents [1, 2]. Bipolar spectrum disorders significantly disrupt the normal development of children, leading to severe behavioral, academic, social, and legal challenges [3]. These disorders are also associated with a heightened risk of suicide, psychosis, sexual risk behaviors, and substance abuse in children and adolescents.

Atypical antipsychotics (AAPs) and mood stabilizers are considered first‐line pharmacologic treatment for bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents [4, 5]. In the management of manic and mixed episodes, atypical antipsychotics such as risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole, and quetiapine may be more effective than mood stabilizers [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Furthermore, AAP augmentation could be effective when monotherapy with mood stabilizer fails to achieve optimal treatment results in the manic episode [11].

In the early 2000s, several AAPs including olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, and quetiapine, received U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval for the treatment of bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents. However, those AAPs extend exclusively to individuals aged 10 years or more. In Korea, only aripiprazole was approved by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) in 2011 for the treatment of manic or mixed episodes in children and adolescents. As a result, most AAPs except aripiprazole, are used off‐label for Korean children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders. Despite this, only a few studies have investigated the use of AAPs in this population in Korea [12]. This study aims to provide real‐world insights into the prescription trends of AAPs for Korean children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders using nationwide data. We evaluated (1) the prevalence and longitudinal trends of bipolar spectrum disorders and AAP prescription, (2) current prescription patterns of AAPs and concomitant medications according to psychiatric comorbidities, and (3) factors associated with AAP prescription in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a population‐based study with an annual cross‐sectional assessment of AAP prescriptions from 2010 to 2022. The study included all Korean residents aged 0–18 years diagnosed with bipolar spectrum disorders using the Korean National Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA).

Data Source

Korea has maintained a government‐run health insurance system (National Health Insurance Service, NHIS) for several decades. HIRA has covered all claims from NHIS (97% of the population) and the medical aid program (the other 3% of the population). The HIRA database encompasses medical information for the entire Korean population, making it a highly representative resource for nationwide studies [13]. We used the HIRA database from 2010 to 2022 (HIRA research data, M20230626014).

The HIRA database includes claim records containing demographic data, diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision (ICD‐10), and prescription details. Prescription records include the drug's Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical code, the date of prescription, the number of prescription days, and information on the prescribing physician's specialty.

Study Population

The study population was defined as individuals aged 0–18 years who had at least one claim for bipolar spectrum disorders between January 1, 2010 and November 30, 2022 based on the most recent data available at the time of the study. Using data on the primary diagnosis and first sub‐diagnosis in each calendar year, we identified patients with bipolar spectrum disorders (ICD‐10: F30, F31, F34.0, F38.00). We excluded individuals diagnosed with organic mental disorders, mental and behavioral disorders due to psychoactive substance use, schizophrenia, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, or disorders of the nervous system.

After reviewing the previous literature [14, 15], we classified ten drugs as AAPs: amisulpride, aripiprazole, blonanserin, clozapine, olanzapine, paliperidone, quetiapine, risperidone, ziprasidone, and zotepine. AAP prescription in a calendar year was defined as having at least one claim record for an AAP prescription with a diagnosis code for bipolar spectrum disorders, recorded either as the primary diagnosis or the first sub‐diagnosis.

Study Design

Analysis of Longitudinal Trends

We estimated the annual prevalence rates of bipolar spectrum disorders and AAP prescriptions from 2010 to 2022, based on the number of patients. These prevalence rates were adjusted for age groups using the 2005 Korean census and the world standard populations as references [16]. The average annual percent changes (AAPCs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) for 2010–2022 were calculated using joinpoint analysis [17] to summarize changes in prevalence and proportion over time.

The annual prescription trends of each AAP were analyzed based on the number of claims. We defined one case as one claim with 28 days of prescription. The number of cases was counted as proportional to the number of prescription days. A claim with more than one antipsychotic prescription was counted as a separate case for each AAP.

Analysis of Current Trends

To evaluate the characteristics and detailed prescription patterns of AAPs and concomitant medications, we focused on data from 2022.

We evaluated the characteristics of children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders prescribed with AAPs including variables as follows: age, sex, types of health security programs, types of healthcare services received, types of healthcare institutions, physician specialty, and psychiatric comorbidities.

A logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with AAP prescriptions. The dependent variables of patients who received at least one AAP prescription during a calendar year were denoted as 1 and 0 if otherwise in the logistic regression model.

For the AAP prescription patterns according to comorbid psychiatric disorders in 2022, we subdivided patients with bipolar spectrum disorders into three groups: those without any psychiatric comorbidities, those with comorbid Attention‐Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), and those with psychiatric comorbidities other than ADHD. We also analyzed the use of concomitant medications among patients prescribed AAPs. In this study, ADHD was the most prevalent comorbid diagnosis, affecting 46.6% of children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders in 2022 (Table S1). Based on this prevalence, we assumed that ADHD could influence AAP prescription patterns for bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents.

The analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide software (version 7.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The joinpoint regression analyses for identifying changes in trend were performed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2, May 2023; Statistical Methodology and Applications Branch, Surveillance Research Program, National Cancer Institute). All the significance levels were set at p < 0.05.

Results

Overall, the data analyzed information from more than 7.5 million children and adolescents annually (2010: 10,979,932, 2022:7,851,681).

Prevalence of Bipolar Spectrum Disorders

The annual prevalence of bipolar spectrum disorders among children and adolescents increased steadily from 0.22 per 1000 person in 2010 up to 1.38 per 1000 person, which is a 6.3‐fold increase (Figure S1). Over the whole period (2010–2022), the total prevalence rate increased by an average of 16.8% per year (AAPC, 16.8; 95% CI, 15.3 to 20.9) (Figure S2A). This trend was consistent across both sexes (Figure S2B,C).

Prevalence, and Proportion of AAP Prescriptions in Bipolar Spectrum Disorders

Figure 1 shows the AAP prescriptions among children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders from 2010 to 2022. As shown in Figure 1A, the prevalence of AAP prescriptions with bipolar spectrum disorder showed a sharp increase from 0.14 per 1000 persons in 2010 to 1.23 per 1000 persons in 2022. The prevalence of AAP prescriptions was consistently higher in boys compared to girls throughout the study period. On the other hand, the prevalence of non‐AAP prescriptions with bipolar spectrum disorder increased modestly from 0.09 per 1000 persons in 2010 to 0.16 per 1000 persons in 2022.

The proportion of AAP prescriptions among children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders rapidly increased from 47.9% in 2010 to 90.8% in 2022, representing a 1.9‐fold increase (Figure 1B). At the beginning of the study, there was a notable gender gap in the proportion of AAP prescriptions (13.1% in 2010), which gradually narrowed at the end of the study (1.8% in 2022). The overall proportion of AAP prescriptions increased by an average of 4.9% annually (AAPC 4.9; 95% CI 4.2 to 6.2) (Figure S3A). As a result of joinpoint analysis (Figure S3C), a steeper rate of increase was observed in the proportion for girls (AAPC 5.0; 95% CI 4.1 to 6.6) than compared to boys (AAPC 4.1; 95% CI 3.6 to 4.8). Additionally, unlike the steady increase seen in girls, two distinct periods of change were observed in boys (Figure S3B): 2010–2016 (AAPC 6.8; 95% CI 5.6 to 8.9) and 2016–2022 (AAPC 1.5; 95% CI 0.3 to 2.3).

Longitudinal Prescription Trend of AAPs With Bipolar Spectrum Disorders

The dominantly prescribed AAPs were aripiprazole, risperidone, quetiapine, and olanzapine (Figure 2A). As shown in Table S2, the proportion of risperidone prescriptions decreased (from 52.3% in 2010 to 29.4% in 2022), whereas that of aripiprazole prescriptions increased (from 35.5% in 2010 to 76.0% in 2022). The proportion of quetiapine and olanzapine remained relatively stable over the whole period. While the total number of prescription cases for other drugs decreased slightly (Figure 2B), the proportion of other drugs declined much more sharply, falling from 6.1% in 2010 to 0.9% in 2022 (Table S2).

Current Prescription Pattern of AAPs With Bipolar Spectrum Disorders

Data on the epidemiology of children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders who were prescribed AAP are summarized in Table S3. In 2022, 90.8% of children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders were prescribed AAPs (Figure 1B). The standardized prescription rates were slightly higher among boys (91.7%) compared to girls (89.9%). The most frequently prescribed AAP was aripiprazole (76.0%), followed by risperidone (29.4%), quetiapine (22.7%), and olanzapine (3.8%) (Table S2). The proportion of other AAPs was less than 1%.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, preschooler (Adjusted Odds Ratio, AOR 2.52; 95% CI 1.36–4.69) and elementary school student (AOR 2.17; 95% CI 1.85–2.55), boys (AOR 1.14; 95% CI 1.01–1.30), hospitalization in calendar year (AOR 3.48; 95% CI 2.33–5.21), visit a psychiatrist (AOR 2.11; 95% CI 1.64–2.73), having comorbid ADHD (AOR 1.47; 95% CI 1.25–1.72), and psychiatric comorbidities other than ADHD (AOR 1.60; 95% CI 1.38–1.86) significantly increased the odds of having an AAP prescription in pediatric bipolar spectrum disorders (Table 1).

Typical antipsychotics were used most frequently in the other comorbidities group, followed by the comorbid ADHD group and the without comorbidities group (p < 0.001).

The comorbid ADHD group had the lowest rate of mood stabilizer use compared to the other two groups (p < 0.001). Valproate was the most commonly prescribed drug in the comorbid ADHD group, while lithium was most commonly prescribed in the other two groups. The comorbid ADHD group was prescribed significantly less lithium than the other two groups (p < 0.001). As shown in Table S5, analysis by age group revealed that mood stabilizers were most frequently prescribed in adolescents, followed by elementary school students and preschoolers (p < 0.001). The trend analysis showed the same result (p < 0.001).

Regarding antidepressants, the comorbid ADHD group had significantly lower rates of antidepressant use than the other two groups (p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the without comorbidities group (69.7%) and other comorbidities group (70.1%). When comparing children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders who were prescribed AAPs to those who were not, no significant difference in antidepressant prescriptions was found (Table S6). However, in the absence of psychiatric comorbidities, a statistically significant difference in antidepressant prescriptions was observed between those prescribed AAPs and those not prescribed AAPs (p < 0.001).

Anti‐anxiety drugs were most frequently prescribed in children and adolescents with other comorbidities group than followed by the without comorbidities group and comorbid ADHD group (p < 0.001). While alprazolam dominated prescription, the second most prescribed drug varied across groups. Lorazepam was the second most prescribed drug in the groups without comorbidities and with comorbid ADHD. Clonazepam was significantly more frequently prescribed in the other comorbidities group than in the other two groups (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study showed that the prevalence of AAP prescription progressively increased among Korean children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders. The major AAP prescribed has shifted from risperidone to aripiprazole over the 13‐year study period. AAPs were more frequently prescribed to children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders who had psychiatric comorbidities, particularly ADHD.

The annual prevalence of bipolar spectrum disorder among children and adolescents in Korea increased steadily over the 13‐year study period. However, the estimated prevalence remains lower than the global rates [1, 2]. This increase could be attributed to more clinical diagnoses, while community prevalence rates are not growing.

AAP prescription in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders has been increasing over the last decade in this study. We could only find population‐based studies that evaluated longitudinal trends in AAP prescription in adults with bipolar spectrum disorders [18, 19, 20, 21]. Our finding of an increased trend in AAP prescription is consistent with previous studies that target adults with bipolar spectrum disorders in Europe [18, 19, 20] and Asia [21]. In children and adolescents, only one retrospective cohort study has been reported in Taiwan [22]. Lin and colleagues reported a significant increase in AAP prescriptions for pediatric patients with bipolar spectrum disorders discharged from two public mental hospitals between 2006 and 2019. Increasing AAP prescription in pediatric bipolar spectrum disorders could be influenced by several factors.

Firstly, one possible reason for this trend is the growing body of evidence supporting the use of AAPs in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders. AAPs such as aripiprazole, olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, and ziprasidone showed greater efficacy than mood stabilizers when used in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. As a result, treatment guidelines have been updated over the years to reflect increasing evidence for AAP use in bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents [23, 24, 25].

Secondly, expanding indications of AAPs in children and adolescents could be another reason. In Korea, risperidone was the first AAP approved by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety for its indication of disruptive behavior disorders in children aged 5 years or more (2003). Subsequently, aripiprazole was approved for schizophrenia, bipolar spectrum disorders, autism spectrum disorder, and Tourette's disorder in children and adolescents (2011). Children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders often have various comorbid psychiatric disorders [26]. Consequently, the broadening of AAP indications for pediatric populations may have contributed to the increased use of AAPs in this population.

Thirdly, the launch of low‐dose formulations, such as 12.5 mg quetiapine (2017) and 1 mg aripiprazole (2021) could make them easier to prescribe for children and adolescents in Korea. Lastly, in contrast to pharmacological treatment, the limited coverage of non‐pharmacological treatments may contribute to the increased use of AAPs in bipolar spectrum disorders.

This study found that prescription rates for AAPs for bipolar spectrum disorders increased more rapidly among girls than boys. This finding may be related to the higher rates of suicidal attempts and self‐injury observed in girls with bipolar spectrum disorders compared to boys [27, 28, 29]. Adolescent girls with bipolar spectrum disorders were strongly associated with suicidal attempts [27, 29] and self‐injury [27, 28]. As AAPs are commonly prescribed to manage self‐harm behaviors [30], this discrepancy in clinical characteristics may partially explain the higher rate of AAP prescriptions among girls.

We found that AAPs were more frequently prescribed to younger children, whereas mood stabilizers were more commonly prescribed to adolescents. Recent studies show a declining trend in mood stabilizer use, particularly lithium, among both adult and pediatric populations with bipolar spectrum disorders [19, 21, 22, 31, 32]. They suggested that potential reasons for the decline in lithium use include a narrow therapeutic window, toxicity risk, concerns about adverse effects on renal and thyroid function, and challenges in adhering to drug monitoring. It is not difficult to assume that younger children are more vulnerable to these risks, which may lead to decreased mood stabilizer use in this population. This also has led to an increase in the use of AAPs, another main pharmacological treatment of bipolar spectrum disorders. Finally, the increase of AAP use could be partially explained by the higher prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities among children requiring AAP treatment. ADHD is one of the most common psychiatric comorbidities in children with bipolar spectrum disorders [33], and AAPs are often used to manage the behavioral problems of ADHD [34]. Similarly, tic disorders, which are common in children aged 6–12 years, are also often treated with AAPs as the primary pharmacological option [35]. The higher prevalence of these psychiatric comorbidities among children likely contributed to the increased prescription of AAPs in this age group.

This study found that antidepressant use was common in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders. Although antidepressant prescriptions were higher in cases with psychiatric comorbidities or AAP use, they were still frequently prescribed even in the absence of psychiatric comorbidities or AAP use. This aligns with recent studies that report an increase in antidepressant monotherapy or combination therapy with AAPs in both pediatric and adult populations with bipolar spectrum disorders [22, 31, 36]. However, treatment guidelines do not recommend antidepressant monotherapy for bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents [4, 37]. Although some research has explored the potential of antidepressant monotherapy in adults [38], further research is necessary to establish the safe and effective use of antidepressants in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders.

In this study, the most commonly prescribed atypical antipsychotic was aripiprazole, followed by risperidone, quetiapine, and olanzapine in recent year (2022). There were a few studies including data on antipsychotic use for bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents. In a previous Korean study, aripiprazole was the most commonly used atypical antipsychotic, followed by risperidone and quetiapine [12]. This ranking is consistent with the findings of our study. In the U.S. study using a private insurance database, it was found that aripiprazole was the most commonly prescribed antipsychotic for pediatric bipolar spectrum disorders (32.8%), followed by quetiapine (28.5%) and risperidone (25.9%) [39]. Aripiprazole was the most frequently used antipsychotic in both studies, consistent with our findings. In Korea, the growing awareness of aripiprazole's favorable side effect profile [12], coupled with its recent market introduction, may be contributing to increased prescriptions. Additionally, aripiprazole is the only atypical antipsychotic approved for bipolar spectrum disorders in Korean children and adolescents aged 10 years or older. Furthermore, its broader range of approved indications, including Tourette's syndrome in children and adolescents, compared to risperidone, is another factor contributing to its higher prescription rate.

Our analysis of recent data (2022) showed that risperidone was more frequently prescribed as an AAP for bipolar spectrum children and adolescents with comorbid ADHD compared to those without. Risperidone is known as an AAP with a relatively well‐established basis for controlling behavioral problems in ADHD [34, 40]. Based on this, clinicians may have been more likely to prescribe risperidone to children and adolescents with both bipolar spectrum disorders and ADHD. Further research is needed to explore the relationship between comorbid ADHD and increased AAP use in children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders.

Some limitations of this study should be noted. Firstly, the generalizability of our findings on AAP use for bipolar spectrum disorders in children and adolescents may be limited due to the influence of sociocultural factors and specific circumstances in this particular context. Secondly, diagnoses were based on ICD‐10 codes from the HIRA database. Those diagnoses were confirmed through structured psychiatric interviews, and we were unable to determine the intended indication for antipsychotic treatment. We use only the primary diagnosis and first sub‐diagnosis data of clinic patients to enhance diagnostic accuracy. However, the accuracy of medical coding remains a potential limitation. Thirdly, we did not classify patients by subtype. Therefore, we were unable to determine whether the commonly used atypical antipsychotics (AAPs) differ across subtypes. Fourth, this study did not include data on the prescription dose and duration of AAPs. The analysis included cases where only a small dosage of AAP was prescribed, either as a single use or discontinued prematurely. Consequently, the interpretation of the results should be approached with caution. Finally, the 2022 data analyzed in this study only covered a portion of the year. The most recent data available at the start of the study was November 2022. While 1 month of data would not significantly alter the overall trend, it is needed to consider this limitation.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to analyze the population‐level use of AAP medications among Korean children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders. While previous studies have explored AAP medication use in pediatric populations with mental illness, none have specifically focused on bipolar spectrum disorders. The findings of this study are worthwhile not only for their high representativeness but also for their contribution to estimating AAP use with this specific population. It could contribute to establishing optimal treatment approaches for children and adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorders.
