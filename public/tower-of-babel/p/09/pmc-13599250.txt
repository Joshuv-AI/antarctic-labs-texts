Caring across cultures: a qualitative exploration of Macedonian carers supporting community-dwelling older adults in a large regional city in Australia
Authors: Jennifer White, Priscilla Viana Da Silva, Shingisai Chando, Ashley Young, Allison Boyes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCarers from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds may experience challenges navigating healthcare systems that require English language proficiency. This study aimed to explore the experiences of carers supporting community-dwelling Macedonian older adults in a large regional city in Australia.DesignAn interpretative qualitative study. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with informal carers of community-dwelling Macedonian older adults between August and November 2022. Data were analysed using an inductive thematic approach.ResultsParticipants ranged in age from 18 to 65 years and were predominantly adult children of the care recipient. Three overarching themes were identified: (1) persevering under the weight of ongoing interpreting responsibilities; (2) organising life around the demands of care; and (3) reconciling cultural expectations with the pressures of caregiving. Carers frequently assumed responsibility for interpreting during healthcare encounters, coordinating services, and navigating complex aged care systems. Many participants reported modifying employment and daily routines to accommodate caregiving responsibilities. Cultural expectations of family responsibility also influenced how carers understood and accepted their caregiving role.ConclusionsFamily carers play a critical role in supporting older CALD adults to access healthcare and aged care services. However, gaps remain in the provision of culturally appropriate information, care coordination and interpreter support. Strengthening culturally responsive models of care and improving access to tailored service navigation support may help reduce caregiver burden and improve access to services for CALD communities.

Introduction

Australia is widely recognized as a multicultural society. This diversity is reflected in the ageing population, with approximately 1.2 million older adults born overseas, representing over one-third (37%) of all Australians aged 65 and over [1, 2]. Older adults from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds have been shown to experience barriers to accessing health and aged care services due to language difficulties, poor health literacy, limited availability of culturally appropriate services, and challenges navigating complex healthcare systems [3, 4]. These barriers place older CALD Australians at risk of poorer health outcomes and reduced access to appropriate care including care that aligns with cultural preferences and needs [5]. Cultural values, family expectations, and caregiving traditions may also influence how individuals engage with formal support service [5].

In Australia, it is estimated that up to 80% of care for older adults is provided by informal carers, including family, friends and neighbours [6]. This proportion may be even higher among CALD populations, where family members are more likely to provide long-term care and are less likely to access aged care services, including residential aged care homes (RACH), compared with Anglo-Australian populations [7–9]. Lower service use in CALD populations has been linked to limited knowledge of ageing conditions such as dementia, lack of awareness of available services, and reluctance to engage with formal aged care systems [10]. Cultural values, including strong family ties, varying levels of adaptation to a new culture, and expectations surrounding family caregiving, have also been shown to shape engagement with aged care systems [11].

Carers from CALD backgrounds may also face additional challenges when navigating healthcare systems that rely heavily on English-language communication. Older CALD adults frequently rely on adult children or other family members to help access services and interpret during healthcare interactions [12]. While this support can facilitate communication, reliance on family members for interpreting may delay access to formal services and place considerable responsibility on relatives who must manage complex, sensitive or confidential health information [13–15]. Likewise, mistrust of formal services arising from past experiences of discrimination or perceptions that services are not culturally responsive, may further reinforce reliance on family support [16]. Rounding out this picture is the experience of additional barriers to culturally competent care including limited resources for staff training, insufficient availability of culturally appropriate health information, and a lack of system-level support to assist patients and families in navigating health services [17]. Overall, these may impact future care planning options [18].

The Macedonian community represents a significant component of Australia’s culturally diverse population. According to the 2021 Census, more than 111,000 people in Australia reported Macedonian ancestry, and Macedonian is the most widely spoken Eastern European language in the country [19]. Macedonian communities in Australia often maintain strong cultural traditions and family-centred values, including expectations that family members will take primary responsibility for supporting ageing relatives [20]. However, little research has explored the caregiving experiences of Macedonian families and how they navigate the Australian health and aged care systems.

As part of a broader research program examining the experiences of carers supporting older adults with and without dementia, this study aimed to explore the experiences of Macedonian carers supporting community-dwelling older adults in Australia. Understanding these experiences may help inform the development of more culturally responsive aged care services and support strategies for CALD communities.

Methods

This interpretative qualitative study employed the use of semi-structured interviews and was informed by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [16]. A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit Macedonian carers with a range of characteristics, including age, gender, living situation, years of caregiving, and whether the older person attended a local Macedonian day centre. Eligible participants were carers of older adults and active members of the local Macedonian community.

A multistage informed consent process was used. The President of the Macedonian Community Centre, who had established relationships within the community, emailed potential participants a study information pack, including the Participant Information Statement and Consent Form, and asked whether they would be willing to speak with a member of the research team about the study. A Macedonian-language video outlining the study and consent process was also made available. Of the 12 potential participants who agreed to be contacted by the primary author, nine subsequently agreed to participate in an interview. All participants provided written or audio-recorded informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee (Ref: H-2023-0074).

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews (n = 9), ranging from 30 to 60 minutes, were conducted via phone at a mutually convenient time Interviews were guided by a semi-structured interview schedule developed by the first author based on the study aims and informed by existing literature. The interviews began by inviting participants to describe their experiences of being a carer for an older family member. Subsequent questions explored participants’ experiences accessing health and aged care services, their knowledge of available supports, and any perceived cultural barriers influencing service access and caregiving.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim by an external transcription service, with identifying information removed. Transcripts were reviewed for accuracy by the first author (JW). Two authors (JW and PS) independently analysed the transcripts using an inductive thematic analysis approach [21]. JW is a female occupational therapist and qualitative researcher with extensive clinical experience working with older adults and their carers, while PS is a female physiotherapist and research assistant. Data analysis followed three stages. First, the researchers familiarised themselves with the data through repeated reading of the transcripts to identify preliminary patterns and ideas. Second, transcripts were coded line-by-line to identify meaningful units of data and generate an initial coding framework. Third, related codes were grouped into broader categories and themes by examining relationships and patterns across the data. Regular discussions between members of the research team were used to refine codes, compare interpretations, and confirm the development of themes. Rigour was enhanced through immersion in the data, reflexive discussion among the research team, and peer debriefing [22].

Results

Nine carers participated in the study. Most participants were male adult children (n = 8), with one participant being a male grandchild (n = 1). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 65 years, and all were living in a metropolitan regional setting. Two carers were living with the older adult. Limited demographic details are reported to protect participant anonymity. Older adults being cared for experienced a range of health conditions including diabetes, arthritis, cardiovascular disease, cancer and dementia. Three older adults were receiving government-funded home care packages in addition to family support.

Theme 1: Persevering under the weight of ongoing interpreting responsibilities

Assuming the role of interpreter from a young age

All participants described acting as interpreters for the older adult they supported. Many reported assuming this role from a young age because they were more proficient in English than their parent or grandparent. In addition to interpreting during medical consultations, participants frequently assisted with completing medical forms and questionnaires.We feel like we have been adults raising children since we were children. We have had to interpret, fill out forms since we could speak… English is my second language, but I’ve been speaking English since I went to school. (Participant (P), 7)

Some participants also described the discomfort associated with translating sensitive medical questions for their parents or exploring in-depth personal questions such as sexual behaviour.It’s very difficult for a son to ask your mother, you know, about sexual contact with your dad. It’s a difficult question to ask because they’re seniors and they’re traditional - they don’t want you to know that. (P1).

Navigating the interpreting role

Participants reported that professional interpreters were typically offered for public health services but were seldom offered when accessing general practitioners (GPs) and private specialists. Many participants noted their loved one could communicate sufficiently with their GP for routine consultations, such as obtaining repeat prescriptions. However, participants emphasised the importance of professional interpreters for more complex medical discussions or “specialist appointments” (P4).

Participants were concerned that older family members might not acknowledge when they did not fully understand medical information. For this reason, participants often preferred to attend appointments with their relative, particularly when new health issues were discussed.

…they just nod their head, even when they don’t understand. (P1)

…they just nod their head, even when they don’t understand. (P1)

Participants also highlighted the importance of non-verbal communication during consultations to help determine whether information was understood. Communication challenges were reported to be particularly evident during telehealth consultations, which became more common during the COVID-19 pandemic. Participants noted that older relatives often required additional support with both language interpretation and digital technology.

Even when professional interpreters were present, some participants felt they were unable to contribute to consultations- especially= if they believed their relatives had not understood the information. Participants also reported that familiarity with interpreters within the local community sometimes created discomfort and concerns about confidentiality.I was basically just sitting there listening, because we were asked not to sort of interrupt as family members and from sort of a legal responsibility from the health carer’s side, from the doctor’s side I understand that, and when they left, I sort of re-explained things a little bit differently. (P7)

Theme 2: Organising life around the demands of care

Adjusting employment and daily routines

All participants described making adjustments to their daily routines in response to their caregiving responsibilities. Many reported the need for flexible employment arrangements in order to attend appointments or provide ongoing support. Some participants reported reducing work hours or retiring earlier than planned to support an ageing parent.

Participants often visited their parents daily to assist with practical tasks and provide companionship.I go there pretty much nearly every day for lunch. So, that’s me checking up on her - and having a catch up with her as well. I have been doing that for a long time now. (P6)

Navigating complex service systems

Participants described increasing caregiving responsibilities as their relative’s health needs grew. Many reported that identifying and accessing available services required significant effort, often involving independently searching online or contacting multiple organisations.

You’re constantly googling and ringing to try to find out what help is out there. (P9)

While some participants found this process manageable, others reported feeling overwhelmed by the complexity of navigating the aged care system.

Sometimes it gets a bit too much. (P6)

Sometimes it gets a bit too much. (P6)

Participants frequently reported limited knowledge about available services or uncertainty regarding which professionals could provide assistance. Several participants emphasised the need for clear, accessible information about available support services.I have never received any assistance, never been offered any assistance or made aware of what assistance I can have….I would like to have access to information that’s clear, concise and easy to access regarding obtaining help. (P7)

I have never received any assistance, never been offered any assistance or made aware of what assistance I can have….I would like to have access to information that’s clear, concise and easy to access regarding obtaining help. (P7)

Participants also noted that cultural factors sometimes influenced their relatives’ willingness to accept external support. Older family members were often described as private, proud, and reluctant to seek help from healthcare providers.The cultural barrier would be where my mother wouldn’t accept help… it took her a while to accept someone else coming in. (P9)

Some participants suggested that targeted education campaigns could help encourage earlier engagement with health and aged care services among CALD communities.

Theme 3: Reconciling culture and the pressure of elder care obligations

Valuing reciprocity and family responsibility

All participants described accepting their caregiving role as a natural extension of family responsibility. Many framed caregiving as a way of reciprocating the care they had received from their parents earlier in life.

All the years that my parents looked after me. (P6).

All the years that my parents looked after me. (P6).

However, some participants also described caregiving as a culturally expected responsibility, even if these expectations were not explicitly discussed within families.

There’s a sense of obligation… but it’s never really verbalised. (P2)

There’s a sense of obligation… but it’s never really verbalised. (P2)

Balancing cultural expectations and personal life

Participants described making significant personal sacrifices to support their parents, including relocating, reducing work commitments, or prioritising caregiving responsibilities over other aspects of their lives. While participants rarely described caregiving as a burden, some reported experiencing additional pressure related to cultural expectations surrounding family life, including expectations to marry and have children.Why don’t you have a family? Why don’t you have children? That’s on top of everything we do… your confidence gets undermined with the constant comments. (P7)

Why don’t you have a family? Why don’t you have children? That’s on top of everything we do… your confidence gets undermined with the constant comments. (P7)

One participant described feeling subject to criticism despite the extensive care they provided.[I feel] constant criticism. I’m constantly reminded every day that I have failed in living a full life because I haven’t produced a family…. That’s on top of everything we do. (P7)

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of Macedonian carers supporting community-dwelling older adults in Australia and identified how caregiving is shaped by the intersection of cultural expectations, language barriers and health system navigation [23]. Three key themes emerged: Persevering under the weight of ongoing interpreting responsibilities, organising life around the demands of care and reconciling culture and the pressures of elder care obligations. Together, these findings highlight how carers from CALD communities often assume complex roles extending beyond traditional caregiving tasks to include acting as interpreters, system navigators and care coordinators.

Consistent with previous research examining caregiving in CALD populations, participants in this study described strong cultural expectations that family members assume primary responsibility for supporting ageing relatives [23]. Participants in this study readily framed caregiving as a natural continuation of family roles and an opportunity to reciprocate care previously provided by parents. However, fulfilling these expectations often required significant adjustments to daily life. Many participants reported reorganising employment and personal routines to accommodate caregiving demands, including reducing work hours or retiring earlier than planned. Such experiences reflect broader evidence demonstrating that informal caregiving can experience substantial economic, psychological and social consequences for carers [24–26]. In Australia, carers frequently report financial strain, reduced workforce participation and increased personal stress as a result of caregiving commitments [27]. While these challenges are experienced by carers across the population, they may be intensified for CALD carers who must also navigate language barriers and unfamiliar service systems.

Participants in this study also described the extent of involvement required to coordinate healthcare and interpretation during medical consultations. Reliance on family members to interpret health information has been widely documented across many CALD populations and reflects ongoing gaps in access to professional interpreter services [14]. In line with previous studies, participants reported assuming interpreting roles from a young age and continuing this responsibility into adulthood. While family interpreting can facilitate communication, it also raises concerns regarding accuracy, confidentiality and the emotional burden associated with translating complex or sensitive health information [13, 14]. Participants described discomfort when interpreting personal medical questions while also noting that older relatives sometimes appeared to agree with clinicians - even when they did not fully understand the information being conveyed. These findings highlight the continued importance of strengthening access to professional interpreters and culturally appropriate communication strategies within healthcare settings.

Participants highlighted the complexity of navigating Australia’s aged care system, particularly when attempting to identify available services or determine which health professionals could provide assistance. Similar challenges have been identified in previous research examining service access among CALD communities, where limited health literacy, lack of culturally tailored information and unfamiliarity with available services can delay referral to and engagement with formal support systems [12, 26]. As a result, carers often become key intermediaries between older adults and healthcare services. Participants in this study frequently described relying on informal information sources or independently searching for services, suggesting that greater efforts are needed to provide accessible and culturally appropriate information about available aged care supports.

These findings have several implications for policy and practice. First, improving access to culturally appropriate information and service navigation support may help reduce the burden placed on family carers. Providing translated documents and informational materials in multiple languages and collaborating with community associations and cultural groups can serve as effective strategies for bridging the language and knowledge gap. Strengthening integrated models of care that support both older adults and their carers is also important. Primary care clinicians are well positioned to identify carers experiencing strain and to facilitate timely referrals to appropriate support services. Programs such as the Australian 75 + Health Assessment provide opportunities for early identification of health and social care needs among older adults [28]; however, uptake remains relatively low and may be even lower among CALD population [29, 30]. Ensuring that referrals generated through such assessments are actively followed up may help prevent older adults and their carers from falling through gaps in the healthcare system [31].

Finally, the findings highlight the importance of recognising carers as key partners in the care of older adults. Health professionals should acknowledge the significant role played by family carers while also ensuring that appropriate interpreter services and culturally responsive communication strategies are available to support both patients and families.

Strengths and limitations.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was small and relatively homogeneous, and thematic saturation was not formally reached. However the data collected was rich and provides valuable insights into caregiving experiences within this community. Participants had also lived in Australia for many years and their experiences may not reflect those of recently arrived migrants. Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the limited evidence on caregiving experiences within CALD communities and highlights areas where health and aged care systems could better support carers.

Future research should explore the effectiveness of culturally tailored service navigation and care coordination interventions for CALD carers. Further studies examining the experiences of carers from other migrant communities and across different geographic regions would also help to inform the development of more inclusive aged care services.

Conclusions

This study highlights the critical role that carers play in supporting older CALD adults to access and navigate health and aged care services. Despite policy efforts to improve service access, gaps remain in the provision of culturally appropriate information, care coordination and interpreter support. Strengthening culturally responsive service models and improving access to tailored support for carers may help reduce caregiver burden and improve access to care for older adults from CALD backgrounds.
