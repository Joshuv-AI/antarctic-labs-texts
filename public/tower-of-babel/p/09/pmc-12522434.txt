Unraveling the impact of indoor air pollution on respiratory health in people living with HIV within a high-prevalence HIV cohort
Authors: Nicole M Robertson, Kirsten Koehler, Christian Jenkins, Hema C Ramamurthi, Megan Wilks, Timothy Green, Gregory D Kirk, Meredith C McCormack, Sarath Raju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic respiratory disease is a significant contributor to morbidity among persons living with HIV (PLWH). In the general population, indoor air pollution is associated with respiratory morbidity. There is limited data on the respiratory health effects of indoor air pollution among PLWH, and the potential role of air pollution exposure in driving respiratory comorbidities in HIV.MethodsParticipant data from the Study of HIV in the Etiology of Lung Disease (SHIELD), a cohort of participants with or at risk for HIV in Baltimore, MD, was utilized. The association between reported household exposures and chronic respiratory symptoms was analyzed from baseline questionnaire data. A complementary pilot study directly measured weeklong average household fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) concentrations in participant homes. Logistic regression models described associations between household pollutants and patient-reported measures of respiratory morbidity for participants with and without HIV after accounting for confounders including smoking and spirometry measures.ResultsA total of 1997 (n = 1109 PLWH) SHIELD participants were included. Most participants were black (83%, n = 1665) and male (65%, n = 1298) with low socioeconomic status. There was a high prevalence of current tobacco use (78%, n = 1553). Most PLWH (69%, n = 767) had HIV RNA < 400 copies/mL. 21% (n = 412) of participants had spirometry-confirmed airflow obstruction. Self-reported household environmental exposures were associated with chronic respiratory symptoms, and PLWH appeared more susceptible to certain sources of pollution. HIV notably modified the effect of reported household secondhand smoke and NO2 producing appliances on respiratory symptoms including chronic wheeze. The SHIELD Home pilot (N = 36) revealed household PM2.5 concentrations above WHO recommend limits, with elevated household PM2.5 associated with cough, sputum production, and worse respiratory quality of life as measured by the COPD Assessment Test.ConclusionsHousehold pollutant exposure was common in an urban cohort enriched for PLWH. Early evidence suggests that HIV may increase susceptibility to certain pollutants. The SHIELD Home pilot provides early evidence of the potential impact of household PM2.5 on respiratory disease in HIV. Studies are needed to enhance our understanding of the contribution of indoor air pollution to lung disease among PLWH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03937-x.Keywords: Indoor air pollution, Respiratory symptoms, Quality of life, HIV

Background

Life expectancy for persons living with HIV (PLWH) has dramatically improved and as such, the management of age-related comorbidities is increasingly important to providing optimal care [1, 2]. As a result, chronic respiratory disease is now a significant contributor to morbidity among PLWH [3–5]. In the general population, lung injury itself is caused and perpetuated by noxious particles, including cigarette smoke and air pollution [6]. Indoor air pollution, including fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), has been shown to elicit inflammatory and oxidative stress responses and is linked to development of chronic lung disease, increases in chronic respiratory symptoms, and exacerbations of underlying lung disease [7–9]. HIV similarly enhances risk for chronic lung disease, through immunologic perturbations increasing inflammation, oxidative stress, and susceptibility to respiratory infections [10]. Epidemiological studies have found that PLWH have higher prevalence of respiratory symptoms and COPD than the general population [11]. While recent literature suggests HIV increases susceptibility to cigarette smoke, the association between HIV and impaired lung function is independent of smoking, which may be due to increased inflammation or small airway damage [12–16]. The impact of indoor air pollutants on pulmonary function and the contribution of indoor air pollutants to respiratory morbidity among PLWH may play a crucial role.

Early studies have begun to explore the impact of household pollutants for PLWH in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), yet no in-depth studies have characterized household exposures and the impact of the environment on respiratory health for those aging with HIV in the United States [17, 18]. Household air pollution remains highly relevant in the United States, as Americans spend more than 85% of their time indoors [19]. Although studies among people without HIV have demonstrated the detrimental impact of household air pollution on respiratory health, particularly among individuals living with chronic respiratory diseases in urban US households [20–23], prior studies have not systematically studied the health effects in PLWH [24, 25].

The Study of HIV Infection in the Etiology of Lung Disease (SHIELD) is an ongoing, prospective cohort of individuals with or at-risk for HIV in Baltimore, MD, investigating how HIV may impact the trajectory of lung function and respiratory morbidity [26]. The cohort primarily consists of low-income Black participants, a population under-represented in research and which recent research suggests is the most likely to benefit from environmental interventions due to the higher exposure of PM2.5 and vulnerability to the effects of air pollution in low-income Black communities [27]. SHIELD provided an opportunity to examine the impact of the indoor environment on respiratory health for PLWH in a primarily low-income Black population. We aimed to utilize reported exposure data from the SHIELD parent study and direct measurements of household pollutants, including PM2.5 and NO2, from a pilot of household pollution monitoring in SHIELD, to characterize (1) potential household exposures in a high-prevalence HIV cohort, (2) the contribution of household exposures to chronic respiratory symptoms among PLWH, and (3) evaluate if HIV may increase susceptibility to exposures.

Methods

Study participants

SHIELD recruited participants from the Johns Hopkins Clinical HIV Cohort and the AIDS Linked to the Intravenous Experience (ALIVE) study [28]. ALIVE recruited residents of Baltimore, MD who were ≥ 18 years of age and had a self-reported history of injection drug use (IDU). Participants with and without HIV completed Standardized interviewer- and computer-administered questionnaires, clinical examination, and pre-bronchodilator spirometry. Although the parent cohort is ongoing, the current analysis is restricted to participants enrolled in the SHIELD with data from baseline spirometry, measurement of indoor air pollutants, and questionnaires about common household exposures and respiratory symptoms collected at enrollment from February 2009 to December 2017. Our analysis was restricted to participants who completed pulmonary function testing during baseline assessment (N = 1997), which are referred to as SHIELD parent participants. From June-December 2019 a subset participated in the SHIELD Home pilot collecting direct measurements of household pollutants for a weeklong period (N = 36) referred to as SHIELD Home participants.

Household exposure assessment

At their baseline visit, SHIELD parent participants completed standardized questionnaires regarding their home environment. Self-reported current household exposures of interest included potential exposure to inhaled irritants including secondhand smoke (i.e. smoking exposure from others in the household) and the presence of NO2 producing appliances (gas stoves and ovens) [29]. Data collected also included self-reported allergen (household mold and pest [rodent/cockroach]) exposure in participants’ current residence. Data on self-reported occupational exposures and employment were also collected. High risk occupations were defined as those including exposure to vapors, dust, gas, or fumes.

Participants in the SHIELD Home pilot completed additional assessments of the home environment, including a home inspection by trained technicians and direct measurements of household pollutants. Air sampling was performed for 1-week in the main living area, identified as the room where the participant spent most of their time while awake. PM2.5 and NO2 indoor air samples were collected. Integrated gravimetric sampling was conducted using impactors designed to collect PM2.5 at a flow rate using an Ultrasonic Personal Air Sampler (UPAS; Access Sensor Technology, Fort Collins, CO, USA) at a flow rate of 1 lpm as used in prior studies [24]. Gravimetric analysis was conducted using a microbalance in a temperature and humidity-controlled room. Airborne NO2 was measured with passive samplers (Ogawa Passive Sampler, Ogawa & Company, USA) according to standard methods [30, 31]. Concentrations were reported as weeklong averages. For SHIELD Home participants, weekly average outdoor PM2.5 was also estimated from the Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) Aerometric Information Retrieval Service (now referred to as the Air Quality System database), for comparative purposes [32]. Data from the monitoring station closest to the participant’s primary residence for the same week as the monitoring was completed was used.

Respiratory health assessments

Pre-bronchodilator spirometry was collected using KoKo pneumotachometers (Pulmonary Data Services, Louisville, Colorado, USA) in accordance with American Thoracic Society guidelines to assess lung function [33, 34]. Percent predicted values for the forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) were calculated using Global Lung Initiative (GLI) reference Eq. [35] Airflow obstruction was defined in primary analyses as pre-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.7 using the fixed definition, and in secondarily as FEV1/FVC less than the lower limit of normal (LLN) based on the LLN definition. For all SHIELD parent participants, respiratory health was assessed through the American Thoracic Society-Division of Lung Disease (ATS-DLD) Questionnaire [36]. The presence of chronic cough, phlegm, and wheeze was determined from ATS-DLD questions including, “Do you usually have a cough?” and “Do you usually bring up phlegm from your chest?” at each visit and was dichotomized to “yes” or “no.” The ATS-DLD questionnaire additionally asks about number of lifetime “wheezing attacks” to capture respiratory exacerbations.

Participants in SHIELD Home received additional validated questionnaires during the monitoring period to assess both respiratory quality of life (COPD Assessment Test [CAT] [37] and St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire [SGRQ]) [38] and dyspnea (modified Medical Research Council [mMRC] dyspnea scale). Participants in SHIELD Home also completed repeated spirometry every six months for updated lung function assessment at the time of household pollutant measurements.

SHIELD participant characterization

Medical conditions, including the presence of chronic respiratory disease, were identified through self-report and confirmed with medical record review. Information on smoking status, IDU, household income and poverty (household income <$15,000), educational attainment, respiratory medications, and anti-retroviral therapy were self-reported and collected at 6-month study visits. Laboratory testing included HIV RNA levels, CD4 + T-cell counts, and antibodies to HIV-1 (for people without HIV) was collected yearly. CD4 count (cells/mm3) was stratified to clinically meaningful categories: >500 cells/mm3, 200–499 cells/m3, and < 200 cells/m3 at baseline visit.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics for demographic, clinical, and exposure characteristics were assessed for the overall cohort and stratified by HIV status. Categorical variables were compared with Pearson’s chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests, non-normally distributed continuous variables, including household pollutant concentrations, were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. For SHIELD parent participants, we analyzed the association between self-reported household exposures and chronic respiratory symptoms from the ATS-DLD questionnaire (chronic cough, phlegm, wheeze, and ≥ 2 respiratory exacerbations per year). We utilized multivariable logistic regression models with robust estimations of variance, adjusted for age, sex, race, current smoking and IDU (use in the past six months), household poverty (family income <$15,000), HIV-serostatus, and lung function (FEV1). Covariates were selected through construction of a directed acyclic graph and purposeful selection based on mechanistic plausibility to identify and control for confounding and sources of bias (Supplemental Fig. 1). Our primary analysis focused on the overall cohort, before stratifying by HIV-serostatus. Interaction models analyzed if HIV-status modified the association between exposures and respiratory outcomes of interest. Among PLWH, association modification by detectable HIV RNA (< 400 copies/mL vs. ≥ 400 copies/mL) and CD4 count (cells/mm3) were analyzed at clinical thresholds.

For SHIELD Home pilot participants, we analyzed the association between directly measured household pollutants (PM2.5 and NO2), chronic respiratory symptoms, respiratory quality of life (CAT and SGRQ scores), and dyspnea (mMRC dyspnea score). We utilized multivariable logistic regression models with robust estimations of variance, adjusted for age, sex, race, current smoking, HIV-serostatus, and lung function (FEV1). Pulmonary function testing in SHIELD parent is updated every six months and the most recent lung function (FEV1) measurements were utilized in SHIELD Home regression models. In logistic regression models CAT (≥ 10 vs. < 10), SGRQ (≥ 20 vs. < 20) and mMRC (≥ 2 vs. < 2) were modeled at clinically relevant thresholds [39, 40]. We additionally evaluated the odds of the symptomatic tobacco exposure with preserved spirometry (TEPS) phenotype, defined as an individual with a CAT score ≥ 10, with an FEV1/FVC ratio ≥ 0.70, based on prior literature [41]. Quantile regression models were generated, given highly skewed outcomes, to describe associations exposures and continuous CAT and SGRQ scores. For all models, results were generated for a 1 Interquartile Range (IQR) increase in the pollutant of interest (PM2.5 =47 µg/m3 and NO2 = 12ppb). The sample size of the SHIELD Home pilot limited the ability to conduct reliable subgroup analyses. In all analyses, two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered Statistically significant. Analyses were performed using STATA version 15.1 (StataCorp).

Results

Among the 1997 participants contributing to the SHIELD analysis, 1109 were PLWH (55%) and 1553 (78%) were current smokers (Table 1). Participants were a median age of 49 (IQR 43–54) years, and the majority were Black 1665 (83%) and male 1298 (65%). Active smoking and current drug use was common with a lower percentage of current smoking (71% versus 87%) and active injection drug use (13% versus 36%) among PLWH compared to participants without HIV. 21% of participants had airflow obstruction using the fixed definition and 20% of participants using the LLN definition. Among SHIELD parent participants lung function was relatively preserved with a median percent predicted FEV1 90% [80–101%]. Among participants living with HIV, 767 (69%) participants had HIV RNA < 400 copies/mL with a median CD4 count of 465 cells/mm3 highlighting inconsistent levels of viral suppression.

Among the 36 SHIELD Home participants, 18 (50%) were PLWH. SHIELD Home participant Characteristics are described in Supplemental Table 1. The majority were current smokers (67%, n = 24) both among PLWH and participants without HIV. Only six participants (16%) had spirometry confirmed airflow obstruction, though lower lung function was common with a median percent predicted FEV1 73% (58–87%).

Exposure characteristics

Among participants in the SHIELD parent cohort, household exposures were common. Current household secondhand smoke (n = 1345 [67%]), presence of NO2 producing appliances (n = 1493 [74%]), and household pest exposure (n = 791 [39%]) were common (Table 1). Self-reported household exposures were comparable for PLWH and participants without HIV. Among SHIELD Home participants, the majority lived in apartments (n = 20 [56%]) or attached townhouses (n = 14 [39%]). The majority used NO2 producing appliances, such as gas stoves or boilers for heating (n = 20 [56%]).

SHIELD Home Participants reported spending a median of 78.9% (IQR 64.2–82.1) of their day indoors. The median household PM2.5 concentration was 37 µg/m3, while estimated weekly average ambient PM2.5 from EPA monitors was lower at 8 µg/m3. There was no statistically significant difference between household PM2.5 concentrations for PLWH (45 µg/m3 [23–73]) and participants without HIV (37 µg/m3 [12–72]) (p = 0.87). Median household PM2.5 concentrations were, on average, over three times higher among current smokers (65 µg/m3) than non-smoking households (13 µg/m3) (p = 0.002). However, even among non-smokers only one participant had household PM2.5 concentrations that were below World Health Organization (WHO) recommended level (< 5 µg/m3) (Fig. 1) [42]. Participants in homes with self-reported secondhand smoke had higher PM2.5 concentrations compared to participants in homes without secondhand smoke exposure (mean PM2.5 39.7 µg/m³ vs. 26.7 µg/m³,respectively, p = 0.11), but this was not a statistically significant difference (Supplemental Fig. 2). Median household NO2 concentrations were 12 ppb [IQR 8–20], with no statistically significant difference by HIV (p = 0.98) or smoking status (p = 0.48). Median household NO2 concentrations were higher in homes that reported the presence of gas stoves for cooking (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Reported household exposure and respiratory symptoms

The relationship between reported household exposure sources including inhaled irritants (secondhand smoke exposure and presence of NO2 producing appliances) and allergens (pests and mold) with chronic respiratory symptoms was analyzed for SHIELD parent participants in adjusted analyses. Exposure to household allergens, including both pests and mold, were associated with greater odds of chronic cough, phlegm, and wheeze (Table 2). The presence of household secondhand smoke was associated with greater risk for cough, wheeze, and respiratory exacerbations. There was no association between the presence of NO2 producing appliances and chronic respiratory symptoms.

We analyzed how HIV modified the association between potential household inhaled irritants and chronic respiratory symptoms. HIV modified the effect of secondhand smoke on odds of chronic phlegm, wheeze, and respiratory exacerbations (Fig. 2, Supplemental Tables 2–3). PLWH were more likely to experience chronic respiratory symptoms than participants without HIV when exposed to household secondhand smoke, even after adjusting for primary smoking exposure and lung function. PLWH who had NO2 producing appliances present in the home were more likely to report chronic wheeze (OR 1.41; 95% CI 1.05, 1.90) and ≥ 2 respiratory exacerbations per year (1.43; 95% CI: 1.01, 2.05), while there was no association observed among participants without HIV.

There was no statistically significant effect modification by HIV status for the relationship between household allergens (pests or mold) and odds of chronic respiratory symptoms (Supplemental Tables 4–5). Among PLWH, viremic control did not consistently modify the association between reported pollutant exposures and respiratory symptoms (Supplemental Tables 6–7). However, among PWLH, CD4 count modified the association between reported secondhand smoke exposures and respiratory symptoms including wheezing and ≥ 2 exacerbations per a year, with those with lower CD4 count facing greater susceptibility to reported secondhand smoke exposure (Supplemental Tables 8–9). There was no effect modification by CD4 count in the association between the presence of NO2 producing appliances and respiratory symptoms.

Associations between household pollutant measurements and respiratory symptoms

For participants in the SHIELD Home pilot, associations between household PM2.5 and NO2 concentrations and respiratory symptoms were analyzed (Table 3). Using linear regression, an IQR increase in household PM2.5 was associated with greater symptoms based on the CAT (median difference 1.03; 95% CI: 0.21, 1.87). Similarly, an IQR increase in household PM2.5 was associated with greater odds of substantial respiratory symptoms (CAT ≥ 10) (OR 1.61; 95% CI: 1.13, 2.29) in adjusted analyses that accounted for demographics, smoking status, HIV, and lung function (Table 3). Among ever smokers, those exposed to higher PM2.5 concentrations had greater odds of persistent symptoms without airflow obstruction (TEPS) (OR 2.05; 95% CI: 1.22, 3.46). There was an association between PM2.5 and greater odds of chronic cough and phlegm but not wheeze. There was no statistically significant association between PM2.5 and respiratory related quality-of-life as based on the SGRQ. Additionally, no association was detected between PM2.5 and dyspnea based on the mMRC dyspnea scale. Higher household NO2 was associated with greater odds of chronic cough (OR 5.51; 95% CI: 2.21, 13.7). However, no other statistically significant associations were detected between NO2 and respiratory outcomes among SHIELD Home participants (Table 3).

Discussion

Findings from the SHIELD parent cohort and SHIELD Home pilot study, which included direct environmental exposure assessment, demonstrated that household environmental exposures are associated with increased respiratory symptoms among a high-prevalence cohort of PLWH. Our study provides early evidence suggesting the importance of the household environment in respiratory health among PLWH in an urban U.S. cohort. The results demonstrate (1) self-reported household exposures, including environmental tobacco smoke and household pests, are associated with increased chronic respiratory symptoms; (2) PLWH may be more susceptible to household pollutants including secondhand smoke and NO2 from gas appliances; and (3) directly measured household PM2.5 is associated with chronic respiratory symptoms. In total these results highlight the need for more comprehensive evaluation of the household environment in studies of HIV-associated lung disease.

Data on the association between household air pollution and respiratory health in PLWH is limited. Previous studies, from LMICs, have suggested a potential association between household air pollution exposure and respiratory symptoms among PLWH [43]. Two cohort studies in rural Uganda demonstrated the association between household air pollution with low prevalence of current tobacco use (12–16%) and primarily biomass use, compared to our cohort with high tobacco use (78%)17,18. One Study found that use of biomass was associated with 41% increased odds of chronic cough among females living with HIV in rural Uganda after adjusting for CD4 count and viral load (p= 0.047)[18]. Another cohort study in Uganda demonstrated that personal carbon monoxide (CO) exposure was higher among PLWH, and that higher CO exposure was associated with increased respiratory symptoms [17]. Two small studies from Malawi have investigated indoor air pollution health effects in PLWH [44, 45]. One study found higher prevalence of respiratory symptoms among PLWH and suggested a potential association between household pollution and elevation in inflammatory markers in PLWH [44]. In contrast to our work, these studies included high levels of pollutants from biomass exposures that are less common in U.S. households.

SHIELD participants reported a high prevalence of adverse household environmental exposures. Most participants in the SHIELD cohort reported exposure to household pollutant sources including environmental tobacco smoke and gas appliances. Data from the SHIELD Home pilot demonstrated highly elevated household concentrations of indoor PM2.5, with a median concentration of 37 µg/m3 overall and 45 µg/m3 in households of PLWH, significantly higher than concentrations measured in the outdoor air in the region (EPA Standard 8 µg/m3)[46]. Smoking was likely a substantial contributor to indoor PM2.5 concentrations, as concentrations were higher in households of active smokers, but even among former and never smokers, concentrations of household PM2.5 were above the WHO recommended thresholds. All but one household had indoor PM concentrations above the WHO recommended threshold of 5 µg/m3[42]. Furthermore, most homes in the SHIELD parent study reported presence of gas appliances, and these households were associated with significantly higher NO2 exposure in the SHIELD Home study. PM2.5 and NO2 levels were consistent with prior studies among PLWH in China [47–49]. Our findings describing household pollutants in SHIELD reinforce the importance of studying the health effects of elevated pollutant exposure among PLWH.

We found increasing odds of chronic wheeze, phlegm, and frequent respiratory exacerbations were associated with household secondhand smoke exposure among PLWH. Prior research has found both secondhand smoke and HIV independently lead to increased systemic inflammation potentially representing an additive effect of secondhand smoke on airways, especially among PLWH [50]. Similarly, PLWH have increased risk for lung function decline after controlling for cigarette smoke and injection drug use with mechanisms hypothesized due to increased immune activation, increased progression of lung aging, and manipulation of lung microbiome [51, 52]. We found that household secondhand smoke exposure was associated with even greater risk for chronic wheeze, phlegm, and frequent respiratory exacerbations among PLWH. In the general population, studies have suggested that active cigarette smokers may be more susceptible to household secondhand smoke. Prior research has also that found both cigarette smoke and HIV independently increase systemic inflammation, with participants with HIV in our study potentially experiencing an even greater additive effect of secondhand smoke on respiratory morbidity than persons without HIV[13, 50].

The SHIELD Home pilot provided the opportunity to further explore the association between household pollutant and chronic respiratory symptoms through direct measurement of household PM2.5 and NO2. SHIELD Home participants living in homes with higher PM2.5 had increased odds of chronic respiratory symptoms and worse respiratory health, as measured by the CAT (score ≥ 10). Epidemiologic studies among cohorts of PLWH report similar frequency of chronic respiratory symptoms [53, 54]. Our data also suggests that PM2.5 may contribute to increased respiratory symptoms, even in the absence of airflow obstruction. The emerging TEPS phenotype, defined by those with chronic respiratory symptoms without airflow obstruction, was associated with higher indoor PM2.5 exposure likely representing consequences of chronic exposure [41, 55].

There was not a consistent association between household NO2 and respiratory health aside from a strong effect size related to chronic cough. This may be, in part, attributed to the small sample size of the SHIELD Home pilot. In the SHIELD parent analysis, the association between NO2 related exposures and respiratory symptoms was evident primarily in PLWH. Though the SHIELD Home pilot lacked the sample size to reliably determine effect modification by HIV status, it demonstrated the feasibility of conducting in-home environmental assessments in urban high-prevalence HIV cohorts.

This study has a few notable limitations. The study population consisted of a primarily low-income, Black, inner-city population with a history of IDU. As a result, our findings may not be generalizable to other populations living with HIV. However, as recent studies have highlighted, black and low-income populations face the greatest risk from long-term PM2.5 exposure and may ultimately gain the most from future environmental health research efforts and interventions [27]. The high prevalence of active smoking in this population creates challenges in studying the health effects of other household pollutants and their contribution to household PM2.5. We were unable stratify our analysis by tobacco use as this study is not powered to evaluate the influence of tobacco use versus indoor air pollution on respiratory health. Early data suggests PLWH may be more susceptible to environmental pollutants including secondhand smoke, highlighting the importance of studying the health effects of household pollutants among active smokers living with HIV [56]. Furthermore, household pollutant exposure (i.e., mold/mildew, pest, secondhand smoke) was self-reported by participants, which is subject to recall bias. The presence of a stove does not necessarily mean the stove was used. Furthermore, the Hawthorne effect may alter typical participant use patterns and underestimate the exposure of PM2.5 and NO2, but our use of unobtrusive measurement devices reduces participant behavior change [57].

Conclusions

Indoor air pollution may ultimately represent a significant modifiable risk factor for multi-morbidity in individuals aging with HIV. The study represents an initial step in describing the contribution of household pollution to respiratory morbidity in PLWH. As noted, our research presents numerous opportunities for future investigation. Our findings suggest that future studies of HIV-associated lung disease should include assessment of the indoor environment and account for the potential impact of household air pollution exposure on lung health. The findings of our study may further support implementing behavioral interventions that target individual and household level indoor air pollution sources to reduce respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction, especially among PLWH. Among today’s aging population of PLWH there is a need for studies to describe the contribution of pollutant exposure in driving respiratory comorbidities.
