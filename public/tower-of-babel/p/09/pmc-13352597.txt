Coverage, pricing, and reimbursement of telemedicine: a qualitative study among implementers of the Akoma Pa mHealth-based telemedicine intervention in Ghana
Authors: Richard Abeiku Bonney, Daniel Boateng, Verena Struckmann, Daniel Opoku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare systems are rapidly using digital health technologies, such as mHealth-based telemedicine, as digitalization in healthcare provision advances. However, there is limited knowledge of the pricing and reimbursement mechanisms for these health interventions, especially in the Global South. This study aimed to investigate healthcare professionals’ perspectives on the coverage, pricing, and reimbursement of telemedicine services in Ghana.MethodsThis study used a qualitative exploratory design. Multistage sampling was employed. All 46 sampled Christian Health Association of Ghana (CHAG) health facilities across Ghana, with a focus on the “Akoma Pa Champions”, were invited; 29 responded and participated, at which point data saturation was reached. Data collection was done in February 2024 through semi-structured interviews. Thematic analysis was done using ATLAS.ti.ResultsParticipants shared that the Akoma Pa intervention targeted hypertensive and diabetic patients, offering free medications and services, but limited drug options raised concerns. Pricing was absent, with free services, although participants debated whether mHealth should be priced less or at the same rate as traditional care. Reimbursement lacked structure and offered only modest non-monetary incentives, while in some cases, others received nothing. Funded by a non-governmental organization (NGO), the project boosted patient attendance through cost savings but faced challenges, including internet issues, patient skepticism, and limited scope. Suggestions from participants included integrating the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), expanding disease coverage, and increasing investment for sustainability.ConclusionThe Akoma Pa intervention improves access to care for hypertensive and diabetic patients in Ghana by reducing costs and increasing engagement. However, its sustainability is limited by a narrow scope, a lack of pricing frameworks, dependence on external funding, and connectivity issues. Expanding its scope, integrating with NHIS, establishing an adequate regulatory framework, and investing in infrastructure can enhance its impact and scalability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-15091-z.Keywords: Coverage, Pricing, Reimbursement, Telemedicine, Akoma Pa, mHealth, Telemedicine

Background

Telemedicine has emerged as a pivotal tool for enhancing medication adherence in the modern digital era. Facilitating remote doctor-patient interactions eliminates the constraints of physical presence and simplifies access to care through flexible scheduling [1]. This technological advancement offers promising opportunities to improve healthcare delivery, particularly by supporting individuals with chronic conditions in managing their health independently.

Evidence from previous meta-analyses has demonstrated the effectiveness of telemedicine interventions in improving clinical outcomes, including significant reductions in blood pressure [2]. In the past five years, the telemedicine industry has experienced rapid growth, with market projections exceeding $130 billion by 2025 [3]. As digitalization progresses across sectors, healthcare systems increasingly incorporate digital health applications, including mobile health (mHealth) [4]. These technological innovations have significant potential to enhance patient access, reduce healthcare costs, and improve the effectiveness and efficiency of healthcare systems [5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), mHealth is defined as “medical and public health practice supported by mobile devices, such as mobile phones, patient monitoring devices, personal digital assistants, and other wireless devices” [6]. mHealth-based telemedicine employs a variety of communication methods, with short message service (SMS) among the most cost-effective. Additionally, web- and internet-based platforms are rapidly gaining popularity and represent the second most common form of intervention [7].

Low-income countries often prioritize expanding access to essential health services and frequently rely on foreign assistance to support healthcare delivery [8]. Donor funding continues to play a critical role in enabling health service innovations in these settings. Meanwhile, middle-income and high-income countries face similar systemic challenges in financing and organizing healthcare services. Notably, over 70% of the global population resides in middle-income countries – defined as those with gross national incomes per capita ranging from US$1,006 to $12,235 – and these nations bear a disproportionately high share of the global disease burden [9].

A fundamental requirement for designing and implementing effective health benefit packages – i.e., defining which services and treatments are covered – is the accurate estimation of healthcare costs [10]. Effective pricing and reimbursement policies are essential components of strategic purchasing, helping to incentivize high-quality care from healthcare providers. Contracting mechanisms, which define terms for service delivery and payment, also play a key role in setting expectations around costs. Furthermore, performance-based payment systems, which reward providers based on service quality and outcomes, must be appropriately priced to reinforce the right incentives [10]. To better align payments with actual service delivery costs, many countries are moving away from traditional line-item budgets toward more sophisticated provider payment mechanisms. These include fee-for-service (FFS), bundled payments, diagnosis-related groups (DRGs), and capitation. Alongside these shifts, countries are revising pricing strategies to reflect the actual costs of healthcare provision [10].

Several studies have evaluated the effectiveness of mHealth interventions in managing chronic conditions such as hypertension. One such initiative involved a combined web-based and SMS text-message tele-monitoring system that provided real-time feedback [11]. This system issued alerts when participants failed to submit enough readings, sent reminders to contact healthcare providers for readings that were critically high or low, and encouraged follow-up when average blood pressure exceeded target thresholds. Additionally, healthcare providers were granted access to patients’ data via a web interface supported by embedded algorithms [11].

In Ghana, the majority of telemedicine services have been used only in pilot projects [12]. The mHealth projects that have been carried out include the SENE Portable Digital Assistant (PDA) Project, the Mobile Technology for Community Health (MoTeCH), Millennium Villages and Mobile Telemedicine, and the USAID-DELIVER Project, among others [13]. Akoma Pa is a collaborative initiative designed to improve patient outcomes by strengthening the diagnosis, control, and treatment of hypertension and diabetes in Ghana. The project focuses on delivering high-quality health services and essential medications, with the overarching goal of reducing life-threatening complications and enhancing quality of life. Collaborators for this project include Medtronic LABS, the Christian Health Association of Ghana (CHAG), and Novartis SSA, with financial support from the German Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development (BMZ) [12]. The German ‘Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit’ (GIZ) supported implementation across 85 CHAG health facilities in eight regions of Ghana [12]. While previous mHealth interventions have demonstrated success in improving clinical outcomes such as blood pressure control [14], this study examines implementation at scale. It addresses the cost and reimbursement structures of the Akoma Pa project.

While these projects demonstrate the potential of digital health technologies, there remains a significant gap in the scientific literature regarding the coverage, pricing structures, and reimbursement mechanisms for telemedicine services in Ghana. Furthermore, evidence on the operational models and scalability of these services is sparse. This study aims to address these gaps by using the Akoma Pa project as a case study to explore practical insights into the implementation, financial sustainability, and policy implications of telemedicine in the Ghanaian context from the perspectives of healthcare professionals.

Methods

Study design

An exploratory qualitative study design was adopted to investigate healthcare professionals’ perspectives on the coverage, pricing, and reimbursement of the Akoma Pa intervention. This approach was chosen for its ability to capture detailed insights into participants’ experiences, perceptions, and suggestions [15].

Sampling and participants

A multistage sampling approach was employed in this study. The Akoma Pa Project was piloted in 85 CHAG health facilities across 8 of Ghana’s 16 administrative regions. These 8 regions represent the complete set of regions in which the Akoma Pa intervention was actively implemented, and all were included in the study.

In the first stage, the 85 facilities were stratified by region to ensure proportional representation across all 8 implementation regions. From each regional stratum, facilities were randomly selected, yielding a total of 46 health facilities included in the study.

In the second stage, purposive sampling was used to recruit participants from the selected facilities, specifically the healthcare professionals designated as the “Akoma Pa Champion” at each facility. These Champions were chosen because they served as the primary point of contact for implementing the intervention at the facility level and were the best-trained and most informed individuals regarding its day-to-day operation. Inclusion criteria required that participants be health professionals actively serving in this implementation role. All 46 sampled facilities were invited to participate in the study. Of these, 29 responded and consented to participate. The remaining 17 facilities did not respond to the invitation despite follow-up, and no reasons for non-participation were provided.

Interviews were subsequently conducted with one participant per responding facility. Retrospective assessment of the coded data indicated that thematic saturation had been reached within the 29 interviews collected; this was determined by systematically reviewing the final three interviews against the established codebook and confirming that no new themes or subthemes emerged, suggesting that the achieved sample was analytically sufficient despite the incomplete response rate. Participants included physician assistants, nurses, biomedical scientists, pharmacists, nutritionists, and other health professionals, ensuring a diverse range of professional perspectives from across the implementation sites.

Data collection procedure

Data was collected through in-depth interviews (IDIs) with healthcare professionals, providing detailed information on the effects of coverage, pricing, and reimbursement on patient utilization and access. The study also gathered recommendations from health professionals on enhancing pricing and reimbursement strategies. Key informant interviews were conducted using an in-depth interview guide in February 2024. The interview guide developed (based on literature findings) [16–19] for this research included questions on participants’ backgrounds, perceptions of appropriate service pricing, cost coverage, reimbursement models for healthcare professionals, accessibility challenges, and patient concerns regarding intervention costs. The guide was reviewed iteratively by the research team before data collection to ensure clarity and alignment with study objectives.

All interviews were recorded and transcribed. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained for both participation and audio recording. Interviews were conducted in person at an agreed-upon location and time with each participant to minimize disruption to clinical duties. Where interviews were interrupted, the recording was paused and resumed following the interruption to maintain continuity. No interviews were conducted remotely, as in-person engagement was considered important for building rapport and ensuring participant comfort in discussing sensitive topics related to financing and reimbursement. The interviews lasted approximately 30 min on average. All the interviews were carried out in English. To ensure accurate interpretation of the data, participants were given a verbal summary of their responses, with the opportunity to confirm, correct, or expand on their statements immediately after the interview.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using the software tool ATLAS.ti (version 24.1.0.30612). Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis [20] using a hybrid deductive-inductive strategy. This method involves six phases: RAB repeatedly read through the transcribed audio recordings to become acquainted with the data. Each transcript was examined line by line, with notable sections highlighted. A coding strategy, including deductive and inductive approaches were devised to analyze responses to the interview questions. One theme, (1) scope of the intervention, served as a contextual framing theme, describing the nature and boundaries of the intervention and providing the descriptive foundation upon which subsequent analytical themes were interpreted. Four themes were developed deductively, guided a priori by the WHO Health Financing Framework [21], which organizes health financing around the functions of revenue raising, pooling, and purchasing: (2) pricing strategies, (3) forms of reimbursement approaches, (4) funding of the project, and (5) patient utilization of services. Two additional themes, (6) challenges encountered with the intervention and (7) improvement of the intervention, were derived inductively, emerging from participant accounts during the coding process, and were not pre-specified by the framework. No further inductive themes emerged beyond these two. The WHO Health Financing Framework thus served as the deductive coding structure, with inductive analysis allowing for themes beyond the framework’s predefined domains to surface.

Coding was conducted by the lead researcher. To ensure consistency and credibility, a sample of coded transcripts was reviewed by the supervisory team, and coding decisions were discussed at consensus meetings held during the analytical process. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until agreement was reached, and the codebook was refined iteratively based on these discussions. No formal inter-rater reliability metric was calculated, as the analytical approach prioritized interpretive consensus within a qualitative framework. A structured codebook was developed prior to analysis, containing the four pre-defined and emergent themes, their definitions, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and representative examples from the data. The codebook was applied consistently across all transcripts and refined iteratively as analytical decisions were made.

Reflexivity was considered throughout the study to acknowledge the potential influence of the researchers’ perspectives on the research process. The research team reflected on their professional backgrounds and possible assumptions related to the intervention. To mitigate potential bias, data collection followed a structured interview guide, and the analysis involved collaborative coding and regular team discussions to ensure that interpretations were grounded in participants’ accounts.

Scope of the intervention

Scope of the intervention

The Akoma Pa intervention exclusively covered two non-communicable diseases: hypertension and diabetes. Eligibility for enrolment was limited to patients diagnosed with either or both conditions. A participant shared that the increasing prevalence and burden could inform the focus of these diseases in the country. As one nurse explained:

… the project came about due to the high level of diseases that we used to have, mostly hypertension and diabetes… at most primary healthcare facilities. (P016, Nurse)

The intervention provided access to a limited range of medications, specifically metformin and amlodipine. The restriction of the intervention to two drugs was largely attributable to funding constraints, which limited the procurement and operational coverage of additional medications. However, this raised concerns, as different patients may respond to different hypertensive or diabetic drugs, as shared in the quote below. This meant that patients who had any other medical prescriptions beyond these two medications had to pay out of pocket or choose not to enroll in the intervention.

…we were limited to only amlodipine and metformin as the drugs that we are supposed to give to them. However, people with hypertension and diabetes, depending on their condition…, they have different medications that they respond to… (P023, Physician Assistant)

2. Pricing strategies

Pricing strategies

Participants reported that the Akoma Pa intervention initially lacked a formal pricing structure, leading to the provision of free services, including medications and diagnostic consumables. While this approach was perceived as increasing accessibility, several participants raised concerns regarding its long-term sustainability. One health worker noted,

So basically, when Akoma Pa started, there hadn’t been any pricing system. (P024, Nurse)

While another emphasized that;

When Akoma Pa came, their pricing system was free. When it comes to not only the medications but also the consumables as well, everything was free. (P013, Senior Health Educator)

The debate over whether telemedicine services should be priced similarly to or lower than in-person care reflects an underlying tension between cost recovery and access equity. While some stakeholders emphasized the need to maintain comparable pricing to ensure financial sustainability and cover operational costs, others argued that reduced pricing would enhance affordability and promote equitable access, particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. A physician assistant and senior nursing officer stated;

I think it should be the same because with telemedicine we are just adding on the digital structures to help with the patient’s data being saved and then to speed up seeing the number of clients you have so I think per where I’m coming from, if you are going to change or adjust the pricing system to go up it will kind of demotivate some clients from coming. (P018, Physician Assistant)

…it should be different. If it can be cheaper than the old system we used to have, it will help. (P014, Senior Nursing Officer)

To support the sustainable pricing of such interventions, participants proposed a range of strategies. First, some recommended cost-sharing mechanisms that account for key service inputs, such as laboratory tests, while advocating for subsidized medication pricing. As one participant suggested,

Regarding the pricing, we should consider their labs… the medication can be subsidized. (P029, Physician Assistant)

Second, the need for a formal policy or regulatory framework was emphasized. Participants argued that standardized pricing established at the national or regional level would enhance equity and coherence. One participant remarked,

I believe if there’s a regulation, then there could be a price that has been decided at the policy level that the people…should pay. (P012, Biomedical Scientist)

Finally, stakeholder involvement, particularly the inclusion of patients in price-setting discussions, was considered essential, underscoring the importance of aligning pricing strategies with users’ willingness and ability to pay. Reflecting this view, a nurse stated,

So, I think the patients should be given that privilege to bring out their ideas on the appropriate pricing they would want. (P024, Nurse)

3. Forms of reimbursement approaches

Forms of reimbursement approaches

Participants shared that the Akoma Pa intervention lacked established reimbursement models that were implemented or used. This lack of a reimbursement framework could be attributed to the intervention’s absence of clear pricing guidelines, which are essential for determining the processes and amounts for reimbursements. Without these guidelines, it becomes challenging to standardize reimbursement procedures.

There isn’t any reimbursement policy or anything like that, because it’s free. (P016, Nurse)

Other participants reported receiving some form of compensation. Compensation was provided through nonmonetary incentives, such as airtime, and occasional monetary incentives, though these were described as modest, especially for supervisory roles. This mixed approach to compensation suggests an effort to motivate and reward participants within the intervention’s budget constraints.

Oh… at the first phase of Akoma Pa, they were giving us some incentives, yeah, in the form of airtime. Sometimes they would give you some money, and as the supervisor, I was getting small, small incentives. (P003, Nurse)

However, the limited and irregular nature of these incentives might not have been sufficient to fully compensate participants for their time and effort, potentially affecting overall engagement and commitment to the intervention.

With the compensations, I think it’s okay… if you are to compensate on the cost of service truly, and then the payment or the time spent, what was being given is just a token. And it wasn’t even regular. Once in a while, like a token. (P006, Mental Health Nutritionist)

Of the 29 participants, 21 reported receiving some form of incentive or reimbursement associated with the intervention, while 8 reported receiving none despite being part of the same program. This disparity was not explained by differences in workload or service provision, with some participants attributing it to decisions made at the facility management level. While funds may have been designated for compensation, they did not always reach those lower in the hierarchy. This lack of transparency and fairness in the distribution process can lead to frustration and demotivation among healthcare workers.

When the money gets to the management level, they don’t give us any. (P015, Nurse)

4. Funding for the project

Funding for the project

The funding of the Akoma Pa project was primarily provided by a nongovernmental organization (NGO). Participants noted that the intervention’s financial support came from an external source, which was crucial to sustaining the program. However, this dependence also creates vulnerabilities, as the program’s continuity and expansion may be contingent on the ongoing availability of external resources.

… it’s an NGO that sponsored the program, but we’re not sure how long the support will last. If they pull out, it might be difficult to keep the services going. (P013, Senior Health Educator)

5. Patient utilization of services

Patient utilization of services

Participants observed that patients were motivated to participate in the Akoma Pa intervention primarily due to the financial relief it provided. By eliminating out-of-pocket expenses for services such as consultations, medications, and diagnostics, the intervention significantly enhanced healthcare access, particularly for patients with chronic conditions. This financial incentive was frequently cited as a key driver of patient engagement and consistent attendance.

They are always happy to come because it will at least spare them some money. (P001, Nurse)

Many participants reported a marked increase in patient attendance following the intervention’s introduction, highlighting both the community’s unmet healthcare needs and the perceived value of the services offered.

Our attendance skyrocketed… So, it had a lot of impact on us. (P020, Nurse)

However, concerns were raised about the intervention’s scalability if services are no longer offered free of charge. Introducing user fees could reduce attendance, particularly among low-income patients.

6. Challenges encountered with the intervention

Challenges encountered with the intervention

The participants shared internet connectivity challenges. The intervention’s reliance on stable internet connectivity meant that compromised connectivity led to delays and extended wait times. Such disruptions could hinder real-time communication, data transfer, and overall coordination, potentially affecting the quality and timeliness of the intervention’s activities.

We sometimes have challenges with the network. Yeah. So, when there is a break in the network, it means the waiting time would have to be extended… (P005, Nurse)

This challenge persisted when healthcare workers sought to follow up with patients by phone.

…and sometimes it is difficult to trace some people’s network coverage when you want to reach them on the phone… (P023, Physician Assistant)

An important challenge identified was that some patients mistook follow-up calls for fraudulent activity. This perception created hesitation in responding to providers and, in some cases, disrupted continuity of care. This finding may reflect broader concerns related to limited digital literacy and trust in remote healthcare interactions.

You see, people were not so used to this whole telemedicine thing where they will have to receive calls from people to check on them… at a point, the coordinator used to report, they get feedback when they call them to check up on them; they feel they are fraudsters who are coming to dupe them so they don’t truly give them the necessary attention they need. That’s a real challenge with it. (P022, Nurse)

7. Improvement of the intervention

Improvement of the intervention

Some participants suggested that integrating the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) could enhance the sustainability and affordability of e-health services. This highlights the potential benefits of collaborating with the NHIS to integrate e-health services into the existing healthcare framework. By including e-health services under NHIS coverage, the intervention could reduce out-of-pocket expenses for patients, making it more accessible and financially sustainable.

So based on my experience so far, I think you shouldn’t neglect involving the use of national health insurance as one of the requirements for e-health service, and then with a little top-up on it. So, you could probably link up with the national health insurance, adding a bit to the protocols to include e-health, so it would at least sustain, or even absorb, some of the cost of care. And then maybe a little top-up. (P006, Mental Health Nutritionist)

Another participant highlighted the need for the intervention to broaden its scope beyond diabetes and hypertension. This suggests a call for a more comprehensive approach to healthcare delivery that considers a broader range of health conditions. By incorporating additional health conditions, such as infectious diseases, maternal and child health issues, or mental health disorders, into the intervention, the program can better meet the diverse healthcare needs of the population it serves.

Yes, I think they have to open up their tentacles not only to diabetes and hypertension. If they can, they should also add other conditions depending on their strength, though. (P004, Nurse)

The need for increased investment by authorities in mHealth interventions was also highlighted. Investment by authorities is crucial for the scalability and sustainability of mHealth initiatives. Such investments could include funding for technology infrastructure, training for healthcare workers, and ongoing support to ensure that interventions are effective and accessible.

Yeah, that’s what I was saying. So, I think that after this survey, as you presented the data to the appropriate authorities, they should take up that challenge and also try to invest… (P025, Medical Health Scientist)

Discussion

This study, conducted with the Akoma Pa Project, explored healthcare professionals’ perspectives on the coverage, pricing, and reimbursement of telemedicine services in Ghana and identified several key findings. The Akoma Pa intervention provided free hypertension and diabetes care, improving patient attendance through cost savings. However, concerns arose about limited medication options, lack of a pricing or reimbursement structure, and reliance on non-governmental organization (NGO) funding. Participants debated appropriate pricing for mHealth and noted challenges, including internet connectivity issues and patient skepticism. Recommendations included integrating the intervention into the NHIS, expanding its scope, and securing sustainable funding. The results were interpreted through the WHO Health Financing Framework [21]. The findings raise particular concerns about equity and financial protection. The uneven distribution of reimbursements suggests that the intervention has not yet achieved the equitable financing arrangements the framework identifies as necessary for progress toward universal health coverage (UHC). Collectively, the themes illuminate weaknesses across all three financing functions, revenue raising, pooling, and purchasing, and point to deeper structural and policy gaps that must be addressed if mHealth interventions are to achieve meaningful integration into Ghana’s health system.

The intervention targeted hypertension and diabetes, reflecting the growing NCD burden in Ghana and Sub-Saharan Africa [22]. While epidemiologically justified, exclusion operated on two levels. The first excluded patients with conditions outside these two diseases, limiting the intervention’s contribution to UHC, which requires essential services across a range of conditions [23]. The second, more immediately consequential level operated within the target diseases: the medication formulary was restricted to metformin and amlodipine due to funding constraints, meaning patients requiring other antihypertensive or antidiabetic agents either paid out of pocket or were excluded from enrolment. A telemedicine program that excludes patients with the most complex medication needs risks widening rather than narrowing inequities within its own target population. This signals that funding adequacy, not just availability, is a critical determinant of program equity. Policymakers should specify a minimum essential medicines list for any reimbursed NCD telemedicine service, rather than allowing formulary scope to be dictated by donor budget cycles. The findings regarding donor funding being the main funding source for the intervention align with the WHO Health Financing Framework’s revenue-raising function, which emphasizes the need for sustainable, diversified financing. Participants’ accounts suggest that the intervention relied predominantly on donor funding, raising concerns about long-term financial sustainability, a gap the framework identifies as a systemic vulnerability in low- and middle-income health systems.

The intervention’s free-of-charge model demonstrably improved access; participants reported attendance increases consistent with the broader literature on financial barriers to chronic disease care [24, 25]. Patient utilization patterns reported in this study reflect the purchasing function’s effectiveness in translating financial inputs into service access. The WHO framework posits that strategic purchasing should maximize population health gain by aligning provider incentives with service delivery goals. However, participants were divided on appropriate pricing at scale: some argued for parity with in-person care to reflect the digital infrastructure involved, others favored lower prices to protect affordability, and a third group proposed cost-sharing with subsidized medications. Participants also stressed that patients should be involved in price-setting, a point supported by willingness-to-pay literature showing that patient-informed pricing better sustains utilization than provider-determined rates [10]. A scalability risk cuts across all positions: participants warned that introducing user fees at any level could reverse attendance gains, particularly among low-income patients. This concern is directly relevant to NHIS integration. If reimbursement translates into co-payments, the documented equity gains could be partially eroded, and any financial sustainability analysis must explicitly model this risk.

The absence of a reimbursement framework reflects a recurring structural pattern across African digital health intervention programs, where donor-funded pilots demonstrate proof of concept without building the financial architecture for continuation [26–28]. The reliance on donor financing observed in this study reflects a broader structural vulnerability in health technology financing in African settings, where dependence on external funding sources has similarly been documented to undermine long-term sustainability and local ownership across other health technology domains, including vaccine procurement [29]. In contrast, Rwanda achieved partial telemedicine sustainability through the Ministry of Health and insurance co-financing [30], and Kenya has progressively integrated mHealth into county health budgets despite declining donor support [31]. The critical variable is therefore not donor involvement per se, but whether an intentional transition plan exists from donor dependency to institutionalized financing. Ghana lacks such a framework for telemedicine, and the absence of billing mechanisms also limits accountability; without service records, quality audits, and program evaluation at scale become impractical, with direct implications for any future NHIS integration.

Compensation inequities among healthcare workers represent a governance as much as a fairness problem. Participants described incentives as irregular and insufficient, with reports of funds failing to reach frontline staff after allocation at the management level. This reflects a breakdown in the pooling function as conceptualized by the WHO framework. Effective pooling requires that collected funds be equitably distributed according to need and entitlement rather than managerial discretion. In telemedicine, where follow-up quality depends almost entirely on individual provider initiative, with fewer structural cues than facility-based care to prompt protocol adherence, motivational deficits translate directly into reduced care continuity. These accounts suggest inadequate financial controls and unclear disbursement protocols that, if unaddressed, would undermine the credibility of any scaled version of the program. Transition to structured, auditable provider payment frameworks linked to service delivery is necessary.

Participants consistently identified NHIS integration as the most viable sustainability pathway, consistent with approaches identified in East Africa [31]. However, practical barriers are substantial. mHealth interventions are not currently in the NHIS benefit package, and inclusion requires a formal multi-stakeholder review process subject to fiscal constraints, given the NHIS’s existing underfunding and claims backlogs [10]. Reimbursement rate-setting is technically complex in the absence of standardized virtual care service definitions. Co-payment risks, as noted above, must be managed through subsidy provisions for vulnerable patients. Claims processing would require investment in data interoperability and provider accreditation frameworks that the NHIS currently lacks, alongside unresolved regulatory questions on data protection and the clinical validity of remote diagnosis. A phased approach, seeking NHIA recognition of the model as a reimbursable pilot with explicit evaluation provisions, would generate the evidence and institutional capacity needed for full benefit package inclusion.

Network connectivity disrupted follow-up care, consistent with findings from comparable mHealth contexts [18, 31]. Yet patients continued to engage despite these barriers, attendance rose rather than fell, suggesting that cost removal was a sufficiently powerful motivator to sustain utilization even when connectivity degraded the user experience. This implies that reducing financial frictions can partially compensate for infrastructure limitations, though offline-capable delivery options remain a technical priority. Patient skepticism about follow-up calls, with some mistaking providers for fraudsters, reflects both limited digital familiarity and a rational response to the prevalence of phone scams in Ghana. Addressing this requires more than generic patient education: trust is built through clear opt-in processes, community health worker involvement, and embedding digital services within existing community health structures rather than presenting them as external innovations [32].

The Akoma Pa Project demonstrates that mHealth can improve chronic disease access in Ghanaian primary care, but its current architecture, donor-dependent, narrowly scoped, lacking formal reimbursement, and marked by compensation governance weaknesses, is not built for equitable scale. Sustainability requires expanding the medication formulary, formalizing reimbursement with transparent governance, pursuing phased NHIS integration that protects utilization gains, and investing in both digital infrastructure and community-grounded trust-building. Ghana’s UHC commitments and the accelerating reduction in donor funding make this transition increasingly urgent.

This study has three key policy implications. Ghana’s Ministry of Health should develop a national telemedicine policy framework that covers service definition, provider accreditation, pricing standards, and transparent compensation for healthcare workers. The National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) should initiate a scoping process for including digital health interventions like mHealth in the NHIS benefit package, with participatory rate-setting and subsidy provisions for vulnerable patients. Development partners should require implementers to develop financing transition plans before pilot phases conclude, prioritize investment in enabling conditions such as digital infrastructure and regulatory capacity, and coordinate with government institutions from the outset to bridge the gap between innovation and institutionalization.

Limitations

Although meticulous measures were taken to avoid bias in the responses, the data collected relied on self-reported information from participants, which could introduce biases such as social desirability bias. The study also included a diverse range of healthcare professionals, but the perspectives of patients, a critical stakeholder group, were not directly captured. This exclusion limits the understanding of patient experiences and satisfaction with the intervention. Additionally, data collection was conducted in February 2024, when the project funding was nearing completion. As a result, participants’ responses may reflect experiences specific to that stage of the program. Consequently, perceptions reported in this study may evolve as the intervention progresses or as longer-term outcomes become evident. Also, coding was conducted primarily by one researcher, and the absence of a formal inter-rater reliability metric may limit the systematic documentation of coding consistency. A further limitation concerns transferability. As this study examined a single donor-funded telemedicine intervention within a specific health system context, the financing arrangements, reimbursement mechanisms, and incentive structures documented may not transfer to government-funded, insurance-based, or hybrid mHealth models. Similarly, participants were all from CHAG health facilities. CHAG facilities represent a distinct faith-based non-profit sub-sector of Ghana’s health system, with primarily different ownership and accountability from government-owned Ghana Health Service facilities. To this effect, findings may not necessarily apply to government-owned Ghana Health Service facilities. Additionally, the non-response from 17 of the 46 sampled facilities may introduce selection bias, and the findings may reflect the views of more engaged or positively disposed participants. The non-responding facilities were distributed across all 8 regions included in the study, though specific facility-level characteristics were not compared. Readers are encouraged to assess the applicability of these findings to their own contexts in light of the description provided in this study.

Conclusion

The Akoma Pa intervention has demonstrated the potential of telemedicine to improve access to care for patients with hypertension and diabetes in Ghana, particularly by reducing out-of-pocket costs and increasing patient engagement. However, its sustainability and scalability are hindered by a narrow scope, a lack of pricing and reimbursement frameworks, dependence on external funding, and challenges with internet connectivity. To enhance its impact, the intervention should be expanded to include other health conditions, integrated with the NHIS, secured diversified funding, and invested in technological infrastructure. The findings also underscore the need for a clear regulatory framework to guide telemedicine pricing and reimbursement, ensuring consistency, transparency, and equity in implementation. Addressing these limitations will ensure Akoma Pa evolves into a sustainable model for telemedicine in low-resource settings, offering a blueprint for similar initiatives in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).
