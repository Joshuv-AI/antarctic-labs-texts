Incidence, Mortality, and Determinants of Ventilator‐Associated Pneumonia Among Pediatric Patients at the National Referral Hospital, Bhutan: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Tashi Tshering, Yeshey Dorjey, Kuenley Pedon, Mimi Lhamu Mynak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVentilator‐associated pneumonia (VAP) is a common healthcare‐associated infection in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs). In Bhutan, baseline data on VAP are limited. This study aimed to determine the incidence, mortality, and factors associated with VAP among mechanically ventilated pediatric patients at the Jigme Dorji Wangchuck (JDW) National Referral Hospital.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted in the PICU of JDW National Referral Hospital from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2019. Children aged 1 month to 12 years who required mechanical ventilation for more than 48 h were included. Demographic, clinical, and outcome data were collected and analyzed using SPSS version 23. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with VAP.ResultsAmong 92 mechanically ventilated patients, 13 developed VAP, corresponding to an incidence of 20.5 per 1000 ventilator days. Infants accounted for 79.3% of the cohort, and 57.6% were female. The most common admitting diagnoses were central nervous system infections (57.6%), congenital heart disease (35.9%), and pulmonary diseases (27.2%). The predominant pathogens were Escherichia coli (30.8%) and Klebsiella species (23.1%), with over 92% of isolates sensitive to second‐generation cephalosporins. Patients with VAP had significantly longer durations of hospitalization, PICU stay, and mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001). The overall mortality rate was 72.8%, with higher mortality observed in the non‐VAP group compared to the VAP group (78.5% vs. 38.5%, p = 0.003), likely reflecting survivor bias. Although several factors were associated with VAP in univariable analysis, none remained statistically significant after multivariable adjustment.ConclusionsThe incidence of VAP in this setting is high, with a notably elevated mortality among ventilated pediatric patients. The findings likely reflect the severity of illness and referral patterns in a national referral center. Strengthening infection prevention practices, optimizing ventilator care, and improving early referral and critical care capacity may help reduce VAP incidence and improve outcomes.

Introduction

Ventilator‐associated pneumonia (VAP) is a hospital‐acquired pulmonary infection that typically develops in patients who have received mechanical ventilation for more than 48 h [1].

Globally, VAP is among the most common intervention‐related infections in PICUs, with an estimated incidence of 13.5 cases per 1000 ventilator days [2]. Rates of VAP are notably higher in low‐ and middle‐income countries compared to high‐income settings.

Within the World Health Organization Southeast Asia Region, India has reported the highest VAP incidence at 40.1 per 1000 ventilator days, while Korea has reported the lowest at 2.1 per 1000 ventilator days [3]. Mortality rates also vary widely, with India reporting a VAP related mortality of approximately 62%, whereas no VAP related deaths were reported from Thailand and Korea [3].

The predominant pathogens responsible for VAP include Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter species, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterococcus species [3, 4]. In rare cases, gram‐positive organisms such as Methicillin‐Sensitive S. aureus, Methicillin‐Resistant S. aureus, and Vancomycin‐Resistant Enterococci have also been isolated from endotracheal aspirates [3].

Independent risk factors for the development of VAP include female sex, mechanical ventilation exceeding 4 days, PICU stay longer than 7 days, repeated re‐intubation, bronchoscopy, central venous catheterization, prior antibiotic use, indwelling urinary catheterization, and administration of corticosteroids [5, 6].

VAP contributes to prolonged PICU stays and increased healthcare costs. Therefore, implementing evidence‐based preventive strategies is essential. Recommended measures include the use of non‐invasive ventilation when feasible, daily sedation interruption with assessment for extubation readiness, preference for endotracheal over orogastric tubes, limited ventilator circuit changes unless visibly soiled or damaged, minimizing intra‐hospital transfers, elevating the head of the bed, using silver‐coated endotracheal tubes, monitoring cuff pressure, aspirating subglottic secretions, performing oral decontamination with antiseptic solutions, and ensuring rigorous hand hygiene among healthcare providers [7].

Although physical examination findings have limited sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing VAP, clinical diagnosis typically involves a thorough medical history, physical examination, and chest radiography to identify new infiltrates. Definitive diagnosis is confirmed by the growth of pathogenic organisms in endotracheal aspirate cultures [8].

Initial treatment of VAP involves broad‐spectrum antibiotics targeting likely pathogens, followed by de‐escalation based on culture and sensitivity results from endotracheal aspirates or discharge samples [9].

In Bhutan, there is currently no baseline data on the incidence, risk factors, or mortality associated with VAP in PICUs. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the incidence rate and associated risk factors for VAP and mortality outcomes of patients in the PICU of the Pediatric Department at the JDW National Referral Hospital, Bhutan. The findings will serve as foundational data to inform national policy and improve clinical outcomes for ventilated pediatric patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A prospective cohort study was conducted over 1 year from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2019 in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) of the Pediatric Department at JDW National Referral Hospital, Thimphu, Bhutan.

Study Setting

Bhutan, located in the eastern Himalayas, has an estimated population of 0.78 million [10]. JDW National Referral Hospital is a 380‐bed teaching hospital situated in the capital city and serves as the apex referral center for the country. It receives critically ill pediatric patients from regional and district hospitals across Bhutan [11].

The Pediatric Department at JDW National Referral Hospital provides comprehensive care for neonates and children under 13 years of age. It comprises multiple specialized units, including a 30‐bed Pediatric Ward, a 14‐bed Neonatal Intensive Care Unit, a 12‐bed Neonatal Ward, an 8‐bed Kangaroo Mother Care Ward, a 7‐bed PICU, a Pediatric Outpatient Clinic, and a Pediatric Emergency Unit. Additionally, the department hosts subspecialty clinics such as the High‐Risk Newborn Clinic, Developmental Clinic, Hemato‐oncology Clinic, Infectious Diseases Clinic, Respiratory Clinic, and Neurology Clinic.

The department is staffed with 12 consultant pediatricians, including one neonatologist, one pediatric critical care specialist, one pediatric nephrologist, and one pediatric palliative care specialist. Supporting them are five pediatric residents and 61 nurses [12].

The PICU is equipped with seven mechanical ventilators, and the nurse‐to‐patient ratio for ventilated patients is maintained at 1:2. A respiratory therapist is also part of the routine care team.

To prevent VAP, the PICU routinely implements a VAP prevention bundle checklist. This includes hand hygiene, adherence to aseptic techniques, daily sedation interruption and assessment of extubation readiness, elevation of the head of the bed to 30°–45°, ensuring ventilator tubing is free of excessive condensation, oral care three times daily with 2% chlorhexidine, prophylaxis for peptic ulcer disease and deep vein thrombosis, and regular monitoring of endotracheal tube cuff pressure and depth.

Study Population

All pediatric patients admitted to the PICU who required mechanical ventilation during the study period were eligible for inclusion.

Inclusion Criteria

Patients aged between 1 month and 12 years who received mechanical ventilation for a duration of at least 48 h were included in the study.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients were excluded if they received mechanical ventilation for less than 48 h, or if they were younger than 1 month or older than 12 years at the time of PICU admission.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was calculated using online software OpenEpi (www.openepi.com) [13], assuming a prevalence of 13.5% based on published literature [2], with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error. Considering a finite population of 190 PICU admissions in the preceding year and a design effect of 1, the minimum required sample size is 93.

Post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software with the following inputs; Test family: Exact; Statistical test: proportions, inequality, two independent groups; Types of power analysis: post hoc, compute achieved power‐given α, sample size, and the effect size. Input parameters; Proportion p1 0.38 (mortality in VAP group 38%), Proportion p2 0.78 (mortality in no VAP group 78%), α error probability 0.05, sample size (group 1—VAP) 13, and sample size (group 2—no VAP) 79. The study power obtained from the above sample size was 81%.

Sampling Methods

A consecutive sampling method was used to include all the pediatric patients aged 1 month to 12 years who required mechanical ventilatory support for at least 48 h in the PICU.

Study Procedure

Pediatric patients requiring intensive care were admitted to the PICU for close monitoring and management. Admitting diagnoses and demographic characteristics were documented upon admission. Patients requiring mechanical ventilatory support were intubated using endotracheal tubes‐uncuffed tubes were used for infants and small children, while cuffed tubes were selected for older children. Airway suctioning was performed using an open method, with frequency determined by clinical assessment of secretion volume and character.

Clinical parameters such as fever, new‐onset cough, and purulent sputum were recorded. Ventilator mode and settings were documented daily, including any adjustments. Vital signs, physical examination findings, oxygen saturation, and patient positioning were monitored routinely.

Intubated patients underwent regular laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, arterial blood gas analysis, serum electrolytes, liver and renal function tests, C‐reactive protein levels, and urine microscopy. When clinically indicated, blood and urine cultures were obtained, along with cultures from suspected sites of infection.

Patients on mechanical ventilation were monitored for the development of VAP using a combination of clinical, laboratory, and radiological criteria until discharge or death. VAP was suspected based on the presence of increased oxygen requirements, new‐onset purulent secretions, changes in sputum character, and increased respiratory secretions or suctioning needs.

In suspected cases of VAP, endotracheal aspirate samples were collected for microbiological culture and antibiotic sensitivity testing. Specimens were promptly transported to the microbiology laboratory and processed according to standard protocols to identify bacterial isolates. The isolated microorganisms were recorded.

Additional data, including duration of hospital stay, PICU admission, and mechanical ventilation, were collected using a structured research questionnaire.

Study Variables

Study variable‐demographic data (age, gender, and date of hospital admission), admitting diagnosis (central nervous disorders, congenital heart diseases, lung diseases, sepsis, trauma, and others), potential risk factors (Glasgow coma scale, type of endotracheal tube, re‐intubation, patient moved out of PICU, pre‐admission antibiotics, use of blood products, head elevation of 30°–45°, central venous line, nasogastric tube, urinary catheter, intraosseous line, and medications), the outcome (length of hospital stay, length of PICU stay, duration of ventilation, and mortality), and the different organisms isolated from the culture of endotracheal aspirate were recorded using a research questionnaire (supplementary file).

Operational Definition

VAP was defined using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria for pediatric patients, supported by clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings from endotracheal aspirate cultures [14].

Data Management

Data collectors and the nurses of the PICU were trained on the study instrument, consent form, data collection procedure, and confidentiality of the respondents. The collected data were checked for completeness daily by the investigators to monitor the overall quality of the data collection process.

Data Analysis

Data were double‐entered and validated using EpiData (Version 3.1 for entry and version 2.2.2.183 for analysis, EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark). Data were exported and analyzed using SPSS (version 23). The Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test was performed to test for the normality of the study data. Normally distributed continuous variables of demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using a t‐test and presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non‐normally distributed two independent variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney test and expressed as medians (IQR). The Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test was used to analyze categorical variables and presented in frequencies and percentages.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to determine factors associated with VAP. Results were reported as crude and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics Considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Board of Health, Ministry of Health, Bhutan (Reference number: REBH/Approval/2016/055). Written informed consent was obtained from all the parents/guardians of the study participants.

Results

During the 1‐year study period, a total of 277 patients were admitted to the PICU. Of these, 143 required mechanical ventilatory support. Forty‐two patients received ventilatory support for less than 48 h and were excluded from the study. The remaining 92 patients required continued ventilatory support for more than 48 h and were included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Among 92 patients who received continued ventilatory support, 13 developed VAP (Figure 1). The cumulative duration of ventilator use was 633 days, yielding an incidence of VAP of 20.5 per 1000 ventilator days.

Demographic and Admitting Diagnosis

Of the 92 patients who required mechanical ventilation in the PICU, approximately 80% (73/92) were infants aged 1–12 months. More than half of the cohort were female (57.6%, 53/92). The most common admitting diagnosis was central nervous system infection (57.6%), followed by congenital heart disease (35.9%), pulmonary diseases (27.2%), and genetic syndromes (27.2%). In over two‐thirds of the patients, the GCS score at intubation was less than 7, and more than half had received antibiotic therapy prior to PICU admission. Factors Associated With VAP

In the univariable logistic regression analysis, several factors were significantly associated with the development of VAP, including age > 36 months, female sex, prolonged hospital stay, PICU admission, ventilator duration > 7 days, use of blood products, prior antibiotic therapy, central line placement, NG insertion, urinary catheterization, intraosseous access, and administration of steroids, paralytic agents, and sedatives.

However, after adjusting for potential confounders in the multivariable logistic regression model, age > 36 months, female sex, NG tube insertion, and use of steroids and paralytic agents remained associated with increased likelihood of developing VAP, although these associations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The incidence of VAP in the PICU at the JDW National Referral Hospital, Bhutan, was found to be 20.5 cases per 1000 ventilator days. This rate is notably higher than the pooled global estimate of 13.5 cases per 1000 ventilator days reported in a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis [2]. VAP rates tend to be elevated in low‐ and middle‐income countries compared to high‐income settings. Within the WHO Southeast Asia Region, for instance, the Republic of Korea has reported the lowest incidence at 2.1 cases per 1000 ventilator days [3]. The comparatively high rate of VAP in Bhutan may be attributed to staffing constraints within the PICU, particularly the nurse‐to‐patient ratio. At present, the ratio stands at 1:2 for ventilated patients, which may limit the capacity for continuous monitoring and timely intervention. This study recommends increasing nursing staff to achieve a 1:1 nurse‐to‐ventilated patient ratio and implementing a standardized intubation protocol. These measures are critical to enhancing the quality of care and reducing the incidence of VAP in Bhutan.

Globally, mortality rates in PICUs vary considerably, ranging from 9.1% to 38.1% across different regions [15, 16, 17]. A retrospective review by Durak and Guvenc (2023) reported a mortality rate of 19.4% among ventilated pediatric patients, while Bacha and Tuli documented a substantially higher rate of 59.1% in a similar cohort [18, 19]. In comparison, the present study revealed an alarmingly high mortality rate of 72.8% among ventilated pediatric patients in Bhutan. The markedly high mortality observed in this study may reflect several contextual factors. As a national referral center, the PICU receives a disproportionately high number of critically ill children, often with advanced disease at presentation, particularly central nervous system infections and sepsis. In addition, delayed referrals from peripheral health facilities and limited pre‐hospital stabilization may contribute to poorer clinical status at the time of PICU admission. Resource constraints, including limitations in specialized critical care staffing and advanced monitoring capabilities, may have further influenced patient outcomes in this setting. Several clinical factors may also have contributed to this elevated mortality rate. One potential explanation is the prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation observed in the cohort. Additionally, the predominance of severe underlying conditions such as central nervous system infections, sepsis, and trauma likely played a significant role in the observed outcomes.

While mortality is generally expected to be higher among patients with VAP, a prospective study by Ahmadipour and Ahmadinejad reported a mortality rate of 11.4% in the VAP group compared to 4.8% in the non‐VAP group [20]. Interestingly, the current study found the opposite trend, with significantly higher mortality in the non‐VAP group (78.5%) compared to the VAP group (38.5%). This paradoxical finding may be explained by survivor bias, where patients in the non‐VAP group had severe underlying illnesses and died early before having sufficient time to develop VAP, thereby underestimating VAP occurrence in the sickest patients. Furthermore, standardized illness severity scoring systems such as the Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) and Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction (PELOD) scores were not available in this study. Instead, surrogate markers of disease severity, including Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores and the need for intensive interventions, were used. The absence of validated severity scoring limits the ability to adjust for baseline illness severity and may have influenced the observed mortality patterns.

The VAP care bundle is a structured, evidence‐based approach aimed at reducing the incidence of VAP in critically ill patients [21]. It is endorsed by several leading organizations, including the Institute for Healthcare Improvement, the Intensive Care Society, the American Thoracic Society, the Infectious Diseases Society of America, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [22, 23, 24]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis by Martinez‐Reviejo and Rello demonstrated a significant reduction in VAP episodes among patients receiving care bundles compared to those who did not (OR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.33–0.54) [25].

In Bhutan, the VAP care bundle has been adopted as part of the routine care checklist for all patients admitted to intensive care units. In the present study, one key component—head‐of‐bed elevation to 30°–45° was implemented in over 94% of ventilated patients. Despite this, the study revealed a high incidence of VAP and elevated mortality rates in the PICU, suggesting potential gaps in the consistent and comprehensive application of the care bundle. These findings underscore the need for stricter adherence to all components of the VAP care bundle. The study recommends reinforcing implementation protocols, enhancing staff training, and conducting regular audits on compliance to VAP care bundle. Such measures are essential to improving patient outcomes and reducing VAP‐related morbidity and mortality in Bhutan's intensive care settings.

In the present study, the leading admitting diagnosis among PICU patients was central nervous system infection (57.6%), followed by congenital heart disease (35.9%) and sepsis (27.2%). In contrast, a prospective cohort study conducted in India reported sepsis (16%) as the most common diagnosis, followed by acyanotic congenital heart disease (14%) [26]. These differences in diagnostic prevalence likely reflect variations in population characteristics, referral patterns, and regional disease epidemiology. Such disparities underscore the importance of contextualizing clinical findings within local healthcare settings and caution against overgeneralizing results across geographically and demographically distinct populations.

A study by Chen and Li investigating the etiology of pneumonia in PICU revealed that bacterial infections were the most frequently identified cause (40.5%), followed by viral pathogens (34.4%), atypical organisms (19.7%), and fungal infections (4.3%) [2]. The predominant bacterial isolates included S. aureus (39.5%), Streptococcus pneumoniae (32.9%), Acinetobacter baumannii (16.3%), Haemophilus influenzae (15.1%), K. pneumoniae (13.9%), and P. aeruginosa (12.2%). These findings underscore the diverse microbial landscape contributing to pediatric pneumonia and highlight the need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship and diagnostic precision in PICU settings.

Among ventilated patients in the same cohort, VAP emerged as the most common complication, with an incidence of 13.5 per 1000 ventilator days, followed by accidental extubation at 7.4 per 1000 ventilator days [27]. These complication rates reinforce the importance of rigorous adherence to ventilator care bundles and continuous quality improvement initiatives to mitigate preventable harm in critically ill children.

In a referenced study, P. aeruginosa was the most frequently isolated organism from endotracheal aspirate cultures (29.8%), followed by K. pneumoniae (21.6%), S. aureus (18.9%), and A. species (13.5%) [6]. In contrast, the present study identified E. coli as the predominant isolate (31%), followed by K. species (23%). Notably, over 92% of the organisms isolated in the current cohort demonstrated sensitivity to second‐generation antibiotics. These differences in microbial profiles may reflect regional variations in pathogen prevalence, antimicrobial stewardship practices, and diagnostic protocols. The high sensitivity to second‐generation antibiotics observed in the present study suggests a potentially favorable resistance pattern, which could inform empiric therapy guidelines and stewardship strategies in Bhutan's PICU settings.

In the current study, univariable logistic regression analysis identified several factors significantly associated with the development of VAP, including age > 36 months, female sex, prolonged hospital stay, PICU admission, ventilator duration > 7 days, use of blood products, prior antibiotic therapy, central line placement, nasogastric tube insertion, urinary catheterization, intraosseous access, and administration of steroids, paralytic agents, and sedatives. However, in the multivariable logistic regression model, no independent predictors of VAP were identified after adjustment, likely due to the limited sample size. These findings partially align with previous studies, which have reported male sex, smoking history, higher Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, prolonged mechanical ventilation, inappropriate antibiotic therapy, lower oxygenation levels upon PICU admission, use of steroids and sedative agents, and re‐intubation as significant risk factors for VAP [6, 28, 29]. The variation in associated factors may reflect differences in study populations, PICU protocols, and underlying comorbidities.

Importantly, while certain risk factors such as prolonged PICU stay and mechanical ventilation are often unavoidable due to the severity of underlying medical or surgical conditions, these findings underscore the need for vigilant monitoring and targeted preventive strategies. Optimizing modifiable risk factors—such as minimizing unnecessary device use, ensuring appropriate sedation practices, and reinforcing infection control protocols—remains critical to reducing VAP incidence and improving outcomes in pediatric intensive care settings.

The 2024 update from the International Society for Infectious Diseases outlines key recommendations for the prevention of VAP in intensive care settings. These include the establishment of robust VAP surveillance systems, implementation of targeted preventive measures, provision of structured education and training programs focused on infection control and VAP‐specific strategies, ensuring adequate PICU staffing with trained and competent personnel, and maintaining effective laboratory infrastructure for timely specimen processing and result reporting [30]. Collectively, these measures are essential to mitigating the incidence and complications of VAP in critically ill patients. In addition to systemic interventions, several device‐related and procedural strategies have demonstrated efficacy in reducing VAP rates. The use of metal‐coated endotracheal tubes has been shown to significantly lower VAP incidence compared to uncoated tubes, thereby contributing to reduced PICU and hospital length of stay [31].

Other evidence‐based preventive practices include the use of non‐invasive mechanical ventilation when feasible, preference for endotracheal over orogastric tubes, limiting ventilator circuit changes to instances of mechanical damage or visible contamination, minimizing intra‐hospital transfers, maintaining head‐of‐bed elevation between 30° and 45°, monitoring endotracheal cuff pressure, aspirating subglottic secretions, performing oral decontamination with antiseptic solutions, and ensuring rigorous hand hygiene among healthcare providers [32]. These multifaceted strategies underscore the importance of a comprehensive, protocol‐driven approach to VAP prevention, tailored to the resource context and clinical demands of each intensive care unit.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, it was conducted at a single health center with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The incidence rate of VAP reported herein may not accurately reflect the national burden. Ideally, a multicenter approach involving all referral hospitals with PICU facilities across Bhutan would have provided a more representative estimate of VAP incidence and allowed for comparative analysis of microbial patterns isolated from endotracheal tube aspirates.

Second, the study population was predominantly composed of infants (approximately 80% aged 1 month to 12 years), which may constrain the applicability of the findings to the broader pediatric population admitted to PICUs. Despite these limitations, a post hoc power analysis indicated a study power of 81%, suggesting that the sample size was sufficient to support meaningful statistical inferences.

Third, the study did not incorporate standardized illness severity scoring systems such as the PRISM or PELOD scores. Consequently, adjustment for baseline severity of illness was not possible, and the high mortality observed may partly reflect the underlying disease severity and referral patterns rather than PICU‐related factors alone. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

In addition, the relatively small number of VAP events may have resulted in imprecise effect estimates, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals in the regression analysis. This also raises the possibility of sparse data bias, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Recommendations

It is recommended to strengthen early referral systems and improve pre‐PICU stabilization to reduce delays in the management of critically ill pediatric patients. PICU capacity should be enhanced through improved staffing, targeted training, and expansion of critical care monitoring and support facilities.

Strict and consistent adherence to all components of the ventilator care bundle should be ensured, supported by regular audits and feedback mechanisms to improve compliance. Standardized illness severity scoring systems such as PRISM or PELOD should be introduced to improve risk stratification and allow better adjustment for baseline severity in both clinical practice and future research.

Continuous training programs for healthcare workers on ventilator care and infection prevention should be implemented to strengthen clinical practice. In addition, antimicrobial stewardship programs and microbiological surveillance should be reinforced to guide timely, appropriate, and evidence‐based therapy.

Conclusion

This study found a high incidence of VAP (20.5 per 1000 ventilator days) and a markedly high overall mortality (72.8%) among mechanically ventilated pediatric patients in a national referral PICU in Bhutan. No independent predictors of VAP were identified after adjustment. The high mortality, particularly among non‐VAP patients, likely reflects early deaths in severely ill children, referral bias, and the absence of standardized illness severity scoring systems such as PRISM or PELOD.

These findings suggest that outcomes in this setting are strongly influenced by baseline disease severity and system‐level constraints, including delayed referral and limited critical care resources. Strengthening early referral pathways, improving PICU capacity, and ensuring strict adherence to ventilator care bundles, alongside implementation of standardized severity scoring systems, are essential to improve patient outcomes.
