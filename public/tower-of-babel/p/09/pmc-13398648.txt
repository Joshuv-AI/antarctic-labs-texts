Successful propranolol therapy for infantile hepatic hemangioma with cardiac failure
Authors: Fadhel Al Ateeqi, Ala K Shaikhkhalil, Haitham Talo, Naeem Jagirdar, Mahdia AL‐Haidar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfantile hepatic hemangioma (IHH) can cause severe complications such as high‐output cardiac failure and growth faltering, requiring prompt diagnosis and intervention. In the absence of standardized treatment guidelines, propranolol has emerged as an effective and well‐tolerated therapy. We report a female infant with multifocal IHH who presented with high‐output cardiac failure, hepatomegaly, and growth faltering. She experienced rapid and sustained clinical as well as radiologic improvement with propranolol therapy. This case reinforces propranolol's role as a safe, effective first‐line therapy for symptomatic IHH and underscores the importance of early multidisciplinary care.Keywords: beta blocker, liver hemangioma, pediatric liver tumors

INTRODUCTION

Hepatic hemangiomas are common liver tumors in infants and are classified into infantile and congenital type, each with distinct clinical courses. Infantile hepatic hemangiomas (IHH) grow rapidly after birth and involute slowly, whereas congenital hemangiomas are fully formed at birth and involute during early infancy. 1

While many IHH are asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during imaging for other reasons, they can present with severe complications including congestive heart failure, consumptive hypothyroidism, compression of surrounding structures, or coagulopathy. 2

Historically, management relied on corticosteroids, vincristine, or interferon, but these agents carried significant adverse effects and inconsistent efficacy. 3 More recently, propranolol has emerged as an effective and better‐tolerated therapy. Nonetheless, treatment failures and severe presentations requiring interventional or surgical management remain reported.

Here, we describe a case of multifocal IHH complicated by high‐output heart failure that responded rapidly to propranolol. This case adds to the growing evidence supporting propranolol as safe and effective first‐line therapy for IHH, even in critical neonatal presentations.

CASE REPORT

The patient was referred to our clinic at the age of 8.5 months for evaluation of extensive liver lesions on ultrasound. She was a term infant with a birth weight of 3125 g. Her perinatal history was notable only for polyhydramnios.

She had been recently hospitalized elsewhere for bronchiolitis. During that admission, a chest radiograph showed cardiomegaly, and echocardiography indicated high‐output cardiac failure. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly with diffusely echogenic liver and hypoechoic mass‐like lesions consistent with multifocal hepatic hemangiomas. After her respiratory symptoms improved, she was discharged home and referred to our hospital.

At presentation, she was receiving breast milk and pureed foods, introduced at 6 months. Her mother reported persistent abdominal distension, intermittent tachypnea, and poor weight gain despite good oral intake.

On examination, she appeared thin with a weight well below the 3rd percentile (Z‐score < 3). A small cutaneous hemangioma was present on her forehead. Respiratory and cardiovascular examinations were normal. The abdomen was distended and soft, and the liver edge was 6 cm below the right costal margin. No splenomegaly was noted.

Laboratory investigations showed microcytic anemia (hemoglobin 9.3 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume 69.6 fL) with otherwise normal complete blood count, liver enzymes, coagulation profile, and α‐fetoprotein.

The patient was referred to pediatric cardiology, where clinical assessment and echocardiographic evaluation confirmed high‐output cardiac failure, with preserved biventricular systolic function, mild left ventricular and left atrial dilation, and increased pulmonary valve flow velocity. Imaging, again, showed multiple hypoechoic liver lesions (Figure 1) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated multifocal enhancing lesions (Figure 2), consistent with multifocal hepatic hemangioma.

Abdominal ultrasound before (A) and after (B) propranolol therapy, showing marked regression of multifocal hepatic hemangiomas.

Abdominal MRI at baseline showing numerous enhancing lesions involving most of the liver parenchyma. MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Propranolol therapy was initiated shortly afterwards at 2 mg/kg/day in three divided doses. Within 4 weeks, her abdominal distension improved, weight gain resumed, heart failure symptoms resolved, and follow‐up ultrasound showed marked reduction in liver lesions with near‐normal liver appearance. Diuretics therapy was tapered off, and she was maintained on propranolol with weight adjustment.

Serial echocardiography demonstrated hemodynamic improvement. At baseline, Doppler assessment of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) showed elevated velocity–time integral with a peak systolic gradient of 18 mmHg, consistent with a high‐output state secondary to hepatic arteriovenous shunting. After propranolol therapy and regression of the liver hemangiomas, repeat imaging revealed a reduction in the RVOT gradient to 9 mmHg, accompanied by normalization of flow velocities (Figure 3). This paralleled the patient's clinical recovery, highlighting the utility of RVOT hemodynamics as a noninvasive marker of resolution of high‐output heart failure.

Pulmonary trans vascular gradient before and after propranolol initiation. The gradient peaked at 18 mmHg before treatment (red dashed line) and declined rapidly with sustained improvement through 8 months of therapy.

After 8 months of follow‐up, she remained asymptomatic, continued to gain weight (weight close to 50th percentile for age), and had no propranolol‐related side effects. Repeat ultrasound showed almost full resolution of the lesions. The family relocated abroad before a repeat MRI could be performed.

DISCUSSION

We present an infant with multifocal IHH complicated by high‐output cardiac failure and growth faltering, who responded rapidly and durably to propranolol. Within 4 weeks, the patient showed regression of liver lesions, stabilization of cardiac function, and catch‐up growth.

Our case parallels several prior reports in which propranolol led to rapid regression of hepatic hemangiomas. Sciveres et al. 3 described a similar patient with high‐output cardiac who responded promptly to propranolol monotherapy. Al Tasseh et al. 4 (reported marked improvement within 1 month in an infant with abdominal distension, cutaneous lesions, and hypothyroidism. Other studies have documented regression as early as 10 days after initiation 5 and continued improvement over months. 6 Together, these findings support propranolol as an effective first‐line therapy for multifocal IHH.

In more complex cases, additional interventions have been required. Wang et al. 7 reported successful resolution using propranolol with trans arterial embolization in an infant with pulmonary hypertension and hypothyroidism, while Velayos et al. 8 described cutaneous ulceration of a lesion requiring surgical management despite hepatic response.

Some reports highlight the limits of propranolol. Zhu et al. 9 described a giant hepatic hemangioma unresponsive to high‐dose therapy, requiring surgical excision. Zhang et al. 10 reported fatal outcomes in infants with severe cardiac compromise or disseminated disease, despite adjunctive therapies. These cases emphasize the need for careful cardiac assessment and close monitoring, especially in fragile neonates or those with diffuse involvement.

Across the literature, propranolol has been well tolerated within the therapeutic range of 1–3.5 mg/kg/day, with few reported adverse effects. Our case adds to this evidence, demonstrating rapid recovery and sustained remission without complications, even in the setting of heart failure.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the value of early recognition, timely propranolol therapy, and multidisciplinary collaboration (pediatric gastroenterology, cardiology, and radiology) in symptomatic multifocal IHH. Although limited by a single‐patient design and a lack of long‐term follow‐up, this report contributes to the relatively small body of literature on IHH and underscores the importance of early intervention to improve outcomes.

Propranolol therapy can achieve rapid clinical stabilization and sustained regression, even in infants with cardiac compromise. Incorporating a multidisciplinary approach ensures timely recognition of complications and optimizes outcomes.
