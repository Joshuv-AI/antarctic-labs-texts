Physical activity and sitting time as correlates of cardiometabolic multimorbidity risk in U.S. adults: an explainable machine learning classification model using SHAP and LIME
Authors: Yi Yang, Zhenxiang Guo, Dong Li, Bin Wu, Changnan Xu, Mengbiao Cai, Zhiming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is an increasing public health concern. Physical activity (PA) and prolonged sitting time are recognized as key modifiable lifestyle factors. This study aimed to develop an explainable machine learning (ML) model incorporating PA, sitting time, and other sociodemographic and clinical predictors to classify CMM status among U.S. adults. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) were applied to improve model interpretability.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 23,635 U.S. adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018. CMM was defined as the coexistence of at least two of the following conditions: hypertension, diabetes, stroke, or myocardial infarction. Self-reported total physical activity (MET-hours/week) and daily sitting time were considered the primary exposures. Associations were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines (RCS), and subgroup analyses. After feature selection using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm, twelve machine learning models were trained on 70% of the dataset. Models were evaluated using stratified tenfold cross-validation and assessed by AUC, sensitivity, and calibration. Survey sampling weights were applied to descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses but were not used during machine learning training or evaluation.ResultsHigher physical activity was associated with lower odds of CMM (adjusted OR for highest vs. lowest quartile = 0.59, 95% CI 0.51–0.68), whereas longer sitting time was associated with higher odds of CMM (adjusted OR = 1.27, 95% CI 1.07–1.49). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship, with a stronger inverse association at lower physical activity levels (inflection point ≈36 MET-hours/week). The gradient boosting classifier showed the best performance (AUC = 0.84, 95% CI 0.83–0.86) and good calibration (Brier score = 0.116; calibration slope = 0.961). SHAP analysis identified age as the most influential predictor, followed by body mass index, hyperlipidemia, income-to-poverty ratio, and physical activity. LIME provided local explanations illustrating the contribution of individual features.ConclusionThis study supports associations between PA, sitting time, and CMM among U.S. adults. SHAP and LIME were used to explain model predictions and identify key contributing features. The explainable ML framework provides a transparent approach for cross-sectional classification and hypothesis-generating, individual-level interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27177-8.Keywords: Physical activity, Sitting time, Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, Machine learning, SHAP, LIME, NHANES

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the co-occurrence of at least two cardiometabolic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, stroke, or myocardial infarction), represents a growing global health burden [1, 2]. Individuals with CMM have poorer clinical outcomes and higher mortality than those with a single cardiometabolic condition, thereby imposing substantial pressure on healthcare systems and society [2, 3]. Its increasing prevalence is driven by genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and modifiable lifestyle behaviors, particularly physical inactivity and prolonged sitting time [3–7].

Physical activity (PA) and sitting time are key modifiable factors associated with cardiometabolic health. Higher PA levels are linked to a lower risk of obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular events [8, 9]. In contrast, prolonged sitting is related to adverse lipid profiles, impaired glucose metabolism, and elevated blood pressure [10, 11]. However, these associations may be attenuated after adjustment for moderate-to-vigorous PA [10]. Most previous studies have examined these behaviours separately in relation to individual cardiometabolic outcomes, rather than their combined association with CMM. Furthermore, traditional regression models may not adequately capture nonlinear and high-dimensional relationships compared with flexible machine learning (ML) methods.

ML algorithms are well suited for modelling complex patterns in large observational datasets and have shown strong predictive performance in medical classification tasks [12, 13]. Incorporating explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), improves model transparency by quantifying each predictor’s contribution to the predictions [14, 15].

Accordingly, this cross-sectional study had two complementary objectives: (1) to examine associations between self-reported PA, sitting time, and CMM using multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and subgroup analyses; and (2) to develop and internally validate an interpretable ML model for classifying CMM status that incorporates PA, sitting time, and relevant sociodemographic and clinical factors, with SHAP and LIME used to enhance interpretability. Data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycles 2007–2018. NHANES is a nationally representative, multistage probability survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to assess the health and nutritional status of the noninstitutionalized U.S. civilian population. The survey protocols were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

A total of 59,842 participants aged ≥ 20 years were eligible for inclusion. Participants were excluded if they had missing data on CMM status (n = 25,726), physical activity or sitting time (n = 169), or covariates (n = 10,312). The final analytic sample included 23,635 participants with complete data on the primary outcome and exposures (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of participant selection from NHANES 2007–2018. CMM = Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity; NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey

Missing covariate data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations with the jomo package in R, generating 10 imputed datasets among participants with complete CMM and primary exposure data. CMM status, physical activity, and sitting time were not imputed; participants with missing values for these variables were excluded from the analysis to preserve the integrity of the primary classification variables.

Assessment of CMM

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) was defined as the presence of at least two of the following conditions identified during the survey examination: hypertension, diabetes, stroke, or myocardial infarction (used as a proxy for coronary heart disease). Hypertension was defined as a mean systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg (based on three measurements), use of antihypertensive medication, or a self-reported physician diagnosis. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol), use of antidiabetic medication or insulin, or a self-reported physician diagnosis [16]. Stroke and myocardial infarction were determined based on a self-reported physician diagnosis. When discrepancies occurred among self-report, medication use, and measured values, objective measurements and medication data were prioritized.

PA and sitting time

Physical activity (PA) and sitting time were self-reported using a modified Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) administered during the household interview. Total PA (TMET) was calculated as metabolic equivalent of task (MET)-hours per week by summing activity across five domains (vigorous occupational, moderate occupational, transportation, vigorous recreational, and moderate recreational) using standard MET values (8.0 for vigorous and 4.0 for moderate activities) [17]. For regression and machine learning (ML) analyses, TMET was rescaled by a factor of 10 to improve numerical stability. Sitting time was assessed using the questionnaire item: “On an average day, how much time do you spend sitting or reclining?” Because these measures were self-reported, recall bias cannot be excluded.

Assessment of covariates

Based on previous epidemiological studies [18], the following variables were considered potential confounders and effect modifiers: sex (male or female), age group, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and history of cancer and hyperlipidemia. PIR is an indicator of socioeconomic status calculated as the ratio of family income to the federal poverty threshold, which is updated annually according to the Consumer Price Index. PIR were categorized as ≤ 1.0 or > 1.0 relative to the poverty threshold. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Smoking status was defined as having smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in one’s lifetime, and alcohol consumption was defined as self-reported regular alcohol intake.

Statistical analyses

Baseline analysis and logistic regression

All descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses accounted for the complex NHANES survey design by incorporating sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units via the R survey package. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means ± standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as weighted percentages. Group differences were assessed using design-based t-tests and χ2 tests.. Total PA and sitting time were categorized into quartiles derived from the full analytic sample before the training–test split. Sensitivity analyses based on quartiles derived solely from the training set yielded nearly identical cut-points and model estimates (see Supplementary Materials). Multivariable logistic regression models estimated associations with CMM (Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for demographic variables; Model 3: fully adjusted). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with three knots were applied to examine potential nonlinear associations.

Model development

Variables selection and ML model construction

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%, n = 16,544) and an independent test set (30%, n = 7,091). Feature selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with repeated tenfold cross-validation and the Boruta algorithm. Twelve supervised machine-learning algorithms were implemented: Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), LightGBM, CatBoost, AdaBoost, Logistic Regression, XGBoost, Random Forest, Extra Trees, SVM, KNN, and Decision Tree. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded, and continuous variables were standardized as required. Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search with fivefold cross-validation within the training set.

Model performance was evaluated in the independent test set primarily using discrimination metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Survey weights were applied in descriptive and regression analyses but not in machine-learning model development or evaluation, as the objective was predictive performance on the observed sample distribution (consistent with TRIPOD-ML guidance) [19]. Model calibration was also assessed. Calibration in the independent test set was evaluated using the Brier score and calibration slope.

Model interpretability

SHAP and LIME were applied to the best-performing model (gradient boosting classifier) to provide global and local interpretability. SHAP quantifies each feature’s contribution to model predictions using game-theoretic Shapley values. LIME constructs a local surrogate model around individual predictions. Conceptual descriptions and implementation details (e.g., number of explained instances) are provided here, whereas full mathematical formulations are presented in Supplementary File S1. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.0 using the survey, rms, glmnet, Boruta, caret, shap, and lime packages.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

The final analytic sample included 23,635 U.S. adults. Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) was present in 4,278 participants (18.1%). Participants with CMM differed significantly from those without CMM with respect to sex, age, race/ethnicity, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity levels (Table 1). Mean total physical activity (TMET) was 34.4 MET-hours/week among those with CMM versus 63.0 MET-hours/week among those without CMM.

Subgroup analysis

The inverse association between physical activity and odds of CMM, and the positive association between sitting time and odds of CMM, were consistent across subgroups stratified by age (< 40, 40–60, > 60 years), sex, BMI category (< 25, 25–30, > 30 kg/m2), race/ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, income-to-poverty ratio, and hyperlipidemia (all P for interaction > 0.05; Fig. 3). For example, adjusted ORs comparing the highest versus lowest physical activity quartile ranged from 0.52 to 0.68 across age groups.

Machine learning model performance

The Gradient Boosting Classifier showed the best discriminative performance on the independent test set (AUC = 0.84, 95% CI 0.83–0.86; accuracy 0.78, precision 0.75, recall 0.79, F1-score 0.77) among the 12 evaluated algorithms (Fig. 5). The GBC model also demonstrated good calibration on the test set (Brier score = 0.116, calibration slope = 0.961).

Discussion

The present analysis examined the relationship between PA, sitting time, and CMM using an interpretable machine-learning framework. The findings support that low PA and prolonged sitting time are associated with CMM. Machine-learning models, particularly the gradient boosting classifier (GBC) combined with SHAP and LIME, were used to characterize complex interactions among clinical, behavioral, and demographic variables.

Individuals with lower PA and longer sitting time had higher odds of CMM. Participants with CMM were more likely to be older, male, non-Hispanic White, and to present adverse socioeconomic and behavioral risk factors, including higher BMI, smoking, and heavy alcohol consumption. These findings are consistent with epidemiological evidence linking adverse socioeconomic factors and unhealthy lifestyles to increased cardiometabolic odds [2, 20, 21]. Regression analyses showed a significant inverse association between PA levels and CMM. When TMET levels were categorized into quartiles, individuals in the highest quartile had lower odds of CMM compared to those in the lowest quartile. This finding is consistent with previous large cohort studies reporting that higher leisure-time PA is associated with increased life expectancy and lower odds of cardiometabolic diseases [8, 22]. Moreover, both the RCS and threshold effect models revealed a nonlinear association between TMET and CMM odds. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent associations between PA, sitting time, and CMM across most subgroups, with no significant interaction effects observed (P for interaction > 0.05; Fig. 3). However, the absence of statistically significant effect modification should be interpreted cautiously, as subgroup analyses may have been underpowered to detect modest interactions.

The superior performance of the GBC model indicates good discriminative performance and highlights the potential of ML for classifying CMM status as a complement to traditional epidemiological methods. Well-specified regression models with splines and interaction terms may achieve comparable performance. SHAP analysis consistently identified age, BMI, hyperlipidemia, PIR, and TMET as major variables contributing to the model’s output. Similarly, LIME helped deconstruct individual predictions, bridging the gap between black-box model and clinical interpretability. Individual LIME explanations should not be interpreted independently of global model behavior.

These findings have important implications for public health and clinical practice. CMM, which is associated with reduced quality of life, higher healthcare costs, and increased mortality [1, 23], underscores the need for targeted interventions that promote PA while reducing sitting time. Public health strategies that encourage gradual, achievable increases in PA, especially among individuals with low weekly MET levels, may help reduce the prevalence of CMM [24, 25]. Additionally, integrating interpretable ML models into risk assessment could help healthcare providers identify high-risk patients and tailor interventions, thereby improving clinical outcomes [26, 27].

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causality, as all data were collected at a single time point without longitudinal follow-up. The results, including those from logistic regression and ML models, reflect associative patterns rather than causal predictions. Residual confounding cannot be ruled out [2]. Second, PA and sitting time were assessed via self-report, which may introduce recall bias. Self-reported measures often underestimate actual sitting time and overestimate physical activity, potentially biasing associations toward the null. Future research employing objective measures such as accelerometry could provide more precise quantification of movement behaviors [28, 29]. Third, although the ML model demonstrated strong predictive performance, validation in external cohorts is necessary to confirm its generalizability across diverse populations. Additionally, factors such as genetic predisposition and environmental exposures, particularly air pollution, were not included in our model. Both have been shown to influence cardiometabolic health via mechanisms like inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation [30]. Their omission may lead to residual confounding. Finally, another limitation of our study is that sedentary behavior was assessed in a generalized manner without distinguishing between types of sedentary activities. Emerging evidence suggests that mentally active sedentary behaviors and mentally passive ones may have differential impacts on cardiometabolic health. The lack of such granularity in our data limits the ability to explore these nuanced associations.

Conclusion

This study applied explainable machine-learning models incorporating both physical activity and sitting time to classify cardiometabolic multimorbidity status. The findings indicate that both PA and sitting time are associated with CMM among U.S. adults and should be interpreted as associative rather than causal relationships. Integration of traditional regression and explainable machine-learning models, exemplified by the GBC model with SHAP and LIME, provides a framework for cross-sectional classification and hypothesis-generating individual-level interpretation. The results suggest that modifiable lifestyle behaviors may be relevant targets for prevention and that interpretable ML approaches may inform the development of clinical decision-support tools, pending external and longitudinal validation.
