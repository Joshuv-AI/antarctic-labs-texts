Study on the mechanism of ROS-induced oxidative stress injury and the broad-spectrum antimicrobial performance of nickel ion-doped V6O13 powder
Authors: Li Liu, Siyu Li, Wei Zhu, Qifu Bao, Yan Liang, Tiangui Zhao, Xiaolong Li, Jianer Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, pure V6O13 and nickel ion-doped V6O13 powders were synthesized by a simple hydrothermal-calcination method, and their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties and mechanisms were investigated. The crystal structure, morphology, and chemical state of the powders were thoroughly analyzed by XRD, SEM, TEM, XPS, and UV–Vis. Their antimicrobial properties and mechanisms were evaluated by the ring of inhibition, bio-SEM, live-dead cell staining, ROS detection, and protein leakage experiments. The results showed that nickel ion doping modulated the oxygen defects of V6O13, generating more reactive oxygen species and leading to more severe oxidative stress, resulting in a broad-spectrum and highly efficient antimicrobial effect. This study also revealed the antimicrobial mechanism based on oxygen defect -induced ROS production, which caused cellular oxidative stress damage, leading to leakage of intracellular substances and cell death. This study not only demonstrates the potential of V6O13 as an efficient antimicrobial agent but also provides a strong experimental basis and theoretical support for the engineering design and optimization of novel antimicrobial materials by modulating material defects through ion doping.Keywords: V6O13, Ni-doped, Oxygen vacancy, Broad-spectrum antimicrobialSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Diseases, Materials science

Introduction

In recent years, inorganic antimicrobial materials, by virtue of their unique antimicrobial mechanisms and excellent physicochemical properties, have become an important direction in antimicrobial technology research. They have shown a wide range of application prospects in many fields, such as medical devices, food packaging, and public facilities1–6. Inorganic antimicrobial materials under specific conditions (such as light or heat) generate highly reactive oxygen radicals (such as hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions). These reactive oxygen radicals can damage the membrane structure of microbial cells, inducing oxidative stress, leading to intracellular damage to proteins and DNA, and ultimately causing the death of microorganisms, thus realizing the antimicrobial effect7–10. Inorganic materials offer significant advantages in terms of antimicrobial durability and reliability, broad spectrum, and harsh environments. They are considered a very promising technology to alleviate the current problems of antibiotic resistance and the spread of pathogenic bacteria11–14.

Studies on inorganic antimicrobial materials such as titanium dioxide, zinc oxide, and silver nanoparticles have shown that these materials can further improve their antimicrobial effect and application range through appropriate modification and functionalization15–17. For example, composites of materials have attracted much attention due to their unique physicochemical properties and wide range of application prospects. By combining different nano-components, these materials exhibit excellent properties that cannot be realized by a single material, especially in terms of antimicrobial activity, magnetic properties, and biocompatibility18–22. In recent years, metal-ion doped metal oxide materials have rapidly become the focus of research in materials science due to their excellent physicochemical properties and wide application prospects. By introducing different metal ions, the structural, optical, thermal, and antimicrobial properties of these materials can be significantly enhanced, showing great potential for applications in biomedicine, environmental protection, and electronic devices23–26.

In particular, nickel ion, as an inorganic antimicrobial material, exhibits significant antimicrobial advantages by virtue of its broad-spectrum antimicrobial resistance, long-lasting stability, environmental adaptability, multiple antimicrobial mechanisms, application flexibility, and relatively low toxicity. This makes it a desirable doping element27–29. On one hand, doping with nickel ions can change the crystal structure, modulate electronic properties, and enhance antimicrobial stability30–32. On the other hand, the release and uptake of nickel ions lead to cell membrane disruption, protein inactivation, DNA damage, and interference with the intracellular homeostasis of other metal ions, leading to microbial mortality33–37.

V6O13, as a transition metal oxide with remarkable features, has shown potential applications in several fields, especially in electrochemistry and as antimicrobial agents38–41. Previously, our group prepared oxygen-deficient V6O13 enriched by hydrothermal method and deeply analyzed it against C. albicans42. However, the study was conducted only on C. albicans, and there is still room for improvement in terms of broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficiency.

Therefore, V6O13, when doped with nickel ions, will exhibit broader-spectrum and efficient antimicrobial properties while maintaining its original advantages. Herein, in this study, pure V6O13 and nickel ion-doped V6O13 powders were prepared by hydrothermal-calcination method using ammonium metavanadate, anhydrous oxalic acid, and nickel chloride hexahydrate as raw materials. The material characterization and antimicrobial effect were analyzed to explore their possible antimicrobial mechanisms. It is hoped that this study can bring new perspectives and strategies in the field of antimicrobials and provide valuable reference information for researchers in related fields.

Experiment

Materials and synthesis

Ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3), anhydrous oxalic acid (C2H2O4), and nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2‧6H2O) were purchased from Aladdin Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and all the chemicals were analytically pure without further treatment. Firstly, 0.01 mol of NH4VO3 was added to 80 mL of deionized water and stirred well, which was recorded as Solution A. Then 0.015 mol of C2H2O4 was added to Solution A and stirred well, which was recorded as Solution B. The solution was then transferred to a 100 mL reaction kettle and kept in an oven at 180 °C for a reaction time of 6 h. After cooling to room temperature, it was washed several times with deionized water and ethanol. After vacuum drying at 65 °C for 12 h, the final powder was calcined in high purity argon gas at 650 °C for 1.5 h to obtain the final powder, labeled as S1. Then, keeping NH4VO3 as 0.01 mol, NH4VO3, NiCl2‧6H2O, and C2H2O4 were added into 80 mL of deionized water at the molar ratio of 1:0.04:1.5, and stirred well by magnetic stirring at room temperature. The subsequent steps were the same as in the previous process, and the final sample was labeled as S2. In the above reaction, ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3) is used as a vanadium source, nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2‧6H2O) is used as a dopant, and anhydrous oxalic acid (C2H2O4) is used as a reducing agent in the reaction to lower the valence state of vanadium. The synthesis process is shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic flow of hydrothermal preparation of S1 and S2 samples.

Materials characterization

The crystal structures of the synthesized samples were analyzed by a Bruker D8 Advance powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker AXS D8-Advance, Germany) and a Cu Kα source. Surface morphology was observed by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, HITACHI SU8010, Japan) and (FE-SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300, Germany). The structure and valence states of the samples were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo-Fischer, ESCALAB Xi+, USA).

Strains, media and conditions

Escherichia coli ATCC25922, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC29213, and Candida albicans ATCC10231 were utilized to evaluate the antimicrobial effect of the samples. The S1 and S2 sample powders, LB (Luria–Bertani) medium, Sartre's medium, and deionized water were autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min.

Antimicrobial circle test

The inhibitory activity of the samples was evaluated by measuring the diameter of the inhibition circle. A mixture of 100 mg/mL of powder and deionized water was first prepared. Then small pieces of bacteriostatic paper were dipped in the mixture. Inhibitory paper sheets coated with antimicrobial material were placed on LB medium plates coated with E. coli, S. aureus, and Sartre's medium plates coated with C. albicans. The plates were placed in a biochemical incubator and incubated for 24 h for E. coli and S. aureus, and 48 h for C. albicans. The size of the inhibition circle was then observed. Relevant papers inform the methodology used for the antimicrobial ring test in this paper43,44.

Microbial morphology scan

Changes in morphology and internal structure of the organisms were observed by biological scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI SU8100, Japan). S1 and S2 powders at a final concentration of 2 mg/mL were co-cultured with 1 OD E. coli, S. aureus, for 24 h and with 1 OD C. albicans, for 48 h. No powder was added to the control group. Cultured samples were fixed in a cold solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 h and then rinsed three times with deionized water. Next, the samples were dehydrated, dried, sprayed with gold, and examined by scanning electron microscopy according to the instructions for SEM use.

Live/dead fluorescent staining

Live microbial cells were stained with green fluorescent dye SYTOTM 9 (λex = 485 nm, λem = 498 nm). Dead microbial cells were labeled with the red fluorescent dye propidium iodide (PI) (λex = 535 nm, λem = 617 nm). After S1 and S2 antimicrobial treatments, the microbial cells were collected by centrifugation (3,500 rpm, 5 min), and stained with SYTOTM 9 and PI for 15 min at room temperature and in the dark. Then, the microbial suspension (0.1 mL) was dropped on a slide for microscopic observation.

Reactive oxygen detection

S2 powder at 2 mg/mL was co-cultured with Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida albicans, and no powder was added to the control. 1 mL of fermentation broth was taken at 0.5 h, 2 h, and 12 h and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded. Microorganisms were collected, washed twice with PBS, and then diluted to the appropriate concentration. Pipette 200 μL of cell resuspension liquid in a clear 96-well plate, place the 96-well plate in a multifunctional enzyme labeller (Männedorf, Switzerland) and read the OD value. After collecting the co-culture organisms in PBS, DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (S0060, Beyotime, China) at a concentration of 10 μM was added, and a metal bath was set at 37 °C for 30 min. To remove the DCFH-DA that had not entered the cells, the cells were washed three times with sterile PBS. 200 μL of the treated cell suspension was aspirated in a black 96-well plate, and the 96-well plate was placed into a multifunctional enzyme labeller (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) to read the fluorescence values. The emission spectra were set to 495 nm and 529 nm, respectively. ROS value = fluorescence intensity (Au)/OD600. Three replicates were performed for each set of experiments.

Protein leakage detection

S2 powder at 2 mg/mL was co-cultured with E. coli, S. aureus, and Candida albicans, and no powder was added in the control group. At 0.5 h, 2 h, and 12 h, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and 30 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 150 μL of Kaomas Brilliant Blue G250 in a 96-well plate. The 96-well plate was put into a multifunctional enzyme labeller and detected by using a TECAN-SparK plate reader (Männedorf, Switzerland). The absorption spectrum was set to 595 nm. Detection values were converted to protein concentration according to the standard curve Y = 0.3138*X − 0.0029. Three replicates were performed for each set of experiments.

Results and discussion

Characterization of materials

Figure 2 shows the XRD maps of S1 and S2. By analysis, both S1 and S2 contain peaks that can be found in V6O13 (JCPDS card number 43-1050, space group: C2/m) phase and VO2 (JCPDS card number 43-1051, space group: P21/c) phase.

XRD patterns of S1 and S2 samples.

From the crystal structure, thermodynamics, and nickel ion doping, this paper provides an in-depth analysis of the V6O13 to VO2 transition. Firstly, analyzed from the crystal structure point of view, V6O13 has a complex layered structure, which contains vanadium ions with different valence states (V4+ and V5+), as well as oxygen atoms with different coordination environments. This mixed valence and complex coordination structure leads to the asymmetry of the material in terms of electronic and crystal structure. Compared to V6O13, VO2 has a higher symmetry and simpler crystal structure. Therefore, from the crystal structure point of view, the transition from V6O13 to VO2 mainly involves structural simplification and increased symmetry. Secondly, from the thermodynamic point of view, the transformation of V6O13 to VO2 is mainly attributed to the decrease of Gibbs free energy. This is demonstrated by the fact that at high temperatures, although the entropy of VO2 is lower, the lower enthalpy and the compensating effect of temperature on the entropy term result in a lower total free energy, which drives the phase transition. This transition reflects the tendency of the system towards the state with the lowest free energy, i.e., the thermodynamically stable state. Again, the lattice structure, valence distribution, and electronic structure of V6O13 are modulated through nickel ion doping, which drives the transition to VO2. These factors work together to make V6O13 tend to form a more stable VO2 phase under specific conditions.

Figure 3a,b show the SEM images of S1 and S2, respectively. It can be seen that S1 has a rod-like morphology. It is about 5 µm long and 1 µm wide. Compared to S1, the morphology of S2 changes and appears smaller and granular. The diameter is about 1 µm. The crystal growth is usually controlled by the diffusion rate and surface energy of ions in solution. In the absence of nickel ions, since the crystal structure of V6O13 has certain specific orientations, these orientations may have lower surface energies, making it easier for atoms or molecules to align and grow in these directions. The inherent properties of the crystal structure dictate faster growth rates in certain orientations, resulting in a rod-like structure. Doping of nickel ions alters the growth kinetics of vanadium oxides in solution by decreasing their surface energy, which inhibits the rapid growth of crystals in a specific crystallographic direction. When nickel ions are doped into the crystal structure, they will occupy some of the lattice positions or introduce lattice defects, changing the original crystal growth path. This doping effect affects the overall morphology of the crystals, preventing them from continuing to grow in the original direction, resulting in the formation of smaller particles.

SEM and TEM images of the synthesized samples: (a) SEM of S1, (b) SEM of S2, (c) TEM of S1, (d) TEM of S2.

In the SEM image, the S1 sample (Fig. 3a) demonstrates a clear rod-like morphology, which usually has a high aspect ratio, indicating that the crystals grow along a particular direction. In the S2 sample (Fig. 3b), on the other hand, the doping of nickel ions resulted in the transformation of the crystal morphology into a granular shape, with a significant decrease in the size of the particles and a more uniform distribution of the particles, indicating that the nickel ions effectively suppressed the anisotropic growth of the crystals.

Figures 3c,d show the TEM images of S1 and S2, respectively. The TEM images show that micrometer rods with high aspect ratio are observed in S1, and S2 presents micrometer irregular spherical shapes. The particle size is consistent with the SEM observation. In addition, the change in morphology may be due to the introduction of nickel ions (Ni2+), which altered the surface chemical properties of the V6O13 crystals. Nickel ions may selectively adsorb on some specific crystal faces, and this adsorption changes the surface energy and growth rate of these faces. Some crystal faces may be inhibited, while others may be promoted, leading to the transformation of the crystal from a one-dimensional rod-like morphology to a multidimensional spherical morphology.

Figure 4a–d show the XPS spectra of the two samples with results that help to characterize the type of elements and their state on the surface of their materials. Figure 4a shows the full spectra of S1 and S2.S1 consists of two elements, vanadium and oxygen, whereas S2 contains not only vanadium and oxygen but also nickel.

XPS spectra of S1 and S2 samples: (a) Full spectra of S1 and S2, (b) high-resolution V2p spectra of S1 and S2, (c) high-resolution O1s spectra of S1 and S2 (d) high-resolution Ni2p spectra of S2.

The high-resolution V2p spectra of the samples in Fig. 4b indicate the presence of two different oxidation states of V ions. The results showed that two different oxidation states of V ions were present in S1; the peaks with binding energies of 516.17 eV and 523.49 eV corresponded to V4+, and the peaks with binding energies of 517.49 eV and 524.71 eV corresponded to V5+. Two different oxidation states of V ions were also present in S2; the peaks with binding energies of 516.58 eV and 523.16 eV corresponded to V4+, and the peaks with binding energies of 517.57 eV and 524.88 eV corresponded to V5+. These binding energy data indicate that vanadium ions exist in different oxidation states in the S1 and S2 samples, and the different binding energy peaks reflect the state of vanadium ions in their chemical environment.

Figure 4c shows the high-resolution spectra of O1s for S1 and S2. The fitting shows that lattice oxygen (Olatt) and vacancy oxygen (Od) are present in the S1 sample with binding energies of 529.77 eV and 530.63 eV, respectively, and the proportion of vacancy oxygen reaches 27.31%. Figure 4b shows the high-resolution spectrum of O1s for S1 and S2 samples, and the fitting shows that lattice oxygen (Olatt) and vacancy oxygen (Od) are present in the sample, with binding energies of 530.23 eV and 531.24 eV, respectively, and the percentage of vacancy oxygen reaches 32.56%. These data indicate that lattice and vacancy oxygen are present in both samples, but the S2 sample has a higher percentage of vacancy oxygen. The presence of vacancy oxygen is usually associated with the antimicrobial activity of the material.

Figure 4d shows the high-resolution spectrum of Ni2p for S2. The two main peaks centered at 856.15 eV and 874.2 eV are characteristic of the Ni 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 levels. These two peaks are accompanied by two satellite peaks at 862.57 eV and 879.71 eV, which are due to the difference in electron configuration between the ground and excited states during photoelectron emission45,46. The presence of main and satellite peaks in Ni2p spectra indicates the characteristic electronic structure of nickel. The positions and shapes of these peaks provide us with information about the chemical state of nickel in the sample, and usually the presence of Ni 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks corresponds to the characteristic Ni2+, which is further supported by the satellite peaks.

Antimicrobial properties

In order to test the broad-spectrum antimicrobial performance of the S1 and S2 samples, we chose Escherichia coli (Fig. 6a), Staphylococcus aureus (Fig. 6b), and Candida albicans (Fig. 6c) as the study objects. In the figure 'Control' represents the blank control group without the addition of antimicrobial powder. These strains were chosen to be representative of the three major groups of common pathogenic microorganisms, namely Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, and fungi, and were able to assess the antimicrobial effects of the samples in a more comprehensive manner.

Circle of inhibition plots of S1 and S2 samples; Control represents the blank control group without addition of antimicrobial powder: (a) 24 h, E. coli; (b) 24 h, S. aureus; (c) 48 h, C. albicans.

From the data in Fig. 6a–c, it can be seen that both S1 and S2 samples showed significant antimicrobial effect against E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans. The diameter of the ring of inhibition is an important indicator for assessing antimicrobial activity; the larger the ring of inhibition, the stronger the antimicrobial activity of the sample. The experimental results showed that the circles of inhibition diameters of S2 samples were larger than those of S1 samples in all three microorganisms, indicating that the antimicrobial performance of S2 samples was better than that of S1 samples. Specifically, in the inhibition test for the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli (Fig. 6a), the diameter of the circle of inhibition of the S2 sample, 1.34 ± 0.02 cm, was significantly larger than that of the S1 sample, 0.59 ± 0.01 cm, which indicated that inhibition of Escherichia coli was more significant with S2. For the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus (Fig. 6b), the diameter of the circle of inhibition of the S2 sample was 1.67 ± 0.01 cm greater than that of the S1 sample, which was 0.63 ± 0.01 cm, suggesting that S2 also had stronger inhibitory activity against Gram-positive bacteria. In the test against C. albicans (Fig. 6c), the diameter of the circle of inhibition of the S2 sample was 1.22 ± 0.02 cm greater than that of the S1 sample (0.68 ± 0.02 cm), which proved the obvious advantage of S2 in inhibiting fungi.

In summary, the S2 sample was superior to the S1 sample in terms of broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, with stronger inhibitory activity against Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, and fungi. This result suggests that the S2 sample has greater potential and advantages for future antimicrobial applications.

The concentrations of S2 samples were set to be 1 mg/mL, 2 mg/mL, and 3 mg/mL with E. coli and S. aureus co-cultured for 24 h, and C. albicans co-cultured for 48 h. The fermentation broths were diluted a certain number of times and then plated on medium, and the inhibition rate was calculated by counting the number of colonies. For E. coli, the corresponding inhibition rates were 75.62 ± 0.01%, 99.22 ± 0.02%, and 99.78 ± 0.03%, respectively. Against S. aureus, the corresponding inhibition rates were 83.26 ± 0.03%, 99.68 ± 0.01%, and 99.9 ± 0.02%, respectively. For C. albicans, the corresponding inhibition rates were 55.36 ± 0.02%, 98.12 ± 0.02%, and 99.38 ± 0.02%, respectively. Therefore, based on the values of bacterial inhibition rate, we finally selected a 2 mg/mL concentration of S2 powder for further experimental studies.

In order to observe whether the cell morphology changed, we performed bio-scanning electron microscopy analysis, as shown in Fig. 7. In this paper, S2 samples with a concentration of 2 mg/mL were chosen to be co-cultured with 1 OD of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans. The control group (Fig. 7(a, b, c)) without S2 sample powder was used as a comparison.

Bio-SEM: (a) E. coli; (b) S. aureus; (c) C. albicans; (d) E. coli co-cultured with S2; (e) S. aureus co-cultured with S2; (f) C. albicans co-cultured with S2.

The SEM results showed that the cell morphology in the control group was intact without significant damage. Specifically, the cell morphology of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans in the control group remained normal with intact cell walls and cell membranes. Figure 7d–f shows the SEM images of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans after co-culture with S2 samples, respectively. As can be seen from the figures, the S2 sample caused obvious damage to these cells. Specific observations were as follows: E. coli (Fig. 7d): the cell surface showed obvious depression and crumpling, indicating that the cell membrane was damaged. This morphological change implies a decrease in the mechanical properties of the cell membrane, leading to the leakage of intracellular substances to the outside. S. aureus (Fig. 7e): the overall reduction in cell volume suggests that the cell membrane has been attacked and the cell has lost its normal morphology. This change in morphology leads to leakage of intracellular material to the outside and cell death. C. albicans (Fig. 7f): the cell membrane is sunken and crumpled, showing significant mechanical damage. Morphological changes in the cell indicate that the integrity of the cell membrane has been compromised, affecting the normal function of the cell.

In summary, the results of the bio-SEM analysis further confirmed the antimicrobial properties of the S2 sample, which led to cell death by disrupting the cell membrane structure and causing the leakage of intracellular substances. This result is consistent with the results of the circle of inhibition experiments, further indicating that the S2 sample has significant advantages in broad-spectrum antimicrobial applications.

The antimicrobial activity of S2 was further confirmed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), and the results are shown in Fig. 8. SYTO-9 is a fluorescent dye capable of penetrating cell membranes and staining both living and dead cells, and therefore emits fluorescence reflecting the state of existence of all cells. In contrast, propidium iodide (PI) is a selective dye that penetrates only dead cells with damaged cell membranes and emits an intense red fluorescence when it binds to intracellular DNA.

Imaging of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans dead and alive: live cells stained in green (SYTO-9), dead cells stained in red (PI) (scale bar = 50 µm).

In the experiment, the control bacterial cells were not treated with S2, and it was observed that these cells showed strong green fluorescence after staining, indicating that these cells were all living cells with intact membranes. On the contrary, the S2-treated bacterial cells showed predominantly red fluorescence after staining, indicating that the membranes of these cells had ruptured, allowing the PI dye to enter the cells and bind to the DNA, further confirming the death of these cells.

This result clearly demonstrates the antimicrobial activity of S2, which is visualized by CLSM images, providing direct evidence for the antimicrobial effect of S2. These findings have important scientific significance and application value for the development of novel antimicrobial materials.

The DCFH-DA fluorescence assay was used to verify that damage to cells by attack causes ROS.DCFH-DA is taken up by cells and deacetylated by esterase to a non-fluorescent compound, which is subsequently oxidized by ROS to 2'-7'dichlorofluorescein (DCF), a fluorescent compound that reflects the intensity of ROS by detecting fluorescence values of the cells.

The degree of ROS production was examined in the co-culture of unspiked and spiked S2 samples with the three microorganisms for 0.5 h, 2 h, and 12 h. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans was stronger than that of the control from 0.5 h of co-culture. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans in co-culture with S2 for 12 h was stronger than that of the control group starting from the co-culture contact at 0.5 h, and the fluorescence intensity at 12 h was about twice as much as that of the control group, indicating that the pathogenic microorganisms were attacked by the ROS caused by the S2 sample, and the body responded rapidly to the stress. A continuous and large amount of ROS was produced, which resulted in the optimization of the damage to the cells (Fig. 9a–c).

Reactive oxygen species assay (a–c) and protein leakage (d–f) for E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans. Control is the control without powdered sample.

Oxidative stress damage by ROS causes protein leakage, and to demonstrate the leakage of cell contents, leading to cell crumpling II death, we examined the protein content in the fermentation broth at 0.5 h, 2 h, and 12 h of incubation. The results showed that E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans co-cultured with S2 showed a rapid increase in protein leakage with time at 12 h. The results showed that the protein leakage in the fermentation broth increased rapidly with time. Combined with the bio-SEM plots of the co-cultures at 24 h and 48 h, the cells had crumpled or ruptured (Fig. 9d–f).

Taken together, these findings confirm that treatment with S2 powder resulted in a significant increase in the concentration of ROS in cells, which in turn induced severe cellular damage. This resulted in leakage of large amounts of cellular contents and subsequent cell death. At the same time, these findings establish a clear link between S2 treatment, elevated cellular ROS levels, extensive cellular damage, and subsequent leakage of cellular contents leading to cell death.

Taken together, these findings confirm that co-culture of S2 powder with pathogenic microorganisms significantly increased ROS concentrations, which in turn induced severe cellular damage, leading to extensive cell content leakage and subsequent cell death. These results clearly demonstrate a strong association between oxygen-deficient S2 powder, abnormally elevated ROS, extensive cellular damage, and subsequent leakage of cellular contents.

Probable antimicrobial mechanism

Combining the results of the above material analysis and antimicrobial activity evaluation experiments, it can be concluded that the nickel ion doped V6O13 powder has excellent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, and its antimicrobial mechanism diagram is shown in Fig. 10.

Antimicrobial mechanism of S2 powder.

Nickel ion doped V6O13 has abundant oxygen defects, which can have an important effect on the electronic structure of the material as well as properties such as surface adsorption. It has been found that ROS generated by oxygen defects lead to the destruction of the cell wall and cell membrane structure of microorganisms by inducing cellular oxidative stress damage. This damage triggers the rupture of the cell membrane, allowing the leakage of intracellular materials, including cytoplasm, nucleic acids, and other important biomolecules, to the extracellular environment, ultimately leading to the loss of physiological functions and cell death47–49. This process demonstrates the key role of reactive oxygen species in the antimicrobial mechanism. Single linear oxygen (1O2), superoxide anion radical (‧O2-), peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radical (‧OH) are considered common ROS. Conclusion

In summary, in this paper, pure V6O13 powders enriched with oxygen defects and nickel ion-doped V6O13 powders were prepared by a simple hydrothermal-calcination method using NH4VO3 as the vanadium source, C2H2O4 as the reducing agent, and NiCl2‧6H2O as the dopant. Their microstructures, antimicrobial properties, and mechanisms were thoroughly investigated. The results showed that nickel ion doping led to significant changes in the physical phase and morphology of V6O13, with the concentration of oxygen defects increasing from 27.31 to 32.56%, and the width of crystalline bands decreasing from 2.31 to 1.9 eV. Inhibition circle experiments, Bio-SEM, and CLSM showed that nickel ion doping enhanced the broad-spectrum antimicrobial property of V6O13. Based on ROS tests and protein leakage experiments, this paper proposes that oxygen-rich defective V6O13 promotes ROS generation, thereby triggering oxidative stress damage in microbial cells, leading to cell membrane rupture and intracellular material leakage. This defect engineering-based material design strategy can be used not only for the development of V6O13 antimicrobial materials but can also be extended to other areas where material properties need to be modulated.
