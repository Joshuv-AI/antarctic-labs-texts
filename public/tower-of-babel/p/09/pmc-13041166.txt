Application of PGCL/AP conductive composite scaffold combined with electrical stimulation in neural tissue engineering
Authors: Longchuan Zhu, Hongye Yan, Hongru Li, Su Pan, Hanwen Yang, Zhe Zhu, Zhiping Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNerve regeneration and axonal growth can be significantly enhanced by electrical stimulation (ES) using conductive polymers. But in addition to ES, nerve cell function and how they react to substrate conductivity are also essential factors in nerve repair. As a result, ES, in conjunction with appropriate materials (such as tissue scaffolds), has been used to treat nerve damage and has demonstrated significant promise for peripheral nerve regeneration. In this work, electrospinning was used to create conductive composite scaffolds made of polyglycolic acid@polycaprolactone/aniline pentamer (PGCL/AP). We later verified that PC12 cells cultured on these scaffolds exhibited improved migration, proliferation, and vitality upon electrical stimulation. Furthermore, In the established 10-mm peripheral nerve injury model, the PAP + ES group achieved outcomes comparable to the autograft group in the assessment of functional and morphological recovery, encompassing SFI, myelination rate, and gastrocnemius muscle evaluation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00620-5.Keywords: Nerve regeneration, Electrical stimulation, Conducting composite scaffold, Electrostatic spinning

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury resulting from trauma, tumors, and various diseases is emerging as a significant medical issue, profoundly affecting patients’ job efficiency and quality of life [1, 2]. Autologous transplantation technology is regarded as the “gold standard” for nerve repair; however, it requires additional surgery to procure the graft and is constrained by diminished distribution sensitivity, limited availability of autologous donor tissue, and the risk of neuroma formation [3, 4]. The swift advancement of regenerative medicine has demonstrated that nerve guide conduit (NGCs) can surmount these constraints and facilitate nerve recovery. Nonetheless, producing an optimal nerve transplant that demonstrates superior performance and fully meets clinical needs remains highly challenging [5].

Peripheral nerves consist of sensory neurons and motor neurons, and are among the tissues that respond to electrical signals. The conductivity varies from a few Siemens per meter (S/m) to several hundred Siemens per meter (S/m), contingent upon the various neural components [6]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that applied external electric fields can direct and enhance cellular migration, guiding cell movement toward either the anode or cathode [7]. This phenomenon has been effectively leveraged to promote the maturation of Schwann cells and the subsequent formation of myelin sheaths, which in turn facilitates axonal regeneration, elongation, and neuronal survival [8]. Regarding the underlying mechanisms, it has been proposed that electrical stimulation modulates voltage-gated calcium channels, primarily by regulating intracellular calcium levels [9]. This modulation is suggested to inhibit the CaSR and PLCβ signaling pathways, thereby contributing to the repair of injured nerves. Furthermore, research has confirmed that external electrical stimulation can activate the MAPK (MEK/ERK) signaling pathway and modulate the immune microenvironment, both of which represent additional mechanisms promoting axonal growth [10, 11]. Although the precise mechanistic details have not been fully elucidated, these collective findings strongly indicate that electrical stimulation (ES) enhances neuronal outgrowth and promotes nerve regeneration in vivo, establishing it as a valuable strategy in the field of neural engineering [12, 13].

Conductive polymers (CPs) serve as appealing scaffold materials for tissue engineering due to their biocompatibility, electroactivity, and ease of preparation and modification. Neurons reportedly attach readily to hydrophilically modified conductive polymer materials and enhance axonal development by electrical stimulation [14]. Moreover, electroactive polymers can improve cellular activity, adhesion, migration, and proliferation by influencing the cellular growth milieu [15]. Conductive polymers have attracted significant research interest owing to their superior electrical properties and their ability to modulate diverse cellular functions through electrical stimulation. Recent research indicates that several conductive polymers, including polypyrrole and polyaniline (PANI), can augment cellular activity by externally regulating the strength and duration of stimulation. The primary drawbacks of current systems are inadequate polymer-cell contact, insufficient cell-interaction sites, hydrophobicity, limited solubility and processability in aquatic environments, and unpredictable mechanical properties. Consequently, individuals started to focus on aniline oligomers, specifically aniline tetramer (AT) and aniline pentamer (AP) [16, 17]. These oligomers can be digested in vitro, circumventing the need for reoperation or long-term deleterious effects in vivo, and are thus anticipated to be utilized in tissue engineering. Various parameters, including the extent of polymer oxidation, the nature of the dopant, the shape of the polymer chain, and the chain length, can significantly influence PANI’s conductivity [11]. Guo et al. developed a hyperbranched copolymer composed of poly-ε-caprolactone (PCL) and carboxyl-terminated aniline pentamer (CCAP), exhibiting a conductivity of 0.2 × 10^-6 to 5 × 10^-6 S· cm^-1, adequate for the transmission of bioelectrical signals in vivo [18].

Electrospinning is a straightforward and widely used method that continuously produces nanofibers from viscoelastic fluids via electrostatic repulsion between surface charges, enabling the assembly of ordered or hierarchical structures through various arrangements, stacking, and folding [19]. In recent years, electrospun fibers have attracted increased interest as drug delivery systems owing to their distinctive characteristics, including a high specific surface area and elevated porosity. To enhance drug-polymer compatibility and improve medication efficacy, polymer-mixed drug delivery methods have been advocated [20, 21]. Despite the significant potential of electrospun nanofibers for biomedical research, the structure and composition of the polymer nanofibers used in tissue regeneration scaffolds require further optimization. Moreover, it has been documented that electrospun nanofiber scaffolds can provide physical stimuli to direct neurons to expand from dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and embryoid bodies (EBs), thereby enhancing neuronal length through functionalization [22, 23]. Both poly(glycolide-ε-caprolactone) (PGCL) and polycaprolactone (PCL) are viable electrospinning materials for nerve repair. However, PGCL, being a composite copolymer, offers distinct advantages over PCL, which suffers from a slow degradation rate and insufficient mechanical strength. The degradation profile of PGCL can be precisely tuned, and it integrates the strength of polyglycolide (PGA) with the flexibility of PCL, resulting in superior overall mechanical properties. Furthermore, PGCL has been shown to enhance cell-cell interactions [24, 25]. In summary, as an engineerable material, PGCL better addresses the complex requirements of neural regeneration by overcoming the limitations of PCL, such as its protracted degradation and mechanical weakness. Therefore, the combination of PGCL with aniline pentamer (AP) is a more promising strategy for developing conductive materials with excellent electroactivity.

Utilizing these benefits, we will develop a hybrid PGCL/AP (PAP) NGC with configurable architecture and conductive characteristics by electrospinning technology to facilitate peripheral nerve regeneration. We implemented electrical stimulation on a conductive scaffold with a structured topology to create an electrical milieu conducive to the restoration of injured nerves. The scaffolds’ efficacy was assessed in vitro using cellular assays and in vivo by assessing the healing of 10 mm nerve lesions. The impact of ES on nerve regeneration was examined using behavioral experiments and histological investigation. We anticipate that tissue-engineered guide conduits exhibiting electrical activity, suitable topographic guidance cues, and excellent biocompatibility will facilitate nerve regeneration.

Materials and methods

Materials

N-phenyl-1,4-phenylenediamine (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), Succinic anhydride (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), Dichloromethane (Shanghai Denuo Chemical Co., Ltd.), DMF (Shanghai Denuo Chemical Co., Ltd), ε-caprolactone (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), PGCL (Suzhou Qihang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), n-hexanol (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), Sn(Oct)₂ (Anhui Kemi Instrument Co., Ltd), N, N’-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (TargetMol), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (Beijing Xilin Buke Network Technology Co., Ltd.), poly-L-lysine (Sigma, USA), hexafluoroisopropanol (Aladdin, China), highly differentiated PC12 cells (Wuhan Shanen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Shanghai Aladdin), 488-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG and Cy3-labeled sheep anti-rabbit IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), rabbit anti-S100β monoclonal antibody (Abcam, UK), NF200 antibody (Abcam, UK), S100 antibody (Abcam, UK), LFB staining kit (Beijing Solebold Technology Co., Ltd.), toluidine blue staining solution (Beijing Solebold Technology Co., Ltd.), hematoxylin-eosin staining solution (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.), Masson staining kit (Beijing Solebold Technology Co., Ltd.).

Synthesis of PCL-AP

0.05 mol of N-phenyl-1,4-phenylenediamine, 0.05 mol of succinic anhydride, and 300 mL of dichloromethane were added to a three-necked flask respectively. The stirring reaction was carried out under nitrogen protection, and the reaction was terminated when a gray precipitate appeared in the reaction system. The reaction product was washed with diethyl ether and then dried in a vacuum drying oven for 12 h to obtain N-phenyl-1,4-phenylenediamine. The obtained N-phenyl-1,4-phenylenediamine was dissolved in 15 mL of DMF, followed by the addition of a pre-cooled mixed solution consisting of 75 mL of DMF, 15 mL of distilled water, and 15 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid. Subsequently, an aqueous hydrochloric acid solution of ammonium persulfate (4.56 g of ammonium persulfate dissolved in 50 mL of 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid) was added to the reaction system, and the stirring reaction was conducted for 2 h. The reaction product was washed with distilled water and then vacuum-dried to obtain carboxyl-terminated aniline pentamer (AP). 30 g (0.263 mol) of ε-caprolactone, 1.3mL of n-hexanol, and 43 µL of Sn(Oct)₂ were mixed. The mixture was heated to 120 °C under magnetic stirring and reacted for 24 h. After the reaction, the product was dissolved in chloroform, precipitated with ethanol, and vacuum-dried to obtain monohydroxyl-terminated polycaprolactone (PCL). 4.0 g of PCL was dissolved in 50 mL of NMP. Then 3.36 g of AP, 0.515 g of N, N’-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC), and 0.315 g of 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) were added to the solution. The magnetic stirring reaction was performed under ice bath conditions for 48 h. After the reaction, the product was filtered, and the filtrate was precipitated in diethyl ether. The precipitated product was filtered, drained, dissolved in chloroform, purified by reprecipitation in diethyl ether, filtered again, and dried to obtain the final product PCL-AP(PAP). Characterization of PAP by NMR (AVANCE NEO 400 M, Bruker, Germany).

Fabrication of conductive PGCL/AP spinning membrane

The conductive PGCL/AP spinning membrane was synthesized using the standard procedure and then enhanced according to our findings [26, 27]. Aniline pentamer (AP) was initially grafted onto polycaprolactone (PCL) to synthesize the conductive polymer material PCL-AP. Subsequently, 50 mg of PGCL and 0.5 mg of PCL-AP were solubilized in 2.5 ml of hexafluoroisopropanol to create a homogeneous solution. The dynamic viscosity of solutions from different groups was measured using a viscometer (NDJ-8ST, Nirun, China). Ultimately, 2.5 ml of the combined solution comprising PGCL and PCL-AP was withdrawn using a 5 ml sterile syringe. The sterile syringe was secured on one side, and the transmitting plate was positioned 20 cm from the receiving plate, and the voltage was set to 20 kV. The feed rate of the combined solution was calibrated to 0.5 ml/h, and the rotational speed was set at 2000 rpm. The liquid feeding direction was controlled to ensure consistency in the preparation of the PGCL/PCL-AP1 (PAP1) electrospinning membrane, which used a 1% PCL-AP concentration and exhibited a directional fiber architecture. To enhance the mechanical properties of the spinning membrane, a mixed solution was used for a secondary random electrospinning process on one side of the membrane at 500 rpm, resulting in the formation of a shell composed of random PAP1 nanofibers. Utilizing the methodology as mentioned above, 50 mg of PGCL and 1.5 mg and 2.5 mg of PCL-AP were solubilized in 2.5 ml of hexafluoroisopropanol to fabricate PGCL/PCL-AP (PAP3, PAP5) electrospinning membranes, exhibiting AP concentrations of 3% and 5%, characterized by a random fiber arrangement externally and a directional fiber arrangement internally. The aforementioned four spinning membranes will be utilized for the subsequent cellular studies.

The electrospun membrane was sectioned into rectangles measuring 15 mm in length and 10 mm in width, aligned with the fiber direction. Later cell tests determined the best AP concentration(PAP5), and the spun membranes were shaped into 15 mm-long, about two mm-wide nerve conduits for further animal research.

Conductive PGCL/AP scaffold characterization

The architecture of synthesized PCL-AP was elucidated by transmission electron microscopy. The electrical properties of the created conductive material, PCL-AP, were tested with an electrochemical workstation (Ivium), and a cyclic voltammetry curve was produced. The changes in the makeup and molecular structure of the created spinning membranes were studied using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The morphology of each category of spinning membranes was examined using scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi, Japan). The fiber diameter was quantified using ImageJ. The mechanical properties of the four electrospun membranes were evaluated using a universal testing machine, and mechanical indicators were computed from the results. The wettability of the electrospun nanofiber mats was measured by measuring the angle of a water droplet placed on them using a JIKAN CAG-20 SE device. A drop of deionized water was applied to each sample at room temperature, and the image was recorded after 10 s. The test was conducted in six repetitions. The thermal stability of each spinning membrane category was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The conductivity of the spinning membranes from different groups was measured using a four-point probe conductivity tester (RTS-1389, 4Probes Tech, China).

Cell culture

PC12 cells (highly differentiated) were acquired from Wuhan Sean Biotechnology Co., Ltd. They were grown in high-glucose DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 5% horse serum after being dissociated with trypsin and collagenase.

Culture of PC12 cells on PGCL/AP spinning membranes at various dosages

Each set of spinning membranes was cut into 15 mm round pieces with clean scissors after being disinfected with UV light for more studies on PC12 cell growth. The experimental groups included PGCL spinning membranes with different amounts of PCL/AP (1%, 3%, and 5%), while pure PGCL spinning membranes were used as control groups. The health of PC12 cells grown on spinning membranes with PGCL and different amounts of PGCL/AP (1%, 3%, and 5% AP) was checked using a live-dead staining kit. The growth of PC12 cells on spinning membranes with PGCL and varying PGCL/AP ratios (1%, 3%, and 5% AP) was measured using the CCK-8 method on the first, third, and seventh days after seeding. Immunofluorescence labeling with DAPI, NF200, and phalloidin was used to assess how well PC12 cells were growing and staying alive, and to examine their structural organization.

The influence of current on PC12 cells cultured on PGCL/AP membranes at varying doses

The spun membranes made of PGCL and different amounts of PGCL/AP (AP 1%, AP 3%, AP 5%) were set up in a special ES device, with one end in the cell culture medium and the other end connected to the electrical stimulator. Poly-L-lysine (0.01%; PLL) was treated for 2 h at 37 °C to improve cell attachment, followed by rinsing with PBS. PC12 cells were subsequently seeded onto the surface of each group of spun membranes and cultured for 12 h before electrical stimulation. Subsequently, ES was conducted in an incubator with a direct current of 100 mV/mm. Following electrical stimulation, the ES device was removed and cultured in the incubator until the subsequent treatment. The viability and survival of PC12 cells in each group under electrical stimulation were assessed as outlined in Sect. “Fabrication of conductive PGCL/AP spinning membrane”.

Rat sciatic nerve damage model construction

Adult female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (220–250 g) were randomly allocated into five groups: autologous transplantation group (n = 6), PGCL group (n = 6), PAP group (n = 6), PGCL + ES group (n = 6), and PAP + ES group (n = 6). The protocols for animal surgery and sciatic nerve tissue preparation were as follows: The animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital at 30 mg/kg. The sciatic nerve was revealed via a skin incision, and the muscles of the left lateral thigh were severed. A portion of the sciatic nerve was excised, resulting in a 10 mm deficit following the retraction of the nerve terminal. The PGCL and PAP groups used their respective composite NGC to repair the nerve defect. In contrast, the autologous transplantation group reinserted the severed nerve segment into the sciatic nerve defect in the opposite orientation.

The electrical stimulation parameters were selected based on an established protocol from previous literature. Briefly, rats were administered electrical stimulation (ES) every other day. The stimulation parameters were set at a frequency of 20 Hz, a duty cycle of 50%, and an operating voltage of 100 mV, with each stimulation session lasting 2 h [28]. Two insulated copper wires were stripped of 3–5 mm of insulation at the apex of the rats in the stimulation group and subsequently employed as electrodes. One end of the two wires was twisted into a loop and secured to the proximal and distal extremities of the PGCL and PAP stents before their implantation. After the stents were surgically sutured to the proximal and distal stumps of the sciatic nerve, the ES device was connected to the other extremities of the wires. To prevent rodents from scratching and biting, the electrode tip was secured to the skin with medical tape when the ES device was connected. The rats were consistently housed and fed under standardized laboratory conditions and euthanized at various time points. Nerves that had been regenerated were collected for additional immunochemical or biochemical analysis. The Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Jilin University (KT202405017, Changchun City) approved all experimental procedures that involved rodents. SD rats, adult females, weighing 200–250 g, were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Jilin University. The experimental animals were maintained in a room with a constant temperature of (23 ± 2) °C and a light cycle of 12/12 h. They were provided with freely accessible water and sustenance until they were required.

Immunofluorescence staining and histological analysis

At the designated period post-surgery, the rats were euthanized, and the regenerated sciatic nerves were harvested and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, a dehydration procedure was conducted utilizing a graded ethanol series, and the specimens were embedded in paraffin for sectioning with a microtome. All sections underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, CD31 immunohistochemistry, toluidine blue (TB) staining, and fast blue (LFB) staining, with images captured using a Leica microscope. The proportion of myelin-positive region was determined using LFB staining, whereas the number of SC-positive areas was assessed by TB staining. The procedure for immunofluorescence staining was as follows. The sections were treated with antigen retrieval solution and incubated with 5% goat serum for 30 min. Immunofluorescence primary antibodies S100β (1:100, Abcam, UK) and anti-NF200 (1:200, Abcam, UK) were applied to slides and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h, subsequently washed with PBS. Following a 40-minute incubation at room temperature in the absence of light, immunofluorescence secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, 1:400; Alexa Fluor 594, 1:800; Invitrogen) and unbound antibodies were eliminated through washing with PBS. DAPI-labeled nuclei were subsequently applied to the slices, which were then sealed with glycerol and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.

Evaluation of gastrocnemius muscle wet weight and histology

Following the sacrifice of the rats, the gastrocnemius muscles from both sides were collected at 6 and 12 weeks post-implantation. The harvested muscles were rinsed in PBS and measured using an electronic scale. The gastrocnemius muscles were subsequently fixed in 4% formaldehyde overnight, incubated in sucrose solutions of varying concentrations (10%, 15%, and 30%) at 4 °C for 48 h, embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, and sectioned into 10 μm cross-sections. Following dewaxing, the muscle sections were stained with the Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (Solarbio, China). Muscle tissue images were obtained via microscopy, with five random fields of view chosen for each group. The extent of muscle fibrosis was assessed through ImageJ software to determine the average percentage of collagen fiber area in the gastrocnemius muscle.

Behavioral analysis

The potential for motor recovery following sciatic nerve regeneration was assessed using walking track and footprint analyses conducted prior to harvesting at 6 and 12 weeks post-transplantation. The hind foot of each rat was stained with black ink, and the foot was tracked on paper during locomotion. The toe spread (TS), defined as the distance between the first and fifth toes, the middle toe spread (IT), which is the distance between the second and fourth toes, and the foot length (PL), measured from the tip of the third toe to the heel, were assessed for both the healthy foot (N) and the affected side (E) using ImageJ software. Data analysis

Statistical significance was assessed using GraphPad Prism 7.0. A one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare individuals across several categories simultaneously. A two-way ANOVA, followed by Sidak’s multiple-comparison test, was used to compare all groups. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of PGCL/AP composite nerve conduits

Figure 1A shows the 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO-d6) spectrum of the PAP copolymer. The proton peaks of PAP copolymer were labeled at their corresponding positions in the 1H NMR spectrum. This result confirms the successful synthesis of the PAP copolymer. Figure 1B illustrates the grafting of aniline pentamer (AP) onto polycaprolactone (PCL) to synthesize the conductive polymer material PCL-AP, which was studied using transmission electron microscopy. B1 illustrates the configuration of an individual aniline pentamer before synthesis, while B2 depicts the composite structure of PCL-AP after the successful grafting of the aniline pentamer onto PCL. PCL-AP exhibits a porous, textured surface, which is conducive to cell attachment and proliferation. Figure 1C illustrates the cyclic voltammetry curve of PCL-AP, confirming the successful grafting of the conductive component AP and indicating that PCL-AP has commendable conductivity. To verify the synthesis of PGCL/AP, we performed an FTIR infrared spectrum analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1D. Notably, the spectrum of the PCL-AP/PCCL composite exhibited distinct changes compared to that of pure PCCL, manifesting as a new absorption peak near 1640 nm. This peak corresponds to the characteristic vibration of specific functional groups within the AP component. This observation indicates that the composite was not a simple physical mixture but involved successful composite formation and interaction, thereby further confirming the successful synthesis of the conductive material PGCL-AP.

SEM scanning was conducted on PGCL/AP to conduct a more thorough examination of its microstructure. Furthermore, the microstructure of PGCL/AP was observed by SEM, with each fiber group precisely organized. As illustrated in Fig. 1E, all of them exhibited excellent directionality. Among them, the fiber diameter progressively decreased as the concentration of the conductive component PCL-AP increased (Table S1). The PAP block copolymer exhibits self-organization in the solvent. The process involves the free and random dispersion of copolymer chains in the solvent. Due to intermolecular forces, the AP segments tend to aggregate. In contrast, the PCL segments are restricted from separating from the AP segments, leading to the gradual close packing and assembly of molecules. When the assembly reaches a certain extent, the copolymer forms a morphology consistent with that observed by TEM (Fig. 1B2). As shown in Table S2, the dynamic viscosity decreases with the increase in AP content, and the self-organization of the PAP block copolymer may be the reason for the decrease in viscosity. Huang et al. also observed the same phenomenon of AP molecular self-assembly when studying the PLA-AP copolymer [31].

After introducing PCL-AP, we observed that the mechanical strength of each spun membrane group increased in subsequent mechanical tests (Table S3). The mechanical strength of the PGCL@PAP5 group was the highest among them, with a maximal tensile load of 2.10 N and an energy that reached the breaking point of 27.8 mJ. Confirming that the mechanical properties of the material can be significantly enhanced by the introduction of PCL-AP, the mechanical strength test results were superior to those of the pure PGCL material. This improvement is more conducive to preserving the nerve conduit structure during subsequent implantation.

In the subsequent contact angle measurements of the spun films from each group (Figures S1 and S2), the results indicated that the PGCL@PAP5 group exhibited the lowest contact angle, approaching 90°. These results confirm that the PGCL@PAP5 electrospun membrane possessed the most hydrophilic surface, which facilitates cell adhesion and proliferation. The thermal stability of each group of spinning membranes was assessed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). As demonstrated in the supplementary material (Figure S3), the weight of each spun membrane group remained relatively stable below 360 °C, indicating good thermal stability. As the temperature exceeded 360 °C, a weight loss was observed across all spinning membrane groups. Notably, the PGCL@PAP5 group exhibited the smallest weight reduction, at 93.9270%. This finding indicates that the spinning membrane of the PGCL@PAP5 group is more stable than those of the other groups. As shown in Table S4, the conductivity of the fibrous membranes from different groups was measured. No conductivity was detected in the PGCL group, while the conductivity increased with the increase in PAP content. The conductivities of PGCL@PAP1, PGCL@PAP3, and PGCL@PAP5 were 0.23 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm, 1.56 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm, and 3.89 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm, respectively.

The flat micropatterned PGCL/AP composite was subsequently rolled into a cylindrical shape and electrospun to create the outer surface of the NGC. Previous studies indicate that the size of the 3D printing needle can accurately modify the diameter of PGCL/AP fibers. In contrast, the orientation of the outer AP nanofibers can be regulated by the rotation speed of the electrospinning process. Figure 1F illustrates the fabricated PGCL/AP composite NGCs (PGCL/AP-NGCs). The NGC structure retains its integrity post-demolding, and the conduit surface exhibits a relatively smooth texture.

Impact of varying concentrations of PGCL/AP spinning membranes on the viability and proliferation of PC12 cells

The investigation initially employed PI/Calcein-AM staining kit to assess the viability of PC12 cells following 3 days of growth on PGCL, PGCL@PAP1, PGCL@PAP3, and PGCL@PAP5 spinning membranes. Figure 2A presents the results. All PC12 cell groups exhibit robust survival, with no significant differences in survival rates across groups (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, we employed the CCK-8 assay to assess the proliferation of PC12 cells cultivated on PGCL, PGCL@PAP1, PGCL@PAP3, and PGCL@PAP5 spinning membranes over 1, 3, and 7 days. The outcomes are depicted in Fig. 2D. All groups exhibit robust cell proliferation and survival; notably, the PGCL@PAP5 group demonstrates superior cell proliferation compared to the other groups, surpassing the Control group. Figure 2B illustrates the subsequent assessment of PC12 cell viability across several groups of spinning membranes, along with their cytoskeletal distribution as determined by immunofluorescence labeling. The PC12 cells cultivated on the spinning membrane surface are predominantly aligned with the nanofibers. Notably, the PAP5 spun membrane group showed a significantly greater number of PC12 cells, with a more orderly organization, compared to the other groups. The NF200 staining results indicated that the PC12 cells in the PAP5 spun membrane group exhibited enhanced proliferative expression. As mentioned earlier, the results demonstrate that a 5% AP concentration exerts a superior effect on the proliferation and differentiation of PC12 cells.

Histological examination of regenerated sciatic nerve

The PAP5 concentration that successfully passed the biocompatibility test and demonstrated optimal effects in promoting PC12 cell proliferation and survival was used to prepare the PGCL/PCL-AP (PAP) nerve conduit for subsequent in vivo experiments, allowing observation of histological repair outcomes. For each group, stained tissue sections were examined under a microscope. Five fields of view were randomly selected from each section for subsequent quantitative analysis. Figure 4A shows that HE staining revealed significantly greater regenerated tissue in the PAP + ES group than in the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups. At the 12-week mark, no significant differences were observed between the regenerated nerve tissue in the PAP + ES group and that in the autologous transplantation group. Furthermore, CD31 immunohistochemistry indicated that all groups exhibited varying degrees of neovascularization, with the PAP + ES group demonstrating the most significant angiogenesis-promoting effect compared with the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups (Fig. 4B, E), closely resembling the results observed in the autologous transplantation group.

Furthermore, TB staining images (Fig. 4D and G) indicated that the quantity of SC-positive cells in the PAP + ES group (6962) exceeded those in the PGCL (6189), PAP (6499), and PGCL + ES (6810) groups, and was similar to that in the autologous transplantation group.

In immunofluorescence labeling, S100β protein can promote axon elongation and serve as an intuitive indicator of Schwann cell density at the injury site. Figure 5A shows that the PAP + ES group had higher SC expression than the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups at both 6 and 12 weeks, with expression levels most similar to those of the autologous transplantation group (Fig. 5B). Moreover, NF200 is a distinct protein implicated in neural regeneration. It is categorized as a type IV intermediate filament found in the cytoplasm of neurons, indicative of axonal regeneration at the site of damage. In Fig. 5A, the PAP + ES group exhibited a greater number of NF200-positive cells than the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups, closely resembling the autologous transplantation group depicted in Fig. 5C. The increase of S100β and NF200 can replicate the milieu necessary for axon regeneration following nerve injury, hence facilitating nerve regeneration. Consequently, as illustrated in Fig. 5, the synergistic application of a PAP nerve conduit and electrical stimulation can significantly enhance Schwann cell proliferation in the wounded region, thereby facilitating the regeneration of myelin and axons.

Evaluation of the gastrocnemius muscle

Following the transection of the sciatic nerve, atrophy of the gastrocnemius muscle on the affected side frequently occurs as a result of denervation. Consequently, assessing the recovery of the affected gastrocnemius muscle may serve as an indirect indicator of sciatic nerve recovery. Figure 6A illustrates the region characterized by collagen fiber deposition and muscle fibers, as evidenced by Masson staining. ImageJ was employed to determine the average percentage of collagen fiber area in the gastrocnemius muscle (Fig. 6D). The collagen fiber area in the PAP + ES group (9.43%) was less than that in the PGCL group (19.56%), the PAP group (12.73%), and the PGCL + ES group (10.57%), while being marginally greater than the autologous transplantation group (7.38%).

Furthermore, measuring the weight of the left and right gastrocnemius muscles in each group to assess muscle regeneration following nerve injury (Fig. 6B, E) revealed that the gastrocnemius muscles across all groups exhibited muscle atrophy. The autologous transplantation group showed the highest muscle weight at 2.14 g, while the PAP + ES group recorded a muscle weight of 1.93 g, closely approaching that of the autologous transplantation group (Figure S1). The results indicate that the PAP + ES group is more effective in mitigating muscle atrophy resulting from axonal truncation post-implantation and in restoring neurological function compared to the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups.

Behavioral analysis and motor function assessment

Following the gait analysis test seen in Fig. 6C, the footprints of the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups were indistinct and discontinuous in the footprint images. Conversely, the footprints of the AN and PAP + ES groups were well-preserved, with the imprints of each toe discernible with greater clarity. The sciatic nerve function index (SFI) for each group of rats was determined from their footprints, as illustrated in Fig. 6F. The index ranges from − 100 to 0, with − 100 signifying total nerve impairment and 0 representing optimal healing. The results demonstrate that the nerve guide, when coupled with electrical stimulation, creates a more conducive microenvironment for nerve regeneration than the sole use of PGCL or PAP nerve guides. PAP, in conjunction with electrical stimulation, can most effectively enhance the functional recovery of rat locomotion.

Discussion

Research on nerve guidance conduits (NGCs), particularly those made of silicone, was pioneered as early as the 1960s. Many fundamental insights into nerve regeneration were derived from the use of this model system [32, 33]. In general, inert guidance conduits can be used to bridge short gaps. However, a consensus holds that inert guidance channels (e.g., silicone) do not support regeneration across defects exceeding 10 mm in rats without external intervention [34]. Consequently, numerous strategies have been explored to develop conduits fabricated from a variety of materials with diverse properties to achieve the successful reconstruction of injured peripheral nerves. Furthermore, over the past few decades, conductive biomaterials combined with electrical stimulation have been found to influence cellular behaviors, including proliferation, gene expression, and differentiation [35–37]. Nevertheless, conventional conductive biomaterials often lack an oriented structure, which prevents electrical stimulation from achieving optimal efficacy. As a result, axonal growth within such conduits remains suboptimal. In this study, we systematically investigated the synergistic effects of a PGCL/AP conductive nerve conduit combined with electrical stimulation on nerve regeneration, using both cellular assays and a comprehensive rat model with a 10-mm sciatic nerve defect.

NGC is constructed as a double-layered structure and is sutured to the proximal and distal stumps of the sciatic nerve, ensuring that the neurofilaments generated during nerve regeneration predominantly manifest in the inner region of the NGC. Furthermore, given that the outer surface of the NGC may solely offer mechanical support for the scaffold with diminished interaction with nerve cells, we decreased the rotation speed from 2000 rpm to 500 rpm during the fabrication of the outer surface to achieve random nanofibers, whose interwoven structure enhances mechanical strength.

The PC12 cell line remains a widely utilized model in preliminary investigations of peripheral nerve repair, owing to its capacity for neuronal differentiation. Despite its limitations, the stability and ease of culturing PC12 cells make them a robust model system for the rapid screening of various growth factors, pharmaceutical agents, or material composites for their potential to promote neural regeneration [38]. Furthermore, when cultured on specific substrates, PC12 cells exhibit an upregulation of key neuronal marker genes, such as Tubb3 and Map2, thereby providing a convenient model for exploring the molecular pathways underlying neuronal differentiation [39]. So, This study assessed the impact of NGC and the simultaneous application of NGC and ES on nerve regeneration by quantifying the adhesion, proliferation, and alignment of PC12 cells on the spun membrane. This experiment verified the aforementioned issues through cell live-death staining and the CCK-8 method. The application of cell ES to PAP spun membrane with conductive components resulted in improved cell viability, and the PC12 cells exhibited a neat arrangement on the surface of the PAP group. Furthermore, PC12 cells exhibited robust proliferation in the PAP + ES group, demonstrating superior proliferation and survival compared to the NGC group lacking conductive components and the blank control group. The results of immunofluorescence staining indicated that ES enhanced the migratory capacity of PC12 cells on the PAP spun membrane, thereby facilitating contact guidance for axon fixation and elongation [40]. We propose that this effect originates from the synergy between the contact guidance provided by the aligned fibrous architecture and the endogenous signaling pathways activated by electrical stimulation. The former offers physical pathways for cell migration and axonal elongation, while the latter likely provides the necessary chemical drive for neural differentiation, potentially through activation of the MAPK pathway and modulation of intracellular Ca²⁺ dynamics. This “physical-electrochemical” dual-signal regulation mechanism may explain the superior performance of the PAP + ES group in guiding cellular behavior. To investigate the functional effects of PGCL/AP composite scaffolds in vivo, we implanted nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) into the sciatic nerve defects of rats and assessed the histological and morphological repair outcomes. In our study, the number of regenerated tissues and newly formed blood vessels in the PAP + ES group was significantly higher than that in the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups at both 6 and 12 weeks post-surgery, as demonstrated by HE staining and CD31 immunohistochemistry. Histological analysis via HE staining revealed a marked increase in regenerated tissue within the PAP + ES group, with no significant difference observed between this group and the autograft group by week 12. Furthermore, the neovascularization area in the PAP + ES group (37%) was closest to that in the autograft group (39%). Additionally, LFB and TB staining results showed that remyelination and Schwann cell density in the PAP + ES group were greater than those in the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups, and most closely resembled those in the autologous transplantation group.

The axons of the sciatic nerve system exhibit regenerative capabilities following injury; however, the molecular mechanisms governing effective nerve regeneration remain inadequately understood. A substantial body of research indicates that various nerve growth factors, such as S100β and NF200, facilitate the proliferation and migration of Schwann cells and enhance sciatic nerve regeneration [41, 42]. Moreover, these cytokines are essential for sustaining axon regeneration capability. S100β directly indicates the quantity of Schwann cells produced in the injured region [43], whereas NF200 reflects the status of axon regeneration [44]. Immunofluorescence staining of nerve tissue sections demonstrated the expression of NF200 and S-100β proteins across all groups, indicating the growth of new axons and the formation of myelin sheaths by Schwann cells. ImageJ was utilized to assess the percentage of positive area of immunofluorescence staining across each group. The autologous transplantation group exhibited the highest percentage of positive area for NF200 and S-100β. The expression level in the PAP group, which included conductive components, was higher than that in the PGCL group, which lacked conductive components, across all nerve conduit transplantation groups. Following the application of electrical stimulation to each group, the PAP + ES group exhibited the most favorable expression effect. The integration of nerve guides with electrical stimulation enhances Schwann cell proliferation and remyelination relative to the use of nerve guides in isolation, thereby promoting axonal regeneration. Unlike studies reporting a dominant effect on only one aspect [45], our strategy concurrently addresses both critical components, thereby facilitating more effective holistic regeneration of the integrated axon-Schwann cell entity.

The sciatic nerve comprises motor, sensory, and autonomic fibers. Sciatic nerve injury typically results in significant motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction, particularly characterized by atrophy of the calf gastrocnemius muscle [46, 47]. It is essential to evaluate the recovery of the affected gastrocnemius muscle and, consequently, the recovery of the sciatic nerve. The results of Masson’s trichrome staining of the gastrocnemius muscle revealed that the autograft group exhibited the least collagen fiber deposition, suggesting optimal recovery of sciatic nerve function in this group. The PAP + ES group demonstrated a collagen fiber deposition area proportion of 9.43%, which was closest to that of the autograft group (7.38%). These findings confirm that the PAP + ES intervention effectively promotes regeneration of the gastrocnemius muscle and supports functional recovery of the sciatic nerve.

This study conducted walking track analysis to examine the pathology and potential treatment of nerve injury following NGC implantation, as well as to assess the functional recovery of the sciatic nerve. The decline in sciatic nerve function typically results in a reduction of toe extension and middle toe extension. Footprint analysis at 6 and 12 weeks indicated that toe extension and middle toe extension in the autograft and PAP + ES groups were broader compared to the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups. Following complete transection of the sciatic nerve, the sciatic nerve function index (SFI) in all groups of rats exhibited a significant reduction. Six weeks post-implantation, the SFI for the PGCL, PAP, and PGCL + ES groups was lower compared to the autograft and PAP + ES groups. At 12 weeks, the SFI of the PAP + ES group remained comparable to that of the autograft group, with values of -52 and − 51, respectively. Our results indicate that a nerve conduit with favorable conductive properties, when combined with electrical stimulation, exhibits a synergistic effect in enhancing nerve regeneration and functional recovery, outperforming experimental groups lacking electrical stimulation. According to the review by Yang et al. [48], many synthetic nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) generally require a longer time to achieve comparable sciatic functional index (SFI) levels in rat models, or are only effective in shorter gap injuries. Our strategy demonstrated significant superiority over groups using conduits alone or electrical stimulation (ES) alone. These results strongly suggest that employing an aligned conductive conduit as a platform for ES can efficiently translate exogenous electrical signals into an endogenous driving force that promotes nerve functional regeneration, ultimately leading to comprehensive repair encompassing both structural guidance and functional recovery.

In this study, we fabricated an aligned, conductive PGCL/AP (PAP) nerve conduit. When combined with electrical stimulation (ES), this construct promoted regeneration and functional recovery across a 10-mm sciatic nerve gap in rats. Evaluations based on motor function and histological analyses at 6 and 12 weeks post-operation demonstrated that the PAP + ES group yielded the most promising outcomes, indicating its potential as a viable alternative to autografts for peripheral nerve repair. In addition, When evaluating the efficacy of the PGCL nerve conduit in this study, it is imperative to account for the confounding effect of the “bridging gap” intervention per se. This concept posits that even a hollow conduit can support nerve regeneration across a limited distance by providing a physical channel and a favorable microenvironment. However, as previously mentioned, the 10 mm gap utilized in this work represents a critical defect size where the benefits of passive bridging alone become substantially limited and insufficient for functional recovery. Consequently, a plain conduit group was intentionally excluded from the experimental design to enable a more focused comparison between the various active repair strategies under investigation. Nevertheless, this study is not without limitations. First, the long-term degradation behavior and biocompatibility of PGCL/AP composite materials remain to be fully elucidated. Determining whether their degradation kinetics appropriately match the timeline of tissue regeneration is a critical prerequisite for potential clinical application. Second, while static contact angle measurements offer initial, macro-scale insights into wettability, the inherently porous nature of electrospun membranes necessitates a more detailed investigation into complex solid-liquid interactions. Finally, although rat models serve as a standard initial screening platform, their inherent regenerative capacity surpasses that of humans. Consequently, the broader relevance and translational potential of our conclusions require validation in large animal models, such as non-human primates, which represents a mandatory step toward clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study presents the design and synthesis of a PGCL/AP (PAP) nerve conduit featuring a directional guidance structure and enhanced electrical conductivity. The integration of this nerve conduit with electrical stimulation facilitated myelin formation and axon regeneration, thereby improving the recovery of motor function following sciatic nerve injury. The findings illustrate a novel approach and offer insights for developing a new nerve conduit (NGC) for sciatic nerve regeneration.
