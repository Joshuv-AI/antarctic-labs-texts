Establishment of disseminated neonatal echovirus 11 infection model in hFcRn transgenic mice
Authors: Fei Li, Qi Li, Xin Zhao, Yiliang Fu, Linlin Zhang, Xiangpeng Chen, Zhengde Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Echovirus 11 (E11) infections can easily cause neonatal sepsis, which is a life-threatening disease. However, the pathogenesis of E11 infections remains poorly understood. Furthermore, the absence of an effective animal model that accurately reflects disseminated neonatal infection has hindered research progress. Hence, we aimed to establish a precise disease mouse model closely resembling E11 associated human neonatal conditions. Three-day-old human neonatal Fc receptor (hFcRn) transgenic mice were administered an optimized dose of E11 through intraperitoneal (IP) injection. E11-infected mice exhibited severe clinical symptoms, including weight loss, limb paralysis, and even death. Viral loads were detected across multiple organs, such as muscle, spinal cord, and intestine, with the highest load in muscle (1 × 105-1 × 107 copies/mg). Additionally, liver and kidney function (ALT, AST and Cr), cardiac enzymes (CK-MB), and coagulation parameters (FDP) were markedly abnormal in the E11 group, in which ALT level showed 3.5 times higher than the normal group. Furthermore, in situ hybridization revealed E11 RNA in the heart, liver, lungs, and brain, with the highest levels in the heart and liver. Serum cytokine analysis showed significant elevation of MCP-1, IL-18, IL-22, and RANTES, with MCP-1 reaching levels as high as 2590 pg/mL. Histopathological examination revealed extensive pathological changes in multiple tissues. The successful establishment of the disseminated neonatal E11 infection model relies on several key factors, including the use of 3-day-old mice, an increased dosage of the infectious agent, an appropriate infection route via IP injection, and the use of viral strains obtained from the clinical case of neonatal mortality. In summary, this mouse model serves as a valuable tool for investigating the pathogenic mechanisms of E11 infection associated disseminated neonatal infection, offering a stable platform for the development and evaluation of related vaccines and therapeutic drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02979-1.

Background

Echovirus 11 (E11), classified as an echovirus within the genus enterovirus (EV) of the family Picornaviridae, is a member of the B (betacoxsackie) group of Enteroviruses. It was first isolated in 1953 from the fecal sample of a healthy individual in the United States. E11 is among the most common viruses causing infections in children, particularly neonates [1–3]. It accounts for approximately 5.16% of detected EV cases, ranking fifth in overall prevalence [4]. Neonatal E11 infections are primarily attributed to mother-to-child transmission [5]. The clinical manifestations of E11 infections vary widely, ranging from mild, non-specific illnesses-such as fever, rash, and herpangina-to severe infectious diseases, including viral encephalitis or meningitis, pleuritis, myocarditis, and acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) [6, 7].

Notably, E11 infections are frequently associated with sepsis in neonates, multi-organ hemorrhage, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), which can result in shock or even death [8, 9]. Between 1970 and 2005, multiple E11 outbreaks were reported in neonatal care units in the United States [2]. Furthermore, epidemic outbreaks involving E11 infections in children, particularly neonates, have been documented in Germany, the United Kingdom, France, and Poland [10–14]. Between 2010 and 2024, at least 27 severe neonatal E11 infections have been reported from various countries, including China (10 cases), France (10 cases), Italy (4 cases), and Japan (3 cases), which were marked by severe clinical manifestations, such as sepsis, myocarditis, and liver failure, with approximately one-third of neonates succumbing to the infection [8, 15–18]. These severe outcomes make E11 a significant threat to both pediatric and public health.

Currently, no disseminated neonatal E11 infection model has been established, and no approved antiviral drugs are available for EV. The only known vaccines available for EVs are for Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) and poliovirus (PV) [19–21]. However, the EV-A71 vaccine lacks cross-protective capabilities against other EV strains. This highlights the urgent need for animal models to investigate disease mechanisms and develop therapies. Although Type I interferon receptor (IFNAR)−/− mice are frequently utilized to model EVs infection, these immunodeficient models may inadequately reflect the clinical progression of neonatal EV infections [22]. Studies, including our own, have demonstrated that the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) functions as the uncoating receptor for E11 infection, facilitating viral uncoating and the release of the viral genome upon cell entry [23, 24]. The identification of FcRn has significantly advanced the development of animal models for E11 infection. So, this study aims aimed to establish disseminated E11 infection murine model.

Materials and methods

Animals

hFcRnTg mice, C57BL/6J strain were specific pathogen free (SPF) and were kindly provided by Gao Fu’s research group. All hFcRnTg mice were raised in independently ventilated cages and were provided with sufficient food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. 2021001).

Virus and cells

Virus used in this study was E11 SD2003-478 (GenBank: MN496161.1), which was isolated from a patient, RD cells were used to propagate this virus.

Animal infection experiments

Three-day-old hFcRnTg mice (n = 4–8 per group) were infected with 106 TCID50 E11 strain 478 at through intraperitoneal (IP) injection routes (25 µl); Mock group of mice were inoculated with maintenance medium (MM) (25 µl), all mice were reared separately. The weights, survival rates, and clinical scores of the mice were recorded daily. The grade of clinical disease was scored as follows: 0, no disease; 1, significant weight loss; 2, inability to right itself when placed on back; 3, hind limb weakness; 4, left/right hind limb paralysis; 5, mortality.

Genomic DNA extraction from animal tissues

Tissue DNA was extracted using the DNA Quick Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, 51404). A 1-cm mouse tail segment was placed in an EP tube, supplemented with lysis buffer and proteinase K, and incubated at 56 °C until complete tissue digestion. Subsequently, Buffer AL and ethanol were sequentially added, and the mixture was transferred to a DNeasy Mini spin column for purification, with DNA ultimately eluted using Buffer AE. Genotyping of hFcRn transgenic mice was performed via PCR, with a total reaction volume of 20 µl (comprising 18 µl premixed reaction solution and 2 µl DNA). The PCR protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C denaturation for 30 s, 55–65 °C annealing for 30 s, and 72 °C extension for 5 min, with a final extension at 60 °C for 5 min. Agarose gel electrophoresis was employed for genotype identification: wild-type (P1/P2 967 bp, no P3/P4 band), heterozygous (P1/P2 967 bp, P3/P4 608 bp), and homozygous (no P1/P2 band, P3/P4 608 bp) configurations.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Mouse tissue samples were routinely embedded in paraffin, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using citric acid buffer in a high-pressure cooker. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by normal serum blocking. The sections were then incubated with anti-hFcRn antibody (Abcam, ab228975, 1:4000) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, biotinylated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (DINGGUO CHANGSHENG, IB-0061, 1:50) and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin solution (DINGGUO CHANGSHENG, IH-0061, 1:200) were sequentially applied. Visualization was achieved using DAB chromogen, with hematoxylin used for counterstaining. Sections were cleared with xylene and mounted with neutral mounting medium. Immunohistochemical staining was examined and images were captured under an optical microscope to evaluate hFcRn protein localization and expression patterns in the tissue samples. Image-Pro Plus software was employed to quantify the cumulative integrated optical density for each tissue section, which was subsequently normalized by dividing the integrated optical density by the image area to determine the mean optical density (MOD) across different tissue samples.

Nucleic acid preparations and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Tissue samples (50–100 mg) were collected for viral nucleic acid extraction. Simultaneously, the laboratory enterovirus universal ribonucleic acid liquid (5 × 105 copies/mL) was used to construct a standard curve (supplementary Table S1). The QIAamp Viral RNA Mini kit nucleic acid extraction kit (52904, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions for viral RNA extraction. Concurrently, additional tissue samples (50–100 mg) were processed in Trizol according to the manufacturer’s directions to extract nucleic acids for other gene expression studies. Reverse transcription was performed using reverse transcription reagents (RR036A, Takara, Japan). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed in a 10 µL reaction mixture containing specific primers and TB-Green Premix Ex Taq II. Amplification was performed in a real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR system (Applied Biosystems 7500).

Determination of virus titers in mouse tissues

Mouse tissues were collected from 3-day-old hFcRnTg mice injected with E11 of 478 through IP route at 3 to 72 h post-infection (hpi). All samples were added to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and homogenized in an automated tissue grinder (SWE-C6, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for three freeze-thaw cycles before centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant collected at each time point was serially diluted 10-fold for 12 gradients and inoculated into RD cells that were cultured in 96-well plates. After 7 days of continuous observation, virus titers were determined using the TCID50 assay.

Biochemical indicators detection

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (E-BC-K235-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (E-BC-K236-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), and creatinine (Cr) (E-BC-K188-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) were quantified using colorimetric methods with an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader. Liquid samples such as serum were directly tested by enzymatic catalytic reactions followed by chromogenic reagent addition, with optical density (OD) measured at the final step. ALT and AST detection principles involve enzymatic amino transfer reactions generating pyruvate, which reacts with phenylhydrazine to produce a reddish-brown color; Cr detection utilizes creatinine oxidase method, with the reaction product generating color at a specific wavelength. Creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) (E-EL-M0355, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) and fibrin degradation products (FDP) (SDM0821, SIMUWU, Shanghai, China) were analyzed using sandwich ELISA, using specific antibody-coated microplates. Target proteins from samples bind to solid-phase antibodies, followed by biotinylated antibody and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin addition, with TMB substrate used for colorimetric development and measured using an ELISA reader. Sample concentrations were calculated using standard curves, ensuring precise quantification of biochemical markers.

RNA In situ hybridization

Following euthanasia, mouse tissues were immediately fixed in tissue fixative solution, then processed through dehydration, embedding, sectioning, deparaffinization, target retrieval, hydrogen peroxide treatment, and protease IV digestion. Specific viral RNA probes were hybridized at 40 °C for 2 h, followed by BaseScopeTM AMP18 signal amplification. Visualization was achieved using Fast RED chromogen, with hematoxylin used for counterstaining. Sections were mounted using VectaMont medium and examined under bright-field microscopy to assess the spatial distribution of E11 viral RNA within the tissues.

Multiplex cytokine analysis

Blood samples were collected using endotoxin-free tubes and allowed to clot for 20–30 min at room temperature. After centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, serum samples were collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Cytokine levels were measured using the Cytokine and Chemokine 36-Plex Mouse ProcartaPlex Panel 1a (EPX360-26092-901, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) array. Thirty-six cytokines were simultaneously analyzed, including IFN-γ, interleukin (IL) series, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and chemokines. Briefly, magnetic beads were prepared and washed with assay buffer. Standards were reconstituted and serially diluted (4-fold, with 8 concentrations). Samples (25 µL for serum, diluted tissue homogenates) and standards were incubated with the beads in a 96-well plate at room temperature for 30 min with shaking (500 rpm), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. After washing, detection antibodies were added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The plate was then washed and incubated with Streptavidin-Phycoerythrin (SA-PE) for 30 min. Following final washes, the beads were resuspended in Reading Buffer and analyzed on the Luminex 200 instrument. Cytokine concentrations were calculated using five-parameter nonlinear regression analysis of the standard curves.

Histopathological and immunofluorescence assays

After IP injection of E11 in 3-day-old hFcRnTg mice, the brains of the experimental and control groups were harvested at 48 hpi. The tissues were fixed in formalin buffer for 24 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5-µm-thick sections. H&E staining was performed after deparaffinization of the tissue sections with xylene. Finally, all images were scanned using a PANNORAMIC DESK/MIDI/250/1000 (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) and analyzed using CaseViewer 2.4 (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical tests and generation of graphs was done in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software). The survival rates of different groups of mice were assessed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. The Wilcoxon test was used to analyze the clinical score curves. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Figures were adjusted and assembled by using Adobe Illustrator (Adobe).

Results

Tissue expression of hFcRn in transgenic mice

We initially performed PCR genotyping and identification of hFcRn transgenic mice, confirming the presence of the Fc gamma receptor and transporter (FCGRT) gene, with results indicating a homozygous genotype (Fig. 1A, primer sequences in supplementary Table S2). To investigate hFcRn expression across tissues in FCGRT humanized transgenic mice, we examined the expression levels in various organs from a total of 4 mice, including heart, liver, lung, kidney, spleen, intestine, muscle, spine, eye, and brain at 3, 7, 14, and 42 days of age (Fig. 1B). As illustrated in Fig. 1C, hFcRn (depicted in brown, red arrow) was expressed in all examined tissues across different age groups, with apparent variations in expression levels. Mean optical density analysis revealed distinct expression patterns: in heart, liver, lung, and spine, 3-day-old transgenic mice exhibited the highest mean optical density; brain showed peak expression at 7 days; kidney expression was highest at 14 days; and splenic expression peaked at 42 days of age.

Establishment of disseminated neonatal echovirus 11 infection model

In terms of survival rate (n = 8), body weight (n = 5) and symptom score (n = 5), the control group had no any symptoms throughout the study. For the E11 group, some mice (60%, 3/5) showed milk refusal and weight loss 24 hpi, but did not die; almost all the suckling mice (100%, 5/5) showed weight loss 48 hpi. Some suckling mice (87.5%, 7/8) were paralyzed and died, with the mortality rate of 75% (Fig. 2A). The E11-infected group exhibited a slower trend in body weight gain after viral infection (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the average symptom score in the E11 group increased from approximately 1 at 24 hpi to 3.8 at 48 hpi, accompanied by significant mortality (75%) (Fig. 2C).

To investigate the distribution of the virus in various tissues, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, muscle, intestine, spine, eye, and brain were sampled (n = 3) at 3, 24, 48, and 72 hpi. Quantitative RT-PCR was used to determine the E11 load in each tissue. The results showed that viral nucleic acid could be detected in various tissues and organs 3 hpi; virus copy number increased in muscles (1 × 106 copy/mg) and spine (1 × 104 copy/mg) 24 hpi, however, no significant changes were seen in other tissues; 48 hpi, there was a significant increase of virus copy numbers in all tissues (Fig. 2D), including heart (1 × 105 copy/mg), liver (1 × 104 copy/mg), spleen (1 × 105 copy/mg), lung (1 × 105 copy/mg), kidney (1 × 105 copy/mg), muscle (1 × 107 copy/mg), intestine (1 × 103 copy/mg), spine (1 × 106 copy/mg), and eye (1 × 104 copy/mg). Subsequent TCID50 assays of critical life-sustaining organs (n = 4) at 48 hpi revealed infectious E11 viral particles, with different viral titers in heart (1 × 107 TCID50/g), liver (1 × 106 TCID50/g), lung (1 × 105 TCID50/g), kidney (1 × 105 TCID50/g) and brain (1 × 105 TCID50/g) (Fig. 2E).

Serum biochemical analysis unveiled significant alterations in the E11-challenged group compared to controls. Specifically, the E11 group exhibited markedly elevated levels of serum ALT (24.63 ± 2.83 IU/L), AST (100.48 ± 24.27 IU/L), CK-MB (28.17 ± 2.65 ng/mL), FDP (279.68 ± 14.98 ng/mL), serum Cr (373.61 ± 11.67 µmol/L), and urinary Cr (384.78 ± 13.35 µmol/L), all statistically significant (P < 0.05) compared to the control group (Fig. 2F-K).

RNAscope-based detection of E11 viral RNA in different organs post-infection

We employed RNAscope to detect and score E11 viral presence in heart, liver, lung, kidney, and brain tissues at 48 h post-infection. Results demonstrated E11 RNA detection across multiple organs (red signal points indicated by red arrows in Fig. 3A-P). Notably, cardiac and hepatic tissues exhibited the highest red signal density (Fig. 3C-F). Within renal tissue, medullary regions displayed higher signal intensity compared to cortical zones (Fig. 3I-L). Viral RNA was prominently detected in meningeal regions, whereas brain parenchyma showed no discernible red signals (Fig. 3M-P). To quantify staining patterns, we utilized the RNAscope semi-quantitative scoring system (supplementary Table S3). Scoring revealed: heart and liver achieved maximum scores of 4, followed by renal medulla (3), renal cortex (2), lung (2), meninges (1), and brain parenchyma (0) (Fig. 3Q). These findings comprehensively characterize the differential viral RNA distribution across critical organ systems in our E11-induced sepsis model.

Serum cytokine profiling in disseminated echovirus 11 infection

Serum was collected to detect the secretion levels of 36 cytokines at 48 hpi (n = 4 per group). The results indicated that the levels of 10 cytokines, including monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) (38-fold, reaching as high as 2590 pg/mL), interleukin-18 (IL-18) (41-fold), monocyte chemoattractant protein-3 (MCP-3) (4-fold), interferon gamma-inducible protein 10 (IP-10) (4-fold), interleukin-22 (IL-22) (47-fold), TNF-α (11-fold), recombinant regulated on activation in normal T-cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) (114-fold), interleukin-28 (IL-28) (5-fold), interferon gamma (IFN-γ) (9-fold), and macrophage inflammatory protein-1 beta (MIP-1β) (30-fold), were significantly upregulated compared to the control group (Fig. 4) (P < 0.05). MCP-1 and TNF-α, the core factors of cytokine storm, were significantly increased, suggesting the appearance of cell storm. In addition, there are 9 other cytokines, including interleukin-9 (IL-9), interleukin 27 (IL-27), interleukin 13 (IL-13), interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), Interleukin 12 p70 (IL-12p70), interleukin 15/15 receptor (IL-15/15R), interleukin 31 (IL-31), and macrophage inflammatory protein 2 (MIP-2), which exhibit approximately 1–10-fold changes, but their overall levels remain low.

Pathological tissue and organ damage

Histopathological examination was conducted on both the E11 and control groups at 48 hpi. The E11 group exhibited pathological changes across multiple organ systems (Fig. 5). Cardiac tissue revealed disorganized cardiomyocyte architecture (Fig. 5A-B). Liver sections demonstrated focal hepatocellular necrosis, obliteration of sinusoidal spaces, and inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. 5C-D). Pulmonary examination showed destroyed alveolar septa, fused alveoli, and interstitial thickening (Fig. 5E-F). Renal tissue displayed epithelial cell loss in tubules, structural deterioration, and tubular fusion in certain areas (Fig. 5G-H). Splenic samples lacked germinal centers entirely (Fig. 5I-J). Skeletal muscle exhibited fragmented and disorganized fibers accompanied by inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5M-N). Inflammatory cell infiltration was also observed in the ocular tissue (Fig. 5Q-R). Notably, intestinal tissue and spinal sections remained histologically normal (Fig. 5K-L, O-P).

Discussion

E11 infection in neonates can quickly progress to disseminated infection and, in severe cases, sepsis. This often results in multi-organ hemorrhage and disseminated intravascular coagulation, leading to shock or even death in infants, and poses a serious threat to pediatric health [8, 25, 26]. However, the precise mechanisms driving E11-induced neonatal disseminated infection remain poorly understood. Key contributing factors include imbalances between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses, dysfunction of the coagulation-fibrinolytic system, and immune dysregulation [27, 28]. Although the prevalence of E11 infection is rising and has become a significant public health concern, further progress is hindered by the lack of appropriate animal models. This has limited investigations into the pathogenesis of E11, as well as the development of effective therapeutics and vaccines [29].

In this study, we successfully established a neonatal enteroviral disseminated infection model using E11-infected hFcRnTg mice, which exhibited significant infection characteristics within 48 hpi. The infected mice displayed typical clinical manifestations, such as weight loss, reduced activity, limb paralysis, and a high mortality rate (> 80%). Histopathological analysis revealed hallmark features of multiple organ injury, including focal hepatocellular necrosis, loss of hepatic sinusoids, inflammatory cell infiltration, destruction and fusion of alveolar septa, marked thickening of pulmonary interstitium, as well as detachment and degeneration of renal tubular epithelial cells, with partial fusion of tubular structures, along with inflammatory cell infiltration in the brain and ocular tissue. Moreover, viral RNA was directly detected in multiple tissues, including the heart and liver, with its titers showing dynamic temporal changes. Biochemical assessments further confirmed the presence of multiple organ dysfunction. These included abnormalities in coagulation function (FDP), liver function (ALT and AST), kidney function (serum and urinary Cr), and cardiac function (CK-MB). In conclusion, the E11-infected hFcRnTg mouse model successfully reproduced the major clinical features and pathological processes observed in neonatal enteroviral disseminated infection. This model provides an ideal experimental platform for studying viral pathogenesis and facilitates further exploration of potential therapeutic strategies.

In the field of E11 infection research, existing models offer valuable insights; however, their diversity and differing conditions highlight the need to establish a more representative experimental platform (Table 1). For example, Morosky et al. developed an E11 infection model by orally infecting 7-day-old hFcRnTg mice with 106 PFU of E11. At 7 days post-infection (dpi), they observed significantly higher E11 titers in the blood and liver of hFcRnTg mice compared to wild-type (WT) mice [24]. Different with Morosky et al., we developed a neonatal sepsis model by intraperitoneally injecting 3-day-old hFcRnTg mice with E11. This model demonstrated higher viral loads and more severe multiorgan damage, providing robust evidence of a neonatal sepsis model. Furthermore, Wells et al. established an another E11 infection model by using 5 to 6-week-old hFcRnTg32-IFNAR−/− mice. They intraperitoneally infected WT, hFcRnTg32, and hFcRnTg32-IFNAR−/− mice with 104 PFU of E11 and assessed viral titers at 72 hpi. Their results revealed that only hFcRnTg32-IFNAR−/− mice showed moderate to high viral titers across multiple organs, along with a systemic immune response and liver damage. This suggests that knocking out the type I interferon receptor (IFNAR−/−) significantly increases the susceptibility of hFcRnTg mice to E11 infection [30]. Possibly due to differences in the age of the mice, we observed that 3-day-old hFcRnTg mice infected with E11 exhibited high viral titers in multiple tissues within 72 hpi, accompanied by significant multiorgan dysfunction. A study by Khetsurian et al. on the surveillance of EV in the United States indicated that neonates are at a higher risk of death compared to individuals aged ≥ 1 month (11.5% versus 2.5%) [1]. Additionally, differences in dosage may also play a role, the dosage in this study was approximately 70 times higher than that employed by Wells et al. Moreover, the method of administration differed significantly, this study utilized IP injection, whereas Morosky S et al. employed oral infection. Su et al. demonstrated that intestinal epithelial cells induce the production of interferon λ (IFN-λ) via the Toll-like receptor 3/interferon regulatory factor 1 (TLR3/IRF1) signaling pathway following EV infection, thereby initiating an antiviral response [31]. Finally, differences in virus strains may also contribute to these observations. The strains used by Morosky et al. and Wells et al. were the E11 prototype strain (Gregory strain), isolated from asymptomatic carriers in the United States. In contrast, the E11 clinical isolates in this study were obtained from neonatal mortality cases and are phylogenetically related to the isolates from AFP cases in Shandong Province, China [32]. Guoyan Zhang et al. successfully established an E11-related brain injury model by intracranially injecting 2-day-old IFNAR−/− mice with a dose of 107 TCID50 E11, and all mice succumbing to the infection by 5 dpi [33]. However, in neonatal E11 infections, most clinical cases exhibit normal immune function. Thus, immunodeficient mouse models may not adequately reflect the clinical progression of EV infections, potentially limiting their use in evaluating therapeutic agents and investigating mechanisms of pathogenesis [22, 34]. Therefore, the E11 disseminated infection model developed in this study serves as a more representative framework for advancing research on drug and vaccine development against neonatal E11 infections.

In severe viral infections, especially those involving multiple organ dysfunction, the release of various inflammatory mediators and cytokines can trigger an uncontrolled cascade-often termed a “cytokine storm”-characterized by their excessive and widespread release [35–37]. Takahama et al. found that the combined effects of TNF with IL-18, IFN-γ, or IL-1β are sufficient to replicate the tissue-specific impacts of sepsis and to explain the majority of the organism-wide inflammatory response to sepsis [38]. Additionally, previous studies have confirmed that TNF combined with IFN-γ or IL-1β exacerbates the severity of sepsis and other inflammatory disorders in vivo [39–41]. Our study also observed significantly elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-18, and IL-1β, which were closely associated with inflammation-induced tissue damage. Existing studies highlight the pivotal role of MCP-1 and TNF-α in cytokine storms, with evidence from multiple viral infections, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, herpes simplex virus, and influenza virus, showing that pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, particularly MCP-1 and TNF-α, are markedly elevated and strongly associated with disease severity [42–46]. In this E11 disseminated infection model, we observed significant increases in MCP-1 and TNF-α, with MCP-1 serum levels reaching up to 2500 pg/mL. These finding suggests that cytokine storm plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of E11 neonatal infection. In summary, the increased levels of MCP-1, IL-18, IL-22, TNF-α, RANTES, and MIP-1β in the E11 mouse model, together with the cytokine storm observed in the mice, closely mimic the excessive inflammatory response observed in human enteroviral disseminated infection.

This study has several limitations. First, the technical challenges associated with real-time dynamic observations of the infection process hinder our ability to visualize infection mechanisms. Additionally, while further evaluation of drug efficacy is necessary, the rapid progression of the disease presents significant obstacles. To address these limitations, we will employ in vivo imaging techniques and investigate alternative experimental approaches to deepen our understanding of infection dynamics and drug responses in future research.

Conclusion

Our study successfully established a neonatal E11 disseminated infection model in 3-day-old hFcRnTg mice. We observed high mortality, multiple organ injuries, disseminated infections, histopathological damage, and a cytokine storm following the E11 challenge. These findings confirm the reliability of our E11 mouse model in replicating neonatal infection conditions and underscore its value as a vital tool for investigating infection mechanisms. Moreover, the model provides a valuable platform for researching E11-related vaccines and assessing the efficacy of potential therapies.
