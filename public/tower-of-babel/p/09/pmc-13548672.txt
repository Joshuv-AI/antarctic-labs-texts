Glycosylation-related gene risk model and functional validation of OSTC and TUBA1C in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xinyu Zhang, Zhengjie Zhou, Xiaofu Lian, Shuo Ma, Huili Chen, Feiyue Zhang, Qi Zhang, Xiangyi Zhang, Shengping Min, Chaoqun Lian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOSTC and TUBA1C drive the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) through the modulation of N-glycosylation and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway; Silencing these two genes markedly suppresses the malignant phenotypes of tumor cells, thereby identifying them as promising novel candidate targets for LUAD diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.MethodsThis study integrated multi-omics data from TCGA and GEO, constructed a risk model (GPRS) for LUAD using 10 machine learning algorithms based on a glycosylation gene set (GRGs). Prognosis was evaluated using Cox regression and survival plots, and analyses of gene function, the microenvironment, and drug sensitivity were conducted. Gene distribution was characterized using single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, and in vitro experiments validated the effects of OSTC and TUBA1C on the function of LUAD cells.ResultsThis study screened 134 GRGs to construct an 18-gene risk model, which showed high predictive precision in the TCGA-LUAD dataset (1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs ranging from 0.966 to 0.982). The high-risk group displayed significant enrichment of the PI3K/AKT pathway, had a low immune score, and was sensitive to EGFR inhibitors; Bcl-2 inhibitors showed significant efficacy in the low-risk group. Single-cell profiling revealed high expression of OSTC and TUBA1C in the course of tumor progression. In vitro experiments confirmed that the knockdown of these two genes impairs LUAD cell proliferation and migration and triggers apoptosis, a mechanism correlated with decreased PI3K/AKT phosphorylation levels.ConclusionThis study identifies OSTC and TUBA1C as glycosylation-related oncogenes that drive LUAD progression via the PI3K/AKT pathway. The GPRS, derived from a multi-omics machine learning pipeline, serves as a discovery tool that captures the prognostic significance of these genes and reflects LUAD metabolic features, immune status, and drug responsiveness. Together, these findings provide mechanistic insights and candidate targets for precision therapy in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03817-w.

Introduction

In 2024, there were nearly 2.5 million new cases of lung cancer worldwide, with over 1.8 million deaths. This accounts for nearly one-eighth (12.4%) of all new cancer cases globally and one-fifth (18.7%) of all cancer deaths, making it the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality. LUAD is the most common histological subtype of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 40%–50% of all lung cancers worldwide, and also constitutes one of the primary contributors to cancer-associated deaths across the globe [1–3]. It is characterized by a rising proportion of cases among women and non-smokers, insidious early symptoms, a high tendency for distant metastases (including brain metastases), a rich spectrum of molecular driver mutations (including classic targets such as EGFR and ALK, as well as rare targets such as MET and RET), and high tumor heterogeneity [4]. Lung cancer treatment has evolved into a comprehensive, multi-modal approach, with precision therapy and immunotherapy serving as core breakthroughs. However, issues such as drug resistance, delayed screening, and challenges in personalized treatment adaptation continue to hinder improvements in overall treatment efficacy [5–8]. Hence, an urgent need exists to uncover novel molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets that enable the design of personalized treatment strategies for LUAD patients.

Protein glycosylation—an essential post-translational modification in proteins—exhibits a close association with the abnormal modulation of tumorigenesis and malignant progression, and has become a central research area within oncology research [9]. N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation, as the primary types, play multiple roles in the pathological progression of tumors by altering protein structure and function [9–12]. Aberrant O-glycosylation structures have been identified in breast cancer and other tumor types, where they modulate mucin molecule function to alter the adhesive and invasive properties of tumor cells [13, 14]. Disruptions in N-glycosylation are even more prevalent; They not only participate in modulating the proliferation and survival of tumor cells but also exert a pivotal role in metastatic processes, such as hepatic metastasis in colorectal carcinoma. For example, N-glycosylation modifications of CTSD can directly influence tumor metastatic potential [15–17]. Abnormal glycosylation drives tumor progression through multiple mechanisms: on the one hand, it regulates the TGF-β signaling pathway and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process, promoting tumor cell motility and maintaining stemness [18–20]; On the other hand, it reshapes the tumor microenvironment (TME), influencing immune recognition and angiogenesis, thereby providing favorable conditions for tumor growth [21, 22]. These abnormal glycosylation features are not only important drivers of tumorigenesis but are also closely associated with clinical prognosis; Some glycosylation molecules have become potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targetts [13, 17]. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of LUAD-associated glycosylation is of great significance for elucidating the pathological essence of LUAD and developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, providing new research insights and directions for precision medicine in LUAD.

N-glycosylation, an essential post-translational modification in eukaryotic organisms, modulates protein folding, subcellular localization and biological function; Its core enzyme is the oligosaccharide transferase (OST) [15]. OSTC protein is an important subunit of the OST complex, providing structural support for efficient glycosylation. The regulatory role of OSTC in glycosylation has been confirmed by multiple studies: proteomic analysis first identified OSTC protein as a novel subunit of the OST complex, involved in the formation of subcomplexes containing components such as Sect. 61 [23]; OSTC expression is upregulated under heat shock conditions, suggesting its role in glycosylation regulation during stress states [24]. Structural biology investigations have further elucidated that OSTC protein furnishes the structural basis for the coupling of protein trafficking and N-glycosylation by mediating the crosstalk between the OST complex and endoplasmic reticulum transport channels, thereby ensuring the accurate and efficient implementation of N-glycosylation modification [25–27]. Recent studies have found that OSTC abnormalities are closely associated with tumorigenesis. Cellular transformation is often accompanied by glycosylation dysregulation, and OSTC participates in tumor progression by regulating glycosylation: in liver cancer research, miR-1307 can disrupt glycosylation balance and promote liver cancer development by targeting the CALR-OSTC-endoplasmic reticulum protein folding axis, confirming OSTC’s pivotal role in tumor-associated glycosylation regulation [28]. Furthermore, OSTC protein can indirectly influence cellular malignant transformation by regulating pathways such as γ-secretase-mediated protein processing, suggesting its multifaceted role in tumorigenesis [29]. In summary, OSTC protein, as a core subunit of OST, maintains N-glycosylation homeostasis. Functional dysregulation of OSTC may indirectly engage in the modulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by altering the glycosylation modifications of pathway-associated proteins, thereby influencing the progression of LUAD; However, this association requires direct experimental validation.

As a core subtype of the α-tubulin family, TUBA1C protein is a key component of the microtubules that constitute the cytoskeleton [30]. It is involved in the regulation of essential physiological processes—including cell division, material transport, and morphological maintenance—and the aberrations in its function are closely associated with the occurrence and development of tumors. The oncogenic function of TUBA1C has been validated by a multitude of studies: in low-grade gliomas, elevated TUBA1C expression correlates with tumor immune cell infiltration and acts as an independent risk factor that influences patient prognosis [31]; In research on LUAD, increased TUBA1C expression facilitates tumor cell proliferation and metastasis through the regulation of the cell cycle, p53 signaling pathways, and glycolytic metabolism, with a significant association with adverse patient prognosis [32]. Subsequent mechanistic research has revealed that TUBA1C protein mediates the expression of critical cell cycle proteins—such as cyclins and CDKs—thereby establishing the molecular basis underlying the malignant proliferation of tumor cells [33, 34]. Emerging research indicates that aberrations in TUBA1C are closely correlated with the malignant progression of a variety of tumors: in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, high TUBA1C expression promotes tumor invasion by activating cell cycle signaling pathways [34]; In renal clear cell carcinoma, it can establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment via the PI3K/AKT pathway, mediating resistance to immunotherapy [30]. Furthermore, TUBA1C protein can indirectly promote malignant transformation through pathways such as aerobic glycolysis [35]. As a core constituent of microtubular structures, TUBA1C protein participates in the regulation of the cell cycle and maintains the dynamic balance of the immune microenvironment. Its functional abnormalities may indirectly participate in the regulation of N-glycosylation and the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway by altering the transport and localization of glycosylation-related proteins, thereby influencing the progression of LUAD [32, 36]; However, this association lacks direct experimental validation in LUAD.

To address these gaps, we integrated multi‑omics data (TCGA, GEO) with single‑cell and spatial transcriptomics, combined with machine learning, to identify GRGs in LUAD. We constructed a GPRS and discovered OSTC and TUBA1C as core oncogenes. Through in vitro functional assays, we demonstrate that OSTC and TUBA1C drive LUAD progression via the PI3K/AKT pathway. Our findings provide a mechanistic basis for targeting these genes and offer a risk stratification tool for LUAD patients. The detailed workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Methods

Data collection and processing

Transcriptomic (RNA-seq), clinical, and copy number variation (CNV) data for patients with LUAD were obtained from the TCGA public data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Following data filtering and cleaning, a final dataset comprising 500 LUAD patients with complete survival data was acquired. Four datasets were retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE31210 (n = 226), GSE50081 (n = 127), GSE72094 (n = 398), and the single-cell dataset GSE189357. The spatial transcriptomics data E-MTAB-13,530 (sample: P16-T1) was collected from the BioStudies Database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk). GRGs were derived from the REACTOME_ASPARAGINE_N_LINKED_GLYCOSYLATION, REACTOME_O_LINKED_GLYCOSYLATION, and REACTOME_O_LINKED_GLYCOSYLATION_OF_MUCINS. The immunotherapy-related datasets GSE78220 (melanoma) and GSE126044 (non-small cell lung cancer) were obtained from the GEO database. RNA-seq data for the 24 tumor types in the pan-cancer analysis were also obtained from TCGA. Data normalization was performed using the ‘DESeq2’ package (v1.46.0). Transcriptomic data from TCGA-LUAD samples were first transformed to log2(TPM + 1) for cross-sample comparability. To mitigate potential technical batch effects arising from differences in sequencing plates and tissue source sites, we employed the ‘ComBat’ function from the ‘sva’ package (v3.54.0). The batch variables were defined as the Plate and TSS (Tissue Source Site) identifiers, extracted from TCGA sample barcodes. Biological covariates of interest were included in the model matrix to preserve genuine biological variation. After correction, the batch-adjusted data were used for all downstream analyses. Differential expression analysis was performed utilizing the R package ‘limma’ (v3.62.2), with the screening criteria for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2-fold change| > 1.

PPI and gene mutation analysis

A PPI network was constructed based on 134 GRGs using the STRING database (confidence level ≥ 0.9) and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.3) according to node degree to highlight core hubs. To explore the somatic mutation lineages in the LUAD patient cohort, analysis was performed using the ‘maftools’ package (v2.22.0).

Signature generated from machine learning-based integrative approaches

A comprehensive ensemble of 101 prognostic models was developed using ten machine learning algorithms: Random Survival Forest (RSF), Elastic Net (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, Partial Least Squares Regression (plsRcox), Supervised Principal Component Analysis (SuperPC), Generalized Boosted Regression Model (GBM), and Survival Support Vector Machine (survival-SVM). Models were internally trained using the TCGA-LUAD dataset. Independent external validation was performed across three GEO cohorts: GSE31210, GSE50081, and GSE72094. Following comparative performance evaluation, the RSF algorithm was ultimately selected as the optimal approach for constructing the final prognostic model.

Nomogram construction and validation based on the GPRS

A nomogram was established to visualize the GPRS signature, with coefficients derived from the final RSF model. Calibration curves evaluated agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years. Discrimination was assessed using the concordance index and time-dependent ROC. Internal validation employed bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to mitigate overfitting. External validation was performed across three independent cohorts (GSE31210, GSE50081, GSE72094). Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to quantify the net benefit of the nomogram across a range of threshold probabilities, thereby assessing its clinical applicability.

Functional enrichment analysis

For the screened GRGs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses utilizing the R packages ‘clusterProfiler’ (v4.14.6) and ‘org.Hs.eg.db’ (v3.20.0). These analyses were performed to characterize the biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and potential signaling pathways linked to glycosylation modifications. Additionally, GSVA was carried out with the R package ‘GSVA’ (v2.0.7), using the reference gene set h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MsigDB). GSEA was further applied to identify differential pathway activity between the two subgroups, with a p-value < 0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance.

TME analysis and drug sensitivity prediction

Expression profiles of the model genes and infiltration levels of diverse immune cell subsets were acquired and quantified by integrating multiple bioinformatic algorithms, namely TIMER, EPIC, MCP-COUNTER, ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT. Moreover, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm and the ‘oncoPredict’ (v1.2) computational tool were utilized to investigate the correlations between GRGs and therapeutic responses to both immunotherapy and targeted therapy in LUAD.

Single-cell data analysis

Preprocessing was conducted using ‘Seurat’ package (v4.3.0.1). Cells were filtered based on gene detection rates and mitochondrial content. ‘SCTransform’ was applied for normalization and variance stabilization. After principal component analysis, graph-based ‘Louvain’ clustering partitioned cells into subpopulations, visualized via ‘UMAP’. ‘DoubletFinder’ (v2.0.4) removed potential doublets. Cell identities were assigned through marker gene overlap combined with reference-based label transfer using ‘SingleR’ (v2.2.0). For multi-sample integration, ‘harmony’ (v1.1.0) aligned low-dimensional embeddings. Differentially expressed was assessed via non‑parametric tests. Functional enrichment was evaluated using hypergeometric testing. Cell–cell communication was inferred with ‘CellChat’ (v1.6.1).

Spatial transcriptomics data analysis

Data preprocessing and quality control were performed using ‘Seurat’ package (v4.3.0.1), filtering spots based on UMI counts and mitochondrial content. Normalization accounted for sequencing depth variations via ‘SCTransform’. To ensure spatially coherent clusters, dimensionality reduction incorporated spatial coordinates as a constraint, followed by graph-based clustering with ‘BayesSpace’ (v1.20.0) to delineate molecular domains. Cellular composition was deconvoluted using ‘Cell2location’ (v0.6a0), mapping single-cell references to each spot. Niche interactions were assessed by ligand-receptor colocalization within a defined physical distance. Finally, spatial trajectories along tissue axes were inferred with ‘SPATA’ (v3.1.4) to characterize transcriptional gradients associated with structural transitions.

Cell culture and transfection

The present study utilized human normal lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) and LUAD cell lines (A549, H1299), all sourced from Procell System (Wuhan, China). Culture conditions involved DMEM (BEAS-2B and A549 cells) and RPMI 1640 (H1299 cells) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), each enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic mixture (penicillin-streptomycin-gentamicin, Biosharp). Incubation was consistently performed at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. For gene silencing, siRNAs targeting OSTC and TUBA1C, alongside a non-targeting negative control, were acquired from GenePharma (Shanghai, China); Their corresponding sequences are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Transient transfections in A549 and H1299 cells were mediated by Lipofectamine 2000, with the medium being replaced by complete medium after 6–8 h to prepare cells for further analysis.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, AG21017), and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Real-time quantitative PCR was subsequently carried out with the SYBR Green Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Kit (Vazyme, China) using the cDNA as template. Primers were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein samples were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking with rapid blocking solution for 30 min, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by the corresponding secondary antibody. Chemiluminescent signals were visualized using an ECL substrate kit (Biosharp, Beijing). All antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

CCK-8 assay

A549 and H1299 LUAD cells were subjected to transfection with siRNA for 24 h. Following transfection, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. Cell proliferation was evaluated at 4, 24, 48, and 72 h by adding 10% CCK-8 reagent (Bio-sharp, Hefei, China) to each well, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Transwell assay

Transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pore size were placed in 24-well plates. LUAD cells (A549 and H1299) transfected with siRNA were seeded at 4 × 10⁴ cells per well. For migration assays, 200 µL of serum-free cell suspension was loaded into the upper chamber and 800 µL of medium containing serum was added to the lower chamber. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was first precoated with 50 µL of matrix gel diluted in serum-free medium (1:9) and incubated at 37 °C for 40 min, followed by the same cell suspension and medium addition as in migration assays. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-migrated or non-invaded cells were removed by washing twice with PBS. Chambers were then fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 15–20 min, rinsed with pure water, and stained with crystal violet solution for 20–25 min. After another rinse with pure water, chambers were air-dried and imaged under a light microscope at 200× magnification.

Wound healing experiment

A549 and H1299 LUAD cells were plated in 6-well plates and transfected with small interfering RNA. After reaching confluency, a scratch was created in the center of each well using a 200 µL pipette tip, and cells were subsequently cultured in serum-free basal medium. Wound closure was monitored and images were captured under a microscope at 0 and 24 h.

Colony formation assay

A549 and H1299 LUAD cells were first transfected with small interfering RNA, then plated into 6-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. After 10 days of culture in a cell incubator, the medium was removed, and colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, air-dried, and photographed.

Flow cytometry

A549 and H1299 LUAD cells were plated in 6-well plates and transfected 24 h post-attachment. For apoptosis analysis, cells were harvested using trypsin without EDTA, rinsed with pre-chilled PBS, and resuspended in 100 µL of 1× binding buffer. The cell suspension was then incubated with 5 µL each of Annexin V-FITC and PI for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by addition of 400 µL of 1× binding buffer. Stained cells were analyzed on a FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

For cell cycle analysis, transfected LUAD cells were harvested with trypsin 24 h post-transfection, and digestion was terminated with complete medium. Cells were pelleted, washed with pre-chilled PBS, and gently resuspended in 1 mL of pre-chilled 70% ethanol for fixation at 4 °C for 12–24 h. Following fixation, ethanol was removed and cells were washed again with pre-chilled PBS. The cell pellets were then suspended in 0.5 mL of propidium iodide staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai) and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Flow cytometric analysis was performed as described above using the same instrument and software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.4.3) and GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2). Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon test, t-test, or two-way ANOVA, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Statistical significance was defined as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Result

Screening for GRGs in LUAD

Differential expression analysis comparing tumor and normal tissues in the TCGA-LUAD cohort yielded 5,186 DEGs (Figs. 2A, B). By intersecting these genes with 418 genes associated with glycosylation, we identified 134 GRGs associated with LUAD (Fig. 2C). The inset figure illustrates the distribution of these 134 GRGs across N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation modifications (Fig. 2D). PPI network analysis revealed the interaction relationships among these GRGs (Fig. 2E). Mutation analysis identified MUC16 as the most frequently mutated gene, present in 51% of LUAD patients, with missense and frameshift mutations being the predominant types (Fig. 2F). Further enrichment analysis of the 134 GRGs revealed that, according to GO annotations, these genes are primarily involved in protein glycosylation, COPII-coated ER to Golgi transport vesicle, and glycosyltransferase activity; KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in glycosylation-related pathways, including Mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis, N-Glycan biosynthesis, and Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (Figs. 2G, H).

Development of GPRS

To construct the GPRS, we first performed univariate Cox regression analysis on the 134 GRGs, identifying 35 genes significantly associated with LUAD prognosis. Based on these 35 genes, we evaluated 101 combinations of 10 machine learning algorithms by calculating the C-index to determine the optimal modeling strategy. The RSF algorithm achieved the highest average C-index across four independent cohorts (TCGA-LUAD, GSE31210, GSE50081, and GSE72094), with values of 0.938, 0.711, 0.618, and 0.624, respectively (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, 18 key prognostic genes were derived from the RSF algorithm to establish the GPRS (Fig. 3B); these 18 genes and their importance scores are listed in Table 1. The chromosomal distribution of these genes is presented in Supplementary Figure A.

Importance Scores of Genes in the GPRS

Patients were then divided into high‑risk and low‑risk groups using the median risk score as the cutoff. Kaplan‑Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the high‑risk group had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) compared with those in the low‑risk group (Figs. 3C, D; Supplementary Figures B, C). Time‑dependent ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the risk score. The AUC values for predicting 1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year survival were 0.966, 0.982, and 0.968 in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 3E); 0.648, 0.643, and 0.807 in the GSE72094 cohort (Fig. 3F); 0.697, 0.685, and 0.725 in the GSE31210 cohort (Supplementary Figure D); And 0.724, 0.603, and 0.660 in the GSE50081 cohort (Supplementary Figure E).

The risk factor distribution plot indicated that mortality events were more concentrated in the high-risk group, and most model genes were predominantly highly expressed in this group (Figs. 3G, H; Supplementary Figures F, G). To assess the clinical applicability of this risk model, a nomogram was developed by integrating risk stratification with clinical and pathological characteristics (Fig. 3I). Calibration curves revealed strong concordance between the model-predicted and actual observed outcomes (Fig. 3J). DCA results showed that this risk score provided a higher net benefit compared with other clinical features, confirming its favorable clinical utility for predictive purposes (Fig. 3K).

Clinical characteristics of GPRS

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that the GPRS served as an independent prognostic factor for LUAD patients (Figs. 4A, B). Analysis of clinical and pathological characteristics revealed that risk scores differed significantly across T, N, and M stages, as well as overall tumor stage, with a progressive increase observed in parallel with advancing tumor malignancy (Figs. 4C-H). Across four independent datasets, deceased patients exhibited significantly higher risk scores than surviving patients (Figs. 4I-L). Sankey diagrams visually illustrated the associations between risk groups, tumor stage, and survival status. The high‑risk group contained a higher proportion of patients with advanced‑stage (Stage III-IV) disease compared with the low‑risk group, and among deceased patients, the proportion with advanced‑stage disease was also higher than that with early‑stage disease (Fig. 4M).

Functional and immune characteristics of GPRS

To explore functional differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups, we conducted GSVA and GSEA enrichment analyses. GSVA revealed significant enrichment of glycolysis, the mTORC1 signaling pathway, the unfolded protein response, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, the TGF-β signaling pathway, and the Notch signaling pathway in the high-risk group (Fig. 5A). GSEA corroborated these findings, demonstrating significant enrichment of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, the G2M checkpoint, glycolysis, EMT, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, and apoptosis in the same group (Figs. 5B-G).

To explore differences in the immune microenvironment between the two risk groups, we applied the ESTIMATE algorithm to calculate Immune Score, Stromal Score, and ESTIMATE Score. All three scores were significantly reduced in the high-risk group compared with the low-risk group (Figs. 5H-J). We then employed four algorithms—MCP-COUNTER, EPIC, CIBERSORT, and TIMER—to evaluate correlations between immune cell infiltration abundance and both risk scores and the 18 model genes. Correlation analysis revealed that infiltration levels of most immune cells were negatively associated with risk scores. Notably, CIBERSORT identified positive correlations between risk scores and several immune cell types, including resting memory CD4⁺ T cells, resting NK cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and resting mast cells. Additionally, immune cell infiltration levels were negatively correlated with the expression of most model genes, whereas RENBP, TUBB6, and GRIA1 expression showed positive correlations with infiltration of most immune cells (Fig. 5K).

We next examined expression differences of 23 immune checkpoint and immune regulatory genes between the two risk groups. Most of these genes were expressed at higher levels in the low-risk group, whereas CD276 and TNFRSF9 showed elevated expression in the high-risk group (Fig. 5L). Correlation analysis indicated that most immune checkpoint and immune regulatory genes were negatively correlated with the risk score, in contrast to CD276 and TNFRSF9, which exhibited positive correlations. Additionally, expression of most GRGs was negatively correlated with these immune-related genes, whereas RENBP, TUBB6, and GRIA1 showed positive correlations (Fig. 6A).

Immuno-therapeutic cohort analysis and drug sensitivity prediction using GPRS

To assess the predictive value of this risk model for immunotherapy response, we performed validation analyses in two immunotherapy cohorts: GSE78220 (melanoma) and GSE126044 (non-small cell lung cancer). In both cohorts, non-responders to immunotherapy had significantly higher risk scores than responders (Figs. 6B, E), indicating that high risk scores are associated with intrinsic resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. In the GSE78220 cohort, responders accounted for 76.47% of patients in the low-risk group, compared with only 11.11% in the high-risk group (88.89% non-responders) (Fig. 6C). Similarly, in the GSE126044 cohort, the low-risk group achieved a 100% response rate, whereas the high-risk group had a 91.67% non-response rate (Fig. 6F). Further survival analysis demonstrated that across both treatment cohorts, OS was markedly worse in the high-risk group relative to the low-risk group (Figs. 6D, G), confirming that this risk score not only distinguishes immunotherapy responders but also exhibits prognostic value in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer.

Immune evasion potential was assessed using the TIDE algorithm across three independent datasets (TCGA-LUAD, GSE72094, and GSE31210). In the high-risk group, TIDE scores and Exclusion scores were significantly elevated compared with the low-risk group, whereas Dysfunction scores were markedly reduced (Figs. 6H-M; Supplementary Figures H-M). Correlation analysis between the 18 GRGs and TIDE scores revealed that most genes were positively correlated with TIDE scores, with the exception of RENBP, GRIA1, and B3GNT8, which showed negative correlations (Fig. 6N).

Drug sensitivity analysis revealed that the risk score was positively associated with the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) for most drugs, whereas negative associations were observed for a subset of agents, primarily including EGFR-targeted inhibitors (Afatinib, Erlotinib, Gefitinib, Osimertinib, AZD3759, Sapitinib), microtubule-targeting chemotherapeutics (Docetaxel, Paclitaxel, Vinblastine, Vinorelbine), and MAPK pathway inhibitors (SCH772984, ERK_6604, VX-11e). Additionally, most GRGs showed positive correlations with IC₅₀ values for the majority of drugs (Fig. 6O). When comparing drug sensitivity between risk groups, the high-risk group exhibited significantly lower IC₅₀ values for the aforementioned EGFR-targeted drugs, microtubule-targeting agents, and MAPK pathway inhibitors (Figs. 7A-M). In contrast, the low-risk group showed greater sensitivity to Bcl-2 family-targeted drugs (Venetoclax, Navitoclax, ABT737, Sabutoclax), Wnt/GSK3 pathway inhibitors (Wnt-C59, LJI308, SB216763, GSK269962A), and PARP inhibitors (Talazoparib, Olaparib, Niraparib, PFI3) (Figs. 7N-Y). The specific IC₅₀ values and p-values are shown in Table 2.

Analysis of cell-to-cell communication

To investigate the regulatory role of glycosylation modifications on intercellular communication in the tumor microenvironment, this study divided all cells into low- and high-glycosylation groups based on the median glycosylation score. Results of cell communication network analysis (Figs. 9A-D) showed that, compared with the low-glycosylation group, both the frequency and intensity of cell-to-cell communication among cancer cells were significantly increased in the high-glycosylation group.

Analysis of differential signaling scatter plots (Figs. 9E, F) indicated significant differences in signaling pathway communication patterns between the two groups. In T cells, the MK pathway was unique to the high-glycosylation group and was characterized primarily by enhanced signal reception; in contrast, the LAMININ and RESISTIN pathways were present only in the low-glycosylation group and were completely absent in the high-glycosylation group. In cancer cells, the APP pathway is unique to the high-glycosylation group, primarily manifested by enhanced signal transmission capacity; simultaneously, both the signal transmission and reception functions of the MK pathway are significantly enhanced in cancer cells of the high-glycosylation group.

Comparative analysis of signaling pathway information flow further confirms that the cellular communication characteristics of the two groups are markedly differentiated. When all cells serve as signal transmitters, the information flow of the LAMININ, JAM, OSM, OCLN, and PTPRM pathways is almost entirely dominated by the low-glycosylation group; conversely, the information flow of the CDH1, CDH, SEMA4, and MK pathways is primarily mediated by the high-glycosylation group (Fig. 9G). Analysis specifically focusing on cancer cells as signal senders revealed that signal transmission in the CDH1, CDH, VEGF, CD99, MK, and APP pathways was almost entirely carried out by cancer cells in the high-glycosylation group, with no significant contribution from the low-glycosylation group (Fig. 9H).

Results from the intergroup difference analysis based on CellChat revealed that, compared to the low-glycosylation group, the probability of communication mediated by multiple ligand-receptor pairs in cancer cells was significantly higher in the high-glycosylation group. These pairs were primarily enriched in pathways related to angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and immune recruitment, including core pathways such as VEGF, MIF, ICAM, CXCL, and APP; only the HLA-DRB5-CD4 and GDF15-TGFBR2 cell communication pairs were significantly downregulated in the high-glycosylation group (Fig. 9I).

Heatmap analysis of outgoing and incoming cell signaling clusters (Figs. 9J, K) revealed that in the high-glycosylation group, the outgoing signaling of MIF and APP (cancer cells → immune cells) was significantly enhanced, while their incoming signaling (immune cells → cancer cells) was simultaneously upregulated, ultimately establishing an abnormal bidirectional communication loop between cancer cells and immune cells. This loop represents the core mechanistic pathway underlying the malignant remodeling of the TME under conditions of high glycosylation.

Expression profiling of the 18 GRGs revealed that TUBA1C, OSTC, and TUBA4A exhibited high expression levels. Among these, TUBA1C and OSTC were primarily expressed in cancer cells and macrophages, whereas TUBA4A was predominantly found in T cells and NK cells (Figs. 10A-F). Furthermore, analysis of expression by stage revealed that OSTC, TUBA1C, and TUBA4A exhibited the highest expression levels in the IAC stage, suggesting that the expression of these three genes may gradually increase with tumor progression (Figs. 10G-I). Additionally, compared with the low glycation group, all three genes showed significantly higher expression in the high glycation score group (Figs. 10M-O). Spatial transcriptomics analysis corroborated these observations, revealing that TUBA1C and OSTC expression was significantly elevated in malignant regions compared with non-malignant regions (Figs. 10P-S).

Patients were stratified into high-expression and low-expression groups according to median expression levels of OSTC and TUBA1C. Those with high expression of either gene exhibited significantly shorter OS than their low-expression counterparts (Figs. 11C, G). Analysis of clinical and pathological characteristics revealed that high OSTC and TUBA1C expression was significantly associated with advanced T, N, and M stages, as well as higher overall tumor stage; Patients with advanced-stage disease were more prevalent in the high-expression groups. To investigate the potential signaling pathways through which these two genes influence the progression of LUAD, KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on DEGs in the high- and low-expression groups of OSTC and TUBA1C, respectively. The results showed that the DEGs were significantly enriched in the cell cycle, apoptosis, p53, and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways (Figs. 11I, J). Correlation analysis indicated a positive correlation between OSTC and TUBA1C expression (Fig. 11K).

To verify the bioinformatics findings, we assessed OSTC and TUBA1C expression in normal lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) and LUAD cell lines (A549 and H1299) using RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Both mRNA and protein levels of OSTC and TUBA1C were markedly elevated in the two LUAD cell lines compared with normal cells (Figs. 11L-N), further corroborating their high-expression profile in LUAD.

Investigating the mechanisms by which OSTC and TUBA1C influence the progression of LUAD

Given the bioinformatics evidence implicating OSTC and TUBA1C in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, we next examined their mechanistic roles using flow cytometry and Western blotting. Discussion

Despite ongoing advances in non-small cell lung cancer treatment, including the clinical success of small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immunotherapy, overall cure and survival rates remain suboptimal. In China, lung cancer continues to rank first in both cancer incidence and mortality [1, 37–40]. Therefore, developing a molecular biomarker model that effectively predicts patient prognosis, characterizes the immune microenvironment, and guides therapeutic selection holds great promise for advancing precision medicine in LUAD.

This study draws upon multiple datasets to comprehensively investigate how GRGs contribute to LUAD progression. In addition to constructing the GPRS with strong predictive performance, we also provided preliminary mechanistic insights into how the key genes OSTC and TUBA1C promote LUAD development. The GPRS demonstrated high predictive accuracy in the TCGA‑LUAD cohort (1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year AUCs: 0.966, 0.982, 0.968). To benchmark our GPRS, we compared it with three previously published LUAD prognostic signatures (PMID: 38644835IF: 3.6 Q1 B4, 39434164, 38949986). In the TCGA-LUAD cohort, our GPRS achieved higher C‑index and superior time‑dependent AUC values (details in Table 3). ROC curve analysis further indicated that this model outperformed traditional clinical and pathological features in terms of clinical net benefit, underscoring its potential clinical utility. When patients were stratified by the median risk score, those in the high-risk group experienced significantly poorer OS compared with the low-risk group. Moreover, multivariate Cox regression analysis established the risk score as an independent prognostic factor.

Pathway enrichment analysis identified significant enrichment of signaling pathways closely associated with glycosylation modifications in the high-risk group. There is a complex interaction between the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and glycosylation: N-glycosylation of EpCAM can regulate EMT in breast cancer cells via the PI3K/AKT pathway [41]; AKT itself undergoes O-GlcNAc modification, which finely regulates its kinase activity by influencing its phosphorylation status and its interaction with PDK1 [42, 43]; mTOR signaling positively regulates OGT protein stability via the c-Myc/HSP90A axis, thereby globally influencing intracellular glycosylation levels [43]; mTORC1 activation is strictly dependent on nutrients such as amino acids and glucose, whereas glucose deprivation can suppress mTORC1’s responsiveness to leucine and downregulate its activity by inducing O-GlcNAc modification of leucyl-tRNA synthetase (LARS1), thereby inhibiting the interaction between LARS1 and the RagD-GTPase [44, 45]; Furthermore, mTORC1 signaling also feedback-regulates the flux of UDP-GlcNAc synthesis by modulating the expression of key enzymes in the Hexosamine Biosynthesis Pathway (HBP), forming a closed-loop metabolic regulatory circuit [46]; These findings suggest that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is not merely a classical kinase cascade but forms a regulatory network through O-GlcNAc glycosylation, jointly sensing nutrient and growth factor signals.

TME analysis revealed that the high‑risk group had reduced immune scores, and infiltration of most immune cells was negatively associated with risk scores, indicating a “cold” tumor microenvironment that compromises immune recognition and elimination of tumor cells [47–52]. Although CIBERSORT showed positive correlations between risk scores and a few immune cell types, this may reflect functional dysregulation: accumulation of M0 macrophages could precede M2 polarization, and M1 macrophages may represent an ineffective inflammatory response that fails to reverse immunosuppression [53–56]. Across independent immunotherapy cohorts (melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer), high risk scores correlated strongly with treatment non‑response and worse OS, supporting the model’s clinical relevance and suggesting that a high-risk score marks intrinsic resistance to anti‑PD‑1/PD‑L1 therapy. TIDE analysis further revealed elevated TIDE and Exclusion scores in the high‑risk group, indicating that immune evasion occurs primarily through T cell exclusion rather than exhaustion, consistent with “cold” tumors [57, 58]. Notably, while some immune checkpoints (PD‑1, CTLA‑4) were highly expressed in the low‑risk group—a pattern of adaptive immune resistance characteristic of “hot” tumors more likely to respond to immunotherapy [59–61] —CD276 was specifically upregulated in the high‑risk group and serves as a key immune evasion molecule that directly suppresses immune responses via myeloid‑derived suppressor cells, independent of conventional T cell infiltration [62, 63].

Drug sensitivity prediction exhibits two distinct patterns. The high-risk group is more sensitive to EGFR-targeted drugs, microtubule-targeting agents, and MAPK pathway inhibitors. Although high-risk tumors are immunologically “cold”, this is not contradictory: “cold” describes the immune microenvironment, while EGFR inhibitors directly target tumor cell-intrinsic pathways. High-risk tumors likely depend on EGFR-RAS-MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling for their aggressive proliferation, rendering them vulnerable to these inhibitors irrespective of immune status. In contrast, the low-risk group shows greater sensitivity to Bcl-2, PARP, and Wnt pathway inhibitors. These tumors reside in an inflammatory microenvironment with sustained immune attack, which drives upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins and DNA repair mechanisms. Thus, Bcl-2 inhibitors (e.g., venetoclax) or PARP inhibitors can synergize with immune cells to eliminate tumors, while Wnt inhibition may further enhance T cell infiltration. These distinct sensitivity profiles directly align with the pathway activities in each risk group. The high-risk group exhibits significant PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation (Fig. 5), which enhances dependency on EGFR-driven proliferation and microtubule dynamics, making these tumors vulnerable to EGFR and microtubule inhibitors [64]. Conversely, the low-risk group shows lower PI3K/AKT activity but may upregulate anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins as a compensatory survival mechanism, explaining their sensitivity to Bcl-2 inhibitors [65].

Through single-cell and spatial transcriptomics analyses, we found that OSTC and TUBA1C are significantly enriched in malignant cancer cells, and their expression levels gradually increase with the progression of LUAD. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that both genes are upregulated in various tumors, with high expression corresponding to poorer prognosis and showing a significant correlation with tumor stage. Additionally, OSTC and TUBA1C expression levels are positively correlated. Importantly, we further confirmed that OSTC and TUBA1C themselves are independent prognostic factors (Supplementary Figure N): OSTC (HR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.28–2.45, P = 0.0006) and TUBA1C (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.09–2.03, P = 0.013). RT-qPCR and Western blot confirmed that both genes were markedly elevated in LUAD cells compared with normal lung epithelial cells. In vitro functional assays have demonstrated that targeting these genes inhibits tumor proliferation and migration by suppressing PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, while also inducing apoptosis.

Regarding the molecular crosstalk between OSTC and TUBA1C, our bioinformatics analysis revealed a modest positive correlation between their expression levels in TCGA-LUAD (Spearman r = 0.51, P < 0.001) and in single-cell data. However, whether they physically interact or function in a linear pathway remains unclear. Given that OSTC is a subunit of the oligosaccharyltransferase complex mediating N-glycosylation in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), while TUBA1C is a cytoskeletal protein, we propose two non-mutually exclusive hypotheses: (i) they may independently but convergently regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway; (ii) OSTC-dependent glycosylation might affect the stability, localization, or post-translational modification of TUBA1C, or conversely, the cytoskeletal network could influence the ER localization of glycosylation machinery. Direct experimental evidence (e.g., co-immunoprecipitation or reciprocal knockdown/rescue) is currently lacking, and we have acknowledged this as a key limitation.

Concerning how aberrant glycosylation directly regulates PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, our data demonstrate that knockdown of OSTC or TUBA1C reduces AKT phosphorylation; however, these data do not prove that the glycosylation event itself is the direct cause. A plausible mechanism is that OSTC-mediated N-glycosylation of receptor tyrosine kinases (e.g., EGFR, IGF1R) is required for their proper trafficking to the plasma membrane and ligand-induced activation; impaired glycosylation would thus dampen PI3K/AKT signaling. Alternatively, O-GlcNAc modification of AKT itself has been reported to modulate its kinase activity and phosphorylation status. Interestingly, our cell-cell communication analysis revealed significantly enhanced APP signaling in the high-glycosylation group. Previous studies have shown that in hepatocellular carcinoma, APP O-glycosylation mediated by the glycosyltransferase GALNT18 promotes tumor progression by activating the EGR1/TGF-β1/Smad signaling axis [66]. Although that pathway differs from PI3K/AKT, these findings raise the possibility that OSTC/TUBA1C might influence LUAD progression by modulating APP expression or its glycosylation status, thereby affecting PI3K/AKT phosphorylation. Alternatively, OSTC/TUBA1C could act upstream of PI3K/AKT to indirectly regulate APP glycosylation. In this study, we did not directly measure glycosylation changes (e.g., via lectin blots or glycosidase treatment), and therefore we have moderated our claims accordingly: our findings indicate that OSTC and TUBA1C are required for full PI3K/AKT activation in LUAD cells, but the precise molecular link between glycosylation and this pathway—including any potential involvement of APP—warrants further investigation.

Despite a relatively comprehensive analysis, this study has several limitations. First, the predictive performance of the risk model varies (with a high AUC value in TCGA but only moderate values in the three GEO cohorts), which may be attributed to platform heterogeneity, differences in staging distribution, or small sample sizes. Second, the analyses of the immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity relied solely on bioinformatics methods and were not experimentally validated. Third, no mechanistic experiments (such as immunoprecipitation) have been conducted to investigate the interaction between OSTC and TUBA1C. Fourth, this study lacks in vivo animal models to validate the in vitro findings. Fifth, off-target effects were not verified through siRNA rescue experiments, and the results were validated in only two cell lines. Sixth, the mechanisms by which OSTC and TUBA1C influence LUAD have currently been studied only at the expression level; further investigation using transcriptomic sequencing or agonists/inhibitors is required; no direct studies on glycosylation modifications (e.g., lectin blotting) have been conducted; and immunohistochemical validation in independent large cohorts is also needed; in addition, perturbation experiments need to be conducted in primary cells or organoids. Seventh, this study is a retrospective analysis using existing cohorts (TCGA, GEO), which may introduce bias. These limitations will be addressed in future studies.

In summary, the GPRS developed here serves as both a promising prognostic tool and a comprehensive metric reflecting LUAD metabolic characteristics, immune status, and therapeutic response. The mechanistic insights into OSTC and TUBA1C further advance our knowledge of glycosylation-mediated regulatory networks in LUAD progression, laying important theoretical groundwork and identifying candidate targets for molecular subtyping and precision therapy optimization in LUAD.
