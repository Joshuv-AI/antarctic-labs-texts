Tracking sources and transformations of dissolved sulfate in karstic sub-basins in Southwest China using dual isotopes and water chemistry
Authors: Jijuan Wang, Zhongfa Zhou, Hui Dong, Shengjun Ding, Xiaoduo Wang, Ye Zhang, Yi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the fragile ecological conditions characteristic of karst terrains, the synergistic effects of soil erosion and anthropogenic activities have significantly intensified sulfate (SO42-) mobilization and dispersion, profoundly influencing aquatic ecosystem stability and regional carbon cycling dynamics. To systematically investigate SO42- geochemical behavior mechanisms in such environments, this study focused on the Chiwu Creek Basin, a representative karst agricultural basin. By integrated water chemistry analysis with multi-isotope tracing techniques (δ³⁴SSO4, δ18OSO4, δD and δ18OH2O) and Bayesian mixing modeling (Simmr) to characterize hydrochemical features and quantitatively apportion SO42-sources and transformation processes. Key findings reveal: Distinct karst hydrochemical signatures (HCO3-Ca·Mg type) with rainfall dilution driving pronounced seasonal SO42-concentration variations; Dominant sulfate contributions from gypsum dissolution (surface water 49.7% > groundwater 28.7%) and soil sulfates (groundwater 33.3% > surface water 28.7%), exhibiting marked wet-season increases that underscore rainfall-erosion coupling in sulfur cycling; Substantial uncertainty (UI90) in natural source contributions (31.3–34.4%) versus more stable anthropogenic inputs (< 12% combined), highlighting natural process amplification in sulfate geochemistry. While anthropogenic sources (sewage/fertilizers/detergents) constitute minor proportions, their environmental significance remains non-negligible. These insights advance fundamental understanding of karst sulfur biogeochemistry while providing critical scientific support for water resource management and carbon sink quantification in Southwest China’s ecologically sensitive karst regions.Keywords: Karst sub-watersheds, Sulfate sources, Sulfur isotopes, Bayesian mixture modeling, Pollution source, Gui zhouSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Sulfate (SO42−) functions as both a pivotal aquatic anion and the principal vector in the global sulfur cycle1–4, exhibiting distinctive geochemical reactivity within karst hydro-systems5,6. The pervasive conduit-matrix architecture of karst terrains enables rapid SO42− exchange between surface runoff and phreatic groundwater6,7, exerting profound controls on hydrochemical evolution and aquatic ecosystem integrity1,8. Elevated SO42−concentrations trigger cascading environmental impacts1: progressive acidification of waters and concomitant mobilization of trace metals9; and2 acceleration of carbonate dissolution, which can shift karst systems from net carbon sinks to net CO2 sources, thereby compromising their sequestration capacity and introducing non-negligible uncertainty into regional carbon budgets10–13. Anthropogenic perturbations have further augmented both the multiplicity of SO42− sources and the magnitude of its ecological repercussions, rendering rigorous source-apportionment analyses increasingly imperative14. Elucidating the provenance and transport pathways of SO42− in karst regions is thus of dual significance: it refines the theoretical framework of karst biogeochemistry while furnishing critical parameters for the precise quantification of karst carbon sinks—an essential component for robust regional carbon accounting under evolving climate-change trajectories.

Sulfur exhibits four stable isotopes (³²S, ³³S, ³⁴S, and ³⁶S) with natural abundances of 95.02%, 0.75%, 4.21%, and 0.02%, respectively7,15. The significant mass-dependent fractionation between ³²S and ³⁴S produces systematic δ³⁴S variations in natural systems, establishing sulfur isotopes as powerful geochemical tracers16. Aquatic SO₄²⁻ originates from multiple sources1: atmospheric deposition (marine aerosols and combustion products)2, evaporite dissolution and carbonate weathering in karst systems3, pedogenic sulfate oxidation, and15 anthropogenic inputs (agricultural fertilizers, wastewater, and industrial discharges)17–19. These distinct sources are characterized by unique δ34S signatures, enabling reliable source apportionment2,20,21.This characteristic isotopic signature enables effective identification and quantification of sulfate source contributions in aquatic systems through δ34S analysis22. However, environmental complexities introduce significant limitations1: overlapping δ³⁴S ranges among potential sulfate sources, and2 isotopic fractionation during biogeochemical cycling of sulfur compounds. These factors constrain the reliability of single δ34S measurements for unambiguous source attribution in natural environments.To address this limitation, the δ³⁴SSO4 and δ¹⁸OSO4 dual isotope approach has been successfully developed and applied23,24. This methodology has demonstrated its effectiveness in multiple environmental contexts, specifically for: (1) pollutant source identification in acid mine drainage systems17,25,26; (2) groundwater contamination tracing from landfill leachates27; (3) hydrological studies in karst wetland catchments10, and (4) water source characterization in karst spring systems19. To better understand SO42− evolution in aquatic systems, researchers have extensively utilized mass balance models incorporating SO42− dual isotopes to quantify source contributions28. However, these conventional models have two key limitations: they disregard isotopic fractionation effects and can only accommodate a restricted number of potential sources. The recently developed Simmr software package29, implemented in the R programming environment, effectively overcomes these constraints by incorporating Bayesian statistical approaches, thereby enabling more accurate quantification of multiple sulfate sources while accounting for isotopic fractionation processes.In recent years, the Simmr model has demonstrated its effectiveness in ecological studies, particularly for investigating plant water use strategies and hydrological tracing in karst systems30–32. Against the backdrop of increasing anthropogenic impacts on aquatic environments, this study innovatively applies the Simmr model to examine surface water-groundwater interactions in karst agricultural watersheds, with three specific objectives: (1) to determine the differential effects of hydrogeological units on sulfate transport dynamics; (2) to identify potential biogeochemical processes involved in sulfate transformation within karst catchments; and (3) to quantify source contributions and associated uncertainties of watershed sulfate. These investigations will advance our understanding of sulfate-mediated carbon sink processes in human-affected karst regions.

Site description

The Chiwu Creek Basin is located in Wenquan Town, Suiyang County, Guizhou Province (107°02′30″−107°25′00″E, 28°08′00″−28°20′00″N). The climate type is characterized as the typical subtropical monsoon climate. The rainfall is mainly concentrated between April and October, with an average annual precipitation of 1,200 mm. The average annual temperature is 15.5 °C33. The geomorphology is dominated by low-middle karst mountains. The mountain range generally belongs to the eastern branch of the Daloushan system, with the main peak of Jinzhongshan at an altitude of 1,714.3 m. The elevation difference between Jinzhongshan and the water surface of the main canal (at an altitude of 670 m), Jiwuxi, is up to 1,000 m. The exposed strata in the study area are mainly middle and upper Cambrian, middle and lower Ordovician and Silurian, middle and upper Permian, lower Triassic and Quaternary strata, with a lack of Carboniferous and Devonian strata. The lithology is mainly dominated by dolomites, dolomitic graywacke, and other carbonate rocks34. There exist various types of geological remains of karst landscapes in the study area, including caves, drop caves, underground rivers, blind valleys and secondary calcium carbonate and calcium sulfate deposits in caves35.The groundwater in the study area is predominantly categorized into carbonate rock fissure-karst water, carbonate rock interbedded with clastic rock fissure-karst water, and clastic rock fissure water. Groundwater serves as the primary recharge source for the watershed, with underground river flows typically exceeding 100 L/s and peaking at nearly 2 m³/s.The areas on both sides of the river are mainly residential and agricultural areas, with grain crops (rice, corn), vegetables (leafy vegetables, tubers) and fruits (citrus, peaches) grown in the fields.‘These agricultural activities are generally accompanied by the use of fertilisers. Soil types are dominated by yellow and yellow-brown soils.

Sampling and analysis methods

Sample collection method

In order to investigate the sources and transformation of dissolved sulfate, sample collection and field monitoring were conducted twice in the Chiwu Creek Basin in February (the dry season) and June (the wet season) of 2023. Based on the hydrologic conditions and geomorphic features of the Chiwu Creek Basin, seven surface water monitoring sites (SW1, SW2, SW3, SW4) and four groundwater monitoring sites (GW1, GW2, GW3) were selected for analysis. During field monitoring, collected water samples were used to determine the HCO3− using an alkalimeter with an error of 0.1 mmol/L (Aquamerck, Germany). The titration was repeated 2–3 times for each set of water samples in order to minimize the error. The WTWMulti-3430 portable multi-parameter water quality analyzer was used for on-site determination of water temperature, pH, electrical conductance (EC) and dissolved oxygen (DO) of water samples, with a measurement error of 0.1 °C, 0.01, 0.1 µS/cm and 0.01 mg/L, respectively. Water samples for experiments were collected in polyethylene bottles. These bottles were rinsed 2–3 times with deionized water before collection. Then, the water samples were filtered through 0.22 μm acetate membranes and filled into the bottles. Anion and cation samples were collected in 25 ml bottles. Water samples for cation detection were added with 1:1 nitric acid to make pH ≤ 2. Water samples for anion detection were directly sealed and stored. Water samples for δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 were collected in 500 mL bottles without filtration, added with 1:1 hydrochloric acid to make pH ≤ 2 and then stored in dark. Two bottles of water samples were collected at each site. After collection, the bottles were ensured to be free of air bubbles, sealed and stored under refrigeration at 4 °C. Then, these samples were returned to the laboratory as soon as possible35.

The anions (Cl−, NO3−, SO42−) were determined by an IS90 ion chromatograph produced by Dionex Corporation, USA, with a range of 0–1000 µS/cm and a resolution of 0.2 µS/cm. The cations (K+, Na+, Mg2+) were determined by a VBTA MPX inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (Varian Corporation, USA)36, with a measurement accuracy of 0.001 mg/L, a relative standard deviation < 2%, a detection limit of 1‰ and an absolute error < 1%. Sample isotopes (δD/δ2H and δ18O) were determined using a liquid water stable isotope analyzer (IWA-35 d-EP) from Los Gatos Research, USA, with an Precision of ± 2.0‰ and ± 0.1‰, respectively. Two 500 ml bottles of water samples from each site were filtered through a 0.45 μm mixed fiber filter membrane. Then, 15 mL of 10% BaCl2 solution was added to precipitate all the SO42− in the water samples. The generated BaSO4 precipitate was boiled for 30 min and filtered through 0.45 μm mixed fiber filter papers. The filtered precipitate was placed in a muffle furnace and calcined at 850℃ for 2 h to obtain BaSO4 precipitate powder22. The δ34SSO4 of BaSO4 was determined using continuous flow gas specific mass spectrometry (Isoprime 100) at the Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The BaSO4 samples were combusted with O2 and Cu2O at 1000℃ using an elemental analyzer connected to the mass spectrometer. The δ18OSO4 of BaSO4 was determined in the same mass spectrometer with CO gas. The samples were combusted with excess glassy carbon at 1250℃ using an elemental score connected to the mass spectrometer. Standardization was based on the international standard NBS127 dialyzer. The precision estimates for δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 were better than ± 0.2‰ and ± 0.5‰, respectively, based on replicate analyses of the internal standard37.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS software (v27.0, IBM Corp) to determine the differences in dissolved SO42− concentrations in groundwater and surface water as well as δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 between hydrogeologic regions. Based on the δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 relationship diagram of water samples from the study area (Fig. 5), the sulfate in the watershed represents a mixture from five potential sources: soil sulfate, gypsum dissolution, domestic sewage, agricultural fertilizers/detergents, and atmospheric deposition, with soil sulfate and gypsum dissolution being the predominant contributors. The majority of samples exhibit δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 values characteristic of soil sulfate sources, suggesting soil sulfate likely serves as the primary SO42- source in the watershed. This predominance reflects the well-developed subsurface connectivity in karst systems, where soil sulfate can efficiently enter surface waters through runoff or infiltrate into aquifers via the vadose zone22. Furthermore, geological sources including evaporite minerals (gypsum/anhydrite), coal-associated sulfides, and oxidized organic sulfides represent another major sulfate contributor30, as evidenced by the isotopic signatures of a significant portion of samples.Previous studies have shown that evaporite rocks are present in the strata of the study area45, and gypsum and other minerals are widely distributed46. Additionally, the development of karst fractures promotes gypsum leaching, making gypsum dissolution the primary potential source of SO42-in the watershed. Although coal-bearing shales contain pyrite22, the Devonian-Carboniferous strata are absent in the watershed, and Permian strata are only sparsely exposed47. Furthermore, the δ34SSO4 values of the samples all exceed the oxidation range of pyrite (−40‰ to + 5‰)48, indicating that the contribution from sulfide oxidation can be neglected49.

Due to inadequate wastewater treatment facilities, untreated domestic sewage and fecal matter from rural areas are discharged into water bodies, potentially leading to increased SO42- concentrations in water bodies. Atmospheric precipitation is an important recharge source for the Chiwu River basin. In summary, the potential sources of dissolved sulfates in the Chiwu River basin mainly include soil sulfates, gypsum dissolution, domestic sewage, fertilizers, detergents, and atmospheric deposition. Sulfide oxidation end members can be largely ignored due to the lack of geological background and geochemical evidence.

Biogeochemical processes of sulfate in surface and groundwater

In karstic water systems, biogeochemical processes can change SO₄²⁻ concentrations and affect δ³⁴SSO4 and δ¹⁸OSO4 values due to isotope fractionation. Processes like Bacterial Sulfate Reduction (BSR), sulfide oxidation, and solidification/mineralization16 influence δ³⁴SSO4 and δ¹⁸OSO4 fractionation. Thus, when using the Simmr model to calculate sulfate source percentages in the Chiwu Creek Basin, it’s essential to determine if biogeochemical processes causing sulfur isotope fractionation occur in the watershed.

Sulfate bacterial reduction processes

Bacterial sulfate reduction (BSR) represents a pivotal biogeochemical process. Sulfate - reducing bacteria generate hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) through the reduction of sulfate (SO₄²⁻) (as shown in Eq. 7). This process, in turn, exerts a significant impact on the distribution and cycling of sulfur isotopes51. Under anaerobic conditions, sulfate - reducing bacteria have a strong preference for utilizing the lighter ³²S isotopes52. As a consequence, the relative amount of ³²S in the reduced sulfide (such as H₂S) increases. This causes the relative amount of ³⁴S in the remaining sulfate to rise, and simultaneously leads to a decrease in the SO₄²⁻ concentration within the water column3,17,53.

Therefore, in order to clarify whether BSR has an effect on SO42− in the Chiwu Creek Basin, the ratio of SO42−/Cl− was plotted versus δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 for surface water and groundwater (Fig. 7).As shown in Fig. 7, there was no significant correlation betweenδ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 and SO42−/Cl− ratio, while the mean values of DO and NO3− in the surface water of the study area were 9.74 mg/L and 7.43 mg/L, respectively, and those in the groundwater were 9.86 mg/L and 7.59 mg/L, respectively, suggesting that the oxidized SO42− reduction conditions were insufficient, indicating that BSR did not play a major role in the study area.

Other biogeochemical processes

In the watershed, due to the thin soil layer and the extensive presence of underground pipelines, soil organic matter is prone to being mineralized into dissolved sulfate following runoff erosion. The δ¹⁸OSO4 values of surface water in the watershed range from 1.85‰ to 6.9‰, while those of groundwater range from 0.78‰ to 6.07‰. These values all fall within the δ¹⁸OSO4 range (0–6‰)19characteristic of SO₄²⁻ mineralization, indicating that the mineralization of soil organic sulfur could be a significant source of SO₄²⁻52.During the solidification process, sulfur isotope fractionation was minimal, generally within ± 2‰, and thus can be considered negligible. Previous studies have shown that isotope fractionation effects can be neglected during the physicochemical transformation of sulphur in aerated forest soils (e.g., mineralisation/immobilisation)19. Since the study area is primarily composed of yellow soil and yellow-brown soil, which have rapid water loss and good aeration, the soil remains aerated. Therefore, immobilisation and mineralisation do not cause isotope fractionation of sulphur and oxygen in the watershed water. Additionally, the study area lacks pyrite strata, so the oxidation-reduction processes of sulphides have a limited impact on the δ³⁴SSO4 and δ¹⁸OSO4 values.

Sulfate source resolution using Simmr modeling

Based on the analysis in Sect. 4.2.1, it was concluded that there are no biogeochemical processes such as the BSR process that can cause significant fractionation in the Chiwu Creek Basin. Therefore, when using the Simmr model for calculations, the fractionation factor (Cjk) was set to 0. The results of the Simmr model analysis show that the six potential sources of dissolved SO42− in the Chiwu Creek Basin exhibit significant differences in time and space (Fig. 8).The spatial distribution of sulfate sources indicates that surface water is primarily composed of gypsum dissolution (GD: 49.7%) and soil sulfate (SS: 28.7%), while groundwater contains a higher proportion of soil sulfate (SS: 33.3%) than gypsum dissolution (GD: 28.7%). Secondary sources include domestic wastewater (SE: 6.3%), fertilizers (CF: 6.1%), detergents (DE: 4.8%), and atmospheric deposition (AD: 4.3%), which collectively contribute 21.5% of the sulfate in surface water, while their contributions in groundwater are slightly higher (SE: 11.0%, CF: 10.9%, DE: 8.3%, AD: 7.9%, totaling 38.1%). Seasonal variations show that during the dry season, GD (44.4%) dominates in surface water, exceeding SS (21.4%), while during the rainy season, SS (39.4%) exceeds GD (34.5%); In groundwater, GD (24.5%) > SS (21.0%) during the dry season, while SS (26.5%) > GD (16.7%) during the rainy season. Three key patterns emerge: GD and SS remain the primary sulfate sources within the watershed, with SS contributions significantly increasing during the rainy season2; GD seasonal fluctuations are greater in surface water than in groundwater; contributions from the four secondary sources (SE, CF, DE, AD) remain stable (6–12%) with minimal seasonal variability.

The dominant contributions of gypsum dissolution (GD) and soil sulfates (SS) to watershed sulfate levels are fundamentally controlled by the study area’s unique geological framework. The prevalent gypsum-bearing formations exhibit extensive karst fracturing, creating preferential pathways for mineral dissolution and solute transport19,36. Hydrological dynamics further modulate these contributions, as Chiwu River’s role as the regional base level facilitates substantial groundwater discharge into surface waters47, accounting for the pronounced GD disparity between surface (49.7%) and groundwater (28.7%) systems. Seasonal hydrochemical variations reveal distinct patterns: dry season low-flow conditions and evaporative enrichment enhance relative GD contributions, while wet season dilution effects cause marked GD decreases (surface water: 44.4%→34.5%; groundwater: 24.5%→16.7%). The karst terrain’s characteristic pedological features - including shallow soil profiles, limited vegetative cover, and enhanced erosional potential28,36 - promote differential sulfate mobilization, yielding lower surface water SS contributions (28.7%) compared to groundwater (33.3%). Precipitation-mediated erosional forcing during wet periods drives substantial SS increases (surface water: 21.4%→39.4%; groundwater:21.0%→26.5%), demonstrating the coupled geochemical-hydrological controls on sulfate sourcing54.

The monitoring data revealed consistently higher sulfate contributions from domestic wastewater (SE), fertilizers (CF), and detergents (DE) in groundwater compared to surface water, with minimal seasonal variation. Field surveys identified inadequate wastewater treatment infrastructure and drainage systems in the watershed. Consequently, sulfate from domestic and agricultural sources primarily enters aquifers through surface runoff, karst fissures, or pipeline leakage36, accounting for groundwater contributions of 11% (SE) and 10.9% (CF), versus approximately 6% in rainfall-diluted surface water. The SO42− signature of DE becomes obscured when mixed with SE during discharge55, resulting in consistently low contributions (< 9%) in both water bodies. Atmospheric deposition (AD) showed the lowest sulfate contributions, aligning with cave monitoring data from the watershed36.

In summary, soluble sulfates in the Chiwu Creek basin are mainly controlled by natural processes (gypsum dissolution and soil sulfates), while human activities (sewage, fertilizers, and detergents) contribute relatively little. Spatial differences are mainly controlled by geological and hydrological structures, while temporal differences are driven by rainfall and runoff processes.

Uncertainty assessment of the contribution rate

When employing Bayesian mixing models for source apportionment calculations, minor variations or overlaps in isotopic signatures can introduce discrepancies in model outputs. To quantify this uncertainty, we utilized the uncertainty indicator (UI90) to evaluate the reliability of SO₄²⁻ source distributions within the watershed. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the watershed demonstrated remarkably low UI90 values for detergent-derived (4.7%), atmospheric deposition (5.1%), sewage (6.9%), and fertilizer sources (8.0%), indicating highly stable contributions from these sulfate sources.In contrast, soil sulfate (SS) and gypsum dissolution exhibited substantially higher uncertainty (UI90 = 31.3% and 34.4%, respectively), reflecting greater variability in their contributions. This spatial variability in SO₄²⁻ inputs arises from several watershed dynamics. While land use patterns remain relatively stable in the short term, agricultural practices (including planting methods and crop rotation) induce fluctuations in soil fertility and organic matter content – a key driver of soil sulfate variability12. Furthermore, precipitation patterns significantly alter hydrological processes, particularly the groundwater-to-surface water ratio in streamflow, thereby affecting the temporal and spatial distribution of sulfate contributions from various sources56.

Conclusions

This study integrates water chemistry analysis, multi-stable isotope tracing (δ34SSO4, δ18OSO4, δD, δ18OH2O) with a Bayesian mixture model (Simmr) to systematically reveal the sources of sulfate in the Chiwu Creek Basin through a combination of qualitative and quantitative methods, a thereby identifying the “natural dominance - human-enhanced” source conversion mechanism of sulfate in the Chiwu River basin, and quantifies the uncertainty of the estimation results. Hydrochemical analysis reveals the Chiwu Creek Basin exhibits a characteristic karst HCO3−-Ca・Mg water type, with atmospheric precipitation serving as the dominant recharge source. Rainfall dilution induces distinct seasonal variations in δ34SSO4 and δ18OSO4 values, demonstrating higher isotopic signatures during dry periods compared to wet seasons. Source apportionment indicates gypsum dissolution (49.7%) and soil sulfate (33.3%) constitute the principal natural sulfate sources, exhibiting marked seasonal variability (UI90: 31.3–34.4%). Among anthropogenic sources, domestic wastewater (contribution rate of 11.0%) can enter the groundwater system through karst fissures and pipelines, becoming the main anthropogenic source of sulfate in the watershed. Further analysis revealed significant spatial and temporal differences in the contributions of various sulfate sources: spatial differences are mainly controlled by the geological and hydrological structure of the watershed, while temporal differences are driven by rainfall and runoff processe.The absence of significant isotopic fractionation suggests that bacterial sulfate reduction plays a negligible role in sulfate transformation, indicating conservative transport behavior of SO42− within the study area.Therefore, based on the research results, it is recommended that ecological restoration and vegetation optimization be implemented in gypsum exposure areas, while simultaneously promoting the construction of sewage treatment facilities, standardizing the use of detergents, and promoting the use of slow-release sulfur fertilizers. This study has limitations such as reliance on isotope data from literature and inherent uncertainty in model estimates. In the future, the isotopic composition of local pollution sources should be analyzed in detail to clarify their narrow range of accuracy, thereby reducing the uncertainty in the analysis of sulfate sources.
