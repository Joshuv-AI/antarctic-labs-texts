Recurrent diabetic ketoacidosis burden and precipitant patterns in established type 1 diabetes using continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion versus multiple daily injections: an 11-year retrospective single-center study
Authors: Ruixia Liu, Tianrong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) remains one of the most important acute hyperglycemic emergencies in diabetes care and continues to impose substantial clinical and health-system burden. Contemporary consensus reports emphasize that DKA management should not end with correction of acidosis, because identifying the precipitating pathway and preventing future events are central parts of care. This broader preventive perspective is especially important in established type 1 diabetes, in which recurrent admissions often contribute disproportionately to resource use and long-term morbidity [1–3].

In people with established type 1 diabetes, common pathways leading to DKA include insulin omission or insufficiency, infection and other intercurrent illness, and interruption of insulin delivery in pump users [4, 5]. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) and European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) consensus for adult type 1 diabetes and current diabetes-technology standards both note that treatment modality changes the context in which DKA develops: continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) may improve long-term glycemic management, but abrupt interruption of rapid-acting insulin delivery can precipitate ketosis quickly when pump or infusion-set problems occur [6, 7].

Existing studies comparing pump users with patients treated by multiple daily injections (MDI) have not produced a single simple message. Some registry and cohort studies suggest lower overall rates of acute complications with pump therapy, whereas other datasets, particularly outside tightly controlled settings, suggest residual susceptibility to interruption-related DKA or heterogeneous risk across eras and populations [8–11]. In adults hospitalized for DKA, Flores and colleagues found different precipitating factors in pump users and non-users, but the focus remained on the admission episode rather than on recurrence burden across the study period [5]. A recent nationwide Swedish study in children found that DKA at type 1 diabetes diagnosis did not independently predict subsequent DKA episodes, further suggesting that recurrence may be influenced by subsequent social and care-related factors rather than by the initial presentation alone [12].

Recurrent DKA deserves specific attention because repeated admissions often cluster in patients with fragmented care, psychosocial vulnerability, socioeconomic disadvantage, or self-management barriers [13–17]. More recent work suggests that repeated DKA admissions may reflect identifiable social and metabolic phenotypes rather than a uniform phenomenon [18, 19].

Chinese data remain comparatively limited. Earlier multicenter and hospital-based studies have described the burden and characteristics of hospitalized type 1 diabetes and DKA in China [20–22]. Recent studies have also highlighted the continuing hospitalization burden of DKA at the population level and the heterogeneity of precipitating factors and in-hospital outcomes across clinical settings, reinforcing the importance of moving beyond a single-admission perspective [3, 23]. We therefore conducted an 11-year retrospective single-center study to compare recurrent DKA burden, recurrent precipitant patterns, and index-admission severity across insulin-delivery modalities in people with established type 1 diabetes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective single-center study reviewed eligible DKA hospitalizations at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University from January 2014 through December 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (YX2025-115). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

Study population

We included hospitalizations for DKA in people with established type 1 diabetes. Eligible admissions occurred in patients with established type 1 diabetes whose usual preadmission insulin-delivery modality was either CSII or basal–bolus MDI. The exclusion criteria were: (1) DKA occurring at the initial diagnosis of type 1 diabetes; (2) preadmission insulin regimens other than CSII or basal–bolus MDI; and (3) discharge against medical advice before metabolic recovery when the information required to confirm eligibility or ascertain key in-hospital outcomes was unavailable. Overall, 180 eligible DKA hospitalizations from 128 unique patients were included.

Chronology and index admission

The study-period index admission was defined as the earliest eligible hospitalization for each patient after sorting all admissions by hospitalization number. One index admission per patient was retained for baseline, severe-DKA, and short-term outcome analyses. All later eligible admissions for the same patient were classified as recurrent admissions. Patient-level recurrent DKA was defined as at least two eligible DKA hospitalizations during the study period.

Treatment-modality assignment

Index-admission analyses used the treatment modality recorded in the earliest eligible hospitalization, yielding 33 CSII and 95 MDI index admissions. For patient-level recurrence analyses, patients were assigned to CSII or MDI according to the predominant treatment modality recorded across eligible hospitalizations during the study period, yielding 35 CSII and 93 MDI patients. Sensitivity analyses additionally regrouped patients by index-admission modality and examined the subset with consistent modality coding across admissions.

Definitions

For the purposes of this study, severe biochemical DKA was defined as pH < 7.0 and/or bicarbonate < 10 mmol/L in index admissions, according to the 2024 international consensus report on hyperglycemic crises [1]. Recorded precipitating factors were grouped into five clinically oriented phenotypes: device/infusion-related failure, insulin omission/insufficiency, infection/intercurrent illness, mixed insulin/intercurrent illness, and other or unidentified. When more than one precipitating factor was documented, a prespecified chart-review rule was used to assign a primary phenotype.

Data collection and outcomes

Collected variables included sex, age, body weight, body mass index, education level, diabetes duration, HbA1c, pH, bicarbonate concentration, acidosis correction time, length of stay, presenting symptoms, comorbidities/complications, month of DKA, concomitant medication, and recorded precipitating factor. The primary outcome was patient-level recurrent DKA. Secondary outcomes were recorded precipitating factors, severe DKA in index admissions, and short-term outcomes including acidosis correction time and length of stay.

Missing data

Data required for patient identification, treatment-modality classification, recurrence ascertainment, age, and index-admission HbA1c were complete for all patients included in the primary recurrent DKA analyses. Therefore, all 128 patients contributed to the adjusted recurrence models, and no imputation was required for the primary analyses. Body weight and BMI were incompletely recorded, particularly BMI; body weight was available in 94/128 patients and BMI in 37/128 patients. These variables were therefore not summarized in the main descriptive table and were not included in multivariable models. Education level was unknown for 2/128 patients and was retained as an “unknown” category in Table 1. pH was missing in 8 index admissions. For the secondary adults-only severe biochemical DKA analysis, an admission was classified as severe if either available biochemical criterion met the severe threshold. When pH was missing and bicarbonate was ≥ 10 mmol/L, severe-DKA status was considered indeterminate and the admission was excluded from that analysis and finally five adult MDI admissions with missing pH and bicarbonate ≥ 10 mmol/L were excluded. For incompletely recorded variables, no missing-at-random assumption was imposed, and no imputation was performed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 20.0). Continuous variables are presented as median [interquartile range] and were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were compared with χ² or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. Logistic regression was used to estimate the association between treatment modality and recurrent DKA in the primary patient-level analysis and to evaluate severe DKA in the adult index-admission subgroup. Because pediatric/adolescent cases accounted for a small proportion of the cohort and may differ clinically from adults in disease context and management, an additional adjusted analysis of severe DKA was performed in adults only. The main adjusted recurrence model included treatment modality, age, and HbA1c. The adult severe-DKA model used the same covariates. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The study cohort included 180 eligible DKA hospitalizations in 128 unique patients (Fig. 1). Of the 128 patients, 111 (86.7%) were adults and 17 (13.3%) were children or adolescents. The CSII group included 28 adults and 5 pediatric/adolescent patients, whereas the MDI group included 83 adults and 12 pediatric/adolescent patients (Table 1). After retention of one index admission per patient, 52/180 hospitalizations (28.9%) were recurrent admissions, and 30/128 patients (23.4%) experienced recurrent DKA during the study period.

Baseline characteristics of index admissions

Characteristics of the 128 index admissions are summarized in Table 1. Median index-admission HbA1c was 9.7% [8.8–11.2] (83 mmol/mol [73–99]) in CSII and 11.1% [9.4–13.2] (98 mmol/mol [79–121]) in MDI. Other demographic, clinical, and biochemical characteristics are presented descriptively.

Recurrent DKA burden at the patient level

In the primary patient-level analysis, defined by predominant treatment modality across eligible hospitalizations, recurrent DKA was more frequent in CSII than in MDI (13/35 [37.1%] vs. 17/93 [18.3%]) (Table 2). In unadjusted analysis, CSII was associated with higher odds of recurrent DKA (OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.11–6.27; P = 0.028), and this association remained significant after adjustment for age and index-admission HbA1c (adjusted OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.07–6.55; P = 0.036) (Table 2). Sensitivity analyses based on index-admission modality and the consistent-coding subset showed the same directional pattern, although effect estimates were attenuated in the index-admission analysis (Table 2).

Discussion

In this 11-year retrospective cohort of people with established type 1 diabetes hospitalized for DKA, recurrent DKA was common and more than one quarter of eligible admissions occurred after a prior study-period event. The central finding was not merely that repeat events occurred, but that recurrent burden clustered differently across insulin-delivery modalities and was accompanied by different recurrent precipitant patterns.

The recurrence burden observed here is clinically important. Nearly one quarter of patients experienced recurrent DKA, and recurrent admissions accounted for more than one quarter of all eligible hospitalizations. This pattern is consistent with adult studies showing that repeated DKA admissions identify a subgroup with disproportionate healthcare utilization, social vulnerability, and future risk [13–17]. It also fits with more recent population-level evidence that DKA continues to generate substantial inpatient burden in adults even where mortality is relatively low, because presentation rates and hospital use remain clinically meaningful over time [3].

The recurrent-DKA signal should be interpreted as a difference in failure mode rather than as a blanket judgment that pump therapy is intrinsically unsafe. CSII removes the pharmacologic buffer provided by long-acting basal insulin, so interruption of insulin delivery can precipitate ketosis rapidly and sometimes with less warning than in MDI. That biological vulnerability may help explain why recurrent events remained concentrated in a subset of CSII users despite lower HbA1c at index admission. By contrast, recurrent DKA in MDI appeared to track more closely with ongoing insulin omission/insufficiency and intercurrent illness. In practical terms, the two regimens may fail in different ways: CSII may be more vulnerable to abrupt technical interruption, whereas MDI may be more vulnerable to progressive under insulinization or delayed response to illness [1, 4, 5].

A major strength of the present study is that it links recurrence burden to precipitant pattern rather than treating all repeat DKA as a single clinical entity. Among recurrent admissions, device/infusion-related failure was concentrated in CSII, whereas insulin omission/insufficiency and infection/intercurrent illness were more prominent in MDI. This distinction is clinically useful because it moves the discussion from simple risk comparison to prevention strategy. In pump users, prevention may need to emphasize infusion-set troubleshooting, availability of backup insulin, rapid ketone testing, and explicit action plans for unexplained hyperglycemia. In MDI users, the more relevant preventive targets may be adherence reinforcement, sick-day rules, infection-triggered dose adjustment, and earlier clinical contact during metabolic deterioration [4, 5, 19].

The index-admission data add an important nuance. CSII users had lower HbA1c levels, shorter hospital stays, and lower adjusted odds of severe DKA in adults. Taken together, these findings argue against the simplistic interpretation that recurrent DKA in pump users merely reflects chronically poorer self-management. A more plausible interpretation is that at least some CSII-related events represent abrupt but more quickly reversible interruption phenotypes, whereas MDI-related events may more often reflect longer periods of insulin deficiency, intercurrent illness, or delayed presentation. This may also explain why lower chronic glycemic exposure and lower adult severe-DKA odds can coexist with a higher recurrent burden in CSII: the issue may be recurrence of a narrower technical-metabolic vulnerability rather than recurrence of globally worse diabetes control [5, 24]. The adults-only adjusted analysis of severe DKA should be interpreted cautiously, because pediatric/adolescent cases were not included in this model and age-related clinical heterogeneity may influence the association.

The Chinese context also matters. Previous studies from China have described the burden of secondary DKA and inpatient type 1 diabetes complications, but regimen-specific recurrence data remain limited [20, 21]. The present study therefore adds a locally relevant perspective to the broader international literature. This is important because recurrence risk is unlikely to be determined by physiology alone; it is also shaped by the organization of diabetes education, access to ketone strips and pump consumables, affordability of supplies, and the ease with which patients can obtain timely advice during early metabolic deterioration [15, 25].

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective, single-center, and modest in size. The medical records did not include direct psychosocial measures, ketone-monitoring behavior, pump brand, continuous glucose monitoring or automated insulin delivery exposure, or structured diabetes education variables. Socioeconomic variables, including household income, insurance coverage, affordability of pump consumables, family support, and access to structured diabetes education, were unavailable. These factors may influence both selection of insulin-delivery modality and DKA recurrence, resulting in residual confounding. Missingness may not have been random, particularly for body weight, BMI, and pH. BMI was available for only a limited subset and was therefore not included in multivariable analyses. This may have implications for the interpretation of our findings. Because the cohort was assembled exclusively from DKA hospitalizations, the study cannot estimate the incidence or overall risk of DKA among all people with type 1 diabetes using CSII or MDI. The recurrence estimates are conditional on having experienced at least one eligible DKA hospitalization. Patient-level recurrence analyses also required assignment of one predominant treatment modality across the study period, which simplified longitudinal exposure. In addition, precipitating factors were abstracted from routine clinical documentation and may have been misclassified, particularly when multiple contributors coexisted. In addition, the adjusted severe-DKA model was restricted to adults because pediatric/adolescent cases were few and clinically heterogeneous; therefore, this result requires cautious interpretation and external validation. Finally, the results require external validation in larger multicenter cohorts.

In conclusion, among patients with established type 1 diabetes who had experienced at least one DKA hospitalization, recurrent DKA was common. Under the predominant-modality classification, recurrence was more frequent among patients classified as CSII users, although statistical significance varied across sensitivity analyses. Recorded precipitating factors showed treatment-specific descriptive patterns. These findings are hypothesis-generating and do not estimate the overall risk of DKA associated with CSII versus MDI.
