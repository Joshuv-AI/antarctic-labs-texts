Unlocking ovarian rejuvenation with platelet-rich plasma: systematic review and proposed clinical framework for Controlled use in poor prognosis patients undergoing in vitro fertilisation
Authors: Arwa A Alageel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlatelet-rich plasma (PRP) is used as an adjunctive treatment in various medical fields, including orthopaedics, plastic surgery, dermatology, and wound healing. However, PRP application in reproductive medicine, particularly for fertility restoration, remains controversial, specifically for women with diminished ovarian reserve (DOR), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and pre-menopause. This systematic review aimed to comprehensively evaluate the existing literature on autologous PRP use for ovarian rejuvenation, with a focus on refining and standardising PRP protocols and identifying appropriate candidate populations.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted on the autologous PRP use using Cochrane, Embase, PubMed, MEDLINE, NCBI, and Google Scholar databases. Studies on PRP application in reproductive medicine were identified and classified into two categories: DOR and POI. The PRP preparation protocol was assessed, and the results were compared. This narrative systematic review, carried out in compliance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines, combined results, although meta-analysis was not feasible due to significant variation in study design, PRP preparation, and outcome measures.ResultsSeveral studies reported restoration of menstruation following intraovarian PRP administration in women with POI. In women with DOR, studies reported changes in ovarian reserve markers, including increases in serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels and reductions in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels; however, these changes were not consistently associated with higher antral follicle counts or oocyte yield. Instead, sseveral studies reported higher proportions of mature oocytes and pregnancy and live birth outcomes, particularly following repeated PRP treatment cycles in women with long-standing ovarian dysfunction.ConclusionsIntraovarian PRP may have a potential role in activating dormant follicles in a selected subset of women with poor ovarian response. However, the current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, supporting its use only within structured clinical or research protocols until higher-quality, standardised studies are available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-026-08338-x.Keywords: Platelet-rich plasma, Poor ovarian response, Premature ovarian insufficiency, Assisted reproduction, Poor fertility outcomes, IVF treatment

Introduction

Infertility is defined as the inability to achieve pregnancy within 12 months of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse. According to the 2023 World Health Organization (WHO) report, an estimated 17.5% of adults worldwide—approximately one in six individuals—will experience infertility during their lifetime [70]. While infertility affects both sexes, the burden is disproportionately borne by women, making female infertility a major global public health concern. The 2021 Global Burden of Disease study estimated that 110.1 million women aged 15–49 years were affected, with an age-standardised prevalence of 2764.6 per 100,000 and over 6.2 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [73]. Compared with 1990, this represents an 84% increase in prevalence and DALYs, underscoring infertility’s growing global significance. The highest burden was observed among women aged 35–39 years, while the most rapid increase in incidence occurred among women aged 30–34 years, suggesting a trend toward earlier onset of infertility [36].

A substantial proportion of infertility cases are associated with ovarian function disorders, particularly anovulatory cycles characterised by the absence of ovulation. Ovarian dysfunction frequently presents with elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, highlighting the need to evaluate and address ovarian function in managing infertility [61]. High FSH levels are associated with diminished ovarian reserve (DOR), which refers to reduced fecundity or a poor response to ovarian stimulation in reproductive-age women with regular menses compared with age-matched women [14]. Ovarian reserve can be assessed by measuring anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) or antral follicle count (AFC) levels, both of which provide comparable insights according to the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) [8].

Fertility treatment presents a challenge for women with DOR and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI). These women can be classified as: (a) those with decreased ovarian response to controlled ovarian stimulation using conventional gonadotropin dose [66] and (b) those with POI [41]. Despite differing aetiologies, these categories share similar clinical challenges in fertility treatment, notably poor fertility outcomes. POI affects 1% of women of reproductive age and is defined by the early cessation of ovarian function before 40 years, characterised by hypoestrogenism, elevated gonadotropins, follicle depletion, and menstrual irregularities ranging from oligomenorrhoea to amenorrhoea, often resulting in infertility [18]. However, women with DOR, accounting for 9–24% of cases, exhibit an inadequate ovarian response to gonadotropin stimulation [17]. In women with DOR, a suboptimal response to ovarian stimulation during assisted reproductive techniques, such as in vitro fertilisation (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), frequently results in a reduction in the number of retrieved oocytes and a decline in the pregnancy rate [22, 64]. With a reported prevalence of 9–24% among women undergoing ovarian stimulation, DOR represents a substantial clinical challenge, potentially affecting up to one in four patients [17].

POI describes a subgroup of infertile patients with a poor response to ovarian stimulation during IVF. According to current ESHRE guidelines, a diagnosis of POI requires meeting at least two of the following criteria: (i) advanced maternal age (≥40 years) or other DOR risk factors; (ii) previous DOR (≤3 oocytes with a conventional stimulation protocol); and (iii) abnormal ovarian reserve test (that is, AFC, 5–7 follicles or AMH, 0.5–1.1 ng/mL) [22]. Patients with POI have a very low chance of spontaneous pregnancy and natural conception [66]. Assisted reproduction using gonadotropin stimulation remains the only viable approach; however, women with DOR continue to face significant challenges in achieving pregnancy through medically assisted reproduction (MAR)

For over three decades, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has been used as a therapeutic tool in regenerative medicine, orthopaedics, dermatology, dentistry, and surgery [10, 39, 63]. More recently, it has emerged as a promising approach, with small-scale studies exploring its potential for ovarian and uterine rejuvenation [50]. PRP was first applied for ovarian rejuvenation in 2016, when Pantos et al. [44] demonstrated that intraovarian PRP could restore menstrual cycles and induce folliculogenesis in eight premenopausal women, leading to successful oocyte retrieval and embryo development via ICSI [44]. This pioneering observation provided the basis for subsequent investigations into its clinical application. Building on this early evidence, Sills et al., [58] reported its use in four patients with poor prognosis (mean age, 42 years) who received laparoscopic intraovarian PRP injection followed by IVF, resulting in live births [58]. In the same year in Greece, similar results were reported for three poor responders with POF (mean age, 38 years) and amenorrhea for over a year undergoing IVF [56]. Notably, intraovarian PRP prior to IVF achieved what appeared to be a qualitative “ploidy rescue” in at least one patient with a history of consecutive aneuploid embryos, with subsequent term delivery of healthy newborns [59].

Blood-derived therapies including autologous PRP, deliver high concentrated platelets, clotting factors, growth factors, chemokines and cytokines that collectively facilitate enhanced tissue regeneration [9]. The potential therapeutic benefits of PRP stem from its high levels of growth factors, such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), insulin-like growth factors (IGF)-1 and IGF-2, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which regenerate tissues by enhancing cell migration and promoting proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis [7, 19, 50, 53]. Despite limited understanding of the mechanisms of action of PRP, its use has continued in clinical settings; however, its cost-effectiveness remains uncertain. Autologous ovarian PRP injections have already been explored in clinical practice [20, 45, 56, 57], nonetheless, the extent of their impact on ovarian tissue among individuals varies. This raises a key concern of inconsistency in injection composition or administration protocols among recipients.

This systematic review evaluated the therapeutic potential of intraovarian PRP in women with DOR or POI. Primary outcomes included ovarian reserve markers (AMH, FSH, oestradiol) in DOR, with AFC and pregnancy outcomes as secondary measures (Fig. 1), while menstrual recovery was used to assess ovarian function restoration in POI. PRP preparation and administration protocols were compared across studies to inform proposed protocol refinements

Results

Study selection

Overall, 333 studies were identified through searches in the Cochrane, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases. After removing 175 duplicate studies, 158 remained. After title and abstract screening, 81 studies were further excluded. The full texts of 77 articles were then assessed for eligibility, and another 52 studies were excluded. In addition to not meeting the core inclusion criteria, an additional exclusion step was applied. Studies with a sample size of ≤10 patients were excluded to minimise bias from small-scale case series and to enhance the reliability of the pooled outcomes. Finally, 17 studies were included in the final analysis. The study selection process followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [35] and is summarised in Fig. 2.

Quality of evidence and risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the 17 included studies was evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomised studies, and the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool where applicable.

Overall, most studies demonstrated a moderate risk of bias primarily due to lack of blinding, heterogeneity in PRP preparation. Studies with robust methodological designs, particularly those by Cakiroglu et al. [11], have consistently reported a low risk of bias across all domains [11]. In contrast, several pilot and retrospective studies reported serious risks in one or more domains, most commonly in the outcome measurement and reporting, where unvalidated endpoints and incomplete follow-ups were noted. Results of the bias assessment are summarised in Supplementary Table 1.

Table 1 shows a summary of the characteristics of the included studies [2, 6, 11, 16, 22, 26, 27, 34, 39, 42, 45, 48, 51, 57, 62, 67, 73] which comprised prospective, retrospective, and before-and-after clinical designs involving 2361 patients. These studies reported various clinical and hormonal outcomes, including patient age, serum AMH and FSH levels, AFC, and IVF-related outcomes such as oocyte retrieval, embryo development, and pregnancy rates. The diversity in study designs and patient populations enabled a comprehensive analysis of the potential role of PRP in restoring ovarian function and enhancing fertility.

Patient characteristics in the non-controlled trials

Table 1 shows the patient demographics and diagnostic profiles. Most participants were diagnosed with DOR or POI based on the established criteria, including the Bologna, ESHRE, and Patient-Oriented Strategies Encompassing Individualised Oocyte Number (POSEIDON) classifications [3]. Across the non-controlled studies, the mean and median ages of the participants were 32.5 and 38 years, respectively, indicating a predominance of advanced reproductive age groups. Most patients had a history of infertility lasting over a year, often accompanied by a diminished ovarian reserve, as indicated by low AMH levels and reduced AFC, and some had experienced previous IVF failures. While age distributions varied across the studies, the overall population reflected the typical clinical profile of women seeking fertility treatment owing to ovarian insufficiency.

Blood collection volume and platelet concentration

Autologous blood volumes for PRP preparation ranged from 8–80 mL, with a 20 mL blood volume reported in at least five studies [2, 11, 33, 40, 65]. However, significant variability was observed, ranging from 8.5 mL in the Petryk and Petryk [49] study to 80 mL in the Pacu et al. [43] study. Platelet concentrations ranged from 250,000 to over 1,700,000 platelets/μL, with most clustering between 900,000 and 1,000,000/μL. However, only approximately half of the studies reported exact values, reflecting inconsistencies in PRP processing protocols that limit their di rect comparability.

Platelet-rich plasma injection volume and frequency

PRP injection volume into the ovaries ranged from 0.4–6.9 mL, with 2–4 mL being the most frequently used volume [11, 25, 43, 55]. Lower volumes, such as 400 μL [38], were used for single cortical injections, whereas higher volumes (including 6.9±2.5 mL in Cakiroglu et al., [71]) were often administered over multiple sites in both ovaries [38, 71]. A correlation was observed between the injection volume and administration frequency, wherein higher total volumes were associated with multiple puncture sites or bilateral injections, whereas smaller volumes were typically delivered in a single cycle (Table 2). However, comparative dose–response analyses were not conducted in any of these studies, and none provided a standardised rationale for injection volume.

Timing of platelet-rich plasma administration and assisted reproductive technology resumption

In patients with regular menses, PRP was most often administered during the early to mid-follicular phase (such as days 3–11), whereas in patients with amenorrhoea or POI, random-day injections were common. MAR cycles were initiated as early as the same cycle [60] or up to 3 months later [2, 55]. Meanwhile, a 6-week interval post-PRP was the most reported. This wide variation in timing reflects the lack of consensus regarding the optimal ovarian recovery period, underscoring the need for standardised monitoring of post-treatment ovarian dynamics (Fig. 3, Table 2).

Activation protocols

PRP was activated using calcium gluconate or calcium chloride in at least six studies, typically at a 1:9 ratio, as reported by Aflatoonian et al. [2], Parvanov et al. [46], and Melo et al. [38]. These agents trigger platelet degranulation, facilitating regenerative growth factor release. However, many studies did not report whether PRP was activated, and none reported a quantification of the growth factor content following activation.

Injection technique and anaesthesia

A 17G needle was the most commonly used (reported in >10 studies), although the gauges ranged from 21–24G (Table 2). Injections were generally administered under sedation or spinal anaesthesia, although the type of anaesthesia used was not specified in some studies. Injection sites included the subcortical, stromal, and intracortical layers, with some studies performing multiple punctures per ovary [6, 71], whereas others performed single punctures [38]. Despite this variation, the impact of injection technique on ovarian response, patient safety, or pain was only assessed in some of the studies, and direct comparisons of different approaches were not conducted in any.

Hormonal and clinical outcomes

The clinical effectiveness of intraovarian PRP was assessed using the primary and secondary outcomes. The primary outcomes focused on ovarian reserve markers (AMH, FSH, and oestradiol levels, and AFC) measured pre- and post-PRP injections to evaluate their effects on ovarian function. Owing to inconsistent reporting and lack of numerical data in several studies, a meta-analysis was not feasible. The findings are summarised in Table 3. Secondary outcomes, including IVF performance, spontaneous pregnancy, menstruation restoration, and live birth rates, are presented in Tables 3 and 4, reflecting the reproductive implications of PRP treatment.

Primary clinical outcomes

Anti-Müllerian hormone

An increase in AMH levels following intraovarian PRP administration was reported in 9 studies including Sills et al. [60], Cakiroglu et al. [11], Melo et al. [38], Sfakianoudis et al. [55], Pacu et al. (2022), Parvanov et al. [46], Cakiroglu et al. [71], Fraidakis et al. [25], and Garavelas et al. [26]. Melo et al. [38] reported a 63% increase in AMH levels, whereas Sills et al. [60] observed a median increase of 167%, including in older patients. In contrast, Farimani et al. [21], Keikha et al. [33], Barad et al. [6], and Tanha et al. [15], reported no significant change in AMH levels. Meanwhile, Aflatoonian et al. [2] described a transient increase in AMH levels at one month, followed by a decline below baseline values by 2 months. Overall, although increases in AMH were reported in several studies, the statistical significance, magnitude, and durability varied considerably across studies (Table 3).

Follicle-stimulating hormone

Of the 12 studies in which FSH levels were evaluated, six reported a significant decrease following intraovarian PRP administration, including Melo et al. [38], Petryk et al. [49], Sfakianoudis et al. [55], Pacu et al. [43], Cakiroglu et al. [71], and Fraidakis et al. [25], suggesting improved pituitary–ovary feedback.

In contrast, Farimani et al. [21], Keikha et al. [33], Navali et al. [40], and Barad et al. [6] reported no significant changes in FSH levels. Interestingly, Garavelas et al. [26] reported a 44% increase in FSH levels following PRP administration [26]. Overall, changes in FSH levels following PRP treatment were inconsistent across studies. These discrepancies suggest that FSH alone may be a less reliable indicator of PRP effectiveness than using AMH or AFC (Table 3).

Antral follicle count

An increase in AFC following intraovarian PRP administration was reported in at least nine studies. Marked improvements were reported in by Melo et al. [38] who observed 75% increase, as well as Cakiroglu et al. [11, 71], and Pacu et al. [43], where AFC increased from 3.45 to 5.65 (p<0.001). Keikha et al. [33] and Barad et al. [6], also reported moderate increases in AFC following PRP treatment. in contrast, Navali et al. [40] and Farimani et al. [21] reported no significant changes in AFC despite positive changes in other clinical parameters such as oocyte development. Despite variability in reported outcomes across studies, changes in AFC were reported more consistently than changes in systemic hormonal measures, suggesting that AFC may be a more reliable indicator of short-term follicular activity than using systemic hormone levels (Table 3).

Secondary outcomes

In vitro fertilisation and pregnancy outcomes

Studies reporting IVF-related endpoints described changes in oocyte yield, embryo development, and pregnancy outcomes following intraovarian PRP administration. Melo et al. [38] reported a statistically higher number of retrieved oocytes and top-quality embryos in the PRP group compared with controls. Similarly, Sfakianoudis et al. [55] reported successful oocyte retrieval in 96.6% of patients with DOR, and a clinical pregnancy rate of 46.6%. Pacu et al. (2022) also reported a two-fold increase in oocyte yield along with improved embryo quality. Cakiroglu et al. [71] presented the most comprehensive dataset, reporting 105 pregnancies and 61 live births across various modes of conception, including spontaneous, fresh/frozen embryo transfer, and preimplantation genetic diagnosis cycles (Table 4).

In contrast, several studies did not demonstrate corresponding increases in pregnancy or live birth outcomes despite reporting changes in oocyte or embryo parameters Keikha et al. [33] and Parvanov et al. [46] reported increased embryo formation without documented pregnancies, while Barad et al. [6] reported no statistically significant changes in oocyte quality or pregnancy rates following PRP administration.

Discussion

The World Health Organisation recognises female infertility as a significant global public health issue. Since 2005, more than 1 million IVF cycles have been performed annually worldwide [1, 72]. In women with diminished ovarian reserve or POI, the severely diminished follicular pool, impaired folliculogenesis, and impaired ovarian microenvironment result in a suboptimal response to conventional ovarian stimulation, posing a persistent clinical challenge. Consequently, standard assisted reproductive approaches often fail to yield adequate outcomes, highlighting the need for strategies that target the underlying ovarian dysfunction. In this context, the concept of ovarian rejuvenation offers a novel therapeutic paradigm, aiming to restore intrinsic ovarian function rather than bypass it through oocyte retrieval alone. Emerging interventions, such as in vitro activation, autologous stem cell ovarian transplantation, and intraovarian injection of PRP, are aimed at reawakening dormant follicles or stimulating the ovarian niche, thereby enhancing folliculogenesis and potentially improving reproductive outcomes in these challenging cases [51].

Following the identification of postnatal ovarian stem cells (OSCs), the concept of ovarian regeneration emerged, challenging the long-held belief that females are born with a finite pool of oocytes [32]. Subsequent experimental and human studies have demonstrated the presence of OSC-like cells with limited proliferative and differentiation capacity in adult and postmenopausal ovaries [67]. Animal models have also emphasised the crucial role of the ovarian microenvironment, showing that aged germ cells can produce functional oocytes within a supportive environment [42]. Building on this, White et al. [69] isolated OSCs from adult human ovaries and reported in vitro oocyte formation and folliculogenesis in xenografted human tissue, highlighting the feasibility of reactivating dormant germline cells under appropriate stimulatory conditions [69]. Disruption of the ovarian niche, particularly due to impaired angiogenesis, is now recognised as a central contributor to ovarian ageing and a target for regenerative strategies, particularly in cases of DOR and POI. Cho et al. (2020) emphasised that inadequate vascular development within the ovarian stroma and follicles significantly contributes to POI pathogenesis and age-related ovarian decline [13].Wang et al. [68] provided further human genetic evidence linking microvascular insufficiency to ovarian dysfunction in POI, demonstrating that lower systemic VEGF levels were causally associated with increased POI risk [68]. Clinically, a recent study in 2024 reported altered VEGF levels in both serum and follicular fluid among women undergoing IVF, particularly in those with reduced oocyte yield. Poor responders demonstrated impaired follicular vascular dynamics, including hypoxic conditions and disturbed perifollicular microcirculation [48].Collectively, these findings underscore the role of vascular insufficiency as a key contributor to ovarian dysfunction in patients with DOR/POI. This provides a mechanistic rationale for therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring angiogenic balance, such as intraovarian PRP, which delivers a concentrated pool of bioactive factors, including VEGF, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and IGF-1, that may enhance ovarian perfusion, support follicular recruitment, and promote activation of endogenous stem cells.

Folliculogenesis is driven by several growth factors, including TGF-β1, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), VEGF, and PDGF [47]. Women with poor ovarian function often lack growth factors in their ovaries [48]. In women with POI or DOR, the molecular network controlling angiogenesis is significantly disrupted [31]. The regenerative potential of platelets, along with their enrichment with growth factors from the TGF‑β1 superfamily, suggests their capacity to rebalance the disturbed angiogenetic process [23].

Experimental studies support the regenerative potential of PRP in human ovarian tissue. Growth factor supplementation has been associated with successful live births from cryopreserved ovarian tissue, and PRP-enriched media improve follicular survival and expansion in both fresh and vitrified samples [12, 29]. Collectively, this experimental evidence reinforces the hypothesis that PRP creates a supportive microenvironment for early folliculogenesis, highlighting its translational promise for fertility-restoring interventions in patients with diminished ovarian reserve or post-cancer treatment infertility [5]. These findings have driven interest in intraovarian PRP for women with poor ovarian prognosis, including POI, menopause, and recurrent IVF failure [30, 45, 54, 58]. However, current clinical evidence is limited to small, heterogeneous case series with inconsistent outcomes, precluding routine clinical application.

With respect to ovarian reserve markers, changes in serum AMH following PRP administration were inconsistently reported across studies, with variable magnitude and durability, suggesting that AMH responses may reflect transient alterations in follicular activity rather than sustained restoration of ovarian reserve. Similarly, FSH responses were heterogeneous, with decreases reported in some cohorts and no change or increases in others, indicating that FSH does not represent a consistent indicator of PRP response. Beyond hormonal and follicular parameters, several studies in this systematic review suggest that intraovarian PRP may exert its clinical effects primarily through qualitative improvements in oocyte competence rather than quantitative increases in follicle number or oocyte yield. In this context, PRP appeared to enhance oocyte maturation, with multiple studies reporting higher proportions of mature oocytes alongside a positive trend toward clinical pregnancy and live birth outcomes. Notably, these outcomes were not uniform and appeared to be influenced by several modifying factors, including baseline ovarian reserve, patient age, and the need for repeated PRP treatment cycles—often up to three—in women with long-standing ovarian dysfunction. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that PRP may act as a biological modulator of the ovarian microenvironment, with its potential clinical impact dependent on careful patient selection and protocol optimisation rather than a single universal treatment effect.

While this systematic review focused on ovarian function and clinical outcomes following intraovarian PRP in women with DOR or POI, marked heterogeneity in PRP preparation and administration was observed across studies. This variability highlights the need to examine methodological differences to improve reproducibility and guide standardisation and patient selection for clinical use. Accordingly, the following sections examine PRP preparation protocols, administration techniques, and proposed clinical frameworks aimed at supporting more consistent and controlled application in fertility care.

Platelet-rich plasma preparation methods and standardisation considerations

Centrifugation protocols and impact on platelet-rich plasma quality

The method of PRP preparation is a decisive factor that influences cellular composition and regenerative efficacy. Variations in centrifugation protocols affect platelet yield, purity, and structural integrity, modulating the therapeutic potential of PRP-based interventions. Both manual and automated preparation techniques were examined, comparing three methods based on centrifugal force and duration.

PRP is prepared via differential centrifugation, separating blood components by specific gravity. Two approaches are used: open systems, which involve manual handling and contamination risk, and closed systems, which use European Conformity-marked commercial devices to maintain sterility and standardization (Andia and Maffulli, 2018). Following venipuncture, blood is subjected to either single- or double-spin centrifugation with manufacturer-defined parameters to optimise yield and consistency. PRP preparation devices are broadly classified into (i) anticoagulant-containing tubes for standard centrifugation, (ii) prefilled anticoagulant tubes used in closed systems, and (iii) syringe-based systems with integrated anticoagulants [37]. The resulting layers consist of red blood cells, a middle PRP fraction, and platelet-poor plasma. Some protocols include chemical activators (such as thrombin and calcium chloride); however, endogenous activation upon injection may be sufficient, rendering external agents unnecessary [4].

Manual double-spin methods consistently show superior performance over automated systems, yielding higher platelet enrichment, reduced inter-sample variability, and more effective depletion of leukocytes and erythrocytes [27]. These are particularly advantageous in minimising inflammatory components and ensuring formulation consistency. A strong correlation between manual preparation and the whole-blood platelet count further underscores its reproducibility and clinical reliability.

Among the manual protocols (Fig. 4), Method 1, involving lower centrifugal speeds (1000 rpm followed by 800 rpm), yielded the highest platelet concentration (3.50 × 10⁸/µL), representing a statistically significant 160% increase over baseline values. In contrast, Methods 2 and 3, employing higher centrifugal forces (2,400/3,600 and 1,400/1,600 rpm, respectively), achieved only moderate and non-significant increases, respectively [52]. The superior performance of Method 1 can be attributed to the reduced shear-induced platelet activation or loss during processing, thereby preserving platelet integrity and allowing for sustained growth factor release within the ovarian stroma.

Different methods can be used to prepare platelet-rich plasma (PRP), each involving a distinct protocol with specific centrifugation speeds and durations. These methods are designed to effectively separate and concentrate platelets from whole blood. The separation spin and concentration spin are the two key steps involved in each method

The absence of quantitative growth factor profiling limits definitive conclusions regarding its biological potency. Future research should incorporate molecular analyses, such as PDGF, VEGF, and TGF-β quantification, to validate functional bioactivity and inform evidence-based optimisation of PRP protocols.

Platelet activation in platelet-rich plasma: methods and clinical relevance

Platelet activation is a pivotal phase in PRP preparation, triggering controlled degranulation of alpha granules and enhancing the bioavailability of key growth factors central to its regenerative efficacy. Commonly used exogenous activators include collagen, adenosine diphosphate, thrombin (either human or bovine), calcium chloride, and calcium gluconate, used individually or in combination. Physical techniques, such as freeze–thaw cycles, have also been applied to promote platelet degranulation [24].

Calcium-based agents are the most used activators for intraovarian PRP administration and related regenerative applications. At least six studies, including those by Aflatoonian et al. [2], Parvanov et al. [46], and Melo et al. [38], reported using calcium gluconate or calcium chloride in a 1:9 ratio with PRP. These agents effectively stimulate the release of bioactive molecules such as PDGF, VEGF, and TGF-β, thereby enhancing the biological efficacy of the PRP preparation.

However, several studies did not report whether activation was performed, and quantitative measurements of growth factor concentrations post-activation were not conducted in any of these studies. This methodological gap contrasts with PRP protocols in orthopaedics and dermatology [34] wherein the growth factor content is often characterised and used to guide treatment dosing. The absence of growth factor profiling in reproductive medicine limits reproducibility and raises questions regarding the biological potency and consistency of PRP preparations. Moreover, the influence of PRP activation status on intraovarian PRP efficacy remains unclear. Further studies linking the key growth factor profiles to reproductive outcomes are needed to optimise and standardise protocols.

Platelet concentration thresholds and mechanistic basis of platelet-rich plasma efficacy

The therapeutic potential of intraovarian PRP appears to be strongly influenced by platelet concentration. A higher baseline platelet count may predict a more favourable ovarian response following PRP administration. Notably, Sills et al. [60] reported significantly higher baseline platelet levels among PRP responders than among non-responders (p<0.001), with corresponding increases in serum AMH concentrations [60]. These findings underscore the potential role of the platelet count as a predictive biomarker of treatment efficacy, supporting the optimisation of PRP formulations to ensure adequate platelet enrichment.

Considerable heterogeneity in the PRP composition was observed among the reviewed studies, with several trials omitting platelet concentration data. Such methodological variability may partially account for the differences in clinical outcomes and highlight the pressing need for standardisation. PRP intended for regenerative purposes should achieve a platelet concentration of up to 10 times higher than the baseline peripheral blood levels to maximise biological activity.

The clinical improvements in patients with diminished ovarian reserves may be attributed to regenerative mechanisms similar to those involved in tissue repair processes. The similarities between postovulatory and wound healing suggest that PRP acts through shared molecular pathways involving cytokine-mediated signalling, angiogenesis, and stromal remodelling. Further research is warranted to elucidate the minimum effective platelet concentration required to induce functional ovarian changes and establish consistent evidence-based preparation protocols for reproductive applications.

Location and volume

Considerable heterogeneity was observed in the volume, site, and technique of PRP administration into the ovarian tissue among the clinical studies. The injection volumes across trials ranged from 0.4 to >4 mL per ovary, with 2–4 mL being the most frequently used volume. Targeting strategies also varied, from single-site intracortical injections to multiple subcortical or stromal deposits. Transvaginal ultrasound-guided injection was the most common approach; nonetheless, variations in the needle gauge, puncture sites, and PRP dispersion techniques further complicated standardisation. These inconsistencies, along with incomplete reporting in several studies, make meaningful comparisons challenging. Additionally, the absence of a consensus on the optimal anatomical target zone, whether cortical, subcortical, or stromal, may affect PRP bioavailability, retention, and therapeutic efficacy. Until robust comparative data are available, no definitive recommendations can be made regarding the optimal injection volume or site.

Injection site optimisation and procedural safety

Precise PRP deposition within ovary is essential for efficacy and requires accurate needle placement, controlled dispersion, and careful withdrawal to prevent leakage and preserve vascular integrity. Transvaginal ultrasound-guided injection is the preferred method due to its minimally invasive nature and real-time visualisation, enabling targeted delivery to the ovarian cortex and subcortex with favourable regenerative outcomes. Laparoscopic injection is reserved for selected cases during concurrent pelvic surgery but is limited by invasiveness and the need for general anaesthesia [49]. A 17G lumen needle is commonly used,however, it is associated with leakage particularly in atrophic ovaries. While narrower needles may reduce leakage, they are prone to blockage, particularly when injected into fibrotic ovarian tissue, frequently observed poor responders [60].

To enhance therapeutic efficacy, a multisite injection approach, delivering small volumes of activated PRP into multiple points across the cortical, subcortical, and stromal regions, has shown greater potential than single-site administration. This technique enables a more uniform enrichment of the ovarian microenvironment with regenerative growth factors, maximising tissue exposure and enhancing the likelihood of a clinical response. Subcapsular deposition has also been linked to preserved vascular integrity and minimal accumulation of peritoneal fluid. Despite these advantages, leakage remains a procedural challenge and is often related to ovarian atrophy or reduced tissue compliance. Standardisation of the injection technique, including optimal needle selection and distribution strategies, is crucial to mitigate this. Additionally, repeating the PRP procedure over two consecutive cycles may provide a cumulative regenerative effect, better aligning with the natural timeline of antral follicle development, particularly in patients with diminished ovarian reserves or POI [11].

Limitation of the platelet-rich plasma protocol

While this systematic review highlights the growing interest in intraovarian PRP as a novel therapeutic approach, several important limitations must be acknowledged. lacked appropriate control groups capable of distinguishing treatment effects from procedure-related influences. In some studies, DOR and POI populations were combined within the same analytical cohorts, which may obscure condition-specific responses and contribute to heterogeneity in reported outcomes.

The wide age range of included patients represents an additional limitation, as age-related differences in ovarian biology may influence treatment responsiveness; future studies incorporating age-based stratification, particularly among women aged ≥ 40 years, may better elucidate subgroup-specific effects and clinical applicability.

PRP administration and treatment frequency constitute key limitations, as the optimal timing of injection appears to be a critical determinant of response and may require synchronisation with the ovarian cycle and the window preceding pre-ovulatory follicular development; however, this parameter remains inconsistently defined and applied across studies.

Although intraovarian PRP was generally well tolerated and some studies reported encouraging outcomes in selected subgroups, the current evidence does not support routine clinical application. Instead, PRP should be considered investigational and limited to highly selected patients within structured clinical or research frameworks. Well-designed randomised, multicentre trials using standardised administration timing and clinically meaningful endpoints are required to validate efficacy, optimise protocols, and define long-term safety.

Materials and methods

Study design and PRISMA 2020 compliance

This systematic review was registered on PROSPERO (registration number CRD420251237523), it was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines (PRISMA 2020) [35]. A structured protocol was developed to identify and evaluate published clinical studies on intraovarian injection of PRP for DORor POI treatment. The study selection process followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and is illustrated in Fig. 2. All records were screened and reviewed by the author, and any uncertainties were resolved through repeated verification against the inclusion criteria. Only peer-reviewed clinical studies reporting original patient data were included, whereas conference abstracts, narrative reviews, and animal or laboratory investigations were excluded. The subsequent section details the search strategy, inclusion criteria, and data-extraction process.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search on the use of autologous PRP was conducted using Cochrane, Embase, PubMed, MEDLINE, NCBI, and Google Scholar databases. The search terms used included the following: (‘ovarian rejuvenation’ OR ‘ovary rejuvenation’) AND (‘intra-ovarian injection’ OR ‘ovarian injection’) AND (‘platelet-rich plasma’ OR ‘PRP’) AND ‘intraovarian injection’ AND (‘in vitro fertilisation’ OR ‘IVF’). Additional combinations, such as ‘PRP and fertility’, ‘platelet-rich plasma and infertility’, ‘PRP and ovaries’, and ‘poor ovarian reserve’, were also applied. The selection of studies focused on patients categorised as (i) poor responders and (ii) DOR based on standardised diagnostic criteria. Notably, combining the data from the included studies into a meta-analysis was not feasible.due to the marked variability in study design, patient selection, and mode of intervention. Outcome reporting lacked standardisation, with many studies presenting results narratively or qualitatively, often without uniform numerical metrics. Data were frequently reported as medians or without accompanying variance measures. These methodological discrepancies precluded statistical pooling and justified the adoption of a narrative systematic review approach accordance with PRISMA 2020 recommendations. Consequently, a structured systematic search was conducted to obtain all relevant articles on the use of intraovarian PRP injection for rejuvenating ovarian tissue published between 2020 and 2023.

Eligibility criteria

To ensure methodological consistency and relevance to the research objective, studies meeting the specific eligibility requirements were included. Eligible studies were peer-reviewed empirical investigations involving human participants and published in English. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) use of intraovarian PRP protocol; (b) reporting of ovarian reserve outcomes; (c) description of PRP preparation methods; and (d) evaluation of any related intervention effects on ovarian function or fertility parameters. No restrictions were imposed regarding study location, ethnicity, or race of the study population.

Furthermore, studies were required to include patients diagnosed with DOR or POI based on recognised diagnostic frameworks, including the ESHRE guidelines, American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) recommendations, and POSEIDON [17, 22]. Studies were excluded if they involved iatrogenic POI resulting from chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgical interventions; included fewer than 10 patients; were published only as conference abstracts or review articles; were conducted on animals; were not published in English; or lacked a clear focus on intraovarian PRP methodology or outcomes. The minimum sample size threshold was applied to reduce potential bias associated with small-scale case reports or pilot studies and enhance the reliability and generalisability of the review findings.

Study selection and data extraction

Studies were selected by a reviewer, who screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of all retrieved articles for eligibility. Clinical trials were considered eligible if they adhered to the diagnostic criteria for established by the ESHRE and ASRM consensus meeting [17, 22] at least two of the following: (i) aged ≥40 years or with known risk factors for DOR (Turner syndrome, ovarian surgery, or cancer treatment history); (ii) previous DOR to stimulation protocol during an IVF cycle, defined as ≤3 oocytes retrieved with a standard protocol; and (iii) abnormal ovarian reserve testing, indicated by an AFC of <5–7 or AMH level <0.5–1.1 ng/mL. Eligible studies included phase II clinical trials or retrospective analyses that investigated the use of intraovarian PRP injections to improve ovarian function. Additionally, studies were required to report sufficient outcome data, including sex hormone levels and other clinical parameters. The data extracted included author name, year, country, sample size, study design, patient characteristics, PRP preparation and administration protocols, and outcome measures such as hormonal responses, oocyte and embryo metrics, pregnancy outcomes, and live births. Because most studies presented hormonal and clinical outcomes as relative percentage changes or qualitative improvements, data were extracted and summarised in the same format to maintain fidelity to the source reports.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed by the author using standardised PRISMA-compliant tools. Based on the extracted data, for non-randomised and observational studies, the the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) was applied whereas randomised controlled trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool [28, 62]. Each study was evaluated across the following domains: bias due to confounding factors, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Studies were categorised as having low risk of bias when no domain was judged to be at serious risk; as moderate risk when a single domain showed potential bias; and as serious or critical risk when high or critical bias was identified in more than one domain. (Table is provided upon request).

Conclusions

Intraovarian PRP therapy is a promising experimental adjunctive approach for patients with DOR and POI. Early clinical findings suggest potential improvements in ovarian reserve markers and reproductive outcomes; however, variability in PRP preparation methods, patient selection, and treatment protocols currently prevents widespread standardisation. The proposed clinical protocol presented in this study provide a framework for the safe and responsible clinical application of PRP in MAR programs while acknowledging the experimental and heterogeneous status of this intervention. Standardised documentation, adherence to preparation and administration protocols, and prospective outcome tracking are essential for quality assurance and patient safety.

Further clinical research is strongly encouraged to refine candidate selection, establish dose–response relationships, and incorporate quantitative molecular profiling of key molecular profiling of key PRP constituents, such as VEGF, PDGF, and TGF-β, using validated immunoassays. Such data will help establish correlations between growth factor content and clinical response, guide dose optimisation, and support the standardisation of therapeutic formulations, thereby improving understanding of the mechanistic basis of PRP’s effects on ovarian tissue.

Until such data become available, PRP should be performed only under controlled clinical with appropriate informed consent and ethical oversight.
