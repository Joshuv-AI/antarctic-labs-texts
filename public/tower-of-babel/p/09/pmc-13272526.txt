Enhancing the diagnostic yield of myasthenia gravis using 7-Hz repetitive nerve stimulation: an 11-year retrospective study
Authors: Cheng Chi Hsieh, Yuan Ting Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the diagnostic utility of augmenting the conventional 3-Hz repetitive nerve stimulation test (RNST) with a 7-Hz protocol in patients with myasthenia gravis (MG).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 11 years of RNST data (2013−2023) from patients at a tertiary medical center. The diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value [PPV], and negative predictive value [NPV]) of the conventional 3-Hz protocol was compared against a combined 3-Hz plus 7-Hz protocol.ResultsAmong 311 MG patients (208 generalized MG [gMG]; 103 ocular MG [oMG]), adding 7-Hz stimulation significantly improved the sensitivity for gMG from 69.0% to 82.1% without compromising specificity (91.7%). Multivariable regression confirmed that the combined protocol was independently associated with higher diagnostic yield in gMG (OR = 2.28, p = 0.019). Using a within-group paired analysis, 7-Hz RNST showed superior sensitivity compared to 3-Hz RNST. This increased diagnostic yield was predominantly observed in the gMG subgroup. Notably, 15% of MG patients were positive exclusively at 7 Hz, with the highest rescue rate observed in proximal muscles.ConclusionIncorporating 7-Hz RNST into standard diagnostic algorithms substantially enhances the detection of gMG, particularly when conventional 3-Hz testing yields negative results in proximal muscles.SignificanceRNST protocol selection may be tailored by clinical phenotype to maximize sensitivity by adding 7 Hz RNST.Keywords: Myasthenia gravis, Repetitive nerve stimulation, 7-Hz stimulation, Sensitivity

Introduction

Repetitive nerve stimulation test (RNST) is a cornerstone in the electrodiagnostic evaluation of myasthenia gravis (MG), especially when advanced techniques like single-fiber electromyography (SFEMG) are unavailable. Standard protocols typically utilize low-frequency stimulation (2–3 Hz) to induce a decremental response in compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs). However, the sensitivity of 3-Hz RNST remains suboptimal, particularly in early or mild cases.

Drawing on our previous observation that a 7-Hz frequency may elicit more pronounced decremental responses by increasing synaptic demand (Sun & Lin, 2004), we implemented a combined 3-Hz and 7-Hz protocol in clinical practice five years ago. This study evaluates the 11-year diagnostic performance of this approach, specifically examining whether the addition of medium-frequency (7-Hz) stimulation can effectively “rescue” diagnoses that would otherwise be missed by conventional testing.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted at National Cheng Kung University Hospital in Tainan, Taiwan. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (Approval No. B-ER-113-178), with a waiver of informed consent granted due to the study's retrospective design and minimal risk to participants.

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Neurology, National Cheng Kung University Hospital, a tertiary medical center in Taiwan, between January 2013 and December 2023. Patients' medical records and RNST data were independently reviewed by two neurologists, including one neuromuscular specialist.

Patients were assigned to the MG group if they had a confirmed diagnosis of MG documented by a National Catastrophic Illness Certificate issued by the Taiwan National Health Insurance (NHI) system, either during follow-up at our hospital or prior to referral from another institution. In Taiwan, certification of MG as a catastrophic illness requires formal review by an expert committee and verification of supporting clinical, laboratory, and electrophysiological evidence.

To address potential incorporation bias, an abnormal RNST was not used as the sole diagnostic criterion. MG diagnosis in this study was defined using a clinical composite reference standard, consisting of characteristic clinical features together with objective supportive finding, including: (1) positive anti–acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody or anti–muscle-specific kinase (MuSK) antibody; (2) a positive ice-packing test; (3) a positive neostigmine test or (4) a clinical responsive to pyridostigmine or immunosuppressant. To enhance diagnostic certainty, patients were required to have at least six months of follow-up in our neurology clinic, during which the longitudinal clinical course and treatment decisions were consistent with a diagnosis of MG. Alternative diagnoses that may mimic MG (e.g., motor neuron disease, primary myopathies, and other relevant neuromuscular disorders) must be excluded based on clinical evaluation and ancillary testing. For patients who were seronegative or had unavailable antibody data, diagnostic confidence was further supported by documentation of MG-directed management during follow-up, including disease-specific interventions such as thymectomy or therapeutic plasma exchange.

MG patients were further classified as ocular myasthenia gravis (oMG) or generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) based on clinical symptoms at the time of RNST. Exclusion criteria were as follows: exclusive use of medium- to high-frequency RNST, testing of fewer than two muscles, non-standard stimulation frequencies, and repeated RNST examinations performed solely for insurance certificate renewal.

Electrophysiological protocol

RNST was carried out by three certified medical technologists randomly according to their shift schedule. Patients were advised to discontinue pyridostigmine for at least six hours before RNST. RNST was conducted by Nicolet Viking EDX system (Natus Medical Inc., Middleton, WI, USA). 3-Hz- and subsequently 7-Hz- RNST sequentially tested on abductor pollicis brevis (APB), deltoid or trapezius, and nasalis muscle with an at least 30 s rest interval between tests. The trapezius muscle was routinely tested, with the deltoid muscle used as an alternative site when technical factors or patient intolerance limited reliable recording. A decrement in the CMAP amplitude between the first and fourth/fifth response >10% was considered abnormal according to conventional laboratory criteria. (AAEM QAC, 2001b). Prior to Jan 2019, patients received only 3-Hz RNST. After Jan 2019, combined 3-Hz and 7-Hz stimulation protocol was implemented. A decrement of >10% was defined as abnormal for all tested muscles, a uniform threshold was used in this study to maintain consistency with our laboratory's routine practice and to allow direct retrospective comparison between the 3-Hz-only and combined 3-Hz/7-Hz protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel, SPSS software version 29.0 and Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). We calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) for 3-Hz RNST alone and combined 3-Hz- and 7-Hz-RNST. Subgroup analysis was performed for gMG and oMG. To compare the diagnostic sensitivities of the 3-Hz, 7-Hz, and combined 3 + 7-Hz protocols, a paired analysis was performed for patients who underwent both stimulation frequencies. McNemar's test was used to compare sensitivities, while the Youden index was calculated to evaluate overall diagnostic performance. Positivity rates across different muscles were also assessed. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were presented as median values with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non-normal distribution. To identify factors associated with RNST positivity, univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted, with p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 1421 patients underwent RNST during the 11-year study period. To ensure the homogeneity of the diagnostic protocols, 14 cases were excluded due to incomplete data or non-standard procedures. Among the remaining 1407 patients, 320 were formally diagnosed with MG, while 1087 served as non-MG controls. Within the MG cohort, nine cases were excluded because their RNSTs were conducted solely for administrative certification renewal during clinical remission.

Ultimately, 311 MG patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1), consisting of 208 patients with gMG (66.9%) and 103 with oMG (33.1%). In the entire MG cohort, serology profiles and antibody titers of the cohort did not differ between the 3 Hz and the 3/7-Hz group (Supplementary Table 1). Because the main increase in diagnostic yield was observed in gMG, baseline clinical and demographic characteristics were further examined in this subgroup. Among gMG patients, the disease severity (MGFA classification), AChR Ab titer and the demographic characteristics, including sex, and BMI, were well-balanced between the 3-Hz and the combined 3/7-Hz groups, providing a robust baseline for comparing diagnostic yields (Table 1).

With respect to procedural tolerability, the frequency of incomplete studies was similar between the 3-Hz protocol and the combined 3/7-Hz protocol (1.1% vs. 0.9%, p = 0.793). Among incomplete studies, pain was the predominant reason for discontinuation. Notably, intolerable testing most commonly involved proximal and facial muscles (Supplementary Table S3).

Diagnostic yield of RNST with various stimulation frequency

The overall diagnostic performance of the two protocols was compared (Table 2). In the 3-Hz-only group (N = 141), 84 MG patients (59.6%) showed a positive result. Sensitivity was 69.0% for gMG and 21.4% for oMG. The overall specificity was 93.5%. In the combined 3-Hz and 7-Hz group (N = 170), a total of 92 MG patients (54.1%) were positive to either stimulation. Sensitivity was 82.1% for gMG and 18.6% for oMG. The overall specificity was 91.7% (Table 2).

To eliminate the confounding effects of differing gMG and oMG proportions across protocols, a paired comparison of the diagnostic performance of 3-Hz, 7-Hz, and combined 3/7-Hz RNST was performed among patients who underwent both stimulation frequencies. In the overall MG cohort (N = 170), both 7-Hz alone and the combined 3/7-Hz RNST demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity than 3-Hz alone (52.4% and 54.1% vs. 38.8%, respectively; both p < 0.001). Furthermore, the combined protocol yielded the highest overall diagnostic utility (Youden Index: 0.458 vs. 0.338 for 3-Hz RNST; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis revealed that this diagnostic advantage was primarily driven by the gMG group (N = 95), in which the combined protocol achieved a sensitivity of 82.1% and a Youden Index of 0.667—both significantly higher than 3-Hz alone (61.1% and 0.501, respectively; p < 0.001). In the oMG group (N = 75), sensitivities remained low across all strategies (Table 4).

RNST positivity of muscle group

RNST positive rates varied in different muscles. Among gMG patients undergoing 3-Hz testing, proximal muscles (trapezius/deltoid) showed the highest positivity rate (58.4%), followed by the nasalis muscle (42.5%) and APB (32.7%) (Table 5). In patients underwent 3-Hz- and 7-Hz-test, the trend of positivity (trapezius/deltoid, nasalis and APB were 66.3%, 47.4% and 25.2%, respectively) was similar to that of 3-Hz only (p > 0.05, chi-square test).

The “rescue” effect of 7-Hz stimulation

The clinical value of the 7-Hz protocol is most evident in the “exclusively 7-Hz positive” subgroup, which accounted for 15.3% (n = 26) of the entire MG cohort who underwent 3/7 Hz protocol (26/170 = 15.3%) and 21.1% of gMG patients (20/95 = 21.1%). In contrast, only 1.7% of patients were positive at 3 Hz alone (Table 5). Within the 7-Hz rescue group, the trapezius and deltoid muscles exhibited the highest diagnostic gain, suggesting that medium-frequency stimulation may better unmask neuromuscular junction fatigue in proximal muscle groups. These findings suggest that a 7-Hz add-on protocol can prevent the misclassification of MG patients as false negatives, thereby facilitating earlier treatment initiation.

Discussion

The present 11-year retrospective study demonstrates that augmenting the standard 3-Hz RNST with 7-Hz stimulation significantly enhances the diagnostic sensitivity for gMG while maintaining robust specificity. Our findings indicate that a medium-frequency protocol can effectively “rescue” a substantial proportion of patients (15.3% of entire MG cohort and 21.1% of gMG patients) who would otherwise be misidentified as false negatives under conventional 3-Hz testing. These findings support the potential role of 7-Hz stimulation as a practical adjunct to conventional 3-Hz RNST in routine clinical practice.

Beyond the observed increase in sensitivity, the robust diagnostic value of the combined 3/7-Hz protocol is further substantiated by our multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, including sex, and BMI, the combined 3/7-Hz protocol remained an independent predictor for a positive RNST result in generalized MG patients (OR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.14–4.55, p = 0.019; Table 3). Notably, while higher BMI is often clinically suspected to interfere with surface electrode recordings and potentially affect RNST results, it did not reach statistical significance as an independent factor in our model (p = 0.115). This finding reinforces the conclusion that the improved diagnostic yield is primarily attributable to the enhanced physiological demand elicited by the 7-Hz stimulation rather than variations in patient demographics or physical characteristics.

Although the overall sensitivity was lower in the 3-Hz and 7-Hz group (54.1%) compared to the 3-Hz group (59.6%), this was primarily due to a significantly higher proportion of ocular MG (oMG) patients in the 3 + 7-Hz cohort (44.1% vs. 19.8%). Since RNST sensitivity is low in oMG, subgroup analysis of gMG patients provides a more accurate reflection of the 7-Hz protocol's diagnostic utility.

An additional consideration is the potential temporal bias arising from the fact that the two RNST protocols were applied during different time periods (3-Hz testing before 2019 and the combined 3-Hz/7-Hz protocol after 2019). However, both cohorts were derived from the same tertiary center and evaluated using a consistent diagnostic framework throughout the study period. Moreover, the baseline clinical characteristics of MG patients and the spectrum of non-MG control diagnoses remained largely comparable between the two periods. Nevertheless, residual temporal bias cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting our findings.

Pathophysiological insights and frequency selection

The observed superiority of 7-Hz stimulation aligns with the physiological principles of neuromuscular transmission. Neuromuscular transmission depends on a safety factor, where the amount of acetylcholine released per impulse exceeds the threshold required to trigger a muscle action potential (Wood & Slater, 2001). Higher stimulation frequencies increase synaptic demand by accelerating depletion of the readily releasable pool of acetylcholine vesicles. In patients with a borderline safety factor, 3-Hz stimulation may fail to elicit a significant decrement, whereas 7-Hz stimulation can unmask these latent defects.

A modeling study by Miralles et al. provides mechanistic support for our findings, suggesting that higher stimulation frequencies restrict the time available for the readily releasable pool (RRP) to recover from depletion between pulses. During the early stages of the stimulation train, before Ca2+-dependent replenishment mechanisms can fully compensate for this loss, this temporal constraint leads to a more pronounced reduction in release probability. Consequently, a higher proportion of borderline neuromuscular junctions fail to reach the threshold for action potential generation, resulting in more clinically detectable decremental changes during RNST (Francesc Miralles, 2016).

The selection of 7 Hz as an intermediate stimulation frequency is supported by earlier observations regarding the cumulative probabilities of decremental responses (Sun & Lin, 2004). While the probability of detecting a decrement increases gradually at lower frequencies, it reaches a distinct plateau at higher frequencies; notably, the cumulative probability does not increase further when stimulation exceeds 7 Hz. Therefore, 7-Hz represents the most efficient balance between maximizing diagnostic yield and minimizing patient discomfort or technical interference associated with high-frequency stimulation. Our results support this concept, showing that the addition of 7-Hz stimulation increased the sensitivity for gMG from 69.0% to 82.1%.

The importance of muscle selection

Our data reinforce the critical role of muscle selection in RNST protocols. Proximal muscles, specifically the trapezius and deltoid, demonstrated the highest positivity rates across both protocols (Amandusson, Elf, Grindlund, & Punga, 2017; Bou Ali et al., 2017; Lamb & Rubin, 2020). Interestingly, the 7-Hz rescue effect was most pronounced in these proximal muscles, where 75% of exclusively 7-Hz positive cases were detected. This distribution likely reflects the typical disease burden in gMG and suggests that clinicians should prioritize proximal and facial muscles (like the nasalis) to maximize diagnostic yield, particularly when a patient's tolerance for an extended protocol is limited.

Recent recommendations from a European consensus group emphasize that testing multiple muscles is a key strategy to improve the diagnostic yield of neuromuscular junction disorders (Tankisi et al., 2025). In our study, RNST was routinely performed across distal, facial, and proximal muscles (APB, nasalis, and trapezius/deltoid), which aligns with this recommendation. Importantly, the addition of 7-Hz stimulation should be viewed as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, multi-site testing, providing an additional diagnostic yield in patients negative on conventional 3-Hz stimulation.

Clinical implications for ocular MG

Consistent with previous literature (AAEM QAC, 2001a), the sensitivity of RNST for oMG remained low at approximately 20%, regardless of the stimulation frequency used. The limited diagnostic utility in oMG likely stems from the localized nature of the disease and the inherent difficulty in stimulating the small, affected orbital muscles using surface electrodes (Witoonpanich, Dejthevaporn, Sriphrapradang, & Pulkes, 2011). For these patients, more sensitive modalities such as SFEMG remain the gold standard.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the clinical significance of our findings, this study has limitations. Its retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Furthermore, the lack of concurrent SFEMG data prevents a direct correlation between 7-Hz RNST results and the most sensitive electrophysiological measure available. Future prospective, multi-center trials are warranted to validate the 7-Hz protocol as a standard diagnostic tool in varied clinical settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our 11-year study underscores the significant diagnostic value of incorporating 7-Hz medium-frequency stimulation into standard RNST protocols for MG. The dual-frequency approach (3-Hz and 7-Hz) markedly enhances diagnostic sensitivity for gMG without compromising the high specificity required for clinical practice. Our findings suggest that 7-Hz stimulation can capture decremental responses in patients who yield negative results at conventional 3-Hz frequencies, providing a crucial “rescue” mechanism that may prevent delayed diagnosis and treatment. Furthermore, the data reinforce the diagnostic priority of proximal muscles, specifically the trapezius and deltoid, which exhibited the highest positivity and rescue rates. These results support the implementation of a combined 3-Hz and 7-Hz RNST protocol as a more robust and reliable electrophysiological screening strategy, particularly in clinical settings where access to advanced diagnostic modalities like SFEMG is limited.
