USP48 promotes tumour progression through the deubiquitination and stabilization of DBF4 in clear cell renal carcinoma
Authors: Lingfeng Zhu, Liuyan Chen, Lvying Wu, Na Lin, Wenyi Deng, Kuanyin Wang, Yaoyao Liang, Jianan Zhang, Jin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe upregulation of DBF4 is correlated with a poor prognosis in patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), yet the mechanisms governing the expression and protein stability of DBF4 remain unknown. Ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), known regulators of protein stability in diverse biological contexts, have been implicated in ccRCC, although their specific functions have not been fully elucidated.MethodsIn this study, 40 USPs were screened to identify potential stabilizers of DBF4. Following the selection of USP48, knockdown experiments were performed, and various assays, including CCK-8, colony formation, apoptosis, cell cycle, and migration assays, were carried out to investigate the impact of USP48 on ccRCC cells. The expression levels and correlation of USP48 and DBF4 in clinical sample were analyzed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. Their protein expression was then further validated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database and our previously established ccRCC cohort. Additionally, coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) and deubiquitination assays were performed to confirm the interaction between USP48 and the DBF4 protein. A rescue experiment involving the overexpression of DBF4 in ccRCC cells was conducted. Furthermore, the effect of USP48 on the growth of ccRCC cells in vivo was assessed through subcutaneous injection of ccRCC cells into mice.ResultsWe initially screened 40 ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs) and found that USP48 exhibited the highest luciferase activity in assays using luciferase-tagged DBF4. Correspondingly, knockdown of USP48 reduced DBF4 expression, which subsequently inhibited ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while also inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Clinical sample analysis revealed that both USP48 and DBF4 were significantly upregulated in ccRCC tissues, and their expression levels showed a significant positive correlation (R = 0.3504, p < 0.0001). The results of the co-IP and ubiquitination assays demonstrated that USP48 can interact with and stabilize DBF4 by modulating its ubiquitination. Overexpression of DBF4 in USP48-deficient ccRCC cells effectively reversed the deficiencies resulting from USP48 knockdown. Additionally, the xenograft assay results showed that USP48 deficiency hinders the development of ccRCC in vivo.ConclusionOur research reveals that USP48 facilitates ccRCC progression by increasing the stability of DBF4, suggesting that USP48 is a promising therapeutic target for ccRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04333-z.Keywords: Clear cell renal carcinoma, USP48, DBF4, Ubiquitination

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common type of kidney cancer that originates from tubular epithelial cells and accounts for approximately 75% of all cases of kidney cancer [1, 2]. Due to the lack of specific biomarkers and obvious symptoms of ccRCC, many patients already have metastasis at the time of diagnosis. ccRCC patients with distant metastasis have a poor prognosis and a high mortality rate, and effective treatment methods are lacking. Therefore, novel druggable targets for ccRCC treatment are urgently needed.

DBF4 zinc finger (DBF4) is a conserved protein involved in the regulation of DNA replication in eukaryotic cells [3]. It is a regulatory subunit of the DNA replication-dependent protein kinase complex (DDK), which phosphorylates the minichromosome maintenance (MCM) complex, an essential component of the replicative helicase, to initiate DNA replication [4]. DBF4 activates the MCM complex by interacting with CDC7 [5]. We found that DBF4 is upregulated in ccRCC tumour tissue and that higher DBF4 expression portends a worse prognosis in ccRCC patients [6]. DBF4 knockdown induces cell cycle arrest and inhibits ccRCC cell proliferation and migration [6]. Therefore, targeted DBF4 inhibitors are a potential strategy for targeted ccRCC therapy. However, although the function of DBF4 is well defined, the mechanism that controls DBF4 expression and protein stability is unclear.

Ubiquitination is a posttranslational modification that regulates a diverse range of biological processes, including DNA damage repair and cell cycle modulation [7–9]. Ubiquitin ligases conjugate ubiquitin moieties to target proteins, while deubiquitinases, such as ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), cleave ubiquitin moieties from proteins to increase their stability [10]. Accumulating evidence shows that USPs are key regulators of cancers. For instance, USP5 promotes non-small cell lung cancer progression by stabilizing PD-L1 and also increases ovarian cancer cell proliferation by deubiquitinating HDAC2 [11, 12]. Furthermore, USP10 contributes to hepatocellular carcinoma progression by stabilizing YAP/TAZ [13]. USPs have also been found to play roles in ccRCC. For example, USP13 and USP37 promote ccRCC development by deubiquitinating ZHX2 and HIF2α, respectively [14, 15]. However, whether other USPs are involved in the regulation of DBF4 expression during the development of ccRCC remains to be further investigated.

In this study, we screened 40 USPs that could stabilize DBF4 and discovered that ubiquitin-specific protease 48 (USP48) was the most effective DBF4 stabilizer. Knockdown of USP48 in ccRCC cells reduced the DBF4 protein level, resulting in decreased proliferation, increased apoptosis, and reduced migration and invasion, as well as cell cycle arrest, in vitro. These effects were reversed by the overexpression of DBF4. USP48 was shown to interact with and deubiquitinate DBF4, thereby increasing its stability. Furthermore, the xenograft assay results confirmed that USP48 deficiency inhibited ccRCC tumour growth in vivo. These findings suggest that USP48 may play a crucial role in controlling ccRCC.

development and may be a promising therapeutic target for this disease.

Methods

Cell lines

The 786-O, A-498, Caki-2, and HK-2 cell lines were obtained from Xiamen Immocell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China) and authenticated through short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. These cells were cultured in either Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium or minimal essential medium (MEM) (Gibco, NY, USA), both supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China). All cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored using quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis.

Plasmids

The cDNA sequences of USP48, USP38, and USP35 were individually inserted into the pCDH-HA plasmid, resulting in the construction of three plasmids named HA-USP48, HA-USP38, and HA-USP35, respectively. Additionally, the cDNA sequence of DBF4 was inserted into both the pLV-Flag plasmid and the pmirGLO plasmid, resulting in the construction of two plasmids named Flag-DBF4 and DBF4-Nluc, respectively. A short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence targeting USP48 was inserted into the pLKO.1-puro plasmid, and the resulting plasmid was named shUSP48. All the primers used for the cloning process are listed in Table S1. The plasmid encoding His-ubiquitin was purchased from Xiamen Anti-HeLa Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Xiamen, China).

Mouse model

Six- to eight-week-old BALB/C nude mice (male, weighing ~ 20 g) were used to establish the 786-O tumour model. Each mouse was injected with a combination of PBS, Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA), and either 1 × 107 USP48-deficient 786-O cells or control 786-O cells. The tumour volume was measured every 4 days beginning on day 24. The mice were euthanized on day 44, and the tumours were excised for further analysis. The mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility, and all the procedures were carried out at the Animal Experiment Center.

Screening of USPs

HEK-293T cells overexpressing luciferase-tagged DBF4 (DBF4-Nluc) were transfected with 40 USPs separately. The cells were incubated for 48 h, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using an Elispot immunSpot® S5 UV analyser.

3-[4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

A-498 and 786-O cells were transfected with either the negative control shRNA (shNC) or shUSP48 plasmid. After 48 h, the cells were resuspended, pelleted, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. MTT reagent (Yeasen) was added to each well at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was removed, and 100 µL of DMSO (Sigma, Michigan, USA) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. Finally, the optical density (OD) of each well at 490 nm was measured and recorded.

Annexin V-FITC staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Cellular apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 minutes and rinsed twice with PBS prior to the addition of Annexin V-FITC staining solution, propidium iodide (PI) staining solution, and binding buffer. The cell suspension was gently mixed and incubated in the dark at 25°C for 20 minutes prior to quantification of apoptosis by flow cytometry. To evaluate apoptosis in xenograft tumour samples, a TUNEL assay was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions. Initially, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and permeabilized, followed by incubation in TUNEL working solution (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) at 37°C for 2 hours. After washing, the sections were treated with 3% H2O2/methanol, incubated with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) working solution, counterstained with haematoxylin solution, dehydrated, and mounted for further analysis.

7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) assay

Cells were detached with trypsin containing 0.25% EDTA and fixed with prechilled 95% ethanol at -20 °C for 12 h. After this, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with a solution containing 20 mg/mL 7-AAD (Beyotime Biotechnology) in PBS at 37 °C for 10 min. The cells were then analysed using flow cytometry, and the data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

Colony formation assay

Cell culture and colony counting were performed as follows: A-498 and 786-O cells were seeded at a density of 1,000 cells per 6 cm dish, and the medium was replaced every 3 days. After two weeks of culture, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 95% ethanol for 5 min at 25 °C. The fixed cells were stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution (Sigma) for 8 min and were then photographed and counted using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

Cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper compartments of transwell chambers with 8 μm pores (Corning) at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. For the invasion assay, 100 µL of a 1:8 dilution of Matrigel was added to each upper compartment, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Each lower compartment was filled with 600 µL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic solution. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2, the upper compartments were washed three times with PBS. Cells that migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 95% ethanol for 10 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 10 min, washed three times with PBS, air-dried, photographed, and counted using ImageJ software. Each condition was analysed in triplicate.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). cDNA synthesis was conducted using Tli RNaseH Plus and Perfect Real Time (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For qPCR, we employed the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system with specific primers designed for the target genes. The comparative threshold cycle method was utilized to analyse the data. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing a phosphatase inhibitor on ice for 30 min. The lysates were sonicated using a Bioruptor for 10 min (30 s on, 30 s off). Proteins were extracted, and the protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Cat. No. PA115-02; TIANGEN Biotechnology, Beijing, China). The proteins were then denatured by incubation at 95 °C for 10 min and loaded onto 10% SDS‒PAGE gels (20 µg per well). Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for 1.5 h, and electrophoretic transfer was carried out at 300 mA for 2.5 h. Subsequently, the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat (skim) milk for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies (Table S3) diluted in 1% TBST buffer overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated with a secondary antibody solution for 1 h at 25 °C. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit and a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system.

Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP)

786-O and A-498 cells were cotransfected with the Flag-DBF4 and HA-USP48 plasmids. After 48 h, the cells were collected, lysed in IP buffer, and sonicated for 15 min. Then, the cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatants were collected. HA-tagged or Flag-tagged beads were added to the supernatant, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The proteins were subsequently eluted from the beads for Western blot analysis.

H&E staining

Excised 786-O tumours were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 25 °C for 24 h and were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5 μm thick sections. The sections were stained with haematoxylin solution for 4 min, briefly treated with 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol, rinsed with tap water, and then subjected to bluing with 0.6% ammonia water. After the sections were rinsed with tap water, eosin solution was applied for 3 min. The sections were subsequently dehydrated, cleared, mounted with neutral resin, and visualized using an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Five-micron-thick paraffin sections were rehydrated and rinsed with PBST. After antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated with 5% goat serum for 15 min at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBST, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG for 20 min at room temperature. The sections were rinsed with PBST, and DAB (catalogue number: P0203, Beyotime) was used for colour development. After counterstaining with haematoxylin (catalogue number: ST2067-5 g, Beyotime) and dehydration, the sections were observed and photographed with an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan) in bright field mode (100× or 200× magnification).

Data acquisition and expression analysis

To investigate the expression pattern of USP48 in clinical samples, RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical information of ccRCC patients and healthy individuals were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. The combined transcriptomic data were downloaded through the UCSC Xena platform (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). To ensure comparability across datasets, the raw gene expression values were normalized and transformed into a log2(TPM + 0.001) format. This combined dataset was utilized to evaluate the differential expression of USP48 and DBF4, as well as their transcriptomic correlation in ccRCC. Furthermore, protein expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) from distinct sources. USP48 expression was assessed using IHC data from The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/), while DBF4 protein levels were evaluated through IHC data from our previously established ccRCC cohort [6].

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0) was used for graphing and for conducting statistical analyses. The significance of differences between two groups of parametric data was evaluated using unpaired Student’s t test. The Western blot data were analysed and quantified using ImageJ software. All values are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs): * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.

Results

USP48 increases the stability of the DBF4 protein

DBF4 protein expression showed significant elevation in ccRCC cell lines (A498, Caki-2, and 786-O) compared to the normal renal proximal tubule cell line HK-2 (Fig. 1A-B). To systematically identify USPs that may regulate DBF4 stability, we overexpressed DBF4-Nluc in HEK-293T cells and transfected these cells with 40 USPs individually. Luciferase activity was strongest in cells overexpressing USP48, USP38, or USP35, indicating that these USPs are the 3 most potent DBF4 protein stabilizers (Fig. 1C). Consistently, the Western blot results revealed that HEK-293T cells overexpressing USP48 had the highest level of DBF4-Nluc (Fig. 1D and E). To further confirm that the DBF4 protein level is regulated by the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, we treated A-498 and 786-O cells, two kidney cancer cell lines that are widely used to study ccRCC [16], with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) in the presence or absence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132. The protein levels of both USP48 and DBF4 were increased in a dose-dependent manner by MG132 treatment in ccRCC cells (Fig. 1F-K), indicating that the levels of these proteins are regulated by ubiquitin‒proteasome-mediated degradation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the DBF4 protein level is controlled by ubiquitination and that USP48 is a potent stabilizer of DBF4.

USP48 and DBF4 are positively correlated and co-upregulated in ccRCC clinical samples

Analysis of clinical samples revealed a positive association between USP48 and DBF4 in ccRCC. At the mRNA level, both genes were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2A, B), and their expression levels showed a significant positive correlation (R = 0.3504, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2C). This upregulation was confirmed at the protein level: data from the HPA database indicated elevated USP48 expression in ccRCC (Fig. 2D, E), and IHC analysis of a clinical cohort consistently demonstrated higher DBF4 protein levels in tumors versus adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2F, G). Collectively, these results indicate that USP48 and DBF4 are co-upregulated and positively correlated in ccRCC.

USP48 deficiency reduces the proliferation of ccRCC cells

To explore the role of USP48 in ccRCC development, we constructed three shRNAs targeting USP48 and found that all of them significantly reduced USP48 mRNA expression (Fig. 3A). Because shUSP48-2 exhibited the highest silencing efficiency for USP48, we used it in subsequent experiments. shUSP48-2 substantially reduced USP48 mRNA expression in both cell lines but did not affect DBF4 mRNA expression (Fig. 3B). However, the DBF4 protein level was markedly reduced by USP48 silencing (Fig. 3C and D), consistent with previous finding that USP48 stabilizes DBF4 in HEK-293T cells. The expression of CDC7, which partners with DBF4 in controlling DNA replication, was not changed in USP48-deficient ccRCC cells, but the expression of genes regulating cell proliferation, such as Cyclin D1 and CDK4, was markedly decreased in these cells (Fig. 3C and D), indicating that USP48 regulates the proliferation of ccRCC cells. In support of this finding, the MTT and colony formation assays showed that the proliferation of USP48-deficient ccRCC cells was significantly decreased compared with that of control cells (Fig. 3E-G). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that USP48 plays an essential role in regulating the proliferation of ccRCC cells.

Knockdown of USP48 induces cell cycle arrest, increases apoptosis, and impairs the migration and invasion of ccRCC cells

To further evaluate the effect of USP48 deficiency on ccRCC cells, we performed a 7-AAD staining assay and found that USP48 knockdown resulted in cell cycle arrest (Fig. 4A and B). Moreover, compared with that in control cells, apoptosis in USP48-deficient ccRCC cells was significantly increased (Fig. 4C and D). In addition, the migration and invasion of ccRCC cells were attenuated by USP48 knockdown, as revealed by Transwell assays (Fig. 4E-H). Taken together, these data suggest that USP48 also controls the cell cycle, survival, migration, and invasion of ccRCC cells.

USP48 interacts with and deubiquitinates DBF4 in ccRCC cells

To determine the mechanism by which USP48 stabilizes the DBF4 protein, we overexpressed HA-USP48 and Flag-DBF4 in ccRCC cells and performed co-IP assays. We found that USP48 and DBF4 can interact with each other in ccRCC cells (Fig. 5A). To investigate whether USP48 directly regulates DBF4 ubiquitination, we introduced His-ubiquitin and purified ubiquitinated DBF4 into ccRCC cells. The IP assay results showed that knockdown of USP48 increased but overexpression of USP48 reduced DBF4 ubiquitination in ccRCC cells (Fig. 5B and C). Consistently, we observed that knockdown and overexpression of USP48 accelerated and inhibited, respectively, the degradation of DBF4 (Fig. 5D-G). These data suggest that USP48 can directly interact with and deubiquitinate DBF4 to regulate its stability in ccRCC cells.

Overexpression of DBF4 reverses the defects caused by USP48 deficiency in ccRCC cells

To determine whether DBF4 acts downstream of USP48 in regulating the proliferation of ccRCC cells, we overexpressed DBF4 in USP48-deficient ccRCC cells and found that the expression of the proliferation markers Cyclin D1 and CDK4 was completely restored (Fig. 6A and B). Consistent with these findings, the MTT and colony formation assay results revealed that overexpression of DBF4 restored the proliferation of USP48-deficient ccRCC cells (Fig. 6C-E).

Knockdown of USP48 inhibits ccRCC development in a xenograft model

To validate the function of USP48 in ccRCC development in vivo, we carried out xenograft assays using control and USP48-deficient 786-O cells. The size and weight of the xenograft tumours were significantly reduced by USP48 knockdown (Fig. 8A-C). The downregulated expression of USP48 and DBF4 was verified by Western blotting (Fig. 8D-E). H&E staining confirmed that the xenograft tumours exhibited characteristics of ccRCC (Fig. 8F), and the immunohistochemical data showed that the proliferation of tumour cells was decreased by USP48 knockdown, as evidenced by the Ki-67 staining results (Fig. 8G and H). Furthermore, the TUNEL assay results revealed increased apoptosis in USP48-deficient tumours (Fig. 8I and J). Based on these results, we concluded that USP48 promotes ccRCC development in vivo.

Discussion

DBF4 plays a crucial role in DNA replication [16], and DBF4 is overexpressed in multiple cancers [17]. We previously demonstrated that DBF4 is overexpressed in ccRCC and is closely associated with poor prognosis (data not shown). DBF4 is an unstable protein, with expression peaking during the G1/S transition and decreasing at the end of mitosis. When DBF4 expression is imbalanced, abnormal DNA replication can occur, resulting in tumorigenesis. However, the regulation of DBF4 expression and protein stability in ccRCC is uncharacterized. In this study, we discovered that the deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) USP48 is upregulated in ccRCC and can interact with DBF4 to regulate its stability, subsequently influencing cell cycle progression and various cellular processes, such as proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion. These findings suggest that USP48 may contribute to the development of ccRCC by stabilizing DBF4.

Ubiquitination and deubiquitination are closely related to the regulation of protein expression. USP48 is a member of the DUBs family. It can antagonize RNF168-mediated ubiquitination of DNA double-strand break sites, thereby promoting abnormal DNA repair and genomic instability. The functions of USP48 in cancers have been intensively investigated. For example, USP48 promotes chemoresistance and metastasis in ovarian cancer [17]. USP48 also attenuates hepatocellular carcinoma progression by regulating the stabilization of SIRT6 [18]. Furthermore, USP48 can facilitate glioblastoma tumorigenesis by stabilizing Gli1 [19]. In addition, USP48 inhibits the proliferation and promotes the differentiation of acute promyelocytic leukaemia cells [20]. Our data showing that DBF4 degradation is suppressed by proteasome inhibitor treatment and that USP48 stabilizes DBF4 by modulating its ubiquitination strongly suggest that modulation of ubiquitination is a key process in regulating DBF4 expression and function.

Notably, other USPs, such as USP35 and USP38, can also stabilize DBF4 in HEK-293T cells, indicating that the ubiquitination of DBF4 is controlled by multiple factors. It would be interesting to investigate whether these USPs control DBF4 stability in ccRCC cells or other disease contexts. On the other hand, the identification of ubiquitin ligases that antagonize the degradation of DBF4 will also expand our knowledge about the mechanisms regulating the DBF4 protein level.

Considering the pro-proliferative function of DBF4 in cancers, the development of inhibitors targeting DBF4 for the treatment of relevant cancers is an attractive prospect [21, 22]. By comparison, inhibitors of USP48 may be useful for treating ccRCC, as USP48 promotes the development of this and similar cancers [23]. Our subsequent functional experiments demonstrated that knocking down USP48 inhibited the proliferation and migration of ccRCC cells and suppressed epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in these cells, although it promoted apoptosis. The results of rescue experiments showed that the protein level of DBF4 is regulated by USP48 and affects ccRCC cell growth, migration, EMT, and apoptosis. However, USP48 inhibitors are not suitable for treating cancers such as APL, in which USP48 inhibits cancer development [20]. Similarly, particular attention must be given to USP48 inhibition in cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma, in which high USP48 expression correlates with poor prognosis but USP48 may actually attenuate cancer development by stabilizing the tumour suppressor SIRT6 [18, 24].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the roles of USP48 and DBF4 in regulating ccRCC development and establish a foundation for further investigation of USP48 as a potential therapeutic target for ccRCC.
