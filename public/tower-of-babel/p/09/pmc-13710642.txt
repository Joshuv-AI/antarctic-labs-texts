Romosozumab does not improve volumetric bone mineral density at distal radius but is associated with early changes in bone strength: a prospective study
Authors: Mariana Diz-Lopes, Caterina Fanti Rovetta, Francesco Pollastri, Francesca Mastropaolo, Rosanna Somma, Mattia Tugnolli, Emma Pasetto, Camilla Benini, Valeria Messina, Angelo Fassio, Davide Gatti, Ombretta Viapiana, Enrico Grendene, Pierluigi Mozzo, Maurizio Rossini, Giovanni Adami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe evaluated how romosozumab affects bone structure, density, and strength at the wrist over 12 months. Volumetric bone mineral density did not increase, but high-resolution quantitative computed tomography imaging showed modest improvements in bone strength. These findings suggest that romosozumab may improve bone quality even in areas where density changes are not seen with standard scans and highlight the importance of using more detailed imaging to fully understand how osteoporosis treatments work across different skeletal sites.PurposeRomosozumab (ROMO) is a monoclonal antibody that inhibits sclerostin, promoting bone formation and suppressing resorption. While ROMO leads to substantial BMD gains at the lumbar spine and the hip, its effects on distal radius volumetric BMD (vBMD) remain unclear. High-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) allows for detailed assessment of bone microarchitecture and strength at peripheral regions. We aimed to evaluate the effects of 12 months of ROMO on vBMD, microarchitecture and biomechanical properties of the distal radius using HR-pQCT.MethodsWe did a prospective study on 49 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis treated with ROMO for 12 months who underwent HR-pQCT with Cone Beam CT and microfinite element analysis (uFEA) assessments at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months. We also performed DXA at lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip. HR-pQCT parameters trajectories were analyzed using mixed-effects modeling.ResultsNo significant increases were observed in cortical or trabecular vBMD or microarchitectural parameters at the distal radius. On the other hand, uFEA showed early increases in cortical failure load and shear strength, suggesting potentially improved bone mechanical behavior. Lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip aBMD significantly increased by 11.6%, 8.3%, and 3.1%, respectively (all p < 0.001).ConclusionROMO treatment was associated with modest early changes in biomechanical parameters, without significant improvement in vBMD at the distal radius. ROMO treatment might improve skeletal health beyond BMD gains.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-08113-3.

Introduction

Romosozumab (ROMO) is a monoclonal antibody that inhibits sclerostin, a key regulator of bone metabolism [1]. ROMO simultaneously stimulates bone formation and inhibits bone resorption, rapidly increasing bone mineral density (BMD) [1–4].

Sclerostin inhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway; is predominantly secreted by osteocytes, and its expression is upregulated by unloading [5–8]. Notably, mice lacking sclerostin (Sost−/−) or osteocyte-deficient are protected from disuse-induced bone loss, maintaining bone formation and Wnt signaling activity [5, 9].

Despite the substantial BMD increase at load-bearing skeletal sites such as the lumbar spine after 12 months of ROMO, at the distal radius, it did not show significant gains [4]. These findings underscore the differential skeletal response to ROMO, suggesting that its anabolic effects may be more pronounced at mechanically loaded sites, while peripheral unloaded cortical-rich regions, like the radius, may have a blunted or delayed response.

With the advent of advanced imaging techniques, we can assess aspects of bone quality that are not captured by traditional dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). High-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) provides a non-invasive, three-dimensional evaluation of both cortical and trabecular bone microarchitecture, particularly at peripheral sites such as the distal radius and tibia [10]. In addition, HR-pQCT-based microfinite element analysis (uFEA) allows for the estimation of bone strength under simulated mechanical loads [10]. These strength estimates have demonstrated stronger correlations with biomechanical competence at the radius than BMD measurements obtained by DXA, providing a more comprehensive understanding of bone integrity and fracture risk [11].

Therefore, monitoring treatment response with HR-pQCT allows for a close analysis of bone quality and strength and may contribute to a better understanding of the treatment-related effects on cortical and trabecular compartments [10, 12–14]. Limited data are available on the effects of ROMO on peripheral bone microarchitecture assessed by HR-pQCT. A recent exploratory study has reported heterogeneous changes at the distal radius and tibia, with variable cortical responses and modest changes in volumetric BMD, highlighting the need for further investigation using complementary approaches such as uFEA to better characterize treatment-related changes in bone strength [15].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of 12 months of ROMO treatment on volumetric BMD, bone microarchitecture, and biomechanical ability at the distal radius using HR-pQCT with Cone Beam CT.

Data collection

High-resolution peripheral quantitative tomography (HR-pQCT) was performed at baseline and months 3, 6, and 12 with micro-CBCT (Cone Beam CT) system ARTiCAT (RAR Srl) with 65 μm voxel resolution at the distal radius of the nondominant forearm. The system operates with a pulsed beam at 80 kV–0.6 mA (focal spot size 33 µm) and reconstructs a cylindrical field of view (83 mm diameter, 65 mm height) after a 36 s scan time acquisition. We used relative positioning of the reference line at the joint space (radiocarpal).

Unlike the Scanco XtremeCT II, which uses a detector of approximately 220 mm × 20 mm and is therefore able to scan anatomical regions with a maximum thickness of about 20 mm, the ARTiCAT system employs a 150 mm × 150 mm detector. This configuration allows ARTiCAT to acquire a scanning thickness of up to 100 mm, after which the extraction of the specific region of interest (ROI) is performed semi-automatically by the operator on the multiplanar images.

In contrast to Scanco, where the examination area is defined during the scout views, ARTiCAT defines the analysis region entirely in post-processing. The operator positions a ROI using rulers and measurement tools that assist in selecting the exact anatomical portion to be analyzed (ruler). The ROI is then extracted and further binarized and segmented (cortical and trabecular compartments). A seed-based LIMSEG tool performs semi-automatic bone segmentation on HR-pQCT images by evolving a contour from user-defined seed point (center of ROI) using intensity gradients and shape constraints. It enables robust separation of cortical and trabecular compartments while maintaining operator control over ROI. Segmentation standard HR-pQCT parameters are extracted: trabecular volumetric bone mineral density (Tb.vBMD, mgHA/cm3), total vBMD (Tot.vBMD, mgHA/cm3), cortical vBMD (Ct.vBMD, mgHA/cm3), cortical bone volume fraction (Ct.BV/TV), trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), trabecular number (Tb.number), bone surface (BS), and cortical thickness (Ct.Th, mm).

Microfinite element analysis (uFEA) was performed using proprietary software (Bone Analyzer-RAR Srl). FEA models, created starting from segmented trabecular and cortical bone components, convert voxel to isotropic brick elements which are assigned a Young’s modulus of 10 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3 [16]. A linear axial compression test was simulated, with nodes located at the proximal face fixed in all directions. Parameters of uFEA included cortical stiffness (kN/mm), shear (kN/m2), percentage of cortical break bricks (%), and trabecular break bricks (%) at 1500 N of tangential force.

Areal bone mineral density (aBMD) was measured at baseline, month 6, and month 12, at the femoral neck, total hip, and lumbar spine (L1–L4) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) with the Lunar iDXA machine (the variation coefficient for the vertebral site was 1%, while it was 1.2% for the femoral neck).

HR-pQCT with Cone Beam CT validation

We included a precision analysis to evaluate intra and inter-operator reproducibility of a novel HR-pQCT technique based on cone-beam acquisition with a large field of view (6 × 6 cm in high-definition mode). Unlike the conventional Scanco system, which relies on two-dimension scout images, this approach enables direct selection of the volume of interest (VOI) from the three-dimension DICOM dataset. Independent segmentation of DICOM datasets was performed by three operators on separate occasions (GA, CFR, MDL). Quantitative bone parameters were extracted on validated segmentations. We tested both segmentation intra-operator precision (CFR1 vs CFR2) and inter-operator precision (MDL vs the averaged values of CFR1 and CFR2) of the obtained HR-pQCT parameters. We also separately tested the intra-operator and inter-operator precision for analysis on the previously segmented VOI (MDL vs CFR1). For each variable, Bland–Altman plots were generated to assess bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA). Bias was defined as the mean difference between paired measurements; LoA were calculated as bias ± 1.96 × SD of the differences. Agreement was quantified using a two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement, and single measures [ICC(A,1)]. 95% confidence intervals were reported to classify reproducibility as poor, moderate, good, or excellent.

The root mean square standard deviation (RMS-SD) and root mean square coefficient of variation (RMS-%CV) were calculated across repeated measurements. The repeatability coefficient (RC) was defined as 2.77 × RMS-SD, corresponding to the least significant change detectable at the 95% confidence level. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) and associated p-values were computed to evaluate linear association between operators.

To validate the quantitative performance of the new HR-pQCT approach, we also performed a phantom study in parallel with a reference device, the Scanco XtremeCT II (manufactured in Brüttisellen Switzerland and located in IRCCS Istituto Ortopedico Rizzoli, Bologna). Two phantoms were employed: (i) an anthropomorphic hand phantom containing real bone specimens, and (ii) the QRM European Forearm Phantom (EFP), a widely adopted reference for densitometric calibration and geometric quality control. DICOM datasets acquired on the Scanco XtremeCT II were processed both with the certified Scanco software and with the new ImgView analysis pipeline. For the EFP phantom, Hounsfield Unit (HU) values were converted to volumetric bone mineral density (mgHA/cm3) using a linear calibration curve (slope = 0.365 ± 0.003, intercept = −3.09 ± 3.29). Results demonstrated excellent agreement between the two systems. For the anthropomorphic phantom, differences in key densitometric and morphometric indices were consistently within ± 10%, including total BMD (−2.6%), cortical BMD (−4.8%), trabecular BMD (−3.7%), cortical thickness (−8.8%), trabecular BV/TV (−5.3%), trabecular number (−4.7%), trabecular thickness (+ 5.5%), and trabecular separation (−5.9%).

Sample size estimation

A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of prior data on which to base assumptions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean and standard deviations or median and interquartile range (IQR) as appropriate.

Missing data were addressed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE), tailored for continuous hierarchical data. This approach accounts for repeated measures nested within subjects. A total of 5 imputed datasets were generated, and analyses were performed on each dataset separately, with results pooled according to Rubin’s rules to account for the uncertainty due to imputation. Analyses were conducted using linear mixed-effects models fitted by REML. For each outcome variable, we included time (0, 3, 6, 12 months) as a fixed factor and pre-treatment exposure to bisphosphonates as a covariate, with a random intercept for participant to account for within-subject correlations. Model fit was evaluated using AIC/BIC, and interaction terms tested whether treatment history modified time trends. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) and pairwise contrasts against baseline were calculated.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1, SPSS Version 29 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and JASP (Version 0.19.0). This study was approved by the University of Verona Ethics Committee (prot. registration: REUMABANK). All patients provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 49 postmenopausal women were included, with a mean age of 73.4 ± 10.1 years old. Mean BMI was 23.1 ± 3.4 kg/m2, and most patients were non-smokers (n = 45, 91.8%).

The majority of the patients had a previous vertebral fracture (n = 41, 83.7%), and a smaller percentage had sustained previous hip (n = 9, 18.4%) and wrist fractures (n = 16, 32.7%). Forty-four patients (89.8%) received past treatment with oral bisphosphonates. The median time since the last administration of oral bisphosphonates was 2.1 years (IQR 1–3.4 years). Similarly, BV/TV showed a mild numerical increase at month 3 and month 12, but this was not significant (p = 0.614 and p = 0.0582, respectively). Cortical parameters, including cortical vBMD and cortical thickness, did not exhibit significant changes over time (p = 0.905 and p = 0.463, respectively). Bone mineral density

Over the 12 months, there was a significant improvement in aBMD in femoral neck and total hip. Lumbar spine aBMD increased from median 0.804 (IQR 0.738–0.925) to 0.884 g/cm2 (IQR 0.822–0.951) with a mean percentage change of 11.6% at 12 months (p ≤ 0.001).

At the femoral neck, there was an increase from 0.657 (IQR 0.601–0.751) to 0.713 (IQR 0.653–0.782) g/cm2 with a mean percentage change of 8.3% at 12 months (p < 0.001). At the total hip, aBMD improved from 0.693 (IQR 0.623–0.774) to 0.734 (IQR 0.641–0.787) g/cm2 (p < 0.001), with a percentage change of 3.1%.

Intra-operator

Repeat analysis performed by the same operator demonstrated excellent precision and agreement across all HR-pQCT parameters. Total vBMD and cortical vBMD exhibited the lowest variability, with RMS-%CV values of 1.82% and 1.72%, respectively, and RCs of 14.19 mgHA/cm3 and 30.56 mgHA/cm3. Both parameters achieved high reliability (ICC = 0.99, 95%CI 0.99–1.00) and strong linear associations (r2 = 0.985–0.986), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Bland-Altman analysis confirmed negligible bias and narrow limits of agreement (Supplementary Fig. 4). Trabecular vBMD showed slightly higher variability (RMS-%CV 7.77%, RC 17.44 mgHA/cm3) but maintained high reproducibility (ICC = 0.98, 95%CI 0.96–0.99; r2 = 0.95). Regarding bone microstructure parameters, Tb.BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp all showed high intra-operator precision with RMS-%CV values of 6.32%, 1.13%, and 1.44%, respectively. ICCs were ≥ 0.97 for all, with negligible bias (≤ 0.001 mm) and narrow limits of agreement, confirming high repeatability of the analysis. Cortical thickness also demonstrated good precision and agreement: RMS-%CV = 1.79%, RC = 0.042 mm, ICC = 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.99, and r2 = 0.96.

Inter-operator

Total vBMD and cortical vBMD maintained strong precision (RMS-%CV = 3.17% and 3.08%, RC = 24.07 and 50.32 mgHA/cm3, respectively). ICCs were ≥ 0.98 (95% CI 0.96–0.99) with r2 = 0.95–0.96. Trabecular vBMD showed higher variability (RMS-%CV = 22.9%, RC = 33.86 mgHA/cm3; ICC = 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.95, r2 = 0.84). For cortical and trabecular geometry, the most reproducible parameters were Tb.Th (RMS-%CV = 1.80%, ICC = 0.99, 95% CI 0.99–1.00, r2 = 0.99) and Tb.Sp (RMS-%CV = 2.50%, ICC = 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–0.98, r2 = 0.94) but Ct.Th also displayed good precision (RMS-%CV = 3.22%, RC = 0.076 mm; ICC = 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.97, r2 = 0.84). Trabecular BV/TV had moderate variability (RMS-%CV = 17.36%, RC = 1.63%; ICC = 0.94, 95% CI 0.90–0.97, r2 = 0.90).

Bland-Altman plots showed no cut-off bias, the errors were independent of the mean values detected (Supplementary Fig. 5), and the correlation plots confirmed the strong linear association (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Discussion

Herein, we investigated the changes in distal radius volumetric BMD, bone microarchitecture, and biomechanical properties in patients treated with ROMO for 12 months. We showed that ROMO slightly increased bone strength, although no significant changes were observed in volumetric BMD or microarchitectural parameters.

Sclerostin inhibition led to substantial BMD gains at the lumbar spine and hip but no improvements in BMD at distal radius [4, 17, 18]. Differences in sclerostin expression may account for the site-specific response observed in our and prior studies [5, 6]. Osteocytes are responsible for sensing mechanical load and translating it into biochemical signaling. In the absence of mechanical load, there is an increase in sclerostin expression and subsequent inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin pathway [5]. The currently approved ROMO dose (210 mg/month) may be insufficient to significantly improve areal BMD at non-weight-bearing sites, where sclerostin expression could be higher. Notably, the phase 1 study of ROMO showed that higher doses produced a more pronounced anabolic effect [19–21].

Interestingly, experimental models of skeletal unloading, such as prolonged spaceflight, showed a marked reduction in tibial vBMD, accompanied by an increase in circulating sclerostin. Upon reloading, sclerostin levels declined and tibial vBMD recovered. In contrast, distal radius vBMD paradoxically decreased at reloading suggesting that it may serve as a mineral reservoir to support recovery of weight-bearing bones [22]. Similarly, in clinical trials and observational studies, ROMO was associated with an acute, albeit modest, decrease in serum calcium, accompanied by a compensatory rise in serum PTH. This effect might resemble a “hungry bone” phenomenon, where calcium shifts from the bloodstream into the skeleton, with the distal radius indeed potentially serving as a calcium source to facilitate mineralization of the tibia and other load-bearing sites [23, 24]. In aggregate, non-weight-bearing bones such as distal radius can exhibit attenuated anabolic responses to sclerostin inhibition over the 12-month period, when compared to bones that sustain mechanical load, like the hip.

In addition to mechanical loading, the mechanism of action of different osteoporosis treatments may contribute to the heterogeneous response observed at the distal radius. Previous HR-pQCT studies have shown that the effect of therapy at the radius differs according to the pharmacological profile of the drug [13, 25]. In the DATA HR-pQCT study, denosumab increased total and cortical volumetric BMD and cortical thickness at the distal radius, whereas teriparatide did not increase vBMD and was associated with increased cortical porosity, despite preserved estimated strength. These findings suggest that strong suppression of bone remodeling promotes secondary mineralization and filling of intracortical resorption cavities, leading to measurable increases in cortical density even at non-weight-bearing sites, while anabolic stimulation may transiently increase remodeling and the proportion of newly formed, less mineralized bone, thereby masking density gains [13]. Bisphosphonates have also been reported to produce smaller BMD changes at the distal radius than at weight-bearing regions, possibly because of the lower turnover and the predominance of cortical bone at this site [25]. ROMO has a dual mechanism of action, simultaneously stimulating bone formation and inhibiting resorption, and experimental studies suggest that early intracortical remodeling followed by progressive mineralization may occur during treatment [14]. This sequence may explain why improvements in biomechanical parameters can be detected before significant changes in volumetric BMD, particularly at peripheral sites with limited mechanical loading.

Although no significant differences in cortical or trabecular vBMD were found, uFEA showed improvements in shear strength and an early modest increase in cortical failure load, revealing a possible biomechanical benefit. These findings are consistent with preclinical evidence indicating that ROMO transiently activated intracortical remodeling at the radius early in the treatment and was associated with a temporary reduction in vBMD [18]. However, this remodeling dynamics occurred without an increase in cortical porosity and with a maintenance or improvement of bone mass and strength, likely due to favorable changes in bone geometry [18]. Likewise, similar results were also obtained with setrusumab (an investigational monoclonal anti-sclerostin antibody) in patients with osteogenesis imperfecta, with significant improvements in uFEA-derived measures of bone strength at the distal radius, while changes in vBMD were modest and dose-dependent, with increases observed in total and cortical vBMD at some dose levels but not consistently across all groups [26]. The agreement between our uFEA-estimated strength and previous experimental results [18] suggests that this approach may be useful in future studies to provide a sensitive assessment of changes in strength over time. Together, these data suggest that ROMO exerts complex and compartment-specific skeletal effects, particularly at non-weight-bearing sites like the radius, where transient remodeling activity may obscure anabolic gains when assessed solely through aBMD.

Importantly, we did not have direct measurements of cortical, trabecular, or total area, which would have helped clarify if increases in area could have contributed to the lack of increase in vBMD. We showed that bone surface numerically increased over follow-up, although not significantly, while cortical BV/TV, trabecular number, trabecular thickness, and trabecular spacing remained stable. Taken together with the small early decrease in total and cortical vBMD at month 3 and the simultaneous improvement in FEA-derived parameters, these findings may support the hypothesis that romosozumab may induce early deposition of newly formed, less mineralized bone and subtle structural changes that are not fully captured by vBMD alone.

Our study included multiple timepoints, allowing us to assess both early and late changes. One prior study analyzed changes in QCT parameters evaluated in thoracic vertebrae in patients treated with ROMO vs placebo over 3 months, demonstrating improvements in vBMD, cortical thickness, and overall stiffness [27]. However, this is the first study to use HR-pQCT to evaluate long-term changes at the distal radius during treatment with ROMO.

When looking at aBMD in the clinically most relevant sites, the changes were in line with previous studies [2, 3], with a significant increase in lumbar spine (+ 11.8%), femoral neck (+ 8.3%), and total hip (+ 3.1%) over the 12 months of treatment with ROMO.

Because standardized short-term precision data are primarily available for the Scanco XtremeCT II, reproducibility results from that system were used as a benchmark for comparison [28]. While the ARTiCAT (RAR Srl) HR-pQCT differs in acquisition and reconstruction parameters, both platforms share comparable results, as shown in our parallel phantom study. Intra-operator RMS-CVs for cortical density (1.7%), cortical thickness (1.8%), and trabecular morphometry (1.1–1.4%) fell within the reference precision ranges (0.4–2.9%) [28, 29].

The magnitude of HR-pQCT changes at the distal radius observed after 12 months of ROMO treatment was below the analytical LSC for most parameters, indicating that differences at the individual level fall within the precision limits of the technique. These results highlight the analytical stability of the HR-pQCT method and confirm that volumetric BMD changes at the distal radius in patients under ROMO are modest.

There are several limitations in our study. Our analysis focused only on the distal radius, a non-weight-bearing cortical bone, and we have no data from other skeletal regions as our imaging device is not capable of scanning the distal tibia. Additionally, the lack of a control group limited our ability to attribute changes over time solely to ROMO.

Strong points of our study were the detailed longitudinal evaluation of bone structural and mechanical changes at the distal radius in postmenopausal women treated with ROMO. The use of HR-pQCT allowed specific analysis of both the cortical and trabecular compartments and of bone strength, which is not possible by relying only on DXA.

In conclusion, ROMO treatment was associated with modest early changes in biomechanical parameters at the distal radius. The preserved mechanical integrity despite modest or absent vBMD gains underscores the value of incorporating HR-pQCT and uFEA in evaluating therapeutic response in patients treated with ROMO.
