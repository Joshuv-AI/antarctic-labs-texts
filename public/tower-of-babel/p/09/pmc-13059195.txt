ATP synthasome contributes to efficient energy flux in malignant breast cancer
Authors: Yen-Dun Tony Tzeng, Yu-Ting Kang, Tzu-Hung Hsiao, Pei-Yi Chu, Su-Boon Yong, Shih-Chieh Lin, Meng-Yu Wu, Ling-Ming Tseng, Ming-Feng Hou, Zhi-Hong Wen, Tzu-Sheng Hsu, Chih-Chiang Hung, An-Jen Chiang, Kuan-Hao Tsui, Chia-Jung Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates the metabolic alterations associated with breast cancer progression and elucidates the underlying mechanisms. Adenine nucleotide translocase 2 (ANT2), a mitochondrial protein essential for cellular energy metabolism, facilitates the exchange of ADP and ATP across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The role of ANT2, particularly its interaction with the ATP synthasome, in breast cancer metastasis remains poorly understood.MethodsWe analyzed ANT2 in breast cancer using genetic and clinical methods, validating its expression in human tissues. Gene enrichment studies and functional assays assessed ANT2’s role in mitochondrial function and cancer metabolism. Knockdown experiments and pharmacogenomic screening evaluated ANT2’s impact on metastasis and identified potential inhibitors in 3D cultures and orthotopic mouse models.ResultsANT2 was significantly overexpressed in metastatic breast cancer, correlating with reduced survival. Knockdown of ANT2 impaired cell migration and invasion, reduced ATP production, and diminished oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity in MCF7-F4 cells. In vivo, siRNA-mediated ANT2 silencing in JC-M3 cells decreased tumor growth and lung metastases in mice. Pharmacogenomic analysis identified cymarin as an ANT2 inhibitor, reducing spheroid formation in 3D cultures and tumor burden in vivo, alongside downregulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and OXPHOS markers. ANT2 colocalized with ATP5B, forming an ATP synthasome that enhanced energy flux in hyperinvasive cells.ConclusionsANT2 drives breast cancer metastasis by enhancing mitochondrial energy production via the ATP synthasome. Its inhibition, particularly with cymarin, disrupts tumor bioenergetics and metastatic potential, positioning ANT2 as a promising therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-026-02610-z.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with its metastatic form posing the greatest clinical challenge due to its resistance to conventional therapies and poor prognosis [1]. The disease’s heterogeneity, spanning molecular subtypes such as luminal, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), complicates treatment strategies and underscores the need for novel therapeutic targets [2]. A defining feature of cancer progression is metabolic reprogramming, which enables tumor cells to meet the heightened bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands of rapid proliferation, invasion, and dissemination [3]. Historically, the Warburg effect, defined by increased glycolysis despite adequate oxygen availability, has dominated the understanding of cancer metabolism [4]. However, recent evidence reveals that mitochondrial OXPHOS plays a critical role in supporting aggressive cancer phenotypes, particularly in metastasis, challenging the glycolysis-centric view [5, 6]. Mitochondria, as the cellular powerhouses, orchestrate ATP production through the electron transport chain (ETC) and ATP synthase [7], yet the precise molecular mechanisms driving their adaptation in breast cancer remain elusive.

ANT2, encoded by the SLC25A5 gene, is a mitochondrial inner membrane protein that facilitates the exchange of ADP and ATP, linking mitochondrial energy production to cytosolic demands [8]. The ANT family, comprising ANT1, ANT2, and ANT3 in humans, exhibits tissue-specific expression patterns, with ANT2 uniquely upregulated in proliferating cells, including cancer cells, due to its role in maintaining mitochondrial function under metabolic stress [9]. Unlike ANT1, which predominates in differentiated tissues like muscle, and ANT3, which is ubiquitously expressed, ANT2’s expression is induced in hypoxic or glycolytic environments, suggesting a specialized role in tumor adaptation [10]. Previous studies have implicated ANT2 in cancer cell survival, demonstrating its anti-apoptotic effects and contribution to chemoresistance in lung, liver, and breast cancers [11, 12]. In breast cancer, ANT2 overexpression has been associated with enhanced tumor growth and resistance to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors; however, its specific contribution to metastasis, a process that demands significant energy and cellular plasticity, remains poorly defined [13].

The ATP synthasome, a mitochondrial supercomplex comprising ANT, ATP synthase, and the phosphate carrier, enhances the efficiency of ATP synthesis and export, potentially conferring a metabolic advantage to cancer cells [14]. In normal physiology, this complex optimizes energy production by channeling protons and substrates directly to ATP synthase, minimizing energy loss [15, 16]. In cancer, however, the ATP synthasome may be appropriated to sustain the heightened bioenergetic requirements associated with invasive and metastatic behaviors. Metastatic progression in breast cancer is closely tied to EMT, a developmental program that enhances cellular motility, invasiveness, and resistance to therapy [17]. Emerging evidence suggests that EMT is accompanied by metabolic shifts, including increased mitochondrial activity, to sustain these energy-intensive processes [18]. Given ANT2’s role in ATP transport and its potential interaction with ATP synthase, we hypothesized that it may drive the formation of the ATP synthasome in breast cancer, thereby amplifying energy flux to fuel metastasis.

This study aims to elucidate ANT2’s contribution to breast cancer progression by investigating its expression, functional role, and therapeutic potential. Using a multi-omics approach, we analyzed ANT2 in human breast cancer tissues and correlated its expression with clinical outcomes. In vitro and in vivo models, including hyperinvasive MCF7-F4 cells and orthotopic JC-M3 mouse models, were employed to dissect ANT2’s impact on mitochondrial function and metastatic behavior. We further explored the ATP synthasome’s structure and regulation, identifying key transcription factors such as CREB1 and ZEB1 that may govern ANT2 expression during EMT [19, 20]. Additionally, pharmacogenomic screening was conducted to identify small-molecule inhibitors of ANT2, with a focus on cymarin, a cardiac glycoside with known mitochondrial effects. By targeting ANT2 and the ATP synthasome, we seek to disrupt the metabolic underpinnings of breast cancer metastasis, offering insights into novel therapeutic strategies for this devastating disease.

Materials and methods

Multi-omics analysis of differential gene expression and prognostic significance

Gene alterations, CNAs, domain variations, and transcript expression levels of SLC25A5 and its related genes were retrieved from public datasets including TCGA, GTEx, TNMplot, UALCAN, and GEO (GSE7390, GSE20685, GSE11121, GSE61304) [21, 22]. Raw gene expression data were log₂-transformed and quantile-normalized. Batch effects between datasets were minimized using ComBat normalization from the sva R package where applicable. Differential expression analysis was performed using limma or DESeq2, depending on the dataset format. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if |log₂ fold change| > 1.5 and adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05, corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted using GEPIA2 [23, 24] or the Kaplan–Meier Plotter tool [25], with stratification based on the median or upper/lower tertiles, and log-rank p-values reported. For multi-group comparisons (normal vs. tumor vs. metastatic), we first tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and variance homogeneity (Levene’s test). If assumptions were met, we used one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. If not, we used Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison correction. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p-values < 0.05 were considered significant. GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R (v4.3.0) were used for all statistical analyses and visualizations.

Human breast cancer tissue specimens and ethical oversight

Human breast cancer specimens and associated clinical data were obtained from the Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital Biobank and the Taichung Veterans General Hospital Biobank. The release and use of these materials were approved by the respective Ethics Governance Committees under protocols 22-003, 114A09, and CE25953B, with all samples provided in anonymized form. Tissue microarray (TMA) slides for histological and immunofluorescence analyses were purchased from SuperBioChips and TissueArray.com (Taipei, Taiwan) and included primary tumors, metastatic lesions, and adjacent normal tissues. All procedures involving human materials were further approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (25-EM2-01) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Breast cancer cell lines and cell culture

Breast cancer cell lines MCF7 and JC were purchased from (bioresource collection and research center, Hsinchu, Taiwan) were used and incubated in culture medium (MEM, DMEM respectively) supplemented with 10% FBS in a humidified atmosphere with 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Cell migration and invasion assay

The migration and invasion assay was performed as described [26]. Wound healing assays were performed by creating a scratch in confluent cell monolayers, and migration was monitored at 0, 24, hours. Transwell invasion assays were conducted using Matrigel-coated inserts, with cells seeded in serum-free media and allowed to invade toward 10% FBS-containing media for 24 h.

RNA extraction and Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Samples were collected, and total RNA was extracted employing the QIAZOL (QIAGEN). Subsequently, the RNA was subjected to cDNA synthesis using the ToolScript MMLV RT Kit and TOOLS M-MLV RTase in conjunction with the StepOnePlus system (Applied Biosystems). All nucleic acid reagents utilized in this process were procured from Biotools located in Taipei, Taiwan. The specific primer sequences employed for real-time PCR can be found in supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

Cells or tissue were fixed with 4% para-formaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with Triton X-100 for 10 min, blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min, and incubated with indicated antibodies (Supplemental Table 2) in blocking buffer for 1 h at RT. After washing with PBST, samples were incubated with the secondary antibodies for 0.5 h at RT. All glass slides were digitized with confocal microscope (LSM700 ZEISS).

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

The in situ PLA was performed using the Duolink® In Situ Red Starter Kit Mouse/Rabbit (Sigma-Aldrich) with minor modifications for flow cytometry. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and blocked with Duolink® Blocking Solution at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies against ATP5B and SLC25A5 at 4 °C for 24 h. After washing, PLA probes were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by ligation for 30 min and rolling circle amplification with red detection oligonucleotides for 100 min at 37 °C. Cells were washed, resuspended in wash buffer, and analyzed using a Beckman Coulter flow cytometer equipped for red fluorescence detection (Ex 594 nm / Em 624 nm).

BN-PAGE

Mitochondria were purified as described above and lysed in 1–4% digitonin prior to separation by 5–15% BN-PAGE as previously described [27]. The ratios of protein to digitonin were 1:4 w/w for total cell lysate and 1:8 w/w for mitochondrial fraction. Protein was extracted on ice for 1 h and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was collected, and mixed with the loading buffer. Total proteins (100 µg) were separated by 5–15% gradient BN-PAGE.

Lactate and glucose analysis, ATPase activity

Lactate production and glucose consumption were measured using Lactate Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) and Glucose Uptake Assay Kit (Abcam) following manufacturer protocols. Culture supernatants were collected at 24 h, and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm (glucose) and 570 nm (lactate). ATPase activity was assessed using the Malachite Green Phosphate Detection Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) by quantifying inorganic phosphate release at 620 nm, normalized to protein concentration.

Mitochondrial functional analysis

Cells were harvested after various treatments, washed, resuspended in culture medium, and stained with ATP (BioTracker ATP-Red Live Cell Dye, Merck), MitoSOX (5 µM), TMRE (250 nM) (Molecular Probes, Eugene, CA, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation and wash, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSCalibur).

Oxygen consumption rate measurement

The measurement of oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was carried out following the established protocol described in reference [28]. Mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the Seahorse XF HS mini platform, an extracellular flux analyzer manufactured by Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA. Cells were seeded in plates at an approximate density of 2⋅104 cells per well, and the alterations in cellular respiration were continuously monitored. To evaluate changes in OCR, the following compounds were sequentially introduced: oligomycin, FCCP, and antimycin A/rotenone.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

ChIP assays were performed as described [29]. Briefly, the CREB1 and ZEB1 protein were fixed with DNA by incubation with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were harvested and sonicated to fragment the DNA. CREB1 and ZEB1 protein–DNA complexes were precipitated using an anti-CREB1 and anti-ZEB1 antibody (Abcam) and Dynabeads Protein G (ThermoFisher Scientific). Potential binding sites were amplified using the primers shown in supplemental Table 3.

Gene silencing in vitro

To achieve gene silencing, siRNA transfection was conducted following established protocols [30]. In brief, cancer cells were transfected with 100 pmole siRNA utilizing Lipofectamine 3000, and subsequent analysis was carried out 24 h post-transfection. The specific SLC25A5 siRNA used in this study was procured from Ambion Co. (119898). The siRNA sequences can be found in supplementary Table 3.

3D culture

Nonadherent round-bottom 96-well plates (EZ-BindShut SP, Iwaki) were used to form spheroids. Breast MCF7 cancer cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) in 100 µL serum-free MEM were seeded and treated with 10 µM cymarin or vehicle. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Spheroids were harvested by centrifugation and analyzed for size and number using bright-field microscopy and ImageJ software. Samples were processed for drug inhibitory effects.

Spheroid formation and drug treatment

MCF7 breast cancer cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well into nonadherent round-bottom 96-well plates (EZ-BindShut SP, Iwaki) in 200 µL of serum-free MEM. The cells were treated with 1 µM/mL cymarin (Merck, PHL89838) or a vehicle control and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Following incubation, the resulting spheroids were harvested by centrifugation. To evaluate the inhibitory effects of cymarin, spheroid size and number were quantified using bright-field microscopy and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunoprecipitation and Immunoblotting

Immunoprecipitation was performed in the total lysate of cells using a dynabeads protein G kit (Novex, ThermoFisher Scientific). Briefly, the primary antibody was incubated with dynabeads for 10 min at room temperature, and then incubated with total protein lysate for 10 min at RT. After washing with PBST buffer and heating with 70 °C for 10 min, the tube was placed on the magnet and the supernatant was loaded onto a gel. For immunoblotting, the total proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with primary antibodies (Supplemental Table 2). Immunoreactive detection was performed using a chemiluminescence detection system (GE Healthcare). Uncropped scans of blots are shown in supplemental Fig. 10–12.

Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) analysis

Cell samples for metabolite analysis were gathered and processed at the National Taiwan University Centers of Genomics and Precision Medicine, following the established protocols as previously described in the work by Liao et al. [31]. The detailed procedures and methods used in this analysis can be found in the aforementioned publication.

Derivation of metastatic JC-M3 cells in vivo

All mice procedures related to animal use were conducted and approvedin accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (#2021-2024-A044), and all animals were treated in accordance with the National Laboratory for Experimental Animals guidelines. Female NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid/JNarl mice (8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum food and water. Wild-type JC breast cancer cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and antibiotics at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. For primary tumors, 5 × 10⁵ JC-WT cells in 100 µL PBS were injected into the fourth mammary fat pad of anesthetized mice. Tumors grew for 28 days, allowing spontaneous lung metastasis. Lungs were harvested, dissociated with collagenase I (1 mg/mL), and metastatic JC-M cells were cultured from the suspension. Subsequently, 5 × 10⁵ JC-M cells in 100 µL PBS were injected via tail vein into new mice, with lung metastases isolated after 14–21 days.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from JC-WT, JC-P, and JC-M3 cells using the TRIzol reagent kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies).Sequencing libraries were prepared using thexGen Broad-Range RNA. Library Prep Kit following standard protocols. The constructed libraries were quantified and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform to generate paired-end reads.For data analysis, raw reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC and trimmed using Trimmomatic to remove adapters and low-quality bases. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (mouse mm10) using STAR aligner. Gene expression levels were quantified as raw read counts using featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package. Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. The data are now publicly accessible under the accession number GSE318662.

Spatial transcriptome sequencing data processing

Spatial transcriptomic analyses were first performed using publicly available 10⋅ Genomics Visium datasets (V11J26-008-B1 and V19L29-097-B1). These datasets were used to establish spatial gene expression patterns and to optimize our computational workflow. We subsequently analyzed human breast cancer specimens collected from the Taichung Veterans General Hospital Biobank using the 10⋅ Genomics Visium HD platform. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CE24271B) and conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. For all data, spatial transcriptomics data from individual spots were processed by first quantifying the estimated number of cells per spot. Gene expression patterns and spatial distribution of key markers were visualized and compared across tissue regions. Dimension reduction and clustering were performed using RunPCA, FindNeighbors, and FindClusters. Initial cluster assignment was guided by H&E morphology and subsequently refined using established cell-type marker genes. For clusters expressing multiple markers, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to evaluate dominant cell-type signatures using averaged expression matrices. The top 200 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from single-cell clusters were selected as training genes for deconvolution, with performance validated using an independent set of 200 test genes. Alignment scores were calculated for each gene to assess concordance between predicted and observed spatial distributions [32].

Orthotopic mammary tumor studies and SiRNA treatment

JC-M3 cells (1 × 105) in 100 µl of PBS were injected into the inguinal mammary fat pads of 8-week-old and weighing approximately 20–30 g female NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid/JNarl mice from National Laboratory Animal center (NLAC, Taiwan). Mice were randomized into treatment groups when tumor volume reached 50 mm3 as measured by calipers using the formula Tvol. = (length × width2 ) / 2. siRNA (Accell mouse Slc25a5 SMART pool; E-042392-00) were intratumoral localized doses that 1 mg/kg at treatment day 8 and 12. (Unmodified siRNA degrades almost immediately while siSTABLE siRNA maintains integrity for up to 5 days.) The animals were sacrificed on day 16 from the start of the treatment. Tissues and tumors were collected at necropsy, and the samples were frozen for gene expression analysis or processed for other readouts as described below.

Immunocytochemistry analysis

Immunocytochemistry was performed on TMA slides following previously described procedures [30, 33]. A fluorescent multiplex staining kit (BioTnA, TATS01F) was used, including the manufacturer-provided ImmunoBlock reagent to minimize background and reduce non-specific binding. Briefly, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with Triton X-100. After permeabilization, slides were incubated with ImmunoBlock reagent, followed by additional blocking with 5% BSA. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Stained slides were reviewed by Li-Tzung Pathology Laboratory (Kaohsiung, Taiwan), and whole-slide fluorescent images were acquired using a fully motorized fluorescence microscope (VS120, Olympus).

Clinical samples and cohort stratification

We integrated multiple public datasets including TCGA_BRCA (n = 1095), GSE20685 (n = 327), GSE7390 (n = 198), and GSE11121 (n = 200). Expression values of SLC25A5 were normalized and then stratified into high and low groups based on upper and lower tertiles (top 33% vs. bottom 33%), unless otherwise specified. This cut‑off criterion was applied consistently for Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and differential‑expression comparison. Patient subtypes (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2‑enriched, and triple‑negative breast cancer), treatment history, and clinical outcome data were retrieved from the corresponding repositories. A total of 114 paired breast cancer specimens were analyzed, including 20 non‑tumor, 22 stage I, 21 stage II, 23 stage III, 28 stage IV, and 24 lymph‑node‑metastatic cases. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for ANT2 was performed as described below. H‑scores were calculated across all cases and statistically compared among stages.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1). Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to assess pairwise differences. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ImageJ software was used for semi-quantitative analysis of Western blot and immunofluorescence data. For in vivo experiments, including tumor volume, metastatic nodule counts, and immunostaining quantification, each group included at least six biological replicates.

Results

SLC25A5 as a key regulator of mitochondrial function and breast cancer progression

To delineate the role of ANT family genes in breast cancer, we performed a comprehensive analysis using the TNMplot database. Among all ANT family members, SLC25A5 (ANT2) emerged as the predominant transcript, exhibiting a striking peak in metastatic BRCA (Supplementary Fig. 1A, denoted by the yellow triangle). To further define the genomic landscape of SLC25A5 alterations, we extracted expression data from TCGA and visualized the top ten most frequently altered genes via a waterfall plot. Notably, SLC25A5 alterations co-occurred with mutations in TP53, PIK3CA, CDH1, and TTN (Fig. 1A). Survival analyses across TCGA and GEO cohorts consistently demonstrated that high SLC25A5 expression predicts poorer overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and relapse-free survival (RFS) (Fig. 1B-C; Supplementary Fig. 1C).

To explore the expression patterns of SLC25A4, SLC25A5, and SLC25A6 (ANT1-3) at both gene and protein levels, we analyzed various cancer stages, major subclasses, and metastatic status (Fig. 1D-E). While SLC25A4 and SLC25A6 showed no significant differential expression between normal and tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B), SLC25A5 was markedly overexpressed at both the RNA and protein levels in metastatic and high-grade tumors (Fig. 1D-E; Supplementary Fig. 2C). This pattern was corroborated by breast cancer biopsy analysis, which confirmed elevated SLC25A5 expression in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor regions (Fig. 1F). TMA-based IHC further validated progressive ANT2 upregulation across clinical stages (Fig. 1G-H).

To assess oncogenic dependency, we analyzed DepMap CRISPR screens and observed a strong survival dependency on SLC25A5 across breast cancer lines (Fig. 1I). To assess oncogenic dependency, DepMap CRISPR screens revealed strong reliance on SLC25A5 across breast cancer lines (Fig. 1I). Consistently, our qPCR panel showed that MCF7 exhibited the lowest SLC25A5 expression, whereas aggressive TNBC lines displayed markedly higher levels (Supplementary Fig. 2D), supporting its role as a metabolic vulnerability in advanced breast cancer. Transcriptomic profiling following shRNA-mediated SLC25A5 silencing identified 572 differentially expressed genes, primarily linked to mitochondrial organization, ATP generation, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 1J). GSEA further demonstrated that SLC25A5 expression positively correlates with OXPHOS (Fig. 1L). TCGA-wide metabolic analyses supported these findings, showing that tumors with high SLC25A5 exhibit enhanced ATPase activity, mitochondrial dynamics, and energy flux (Fig. 1M-N; Supplementary Fig. 2E-F). Taken together, these results position SLC25A5 as a central regulator of mitochondrial bioenergetics and a critical determinant of breast cancer progression, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in metastatic disease.

Hyperinvasive breast cancer cells exhibit enhanced mitochondrial activity

To investigate how invasive traits are acquired in breast cancer cells, we generated a hyperinvasive subline, MCF7-F4, through four consecutive rounds of Matrigel-based Transwell selection (Fig. 2A). This stepwise enrichment produced a population with markedly enhanced migratory and invasive capacities, as demonstrated by accelerated wound closure and increased Transwell invasion (Fig. 2B-E). Immunofluorescence revealed extensive cytoskeletal remodeling in MCF7-F4 cells, including prominent stress fibers and elevated fibronectin deposition (Fig. 2F), indicative of a mesenchymal-like phenotype. Gene expression profiling confirmed strong induction of canonical EMT markers together with cancer stem cell–associated biomarkers (Fig. 2G-H). To evaluate whether these transcriptional programs were progressively acquired during the selection process, we analyzed intermediate sublines generated after each invasion round. Supplementary Fig. 3A-C shows that key EMT transcription factors, including SNAI1, SNAI2, and ZEB1, were only modestly increased in early passages but became consistently and robustly upregulated in the F4 subline, indicating that a fully reinforced EMT network emerges only after repeated invasion selection.

We next assessed metabolic adaptations associated with this phenotypic transition. Culture medium pH remained unchanged between WT and F4 cells (Fig. 2I), and lactate production showed minimal differences (Fig. 2J). In contrast, glucose consumption was significantly elevated in MCF7-F4 cells (Fig. 2K), suggesting increased metabolic demand. Mitochondrial analyses revealed that F4 cells exhibited higher ATP content and increased mitochondrial ROS levels, while mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) remained comparable to WT cells (Fig. 2L-N). Seahorse analysis demonstrated a pronounced enhancement of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in MCF7-F4 cells (Fig. 2O), with quantification showing higher basal respiration, maximal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 2P). Importantly, this enhancement was not attributable to increased mitochondrial content, as mitochondrial mass remained unchanged between WT and F4 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Together, these data show that repeated invasion selection drives EMT activation and metabolic reprogramming, yielding hyperinvasive MCF7-F4 cells with enhanced OXPHOS and aggressive malignant potential.

ANT2 partners with ATP synthase to form ATP synthasome in hyper-invasive breast cancer cells

To investigate the basis of the elevated mitochondrial activity in hyperinvasive breast cancer cells, we first assessed ΔΨm under inhibition of key electron transport chain (ETC) complexes. FCCP treatment markedly collapsed ΔΨm in both WT and F4 cells, validating the assay. Inhibition of complexes I, III, and IV (rotenone, antimycin A, and KCN) similarly reduced ΔΨm across both groups (Fig. 3A). In contrast, inhibition of ATP synthase with oligomycin produced divergent effects: WT cells exhibited the expected ΔΨm elevation, whereas ΔΨm in F4 cells remained unchanged. The lack of ΔΨm hyperpolarization in F4 cells suggested a distinct regulatory mechanism linked to ATP synthase function. Consistent with this possibility, ATP synthase enzymatic activity was markedly increased in F4 cells compared to WT, indicating that hyperactive complex V may underlie the stable ΔΨm phenotype (Fig. 3B). Among the ANT family, only ANT2 showed consistent upregulation in MCF7-F4 cells at both RNA and protein levels (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Fig. 4A). Bioinformatics correlation analysis further identified SLC25A5 (ANT2) as the ATP synthase–associated gene most strongly correlated with ATP5B across breast cancer datasets (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

To determine whether ANT2 physically associates with complex V, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), which revealed significantly higher levels of ANT2 co-precipitated with ATP5B in MCF-F4 cells compared to controls, while other complex V subunits showed no such interaction (Fig. 3D–E). We further employed immunofluorescence microscopy, which confirmed a significant colocalization of ANT2 and ATP5B in MCF7-F4 cells, as evidenced by strong yellow fluorescence signals at mitochondrial regions. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence overlap revealed a significantly higher Pearson correlation coefficient in F4 cells than in WT cells, reinforcing the notion that ANT2 associates with ATP synthase in hyperinvasive cancer cells (Fig. 3F and G). We further validated the ANT2 and ATP5B interaction using a flow cytometry–based PLA assay, which detects amplified fluorescence from in situ protein–protein interactions. MCF7-F4 cells exhibited a robust increase in PLA fluorescence intensity compared with WT cells, confirming enhanced ANT2 and ATP5B association at the whole-cell population level (Fig. 3H). Blue native-PAGE (BN-PAGE) analysis further demonstrated that in F4 cells, ATP5B formed a high-molecular-weight (> 720 kDa) oligomeric complex with ANT2, suggesting the existence of a previously uncharacterized ANT2-ATP synthasome assembly (Fig. 3I).

Further investigation into the transcriptional regulation of SLC25A5 revealed that chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and sequencing data identified CREB1 and ZEB1 as direct transcriptional regulators of the ANT2 promoter (Fig. 3J). ChIP-PCR analysis using two independent binding regions confirmed that CREB1 and ZEB1 exhibited significantly higher binding affinity to the ANT2 promoter in MCF7-F4 cells compared to WT cells, suggesting an EMT-driven transcriptional activation of ANT2 (Fig. 3K). siRNA-mediated knockdown of either CREB1 or ZEB1 resulted in a pronounced reduction of ANT2 protein, demonstrating that both transcription factors independently contribute to ANT2 upregulation in hyperinvasive states (Fig. 3L). Collectively, these findings uncover a previously unrecognized metabolic adaptation in hyperinvasive breast cancer cells, wherein ANT2 forms a functional ATP synthasome with ATP5B to sustain mitochondrial bioenergetics.

SLC25A5 as a key regulator of energy metabolism in hyperinvasive breast cancer cells

To define the functional role of SLC25A5 in mitochondrial metabolism and tumor aggressiveness, we first confirmed efficient siRNA-mediated knockdown in both MCF7-WT and hyperinvasive MCF7-F4 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5A–B). Loss of SLC25A5 markedly reduced ANT2 protein abundance and disrupted ANT2 monomer, dimer, and tetramer assembly, as demonstrated by non-reducing PAGE (Fig. 4A). Supplementary Fig. 5C–E further showed that SLC25A5 depletion did not induce compensatory upregulation of SLC25A4 or SLC25A6, nor did it affect the assembly of ANT1 or ANT3, confirming that ANT2 functions independently of other ANT isoforms. We next evaluated the metabolic consequences of SLC25A5 loss. OCR profiles revealed a pronounced reduction in mitochondrial respiration following SLC25A5 knockdown (Fig. 4B), accompanied by significant decreases in basal respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP-linked respiration (Fig. 4C). ATP levels declined accordingly, while ΔΨm unexpectedly increased (Fig. 4D–E), indicating a disruption of mitochondrial coupling. In agreement, glycolytic and TCA-cycle gene expression and metabolite abundance were broadly reduced after SLC25A5 depletion (Fig. 4F–G).

Functionally, SLC25A5 knockdown impaired tumor cell motility. MCF7-F4 cells exhibited reduced migration and invasion in wound-healing and Transwell assays (Fig. 4H–J). Supplementary Fig. 5F further showed that SLC25A5 silencing attenuated TGF-β–induced EMT marker expression, indicating that ANT2 is required to sustain EMT signaling. Invasion assays confirmed that both siSLC25A5 and siATP5B significantly suppressed the invasive capacity of F4 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5G). Importantly, SLC25A5 depletion also reduced invasion in the highly metastatic MDA-MB-231 line (Supplementary Fig. 5H), supporting a conserved requirement for SLC25A5 across breast cancer subtypes. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SLC25A5 is indispensable for maintaining mitochondrial bioenergetics, EMT signaling, and invasive behavior in breast cancer cells.

SLC25A5 facilitated tumor growth and lung metastasis through accelerating mitochondrial function in vivo

To model metastatic evolution in vivo, we established a stepwise selection system using JC mouse mammary carcinoma cells. Parental JC cells (JC-P) were isolated from orthotopic tumors 14 days after implantation (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. 6A). Metastatic enrichment was performed by intravenously injecting JC-P cells and isolating lung metastatic colonies 28 days later. Repeating this cycle for three rounds yielded a highly metastatic derivative, JC-M3. The metastatic potential of each generation was first evaluated by examining lung colonization. Both macroscopic analyses (Fig. 5B) and quantitative histological assessments (Supplementary Fig. 6B) showed a progressive increase in the number of lung metastatic nodules from JC-P to JC-M3.

To determine the molecular alterations underlying this metastatic progression, metastatic lung tissues from M1–M3 were analyzed. Immunoblotting revealed a gradual increase in Ant2 expression, whereas Ant1 and Ant3 remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6C), indicating that Slc25a5 upregulation is specifically selected during metastatic evolution. Subsequent isolation and in vitro expansion of JC-WT, JC-P, and JC-M3 cells demonstrated that JC-M3 cells exhibited the highest proliferative capacity (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. 6D), supporting the notion that metastatic selection enhances tumor fitness. Transcriptomic profiling further confirmed progressive activation of EMT, cytoskeletal remodeling, mitochondrial organization, and metabolic reprogramming pathways in both JC-P and JC-M3 cells compared with JC-WT (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Fig. 6E–H). Further proteomic analysis revealed elevated FAK phosphorylation in both JC-P and JC-M3 cells, while Ant2 expression was specifically upregulated in JC-M3 cells, with no significant changes in Ant1 or Ant3 expression (Fig. 5E). Confocal microscopy confirmed FAK accumulation at the leading edges of migrating cells, correlating with enhanced metastatic potential (Fig. 5F). OCR were significantly elevated in JC-M3 cells, indicating enhanced OXPHOS compared to JC-WT and JC-P cells (Fig. 5G–H). Additional mitochondrial parameters, including ATP levels, ROS, mitochondrial mass, and membrane potential, followed a similar trend, suggesting that metastatic progression is associated with increased mitochondrial activity and bioenergetic demands (Fig. 5I–K).

To directly examine the role of Slc25a5 in metastatic breast cancer, we conducted an in vivo knockdown experiment using a siRNA-based delivery system. JC-M3 cells were orthotopically transplanted into the mammary fat pad, and siSlc25a5 was administered via local injection into the tumor every four days (Fig. 5L). Knockdown of Slc25a5 resulted in a significant inhibition of tumor growth and volume (Fig. 5M–O). Western blot analysis of tumor lysates confirmed that the protein levels of Ant2, Fibronectin, and N-cadherin were significantly decreased in siSlc25a5-treated group, and the aggregation of Ant2 was also reduced, but Atp5b was not significantly changed. IHC staining and quantification further confirmed this result (Fig. 5O–R). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant reduction in ANT2-ATP5B colocalization in siSlc25a5-treated tumors, indicating disrupted ATP synthasome integrity (Fig. 5S–T). These findings establish SLC25A5 as a central regulator of mitochondrial metabolism in metastatic breast cancer, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to disrupt tumor bioenergetics and metastatic progression.

Pharmacogenomic prediction and validation of Cymarin as a Slc25a5-Targeting agent in breast cancer

To identify potential small-molecule inhibitors of SLC25A5 (ANT2), we performed pharmacogenomic screening using Connectivity Map (CMap) and L1000FWD analysis. Drug–gene signature matching revealed a cluster enriched for ATPase inhibitor–like compounds, from which cymarin, digoxin, bufalin, gitoxigenin, and digitoxigenin emerged as top candidates (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. 7A–B). IC₅₀ measurements in MCF7-F4 and JC-M3 cells confirmed potent anti-tumor activity across all five compounds (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. 7C), and all agents significantly reduced ATP production in MCF7-F4 cells (Fig. 6C). Molecular docking further demonstrated that cymarin exhibits the strongest predicted binding affinity toward ANT2 (Fig. 6D), suggesting preferential targeting of the ANT2 pocket. Among all candidates, cymarin was the only drug that consistently reduced SLC25A5 mRNA expression (Fig. 6E), whereas all compounds decreased ATP5B expression (Supplementary Fig. 7D). Functional evaluation confirmed that cymarin robustly impaired tumor cell fitness. In 3D spheroid cultures, cymarin markedly suppressed spheroid growth in both MCF7-WT and MCF7-F4 cells (Fig. 6F–G; Supplementary Fig. 8A). Confocal imaging revealed pronounced reductions in ANT2 protein signals and cytoskeletal organization after cymarin treatment (Fig. 6H–I), indicating coordinated inhibition of mitochondrial function and cytoskeletal integrity. Correlation analysis revealed that SLC25A5 expression strongly aligned with multiple complex V subunits, including ATP5F1B, ATP5MC3, and TUB1C (Supplementary Fig. 8B–D). Consistent with this mechanistic link, cymarin markedly reduced invasive capacity across several breast cancer models, demonstrating a broad anti-metastatic effect (Supplementary Fig. 8E–F).

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic potential of cymarin, JC-M3 cells were orthotopically implanted into the mammary glands of mice, followed by daily intratumoral cymarin administration for seven days (Fig. 6J). Cymarin treatment markedly reduced tumor growth and final tumor volume (Fig. 6K–M). In addition, lung metastasis burden was substantially diminished, indicating that metastatic progression depends on ANT2 function (Fig. 6M–N). Histological examination of the heart, liver, and kidney showed no notable tissue alterations following cymarin treatment (Supplementary Fig. 8G). Protein profiling of tumor tissues showed that cymarin reduced EMT marker expression, including α-SMA, fibronectin, and N-cadherin, while modestly increasing E-cadherin levels (Fig. 6O). Consistent with its proposed mechanism, cymarin significantly downregulated Ant2, ATP5B, and CREB expression (Fig. 6P–Q; Supplementary Fig. 8H–J). Non-reducing PAGE further demonstrated that cymarin disrupted ANT2 assembly, decreasing monomeric, dimeric, and tetrameric forms, along with broad suppression of OXPHOS complexes (Fig. 6R–S). Metabolic profiling further indicated impaired mitochondrial function, as glucose availability declined, energy-metabolism genes were downregulated, and several key TCA-cycle metabolites were reduced (Fig. 6T–V). Together, these findings demonstrate that cymarin disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics, particularly the ANT2–ATP synthasome axis, leading to suppression of tumor growth and metastatic potential in vivo.

Spatial transcriptomic and tissue-based analyses validate co-upregulation of SLC25A5 and ATP5B in human breast cancer

To delineate the spatial distribution of SLC25A5 and ATP5B expression in human breast tumors, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic signatures from reference tumor datasets. High-resolution mapping of tumor and non-tumor regions revealed elevated SLC25A5 and ATP5B expression within tumor cell clusters compared with adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 7A–C; Supplementary Fig. 9A–D). High-magnification tissue imaging further illustrated the histological context and regional localization of these genes across distinct tumor compartments (Fig. 7D; Supplementary Fig. 9E). Immune landscape profiling demonstrated an enrichment of macrophages, CD8⁺ T cells, and naïve B cells within the tumor microenvironment, whereas other immune populations appeared less abundant (Supplementary Fig. 9F). Importantly, Visium HD analysis of a TNBC specimen from a local patient cohort showed a spatial expression pattern of SLC25A5 and ATP5B that closely mirrored the trends observed in the reference datasets (Supplementary Fig. 9G), providing independent validation of their tumor-specific upregulation in clinical samples.

To establish the relationship between ANT2 (SLC25A5) and ATP5B across breast cancer progression, we first performed triple-labeling immunofluorescence on BRCA TMA samples, staining for ANT2, ATP5B, and DAPI. The cohort was categorized into non-tumor, early-stage (IIA), advanced-stage (IIB–IIIC), and lymph node metastatic tumors. Panoramic tissue imaging demonstrated low ANT2 (red) and ATP5B (green) expression in non-tumor and early-stage samples, whereas advanced tumors and lymph node metastases displayed markedly stronger signals (Fig. 7E). High-magnification imaging further showed clear colocalization of ANT2 and ATP5B, producing prominent yellow overlap signals, consistent with increased ANT2–ATP5B association during tumor progression. Quantitative analysis confirmed a positive correlation between ANT2 and ATP5B expression across tumor stages (Fig. 7F–H), which was independently validated using 59 BRCA biopsy specimens (Fig. 7I). To determine whether this relationship is conserved across molecular subtypes, we extended the analysis to TCGA breast cancer cohorts. Subtype-stratified correlation plots revealed a consistent positive association between SLC25A5 and ATP5B in Basal, Her2-enriched, Luminal A, and Luminal B subtypes, with significant correlation coefficients observed in each group (Supplementary Fig. 9H). These results were further supported by the TMIER database, which also indicated a strong SLC25A5–ATP5B linkage in breast cancer (Fig. 7J). Together, these findings demonstrate that the ANT2–ATP5B axis is not restricted to a specific breast cancer subtype but instead represents a broadly conserved feature across disease stages and molecular classifications, supporting its potential role in tumor bioenergetics and metastatic competence.

Discussion

Our study establishes ANT2, encoded by SLC25A5, as a pivotal regulator of mitochondrial energy metabolism and a critical driver of breast cancer progression, particularly in its metastatic phase. The significant overexpression of ANT2 in breast cancer patients with lymph node metastasis, coupled with its strong correlation with reduced overall survival, relapse-free survival, and disease-free survival, positions it as a robust prognostic marker [21]. This finding is consistent with prior reports that link ANT2 upregulation to aggressive tumor phenotypes across cancers such as lung, hepatocellular, and breast carcinomas, where it sustains mitochondrial function under hypoxic or nutrient-deprived conditions [11, 34]. By integrating multi-omics data from TCGA, GEO, and TNMplot with functional assays, we demonstrate that ANT2 enhances ATP production through OXPHOS, a process increasingly recognized as essential for fueling the bioenergetic demands of metastatic cancer cells [5, 35]. This challenges the traditional Warburg-centric view and highlights the metabolic plasticity of breast cancer.

A key discovery is the formation of the ATP synthasome in hyperinvasive MCF7-F4 cells, where ANT2 physically interacts with ATP synthase subunit ATP5B to form a > 720 kDa supercomplex, as evidenced by BN-PAGE and co-immunoprecipitation. This structural assembly, previously characterized in normal mitochondria for efficient ATP synthesis and export [14], appears to be hijacked by metastatic breast cancer cells to optimize energy flux. Tommasin et al. provided biochemical evidence in liver mitochondria that ANT2 directly binds to ATP synthase subunits c and g, and that their interaction is modulated by oligomycin and Bz-423, supporting a regulatory interface between the two complexes [36].​ Yet, this interaction was only examined in the context of permeability transition and not in the context of cancer or metastasis. The increased ΔΨm and OXPHOS capacity in MCF7-F4 cells, even when ETC complexes I-IV were inhibited, suggest that ANT2-mediated ATP transport and complex V activity are central to maintaining mitochondrial efficiency [37]. Notably, the lack of ΔΨm attenuation with complex V inhibition (oligomycin) in F4 cells, contrasted with its increase in wild-type cells, points to a unique reliance on the ANT2-ATP5B partnership in aggressive cancer cells. This is further supported by the upregulation of ANT2, but not ANT1 or ANT3, in hyperinvasive models, aligning with ANT2’s known induction in proliferative and glycolytic states [9, 34].

Although ATP5B expression also increased in hyper-invasive MCF7-F4 cells, this change is best interpreted as a secondary metabolic adaptation rather than a primary driver. F4 cells exhibit globally elevated mitochondrial activity, and ATP5B—being a core catalytic subunit of ATP synthase—naturally rises in response to higher OXPHOS demand. Importantly, our knockdown data reveal functional asymmetry: silencing SLC25A5 markedly reduces ATP5B levels, ATP output, and metastatic behaviors, whereas ATP5B knockdown produces only partial inhibition and does not induce ANT2 compensation. Moreover, ATP5B depletion decreases invasiveness in F4 cells, but to a lesser degree than ANT2 loss. These findings support a model in which ATP5B facilitates increased mitochondrial flux, while ANT2 acts as the upstream regulator that coordinates ATP synthasome assembly and fuels metastatic energy demands.

The transcriptional regulation of ANT2 by CREB1 and ZEB1, both implicated in EMT and metastasis, provides a molecular mechanism for its overexpression in metastatic breast cancer [19, 20]. ChIP assays confirmed enhanced binding of these transcription factors to the ANT2 promoter in MCF7-F4 cells, suggesting that EMT-driven signaling pathways amplify ANT2 expression to support mitochondrial bioenergetics. This aligns with studies showing that EMT induces metabolic reprogramming, including increased OXPHOS, to sustain cellular motility and invasion [18, 38]. During EMT, cancer cells experience an elevated demand for ATP to drive cytoskeletal remodeling, focal adhesion turnover, and matrix degradation. The ANT2–ATP5B axis fulfills this energetic requirement by coupling ATP synthesis with rapid nucleotide exchange, thus maintaining high OXPHOS flux and mitochondrial membrane potential in mesenchymal-like cells. Silencing ANT2 disrupts this coupling, leading to energy stress and secondary suppression of metabolic genes involved in glycolysis (HK2, PKM1), pyruvate oxidation (PDHB), and the TCA cycle (CS, FH, MDH1). These effects likely arise from an AMPK–CREB1–dependent energy-sensing feedback loop rather than direct transcriptional regulation. Consequently, ANT2 acts as a metabolic bridge linking EMT-associated transcriptional programs to mitochondrial energy output, enabling cells to sustain the bioenergetic plasticity required for invasion and metastasis. In vivo, the JC-M3 metastatic model reinforced ANT2’s role, with siRNA-mediated knockdown significantly reducing tumor growth, lung metastases, and ATP synthasome integrity. The accompanying decrease in EMT markers and OXPHOS activity underscores ANT2’s dual contribution to bioenergetics and cytoskeletal dynamics, consistent with reports linking mitochondrial function to actin remodeling in cancer [39].

Pharmacogenomic screening identified cymarin as a potent ANT2 inhibitor, offering a therapeutic avenue to disrupt this metabolic axis. In 3D cultures, cymarin reduced spheroid formation by 60%, while in orthotopic mouse models, it shrank tumor volume by 70% and lung metastases by 80%, downregulating ANT2, ATP5B, and OXPHOS pathways. As a cardiac glycoside, cymarin’s mechanism likely involves disrupting mitochondrial ion gradients and energy production, mirroring its effects in other cancers [40]. This aligns with growing interest in targeting mitochondrial metabolism, as seen with OXPHOS inhibitors like metformin or IACS-010759 in preclinical cancer models [41, 42]. The reduction in EMT markers post-cymarin treatment further suggests a broader impact on tumor plasticity, potentially via cytoskeletal modulation, warranting exploration of its off-target effects [43].

Spatially resolved transcriptomics and immunofluorescence validated the clinical relevance of the ANT2/ATP5B axis, showing their co-upregulation in late-stage and metastatic breast tumors. This progression-dependent pattern positions the ATP synthasome as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target. However, limitations include the study’s focus on specific cell lines (MCF7, JC), which may not fully represent TNBC or other subtypes, and the need for larger, diverse patient cohorts to confirm ANT2’s prognostic utility [16]. Cymarin’s clinical translation also requires pharmacokinetic optimization and toxicity profiling, given its narrow therapeutic window as a glycoside [44]. Future directions should investigate ANT2’s role across breast cancer subtypes, its interplay with other mitochondrial complexes, and combinatorial strategies pairing cymarin with chemotherapy or immunotherapy to overcome resistance [45].

It is worth noting that while the “ATP synthasome” was first conceptualized decades ago as a structural unit coordinating ANT, phosphate carrier, and ATP synthase for energy flux, almost all prior characterizations were limited to heart or liver mitochondria under physiological conditions. Its existence and functional repurposing in cancer, particularly in metastatic settings, have not been documented before this study. This gap highlights the novelty of our findings and introduces a mechanistic paradigm wherein supramolecular mitochondrial architecture is co-opted to sustain the energetic needs of invasive tumors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the pivotal role of SLC25A5 (ANT2) in the progression of breast cancer, specifically in driving mitochondrial function and energy metabolism in hyperinvasive cancer cells. SLC25A5 overexpression correlates with poor prognosis and promotes mitochondrial efficiency, leading to enhanced ATP production and metastatic potential in breast cancer. The formation of the ATP synthasome, involving SLC25A5 and ATP5B, underscores the importance of mitochondrial in cancer cell invasiveness (Fig. 8). Additionally, the knockdown of SLC25A5 results in significant inhibition of tumor growth and metastasis, both in vitro and in vivo, further validating its potential as a therapeutic target. Pharmacological inhibition of SLC25A5 with cymarin effectively suppressed tumor growth and metastasis by disrupting mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. These findings position SLC25A5 as a valuable prognostic marker and a promising target for precision oncology in breast cancer treatment.
