Early decision regret and its relationship with decision participation among breast cancer patients undergoing surgery
Authors: Qinqin Cheng, Qianlin Jiang, Xi Chen, Xiaobo Hu, Mingzhi Xia, Yingchun Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDecision regret is a negative emotion that persistently impacts the patients’ quality of life and future decision-making process. This study aimed to explore the early decision regret among breast cancer patients undergoing surgery and its relationship with their perceived importance and actual participation in decision-making.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from August 2024 to March 2025. Breast cancer patients undergoing surgery were recruited by convenience sampling. All the participants completed the decision regret scale and the questionnaire measuring perceived importance of participation and the degree of participation in decision-making about treatment, in one month after they received surgery.ResultsA total of 213 breast cancer patients undergoing surgery participated in this study. Among them, 85.4 % (n = 182) experienced early decision regret, with 43.2 % of them (n = 92) having a low level of decision regret and 42.2 % (n = 90) having a high level of decision regret. Their decision regret was significantly associated with their actual participation in decision-making (B = 8.246, 95 % CI: 2.037–14.455, p = 0.009). but not with their perceived importance of participation. The lower the extent of participation in decision-making, the higher the level of decision regret.ConclusionThe majority of the breast cancer patients receiving surgery experienced early decision regret. It is associated with their actual participation in decision-making. Promoting breast cancer patients’ participation in their own decision-making, such as patient-centered communication and a shared decision-making model, may have the potential to alleviate their regrets about their early decisions.Keywords: Breast cancer, Surgery, Decision regret, Decision participation

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer type among women [1]. In its early stages, surgery is the primary approach to treatment. With the advancement in medical care, patients now have several surgical options to choose from, such as mastectomy, breast reconstruction, or breast-conserving surgery [2]. Women who need to have a mastectomy may consider whether they would like breast reconstruction, what type of reconstruction, and when. Women diagnosed with breast cancer need to make their own decisions about treatment after considering various factors, such as the treatment complications, the impact on their body image, concern about recurrence, subsequent radiotherapy, fertility issues, or financial concerns [[3], [4], [5]]. As a result, choosing the appropriate treatment is often a complex and challenging process.

One possible consequence of choosing alternative treatment options for breast cancer patients is patients' regret about their decisions, especially when they are dissatisfied with the outcomes of the treatment they selected. Decision regret is a negative cognitive-emotional experience that arises from a health-related decision in an immediate or delayed occurrence [6,7]. Research has shown that decision regret is common among breast cancer patients [[8], [9], [10]]. One study has explored the decision regret among breast cancer patients 14 months to 4 years after their completion of adjuvant radiotherapy. Results showed that 72.1 % of them expressed decision regret, among which 58.2 % experienced mild decision regret and 13.9 % experienced high decision regret [11]. Another study investigated the breast cancer patients' decision regret at 9 months and 4 years after diagnosis, finding that the level of regret remained relatively stable over time [12]. Persistent decision regret can lead to patients' negative emotions, such as depression, sadness, and guilt, and may adversely affect their quality of life [13,14]. In addition, it can impact the patients’ future decision-making process [6,14].

Research has shown that decision regret was associated with time since operation [15]. Nevertheless, most of the current studies investigated the decision regret a long time after surgery, with limited attention to early decision regret. Early decision regret may occur within the first few months following treatment, a period when patients begin to process the physical, emotional, and psychological outcomes of their treatment choices [16]. In the context of breast cancer, this period often coincides with the initial recovery phase after surgery, when patients encounter immediate treatment effects such as surgical outcomes, side effects, or changes in body image [17]. Given the high likelihood of early decision regret post-surgery and its potential long-term psychological impact, recognition of early decision regret is critical. Early identification can facilitate timely interventions to prevent long-term psychological consequences.

A variety of factors have been found to be related to the degree of decision regret among breast cancer patients, such as age, education level, treatment factors, and postoperative complications [8,11,18]. The majority of the abovementioned factors are not modifiable, highlighting the importance of identifying modifiable variables to inform future interventions.

In recent decades, shared decision-making between physicians and patients has been increasingly promoted [19]. However, breast cancer patients' readiness to engage in decision-making may vary due to factors such as age, cultural background, educational background, close relationships, emotional distress, etc. [20]. These factors can affect the patients’ perceived importance and actual participation in decision-making about their treatments. Cancer patients hold different attitudes towards participation in decision making, and their preference for their treatment varies widely. Some prefer active participation while some prefer passive or shared involvement [21]. Yet, the relationship between their perceived importance and actual participation and subsequent decision regret remains unknown.

To address these gaps, the present study was conducted to explore the early decision regret among breast cancer patients undergoing surgery and its relationship with their perceived importance and actual participation in decision-making.

Methods

This study was a cross-sectional study. Ethical approval of this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at Hunan Cancer Hospital (N0. 2025[88]) and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. The report of this study conformed to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology.

Participants

Breast cancer patients undergoing surgery were recruited using convenience sampling from August 2024 to March 2025 at a tertiary cancer hospital in China. The inclusion criteria for the participants were: 1) being diagnosed with breast cancer; 2) being aged 18 or above; 3) having received any curative surgery for the breast due to breast cancer. Patients who were unable to read and understand the content of the questionnaires or those with severe psychological or cognitive impairment were excluded.

Variables

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

The participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (i.e. age, gender, education level, marital status, current working status) and clinical characteristics (stage of cancer, time since diagnosis, receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, receipt of adjuvant endocrine therapy, type of surgery, and decision maker) were collected.

Decision regret

Decision regret was measured using the Chinese version of the decision regret scale (DRS). DRS is a unidimensional scale and includes five items. All the items are responded to with a five-point Likert scale scoring from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree). The responses are converted to a total score from 0 (no regret) to 100 (high regret). The higher the score, the greater the degree of regret [22]. There is no consensus on the cut-off score for distinguishing low or high regret. In this study, a cutoff score of 25 was selected, supported by prior research validating the Chinese version of the DRS through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis [23]. Scores of ≤25 are classified as indicating low regret, while scores >25 as high regret. This criteria was consistent with the previous studies [24]. The internal consistency of the Chinese version of DRS was 0.74 and the test-retest reliability was 0.71, which demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and test-retest reliability [23].

Participation in decision-making

Participation in decision-making was measured by the questionnaires designed by Sainio and Lauri [25]. It was composed of 12 items, which were answered by two subscales: perceived importance of participation in decision-making and the degree of participation in decision-making about treatment. Patients' perceived importance was responded to using a 3-point Likert scale from “important” (1) to “not at all” (3). The degree of participation was also responded to using a 3-point Likert scale from “to a great extent” (1) to “not at all” (3). Average item scores were calculated to indicate the patients' attitudes towards and the degree of participation in decision-making, with a lower score indicating more importance and a greater extent of participation in decision-making. The original version was translated into Chinese by Ma and has sound reliability and validity [26]. The content validity was 0.89 and the Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.851 and 0.838 for the two subscales, respectively [26].

Data collection procedure

The data were collected by a research nurse. As this study aimed to investigate the early decision regret, the research nurse screened the eligibility of the breast cancer patients one month after they received breast surgery. At 1 month post-surgery, patients have typically progressed beyond the immediate postoperative period (e.g., acute pain and wound healing) but are still early in their adjustment to the physical, emotional, and psychological impacts of their treatment decision [17]. This period allows patients to begin reflecting on their surgical choice while the decision-making process remains relatively fresh, making it an optimal time to capture early decision regret. Eligible patients were invited to participate in this study after being informed of the purposes, what they needed to do, and the potential benefits or harms of this study to the patients. After obtaining the participants' informed consent, the participants were invited to fill out the questionnaires. The research nurse would answer the participants’ inquiries during the process.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 26.0. Continuous data were evaluated for normality by assessing skewness and kurtosis and by a visual inspection of Q-Q plots [27]. All continuous data follows the normal distribution. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were described using means and standard deviations when they were continuous data, if they were normal, and using numbers and frequency when categorical data. Univariate analysis, including two-sample t-test, one-way analysis of variance, and Pearson correlation analysis, was used to screen the potential factors that would be included in the regression model. Pearson correlation analysis was also conducted to assess the linear relationships between the independent variables and the dependent variable [28]. Then, a hierarchical regression model was conducted. Sociodemographic and clinical variables that were associated with decision regret at p < 0.25 in univariable analysis were entered in Step 1 [29]. Then the perceived importance and participation in decision-making were entered in Step 2. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to measure the multicollinearity among the independent variables in a multiple regression model. Multicollinearity was not present among the independent variables when the VIF was lower than 5 [30]. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 220 breast cancer patients undergoing surgery were invited to participate in this study, with 213 of them filling out the questionnaires. The response rate was 96.4 %. All the patients were female. The mean age of the participants was 47.60 ± 9.00 years. The vast majority of them had spouses (90.1 %) and had medical insurance (91.1 %). Most of the participants had an educational level of middle school (46.0 %) and lived in urban areas (51.2 %). Regarding clinical characteristics, most of the participants were at stage II (46.9 %), had neoadjuvant chemotherapy (71.8 %), and had implant-based reconstruction after mastectomy (53.1 %). The medical decision for most of the participants (62.0 %) was made jointly by patients and their family members (Table 1).

Decision regret and participation in decision-making

The mean score of decision regret was 25.45 ± 17.66. While the majority (n = 182, 85.4 %) of patients did report early decision regret, actually 43.2 % reported low levels of regret and 42.2 % reported high levels of regret. The mean item-average score of perceived importance of participation in decision-making was 1.25 ± 0.37, while the mean score of participation in decision-making was 1.39 ± 0.46. The Pearson correlation analysis showed that the score of decision regret was positively related to the scores of perceived importance (r = 0.196, p = 0.004) and actual participation (r = 0.280, p < 0.001) in decision-making.

Multivariate analysis

The results of the univariate analysis show that the score of decision regret was related to education level, current working status, stage of cancer, and receipt of adjuvant endocrine therapy at p < 0.25 (Table 1). The multiple regression analysis was performed with decision regret as the dependent variable and perceived importance and actual participation in decision-making as independent variables. Those variables that were correlated with decision regret at p < 0.25 were adjusted. All VIF values were below 5, demonstrating that no multicollinearity existed among these independent variables. The results showed that patients’ participation in decision-making was related to their decision regret (B = 8.246, 95 % CI: 2.037–14.455, p = 0.009) after controlling the covariates (education level, current working status, stage of cancer, and receipt of adjuvant endocrine therapy). The lower the extent of participation in decision-making, the higher the level of decision regret. The change of R-squared for participation in decision making was 6.6 % (Fchange = 7.799, pchange = 0.001). The final model explained 10.8 % of the variance (F = 5.226, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

To our best knowledge, this study was the first to investigate the decision regret among breast cancer patients at an early period after surgery. As the majority of studies focused on decision regret long-term after treatment, early decision regret is critical for early intervention to prevent the long-term impact on patients’ quality of life and future decision process. The results of this study can inform the early intervention for decision regret among breast cancer patients receiving surgery.

In this study, we found that 85.4 % of the patients experienced decision regret, which is higher than the rate reported in a previous study that explored decision regret among breast cancer patients after receiving different treatment modalities [9]. This variation in the prevalence of regret may be attributed to differences in decision-making contexts, cultural influences, healthcare systems, or the timing of treatment decisions assessed. Our study investigated the decision regret of breast cancer patients who had just completed surgery. The average score of decision regret above 25 highlighted a relatively high level of regret during the immediate postoperative period. Given that decision regret can negatively impact quality of life and influence subsequent medical decisions after surgery [6,13,14], this result underscores the need for early intervention to mitigate regret. However, the proportion of patients experiencing regret in this study was lower than that reported in another study involving breast cancer survivors in China who received surgery a long time ago [13]. This indicated that the regret among breast cancer patients may persist for a long time. Nevertheless, this hypothesis needs verification through a rigorous longitudinal study to better understand the trajectory and persistence of decision regret in breast cancer patients.

Notably, 43.2 % of the breast cancer patients had a low level of decision regret and 42.2 % of them had a high level of decision regret. Breast cancer surgery had a significant impact on women's body image, self-confidence, and overall quality of life [17]. Patients must carefully weigh their prognosis against personal concerns and preferences, making the choice of surgical treatment particularly challenging. The high proportion of high-level decision regret highlights the importance of providing support and patient-centered communication throughout the treatment process to minimize the likelihood and severity of decision regret [31].

In this study, the regression model explained 10.8 % of the variance in decision regret, which suggests the model captures a meaningful but limited portion of the factors influencing decision regret. It was typical in behavioral or psychological studies where complex outcomes like decision regret are influenced by numerous unmeasured factors (e.g., emotional state, social support, or patient expectations) [24]. In future studies, additional factors influencing decision regret are expected to be explored. In addition, the ΔR2 of 6.6 %, contributing significantly to the total 10.8 %, highlights that patients' participation in decision-making was a key factor related to their decision regret. Specifically, those patients who participated less in their own decision-making experienced a higher level of decision regret. These results may be explained by the following reasons. Limited participation in decision-making of breast cancer patients may be caused by lack of sufficient information, including the risks, benefits, or long-term impact of their treatment options, due to inadequate communication with their physicians [32]. This information gap may lead patients to question the decision later, thereby increasing the likelihood of decision regret. Moreover, less engagement in the decision process may make the patients feel like the choice was made for them rather than with them. Lack of control may result in later questioning or second-guessing those decisions, particularly if the outcomes are not as expected [33]. This result suggests that promoting the patient's self-participation in decision-making could be a potential strategy to mitigate decision regret.

Another noteworthy finding of this study was that the perceived importance of participation was not significantly associated with decision regret in the multivariate analysis, despite showing a correlation in the univariate analysis. This finding may indicate a potential discrepancy between patients' perceived and actual involvement in decision-making [21]. Clinically, these findings highlighted the importance of not only recognizing patients’ desire for involvement but also ensuring that they were effectively engaged in decision-making through practical interventions such as decision aids, preoperative counseling, or structured shared decision-making tools to improve patient support [34].

Implications for practice

The results of this study demonstrated that participation in decision-making was associated with decision regret among breast cancer patients undergoing surgery. Healthcare providers could encourage patients to engage in their own decisions regarding surgical approaches. First, patient-centered communication is critical in the decision process. Enhancing communication between healthcare providers and patients, actively involving patients in discussions about their options, and ensuring they understand the risks and benefits of each choice may help reduce the risk of decision regret. Second, health care providers need to advocate shared decision-making in clinical practice to reduce decision regret [14]. Shared decision-making is an important component of patient-centered care, allowing patients to align medical decisions with their personal values and preferences [35]. Our findings underscore the importance of fostering a collaborative decision-making environment that involves patients themselves in clinical practice. Additionally, strategies and tools in preoperative consultations that support patient engagement throughout the treatment decision process can be considered, ultimately reducing the likelihood of postoperative decision regret.

Strengths and limitations

This study can provide insight into improving decision regret among breast cancer patients receiving surgery. Limitations of this study also need to be acknowledged. First, the sample of this study was selected from one hospital by convenience sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, this study employed a quantitative design, which provided valuable insights into the prevalence and patterns of decision regret among participants. However, it lacked a qualitative component to understand more about the participants’ decision regret and how the caregivers have an impact on their feelings of regret. In the future, a qualitative approach could have offered a deeper understanding of decision regret. Third, this study recruited patients with different surgeries, which may have varied recovery and complications, further influencing the feelings of regret. However, we did not collect data related to recovery and complications. The imbalanced proportions between breast cancer patients who underwent breast-conserving surgery and other surgical approaches may obscure significant differences in decision regret. Future studies may include comparable numbers of patients across different surgical methods and assess recovery-related factors to better explore potential associations with decision regret.

Conclusions

The majority of the breast cancer patients receiving surgery experienced early decision regret, and the level of decision regret was relatively high. It is associated with the actual participation in decision-making. Potential strategies that can facilitate the patients’ engagement in decision-making, such as patient-centered communication and a shared decision-making model, may has the potential to reduce decision regret.
