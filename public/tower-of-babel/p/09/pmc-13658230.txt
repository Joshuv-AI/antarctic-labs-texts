Lung adenocarcinoma cell-derived lactate activates CAFs to promote macrophage M2 polarization via exosomal miR-214-5p
Authors: Leilei Tao, Fengbiao Guo, Xiaobei Liu, Xiao Zhao, Ya Xue, Weigang Bian, Jing Chen, Shaoyi Zhang, Bin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key components of the tumor microenvironment that can drive cancer progression and immune evasion. Here, we identify a novel pathway in which lactate produced by lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cells activates normal fibroblasts into CAFs, inducing high expression of microRNA miR-214-5p. The CAFs secrete miR-214-5p via exosomes, which in turn polarizes tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, exosomal miR-214-5p targets the transcriptional repressor BHLHE41 in macrophages, relieving its inhibition on M2-polarization genes. We further demonstrate that lactate accumulation epigenetically upregulates miR-214-5p in CAFs by increasing histone H3 lysine 9 lactylation (H3K9la) at the DNM3OS/miR-214 gene locus, independently of the lactate receptor GPR81. Lactate also enhances the loading of miR-214-5p into exosomes by inducing lactylation of the RNA-binding protein YBX1, which binds miR-214-5p and facilitates its exosomal export. In a mouse co-implantation model of LUAD, pharmacological blockade of tumor lactate production (LDH inhibition) reduced CAF activation, TAM M2 polarization, miR-214-5p levels, and tumor growth. Our findings uncover a lactate-driven CAF-TAM signaling axis via exosomal miR-214-5p, highlighting potential therapeutic targets to counteract tumor immune evasion in lung adenocarcinoma.Subject terms: Non-small-cell lung cancer, Non-coding RNAs

Introduction

The tumor stroma, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), plays a crucial role in modulating tumor progression. CAFs are phenotypically altered fibroblasts that have been “educated” by cancer cells, acquiring highly heterogeneous features and secreting a plethora of factors that promote cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Recent studies indicate that CAFs also contribute to tumor immune evasion by modulating immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. For example, CAFs can establish an immunosuppressive niche by interacting with macrophages and other immune cells [1, 2]. In different oncological diseases, subsets of CAFs including CD36+,CCL19+, AKAP12+ have been shown to promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment via the secretion of cytokines that affect macrophage function [3–5]. suggesting that CAF-mediated immune modulation is a broad phenomenon not limited to one cancer type.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are often polarized to an “M2” phenotype in tumors, which is associated with immunosuppressive and pro-tumoral activities such as promoting angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and inhibition of cytotoxic T cells. CAFs and TAMs engage in dynamic crosstalk: CAF-derived factors can skew macrophage polarization towards M2, while TAMs secrete signals that further activate fibroblasts, creating a feed-forward loop of immunosuppression. However, the detailed molecular mechanisms by which CAFs induce M2 polarization of macrophages remain incompletely understood.

One emerging mode of stromal-tumor communication is through exosomes, the small extracellular vesicles that shuttle microRNAs (miRNAs) and other biomolecules between cells. miRNAs are abundant noncoding RNAs that can be selectively packaged into exosomes by donor cells and influence gene expression in recipient cells [6, 7]. For instance, Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) has been reported to bind and load certain miRNAs (e.g., miR-223) into exosomes [8]. and SRSF1 (also known as SFRS1) can selectively enrich miRNAs like miR-1246 into exosomes [9]. We hypothesized that CAFs might deliver pro-tumoral signals to macrophages via exosomal miRNAs, and set out to identify such factors.

Another key feature of tumor metabolism that influences the microenvironment is the accumulation of lactate. Due to the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis), cancer cells such as lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cells produce and excrete large amounts of lactate [10]. Lactate is no longer viewed as a mere waste product; instead, it serves as a signaling molecule and substrate that surrounding stromal cells can uptake and respond to [11, 12]. In fact, lactate has been shown to drive the transformation of normal fibroblasts into inflammatory CAFs in hypoxic tumor regions [13–15]. Lactate can also induce epigenetic modifications, notably histone lysine lactylation (Kla), which directly upregulate gene transcription. Histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) has emerged as a key marker of lactate-driven gene activation [16, 17]. For example, in bladder cancer cells, H3K18la enrichment at gene promoters activates transcription of YBX1 and YY1, two transcription factors that promote chemotherapy resistance [18]. Similarly, in hepatocellular carcinoma, lactate-activated H3K18la forms a positive feedback loop by enhancing YBX1 gene expression, which in turn promotes glycolysis and further lactate production [19].

Given this context, we aimed to investigate whether lactate produced by LUAD cells could modulate CAF behavior and the CAF-macrophage interaction through epigenetic upregulation and exosomal transfer of specific miRNAs. Our study uncovers that LUAD-derived lactate induces the expression of miR-214-5p in CAFs via histone lactylation and facilitates its loading into exosomes via YBX1 lactylation. Exosomal transfer of miR-214-5p from CAFs to macrophages drives M2 polarization by targeting the transcriptional repressor BHLHE41. We also validate the physiological relevance of this pathway in vivo, providing new insights into how metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells (lactate secretion) can reshape the immune microenvironment through epigenetic and exosomal mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

A549, H1975, and THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, PM150110) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (Pricella, PB180120). RAW264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM (Procell, PM153210) with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were grown in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cultures were passaged every 2–3 days, and cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for experiments. All cell lines were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were used within 10 passages after resuscitation.

Isolation of normal fibroblasts (NFs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)

Human lung cancer tissues and paired adjacent normal lung tissues (at least 5 cm from the tumor margin) were obtained from surgically resected specimens with ethical approval and all patients. All tissues were minced into ~1 mm³ fragments, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and digested in 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I at 37 °C with agitation (90 min for normal tissue; 2 h for tumor tissue). The digested suspension was filtered through a 100 μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. Fibroblasts were purified by differential adhesion: after plating, cells were allowed to attach for 1.5 h; the medium containing non-adherent cells was then removed and replaced with fresh DMEM/F12 (Procell, PM150312). Cultures were continued with medium changes every 3 days.

Co-culture models

LUAD–NF co-culture: 1 × 105 normal fibroblasts (NFs) were seeded in the lower chamber of a Transwell plate. After the NFs attached, 1 × 105 A549 cells (lung adenocarcinoma) were added to the upper chamber. The cells were co-cultured for 24 h, after which NFs and tumor cells were collected separately for downstream analyses.

LUAD–macrophage co-culture: THP-1 monocytes were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Biosharp, BL1127A) for 24 h to differentiate them into resting (M0) macrophages. Then, 1 × 105 CAFs were seeded in the lower Transwell chamber. Once the CAFs adhered, 1 × 105 THP-1-derived macrophages (M0) or 1 × 105 RAW264.7 macrophages were added to the upper chamber. Co-culture was carried out for 24 h.

Drug treatments

CAFs were treated with 10 μM GW4869 (Selleck, S7609) for 48 h to inhibit exosome secretion, and LUAD cells were treated with 30 μM lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) inhibitor FX-11 (Selleck, S8928) for 48 h. After treatment, culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove debris. The clarified supernatant was then added to NFs to establish an exosome “blockade” model. Separately, NFs and CAFs were treated with sodium L-lactate (MCE, HY-B2227) at various concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20 mM) for 24 h.

Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596018CN). RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, RR037A). Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, RR820A) on a real-time PCR system. β-Actin was used as the internal control, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Tables 2.

Western blotting

Cells were harvested and lysed on ice in RIPA buffer for 5 min, followed by brief sonication. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Solarbio, PC0020). Proteins were denatured and separated by SDS-PAGE, then transferred to PVDF membranes at a constant current of 200 mA for 1.5 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Proteintech, PK10002) and imaged; band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. Primary antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Tables 3.

Scratch wound and transwell invasion assays

For the scratch assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 100% confluence. A straight scratch (wound) was made through the monolayer using a 100 μL pipette tip. Wells were washed three times with PBS to remove detached cells, and serum-free medium containing 2% FBS was added. Cell migration into the wound area was imaged at 0 h and 24 h using an optical microscope. The wound closure area was quantified to assess cell migration.

For invasion assay, the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (Corning, 3422) was coated with Matrigel matrix (R&D Systems, 3632-005-02), which was allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 4 h. Then, 600 μL of medium containing chemoattractant (10% FBS) was added to the lower chamber. A total of 2 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-invading cells were removed from the upper surface. Cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Beyotime, C0121) for 20 min, and washed. Invading cells were counted in five random fields per membrane under a light microscope (200×).

Exosome isolation and characterization

Exosomes were isolated from conditioned media by differential ultracentrifugation. First, culture medium was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove cells. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min and 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove debris and larger vesicles. The cleared supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min to pellet exosomes. The pellet was washed with PBS and ultracentrifuged again at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The final exosome pellet was resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) and incubated on ice for 3 min to prepare exosome lysate. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4 °C. Isolated exosomes were characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and Western blotting.

Exosome uptake assay

Purified exosomes were labeled using the Hieff® exosome tracker kit (PKH26, Yeasen, 40780ES20) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the exosome pellet was resuspended and incubated with the PKH26 fluorescent dye solution at room temperature in the dark. Staining was stopped by adding an equal volume of 1% BSA. Labeled exosomes were purified by ultracentrifugation to remove excess dye and resuspended in PBS. These labeled exosomes were then incubated with recipient cells. Exosome uptake was verified by observing red fluorescence within cells under a fluorescence microscope.

Plasmid and siRNA transfection

Plasmid transfection: A synthetic miR-214-5p mimic (Shanghai Sangon Biotech) was used. The BHLHE41 coding sequence was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-Flag vector for overexpression. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to ~70–80% confluence. Plasmid DNA was mixed with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000008) in serum-free medium and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The transfection mixture was then added to cells, and plates were gently swirled. Transfection was allowed for 48 h before downstream analysis.

siRNA transfection: Gene-specific siRNAs targeting GPR81, YBX1, and SFRS1 were designed. Cells were plated and grown to 70–90% confluence. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778075) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The ratio of transfection reagent to siRNA was 0.3 μL per 1 pmol (equivalent to 1.5 μL reagent per 50 pmol siRNA). The sequences of siRNAs and other oligonucleotides used are listed in Supplementary Tables 4.

Flow cytometry

Cells were collected and washed with PBS, then resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer. Fluorescent antibodies were added: anti-CD206 (PE-conjugated; Proteintech, cat. 98031 for mouse, 65155 for human) and anti-CD68 (FITC-conjugated; Proteintech, cat. 65593 for human, 98029 for mouse). Corresponding isotype controls were included. Cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, washed twice with PBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer.

RNA pulldown assay

A biotin-labeled RNA pull-down kit (Beyotime, P0654S) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed in binding buffer with protease inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined by BCA. Lysates (500–1000 μg total protein) were incubated with 100 nM biotin-labeled miR-214-5p probe or miR-214-5p mutant probe at 4 °C for 2 h. Streptavidin magnetic beads (50 μL) were added to capture RNA–protein complexes. Beads were washed three times with binding buffer. Bound proteins were eluted with 2× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 5–10 min. Eluted samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blot. Probe sequences were: 5×-miR-214-5p probe (5′-AAA GGA UUC UGC UGU CGG UCC CAC UAA AGG AUU CUG CUG UCG GUC CCA CUA AAG GAU UCU GCU GUC GGU CCC ACU AAA GGA UUC UGC UGU CGG UCC CAC UAA AGG AUU CUG CUG UCG GUC CCA CU-3′) and mutant (5′-AAA GGA UCU CAU CGU CGG UCC CAC UAA AGG AUC UCA UCG UCG GUC CCA CUA AAG GAU CUC AUC GUC GGU CCC ACU AAA GGA UCU CAU CGU CGG UCC CAC UAA AGG AUC UCA UCG UCG GUC CCA CU-3′).

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Promoter fragments of NF-κB, STAT3, STAT6, PPARγ, and DNM3OS were each cloned into the pGL3 luciferase reporter vector. The wild-type or mutant 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of BHLHE41 was cloned into the pmirGLO vector. Cells were transfected with 2 μg of the firefly luciferase reporter plasmid and 100 ng of the Renilla luciferase control plasmid (pRL-TK) using Lipofectamine 2000 (GLPBIO, GK20005). For 3′UTR assays, cells were transfected with 2 μg of pmirGLO-BHLHE41-3′UTR plasmid. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for quantification. Primer sequences for cloning are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min and washed three times with PBS. Samples were blocked in blocking buffer for 2 h at room temperature. For tissue analysis, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm sections; sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and washed. Cells or tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, samples were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, then counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1002) for nuclei. Imaging was performed on a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope. Primary antibodies used included FAP (ABclonal, A23789, 1:400), CD206 (CST, 24595, 1:400), and YBX1 (Proteintech, CL488-84993-4, 1:500).

Lactate measurement

Extracellular lactate concentrations were measured using a colorimetric lactate assay kit (Sigma Aldrich, MAK065) per the manufacturer’s instructions. In a 96-well plate, 25 μL of sample was mixed with 25 μL of assay buffer (1:2 dilution) and 50 μL of reaction mix. After incubating at room temperature in the dark for 30 min, absorbance was read at 450 nm using a plate reader (Tecan Spark). Lactate concentrations were calculated from a standard curve.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP® Plus Sonication Chromatin IP Kit (CST, 56383). Approximately 2 × 107 cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, quenched, and lysed. Chromatin was sonicated to ~<1 kb fragments. Five micrograms of anti-H3K9La antibody (PTM Bio, PTM-1419RM) or control IgG were incubated with 500 μL of chromatin at 4 °C overnight. Immunocomplexes were captured with Protein G magnetic beads, washed, and eluted. Crosslinks were reversed, and DNA was purified. Enriched DNA fragments were quantified by qPCR. Data were normalized to input and IgG controls.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed using the Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, 17-700) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were lysed in RIP buffer with protease and RNase inhibitors. Lysates were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated to the antibody of interest at 4 °C overnight. Beads were washed with TBST, and bound RNA–protein complexes were digested with proteinase K to remove proteins. Co-immunoprecipitated RNA was isolated, reverse-transcribed to cDNA, and analyzed by RT-qPCR.

Co-immunoprecipitation (IP)

Cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (Yeasen, 20103ES60) containing protease inhibitors and PMSF. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Supernatants were incubated with antibody-bound Protein A/G magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times with IP wash buffer, and bound proteins were eluted with SDS-PAGE sample buffer. Eluates were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

Animal experiments

Male BALB/c-nu nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were purchased from Hunan Slack and housed under standard conditions with approval from the institutional animal care committee. Ten mice were randomly divided into control and experimental groups (n = 5 each). A549 cells and NFs were co-cultured for 24 h, then collected for injection. Each mouse was injected subcutaneously with a suspension containing 1 × 107 A549 cells mixed with an equal volume of NFs. Seven days after injection, the experimental group received daily intraperitoneal injections of 2 mg/kg FX-11, while the control group received saline. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured twice per week with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W²) /2. After 3 weeks, mice were euthanized, tumors were excised and weighed, and samples were collected for further analysis.

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, while comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For nonparametric data, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used as appropriate. Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All experiments were performed with biological replicates, with each experiment including three technical replicates unless otherwise indicated. Data reproducibility was confirmed in at least three independent assays.

For animal studies, sample size (n = 5 per group) was determined based on preliminary experiments performed in triplicate and no formal power analysis was performed. Mice were randomly assigned to control and treatment groups using a random number table. The investigator was blinded to group allocation during tumor volume measurement and outcome assessment. No samples or animals were excluded from the analysis.

Results

LUAD co-culture induces a CAF phenotype in fibroblasts with upregulation of miR-214-5p

To model the influence of lung adenocarcinoma cells on fibroblasts, we co-cultured two LUAD cell lines (A549 and H1975) with normal lung fibroblasts (NFs). After co-culture, the fibroblasts exhibited molecular marker alterations characteristic of CAFs [20]. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that co-cultured fibroblasts had significantly increased expression of fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and α-SMA, compared to control NFs (Fig. 1A). Western blotting confirmed these changes at the protein level, including decreased Cytokeratin expression and increased expression of the mesenchymal marker Vimentin. (Fig. 1B). These data indicate that factors from LUAD cells effectively “educate” normal fibroblasts into a CAF-like state.

B Western blot confirming increased α-SMA, FAP and Vimentin, and decreased Cytokeratin of NFs in co-cultured A549 or H1975 vs NFs. C Scratch wound healing assay showing enhanced migration of LUAD-induced CAFs. D Transwell invasion assay showing increased invasiveness of LUAD-induced CAFs. E Volcano plot and Heatmap of miRNA sequencing data, highlighting upregulated miRNAs in CAFs induced by A549 co-culture (relative to control). F qRT-PCR validation of top 10 upregulated miRNAs in A549-treated NFs; miR-214-5p is the most stably and strongly induced. G qRT-PCR showing miR-214-5p is highly enriched in exosomes isolated from CAFs compared to those from NFs.

Functionally, the activated fibroblasts showed enhanced motility and invasive behavior [21]. which are hallmarks of CAFs. In scratch wound healing assays, LUAD-educated CAFs migrated more rapidly to close the wound area than normal fibroblasts (Fig. 1C). Similarly, in Transwell invasion assays, co-cultured fibroblasts demonstrated significantly greater invasion through Matrigel, confirming their acquisition of pro-migratory and pro-invasive traits (Fig. 1D). Together, these results validate the successful generation of CAF-like cell via LUAD cell co-culture.

To explore changes in the noncoding RNA profile of LUAD-educated CAFs, we performed high-throughput microRNA sequencing on LUAD-activated CAFs versus control NFs. Differential expression analysis identified a set of miRNAs that were upregulated in LUAD-educated CAFs upon co-culture with A549 cells (p < 0.05, fold change > 1). A volcano plot illustrating the global miRNA expression profile is shown in Fig. 1E and Fig. S1A. A heatmap of the top 10 upregulated miRNAs is shown in Fig. 1E (left), and were further validated in NF/Ctrl and NF/A549 cells (Fig. 1F). Among these candidates, miR-214-5p was one of the most consistently and highly upregulated miRNAs. Notably, we found that miR-214-5p was not only enriched in CAF cells but also highly enriched in the exosomes released by CAFs (Fig. 1G). This suggests that miR-214-5p is packaged into CAF-derived exosomes at high levels. These findings raise the question of what functional role this CAF-induced miR-214-5p might play, especially in the context of intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment.

CAF-derived exosomal miR-214-5p drives macrophage M2 polarization

We next examined whether CAFs could influence macrophage polarization, given prior evidence that CAFs can modulate tumor immunity by affecting macrophages [1]. Conditioned media and exosomes from CAFs were hypothesized to carry factors (such as miR-214-5p) that might induce macrophages to adopt an M2 phenotype. To test this, we first isolated exosomes from NFs and CAFs. The exosome preparations were verified by nanoparticle size analysis (showing vesicles in the expected 50–150 nm range, Fig. 2A) and by Western blot for exosomal markers (enrichment of positive exosome markers Tsg101 and CD63, and absence of the negative marker Calnexin, Fig. 2B).

We then co-cultured LUAD-activated CAFs with M0 macrophages, using both a human monocyte-derived macrophage model (THP-1 cells differentiated into macrophages) and a mouse macrophage cell line (RAW264.7) (Fig. 2C). After co-culture with CAFs, the macrophages showed clear signs of M2 polarization. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that M2-associated macrophage markers CD206 (MRC1) and Arginase-1 (Arg1) were significantly upregulated in macrophages co-cultured with CAFs, compared to macrophages cultured alone, while the expression of the M1-related macrophage marker CD86 was downregulated (Fig. 2D). Consistently, Western blotting showed increased protein levels of CD206 and Arg-1 and decreased protein levels of CD86 in the macrophages after co-culture (Fig. 2E). These data indicate that CAFs can drive macrophages from an unpolarized (M0) state toward an M2, pro-tumoral state.

Given that miR-214-5p was highly enriched in CAFs and their exosomes, we hypothesized that miR-214-5p might be transferred to macrophages and contribute to this polarization. Indeed, when we measured miR-214-5p levels in macrophages after CAF co-culture, we found a significant increase in miR-214-5p expression in the macrophages (Fig. 2F), suggesting successful transfer of miR-214-5p from CAFs to macrophages, likely via exosomes.

To directly test the role of exosomes in this transfer, we treated macrophages with isolated exosomes from CAFs or NFs. Fluorescent labeling confirmed that macrophages efficiently take up CAF-derived exosomes (Fig. 2G). Macrophages incubated with CAF exosomes showed a marked elevation of miR-214-5p, whereas those treated with NF exosomes did not (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, if we pre-treated CAF conditioned medium with the exosome secretion inhibitor GW4869 (to block exosome release from CAFs), the resulting conditioned medium failed to raise miR-214-5p levels in macrophages (Fig. 2I). This supports that exosomal transfer is the main route by which CAFs deliver miR-214-5p to macrophages.

Finally, we asked if miR-214-5p is sufficient to promote M2 polarization in macrophages. We transfected THP-1-derived macrophages with miR-214-5p mimics to elevate its intracellular level as confirmed in Fig. 2J. Strikingly, mimic-transfected macrophages showed increased expression of M2 markers CD206 and Arg1 by qRT-PCR (Fig. 2K). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that the surface level of CD206 was higher in miR-214-5p-overexpressing macrophages than in control cells (Fig. 2L). These results indicate that miR-214-5p uptake by macrophages skews them toward the M2 phenotype. Taken together, the data from Fig. 2 establish that CAFs can promote macrophage M2 polarization via exosomal transfer of miR-214-5p.

miR-214-5p promotes macrophage M2 polarization by targeting BHLHE41

We next sought to identify the molecular target of miR-214-5p in macrophages that could explain the promotion of M2 polarization. Using three miRNA target prediction databases (miRmap, microT, and PicTar), we compiled a list of candidate mRNA targets of miR-214-5p. The intersection of the three prediction sets yielded 11 potential targets (Fig. 3A and Fig. S2A). To narrow down which of these targets are functionally relevant, we examined their expression changes under two conditions: (1) in macrophages that had undergone M2 polarization due to CAF co-culture, and (2) in macrophages transfected with miR-214-5p mimics.

qRT-PCR analysis of the 11 candidate genes in CAF-co-cultured (M2-polarized) macrophages revealed that several transcripts were downregulated upon M2 polarization (Fig. 3B, heatmap). Notably, BHLHE41 (Basic Helix-Loop-Helix Family Member E41, also known as DEC2 or SHARP1) showed one of the strongest decreases in expression in M2-polarized macrophages. Separately, when we overexpressed miR-214-5p in macrophages, we observed a similar pattern: among the 11 candidates, BHLHE41 mRNA was significantly downregulated by miR-214-5p overexpression (Fig. 3C). Other genes, such as DPYSL3 and KLF12 also decreased with miR-214-5p, but BHLHE41 was the common factor reduced both in CAF-induced M2 macrophages and in miR-214-5p mimic-transfected macrophages. Therefore, we focused on BHLHE41 as a likely direct target of miR-214-5p.

BHLHE41 is a transcription factor known to act as a transcriptional repressor. We hypothesized that miR-214-5p might directly bind to the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of BHLHE41 mRNA to inhibit its expression. Bioinformatic analysis indicated a putative miR-214-5p binding site in the 3’UTR of BHLHE41 (Fig. 3D). To validate this interaction, we performed an RNA pulldown assay. A biotinylated miR-214-5p mimic was used to pull down target mRNAs from macrophage lysates. qRT-PCR on the pulled-down RNA revealed a significant enrichment of BHLHE41-3'UTR in the product compared to a control (indicating that BHLHE41 mRNA was pulled down by the miR-214-5p probe, Fig. 3E). This suggests that miR-214-5p physically binds to BHLHE41 transcripts.

We further confirmed the direct targeting using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. We cloned the wild-type BHLHE41 3’UTR (containing the predicted miR-214-5p site) downstream of a luciferase reporter, as well as a mutant version of the 3’UTR with point mutations in the seed-binding sequence. Co-transfection of the wild-type reporter with miR-214-5p mimic resulted in a significant reduction of luciferase activity, indicating miR-214-5p can repress expression through that 3’UTR (Fig. 3F). In contrast, the mutant BHLHE41 3’UTR reporter was unaffected by miR-214-5p overexpression, confirming that the predicted site is indeed the functional miR-214-5p binding site mediating the repression.

Having established that miR-214-5p downregulates BHLHE41, we next investigated the role of BHLHE41 in macrophage polarization. We reasoned that if BHLHE41 is a direct target of miR-214-5p, then reducing BHLHE41 might be necessary for M2 polarization, since BHLHE41 might normally act to restrain the M2 program [22]. To test this, we overexpressed BHLHE41 in macrophages even in the presence of CAF signals. THP-1 macrophages were co-cultured with CAFs to induce M2 polarization as before, but one group was transduced with a BHLHE41 overexpression vector (Fig. 3G shows qRT-PCR confirmation of BHLHE41 overexpression; Fig. 3H shows the corresponding increase at protein level by Western blot). Strikingly, enforced expression of BHLHE41 antagonized the M2 polarization induced by CAFs. qRT-PCR analysis showed that in BHLHE41-overexpressing macrophages, M2 markers (CD206, Arg1) were significantly decreased relative to control (Fig. 3I). This indicates that BHLHE41 actively suppresses the M2 polarization program and/or promotes an M1-like state. To further determine whether the effects of miR-214 on macrophage polarization are mediated specifically through BHLHE41, we simultaneously overexpressed miR-214 and BHLHE41 in THP-1 cells, and the results showed that BHLHE41 reversed the effect of miR-214 on THP-1 polarization (Fig. 3J). Thus, the downregulation of BHLHE41 by miR-214-5p is a critical step for macrophages to acquire M2 characteristics in the presence of CAFs.

To further elucidate how BHLHE41 might exert these effects, we examined the activity of key transcription factors that drive M2 polarization. M2 macrophage polarization is known to involve transcription factors such as STAT3, STAT6, PPARγ, which induce the expression of M2-related genes; while NF-kB is M1-related pathway. We constructed luciferase reporter plasmids containing the promoter response elements of these transcription factors (so that luciferase activity would indicate the transcriptional activity of STAT3, STAT6, PPARγ, or NF-κB pathways). In CAF-induced M2 macrophages, overexpression of BHLHE41 led to a significant reduction in luciferase activity from reporters of STAT3, STAT6, PPARγ, but increased NF-κB reporter activity (Fig. 3K). This suggests that BHLHE41 broadly suppresses the transcriptional programs associated with M2 polarization, likely through its function as a transcriptional repressor. In summary, Fig. 3 demonstrates that miR-214-5p promotes macrophage M2 polarization by directly targeting and downregulating BHLHE41, thereby relieving BHLHE41’s suppression of pro-M2 transcriptional pathways.

LUAD cell-derived lactate upregulates miR-214-5p in CAFs via histone lactylation

Our results so far demonstrate that LUAD-educated CAFs highly express miR-214-5p, which they deploy in exosomes to modulate macrophages. We next investigated how miR-214-5p is upregulated in CAFs at the molecular level. Given the importance of lactate in tumor metabolism and fibroblast activation, we hypothesized that lactate produced by LUAD cells might be a key factor driving miR-214-5p expression in CAFs. Lactate is known to be abundant in the tumor microenvironment of lung cancers due to the Warburg effect, and it can affect adjacent stromal cells [23]. Indeed, CAFs often exhibit an altered metabolic phenotype in lactate-rich environments [24].

To test the role of LUAD-derived lactate, we treated normal fibroblasts (NFs) with conditioned medium from LUAD cells and measured intracellular lactate levels in the fibroblasts. After 24 h of exposure to A549 or H1975 conditioned media, the fibroblasts showed a significant increase in lactate content (Fig. 4A), confirming that cancer cell-derived lactate can be transferred to or taken up by fibroblasts. Concurrently, these fibroblasts displayed an upregulation of miR-214-5p (Fig. 4B), recapitulating the effect seen in the co-culture CAFs. This suggests that a soluble factor from LUAD cells—likely lactate—is sufficient to induce miR-214-5p in fibroblasts.

To directly implicate lactate, we created a lactate blockade model. We treated LUAD cells with FX-11, a small-molecule inhibitor of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), which inhibits the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, thereby reducing lactate production. LUAD cells (A549) were pre-treated with FX-11 and then used to condition fibroblasts. Treatment with FX-11 substantially reduced the lactate concentration both inside the LUAD cells and in their culture supernatant (Fig. 4C, D), confirming effective blockade of lactate production.

When fibroblasts were cultured in conditioned medium from FX-11-treated LUAD cells, the fibroblast lactate levels no longer increased (unlike the rise observed with normal LUAD medium; Fig. 4E). Importantly, under these lactate-deficient conditions, the induction of miR-214-5p in fibroblasts was abolished (Fig. 4F). This result demonstrates that LUAD cell-derived lactate is indeed necessary for triggering miR-214-5p upregulation in fibroblasts.

We next asked if lactate alone is sufficient to induce miR-214-5p. Supplementing the culture medium of NFs with exogenous lactate led to an increase in miR-214-5p expression in the fibroblasts (Fig. 4G). Moreover, lactate treatment also elevated the levels of the primary transcript (pri-miR-214) and precursor (pre-miR-214) of miR-214 within fibroblasts (Fig. 4H), indicating that lactate acts at the level of miR-214 gene transcription or pri-miRNA processing.

Lactate can signal through a cell-surface G-protein-coupled receptor, GPR81 (also known as HCAR1), which is expressed on certain cell types and mediates lactate’s extracellular signaling. To determine if GPR81 is involved in lactate-induced miR-214-5p expression, we knocked down GPR81 in fibroblasts (Fig. 4I shows knockdown efficiency) and then treated them with lactate. Surprisingly, GPR81 silencing did not alter the level of miR-214-5p by lactate (Fig. 4J). This suggests that lactate’s effect on miR-214-5p is not through the canonical lactate receptor pathway, but likely through an intracellular mechanism after lactate uptake.

miR-214-5p is encoded within an intron of the DNM3OS (dynamin-3 opposite strand) gene as part of a noncoding transcript that gives rise to miR-199a and miR-214. Specifically, miR-214-5p is processed from the 3’ end of a long pri-miRNA (DNM3OS) transcript (Fig. 4K illustrates the genomic context). We found that lactate treatment increased the mRNA level of DNM3OS itself in fibroblasts (Fig. 4L), indicating that lactate might be enhancing the transcription of the miR-214 host gene.

To check if lactate was directly affecting transcription through promoter activation, we cloned the promoter region of the DNM3OS/miR-214 gene (the pri-miR-214 promoter) into a luciferase reporter construct. Fibroblasts were treated with or without lactate and assayed for luciferase activity. The lactate treatment did not produce a significant change in promoter-driven luciferase activity (Fig. 4M), implying that lactate does not simply turn on the miR-214 gene via classical transcription factor activation. This result hinted that lactate might employ an epigenetic mechanism to boost miR-214 transcription.

Emerging evidence has highlighted histone lysine lactylation (Kla) as a novel epigenetic mark derived from lactate that can directly activate gene expression. In particular, lactylation of histone H3 at lysine 18 (H3K18la) and lysine 9 (H3K9la) has been reported to enhance the transcription of associated genes [25, 26]. We therefore examined whether lactate treatment of fibroblasts would increase global histone lactylation. Using a pan-anti-lactyl lysine antibody (Pan-Kla) in Western blot, we observed that total histone lactylation levels in fibroblasts were markedly elevated by lactate in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4N). This suggested that extracellular lactate can enter fibroblasts and be converted to lactyl-CoA donors that modify histones, consistent with previous reports [27].

We then probed specifically for H3K9la and H3K18la marks on histone H3 under lactate treatment. Western blots with antibodies specific to H3K9la and H3K18la revealed that both marks increased with lactate exposure, but the H3K9 lactylation appeared to increase more prominently (Fig. 4O). This suggested that H3K9la might be a dominant lactylation event in these fibroblasts in response to lactate.

Crucially, to link this epigenetic change to the miR-214 gene, we performed a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay for H3K9la at the DNM3OS/miR-214 promoter region. In fibroblasts treated with lactate, there was a strong enrichment of H3K9la at the pri-miR-214 promoter compared to untreated cells (Fig. 4P). This provides direct evidence that lactate induces lactylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 in the vicinity of the miR-214 gene, and this histone mark is likely responsible for the enhanced transcription of miR-214-5p in lactate-conditioned CAFs.

Lactate enhances miR-214-5p loading into exosomes via YBX1 lactylation

Having established that lactate can induce the production of miR-214-5p in CAFs, we wondered whether lactate also influences the export of miR-214-5p into exosomes. Exosomal loading of miRNAs is an active process often mediated by RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) [8]. Our earlier results showed that lactate-treated CAFs have more miR-214-5p inside the cells; here, we examine if a greater fraction of that miR-214-5p is preferentially sorted into exosomes under lactate influence.

We conducted an experiment using actinomycin D (ActD) to halt new RNA synthesis, to focus on the distribution of existing miR-214-5p between cells and exosomes. CAFs were pretreated with ActD to stop transcription, then exposed to lactate or control, and miR-214-5p levels were measured in both the cells and the secreted exosomes. We calculated the exosome-to-cell ratio of miR-214-5p as an index of sorting efficiency. Strikingly, lactate treatment led to a higher exosomal/cellular ratio of miR-214-5p (Fig. 5A), indicating that lactate promotes the packaging of miR-214-5p into exosomes above what would be expected from just the increase in cellular levels.

The selective enrichment of miRNAs into exosomes typically involves RBPs that recognize specific motifs on the RNAs [28]. To identify RBPs that might bind miR-214-5p, we utilized the RBPDB database, which predicted several candidate RBPs with putative miR-214-5p binding motifs (Fig. 5B). Among the candidates with high binding score (>0.6) were SRSF1 (also known as SFRS1) and YBX1, both of which have been previously implicated in miRNA sorting into exosomes [8, 9]. Therefore, we focused on these two RBPs as likely mediators of miR-214-5p exosomal loading.

We knocked down SRSF1 and YBX1 in CAFs using siRNAs and confirmed efficient knockdown by qRT-PCR (Fig. 5C) and Western blot (Fig. 5D). We then assessed miR-214-5p levels in exosomes versus cells from these knockdown CAFs. Notably, silencing YBX1 caused a substantial decrease in the relative amount of miR-214-5p in exosomes (exosome/cell ratio dropped), whereas YBX1 overexpression restored its exosomal loading. In contrast, silencing or overexpressing SRSF1 had little to no effect (Fig. 5E). This suggests that YBX1, but not SRSF1 is the major mediator of miR-214-5p exosomal loading. To determine whether YBX1 is functionally relevant to the downstream effects of CAFs on macrophages, we performed YBX1 loss and rescue experiments in the CAF–macrophage co-culture system. YBX1 silencing in CAFs increased the M1 marker CD86 and decreased the M2 markers Arg1 and CD206 in co-cultured macrophages, whereas YBX1 re-expression reversed these changes (Fig. 5F). This suggests that YBX1-dependent exosomal signaling in CAFs contributes to macrophage M2 polarization.

To focus on immediate, transcription-independent effects of lactate on YBX1, we again used ActD to block new mRNA synthesis in CAFs. Under these conditions, lactate treatment did not significantly change YBX1 mRNA levels (nor miR-214-5p levels beyond the already present transcripts) in the short term (Fig. S3A), indicating any rapid changes in YBX1 function are likely post-transcriptional.

We then examined whether lactate alters the binding of YBX1 to miR-214-5p. We performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) using a YBX1 antibody on lysates from control vs lactate-treated CAFs, followed by qRT-PCR for miR-214-5p associated with YBX1. In basal conditions, YBX1 already pulled down a measurable amount of miR-214-5p, consistent with YBX1’s ability to bind this miRNA. Upon lactate treatment, the amount of miR-214-5p co-immunoprecipitated with YBX1 significantly increased (Fig. 5G). This indicates that lactate enhances the interaction between YBX1 protein and miR-214-5p. For comparison, we also checked if YBX1 binds the precursor pre-miR-214; YBX1 showed much lower affinity for pre-miR-214 and lactate did not alter that (Fig. S3B), suggesting YBX1 specifically recognizes the mature miR-214-5p sequence or structure.

To pinpoint how YBX1 binds miR-214-5p, we looked at the miR-214-5p sequence for known YBX1 binding motifs. YBX1’s cold shock domain typically binds sequences with a UC-rich motif (such as “UCATC” or similar). We identified a candidate motif in miR-214-5p’s 5’ region: AAAGGAUUCUGCUGUCGGUCCCACU (the underlined part CUGCU is close to a UC-rich motif). We designed a biotinylated RNA probe corresponding to miR-214-5p with either the wild-type sequence or a mutated version (mutating the putative YBX1-binding nucleotides CUGCU to CAUCG). Using these probes in an RNA pulldown assay, we fished out proteins from CAF lysates and then checked for YBX1 in the pulled-down complexes. The wild-type miR-214-5p probe successfully pulled down YBX1 protein as detected by Western blot, whereas the mutant probe did not (Fig. 5H). Furthermore, we found that the expression level of YBX1 was higher in CAFs than in NFs (Fig. 5I). This result verifies that YBX1 directly binds to miR-214-5p via the specific sequence motif, and disruption of that motif abrogates binding.

To directly visualize a potential interaction between lactate and YBX1, we used a chemically labeled lactate analog. We treated CAFs with biotinylated lactate and then performed immunofluorescence: biotin-lactate was detected using a fluorescent streptavidin (Cy3, red), and YBX1 protein was stained with an anti-YBX1 antibody plus a green-fluorescent secondary. Under the microscope, we observed co-localization of the biotin-lactate signal with YBX1 in the cells (yellow signal in the overlay) (Fig. 5J). This suggests that lactate (or lactyl groups) might physically associate with YBX1 protein, supporting the hypothesis that YBX1 might be lactylated.

To test if YBX1 undergoes lactylation, we performed an immunoprecipitation (IP) of YBX1 from control and lactate-treated CAFs, and then probed the immunoprecipitated YBX1 for lysine lactylation. The potential lactylation sites on YBX1 were predicted using the online tool DeepKla, which identified K296 as a putative modification site (Fig. S3C). Using the Pan-Kla antibody on the YBX1 IP samples, we found that lactate treatment led to a dose-dependent increase in the lactylation signal on YBX1, which was significantly higher than in the control group. (Fig. 5K). These results suggest that YBX1 could be post-translationally modified by lactylation in the presence of high lactate.

We also note that YBX1 lactylation could potentially affect its protein stability. Indeed, our observation of increased YBX1 protein levels with lactate (Fig. 5L) could result from lactylation preventing YBX1 degradation. To verify the necessity of lactylation modification of YBX1, we mutated the lactylation sites of YBX1 and found that after lactate treatment, the mRNA content in exosomes showed a significant decrease compared to the group without mutations (Fig. 5M). YBX1’s role in exosomal miRNA sorting might be augmented both by having more YBX1 available and by altering YBX1’s affinity for target miRNAs. Our data indicate that lactate does both—it increases YBX1 levels and strengthens YBX1’s binding to miR-214-5p.

In summary, our data shown that lactate not only drives more miR-214-5p production in CAFs but also promotes its loading into exosomes. Lactate-induced YBX1 lactylation emerges as a key mechanism: by lactylating YBX1, lactate increases the recovery of miR-214-5p in YBX1- containing complexes and possibly its incorporation into exosomes, thereby boosting the export of this miRNA from CAFs to ultimately impact macrophages.

In vivo blockade of lactate attenuates CAF activation and macrophage M2 polarization

To validate our findings in a more physiological setting, we employed an in vivo tumor model and tested whether blocking lactate production could disrupt the CAF-miR-214-5p-TAM axis and impede tumor progression. We used a co-implantation model where LUAD cells (A549) and normal fibroblasts (NFs) were mixed and co-injected subcutaneously into immunodeficient Balb/c-nu (nude) mice (Fig. 6A). This was done to allow fibroblasts to become activated into CAFs in the presence of tumor cells within the tumor graft, better recapitulating a stromal-rich tumor microenvironment. Starting one week after injection (when tumors were established), one group of mice received intraperitoneal injections of the LDHA inhibitor FX-11 (2 mg/kg daily) to systemically reduce lactate production in the tumor microenvironment. Control mice received equivalent injections of vehicle (saline).

B Photographs of representative tumor xenografts from control vs FX-11-treated mice, showing smaller tumors with lactate inhibition. C Tumor growth curves over time for the two groups, illustrating slowed growth in FX-11-treated tumors. D Final tumor weights at sacrifice, significantly lower in FX-11 group than control. Throughout the treatment, tumor growth was monitored. Remarkably, tumors in FX-11-treated mice grew much more slowly than in control mice. Representative images of excised tumors at the end of the experiment show visibly smaller tumors in the FX-11 group (Fig. 6B). The growth curve (tumor volume over time) demonstrated a significant growth delay in the lactate-inhibited group (Fig. 6C). At sacrifice, the average tumor weight was significantly lower in FX-11 treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 6D). These data indicate that reducing lactate accumulation in the tumor can suppress tumor expansion, consistent with lactate being a pro-tumor factor.

We confirmed that FX-11 treatment indeed lowered tumor lactate levels in vivo. Lactate quantification in tumor tissue extracts showed that tumors from FX-11-treated mice had substantially reduced lactate concentration relative to controls (Fig. 6E), verifying the efficacy of LDH inhibition in our model.

Next, we evaluated the effect of lactate inhibition on CAF activation and TAM polarization in the tumor tissues. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on tumor sections for FAP (a CAF marker) and CD206 (an M2 macrophage marker). In control tumors, we observed abundant FAP-positive fibroblasts and CD206-positive macrophages infiltrating the tumor, indicative of active CAFs and M2-polarized TAMs. In contrast, FX-11-treated tumors showed a marked reduction in FAP staining (suggesting fewer or less activated CAFs) and a decrease in CD206+ macrophages (Fig. 6G). This histological evidence supports that lowering lactate in the tumor microenvironment leads to diminished CAF activation and less M2 macrophage polarization, aligning with our proposed mechanism.

We also extracted RNA from the tumor tissues to analyze the molecular changes. qRT-PCR on tumor homogenates revealed that miR-214-5p levels were significantly lower in the FX-11 group tumors than in controls, indicating that CAFs in those tumors had less induction of miR-214-5p when lactate was limited. Concurrently, M2 polarization genes (such as Arg1, CD206/MRC1) were downregulated in FX-11 tumors, while BHLHE41 expression was upregulated (Fig. 6H). Importantly, the gene expression level of the macrophage marker F4/80(EMR1) was similar between the DMSO group and the FX-11 group (p > 0.05), indicating that FX-11 treatment did not affect the total number of macrophages. Since F4/80 levels remained unchanged, the decrease in the expression of M2 markers (CD206 and Arg-1) reflects impaired M2 polarization rather than a reduction in macrophage numbers (Fig. 6F). The increase in BHLHE41 is consistent with our in vitro data showing that lactate-driven miR-214-5p normally suppresses BHLHE41; without high miR-214-5p, BHLHE41 remains elevated and can impose its restraining effect on M2 polarization. At the protein level, Western blot analysis of tumor lysates confirmed that BHLHE41 protein was higher in the FX-11-treated tumors compared to controls (Fig. 6I), corroborating the mRNA data.

Overall, the animal experiments provide in vivo confirmation that targeting tumor lactate disrupts the chain of events we uncovered: it prevents full CAF activation (less FAP), reduces miR-214-5p induction and exosomal output, keeps BHLHE41 levels high in macrophages, and thereby impairs the polarization of TAMs to the immunosuppressive M2 state (less CD206, Arg1, etc.). This leads to a less favorable microenvironment for tumor growth and indeed correlates with reduced tumor size.

Discussion

In this study, we uncover a metabolic-epigenetic signaling cascade in the lung tumor microenvironment whereby tumor-derived lactate activates CAFs and enables them to induce an immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype via exosomal miR-214-5p. Our findings tie together multiple emerging concepts: the role of lactate as a signaling and epigenetic modifier in cancer, the communication between stromal fibroblasts and immune cells via exosomal miRNAs, and the regulation of macrophage polarization by specific transcriptional repressors.

Lactate as a driver of CAF activation

We demonstrate that lactate from LUAD cells is a key factor that converts normal fibroblasts into CAFs, as evidenced by changes in fibroblast markers and behavior. This is in line with recent studies showing that lactate is not merely a metabolic waste but a signaling molecule that can reprogram stromal cells [11]. Our data extend those findings by showing lactate’s influence on the noncoding transcriptome of CAFs, notably upregulating miR-214-5p. The mechanistic link we identified is histone lactylation: lactate induces H3K9 lactylation at the DNM3OS/miR-214 gene promoter, thereby boosting transcription of miR-214’s precursor. Histone lactylation has been recently recognized as an epigenetic mark that directly stimulates gene expression in response to high lactate levels [29]. We observed both H3K18la and H3K9la increase with lactate, but H3K9la at the miR-214 promoter was particularly robust. H3K9 lactylation is relatively less reported than H3K18la; our findings suggest that H3K9la can also function as an activating mark at certain promoters.

It is interesting that blocking the lactate receptor GPR81 did not affect miR-214-5p induction, suggesting the lactate acted intracellularly. CAFs might take up lactate via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) and convert it to lactyl-CoA for histone modification, bypassing membrane receptor signaling. This could be a general mechanism by which lactate alters gene programs in stromal cells. Targeting the enzymes that write or erase lactylation marks (such as p300, which can catalyze lactylation similarly to acetylation [30, 31]. might be a strategy to prevent such gene activation.

miR-214-5p as a CAF-derived immunomodulatory miRNA

miR-214 has been studied in various cancers, often with context-dependent roles, but here we identify miR-214-5p as a factor used by CAFs to manipulate macrophages. We show that miR-214-5p-enriched exosomes from CAFs are taken up by macrophages and drive them towards an M2 phenotype. TAM polarization is a complex process regulated by cytokines (e.g., IL-4/IL-13 for M2) and intracellular signaling. Our results add miR-214-5p as a novel factor that can intrinsically tilt macrophage gene expression towards the M2 state.

The key target of miR-214-5p in macrophages was found to be BHLHE41, a transcriptional repressor. BHLHE41 (also known as DEC2) has not previously been well-characterized in the context of macrophage polarization. We found that forcing BHLHE41 expression skews macrophages away from M2 and more towards an M1-like state, likely through repressing transcription factors like STAT6/PPARγ that drive M2 gene expression. This aligns with our data that miR-214-5p’s suppression of BHLHE41 is required to allow full M2 activation. It will be interesting in future studies to see if BHLHE41 directly represses certain M2 genes or works via intermediate pathways. Notably, BHLHE41, along with its homolog BHLHE40, has been implicated in regulating inflammatory responses and maintaining tissue macrophage identity in other settings [32, 33]. So our findings in TAMs resonate with the idea that these factors act as brakes on alternative activation.

Exosomal miRNA sorting by YBX1 and lactylation

Our study also sheds light on how lactate can influence not just what signals CAFs produce, but how they send them. We identified YBX1 as the critical RBP for loading miR-214-5p into exosomes. YBX1’s role in exosomal sorting of miRNAs is supported by prior work (e.g., binding and sorting of miR-223) [8]. and here we show YBX1 binds directly to miR-214-5p via a specific motif. The novel twist is that lactate, through inducing YBX1 lactylation, enhanced this sorting process. Protein lactylation is a very recently discovered modification, and to our knowledge, this is one of the first demonstrations of lactylation affecting an RBP’s function in exosome biogenesis. Lactylation of YBX1 might cause a conformational change or affect its interactions, potentially increasing its association with miR-214-5p or its localization to exosome-loading sites (e.g., multivesicular bodies). Additionally, lactylation correlated with increased YBX1 stability, as we saw higher YBX1 levels with lactate. This could be due to reduced proteasomal degradation – perhaps lactylation blocks a degron or the interaction with ubiquitin ligases.

Moreover, earlier research noted that YBX1 expression itself can be upregulated by histone lactylation in cancer cells [34]. Thus, lactate has a dual effect on YBX1: epigenetically increasing its gene transcription and post-translationally modifying the protein. Both would result in more YBX1 activity, aligning with a scenario where under high lactate, CAFs become highly competent at secreting certain miRNAs. We also considered SRSF1, another RBP known for miRNA sorting [35]. but in our system YBX1 was the dominant player. It’s possible that different RBPs sort different subsets of miRNAs; miR-214-5p appears to be a YBX1-dependent cargo.

Implications for tumor immunity and therapy

The cross-talk between lactate metabolism, CAF activation, exosomal communication, and macrophage polarization highlights how intricately metabolic and immune pathways are intertwined in cancer. A high-lactate tumor microenvironment, common in aggressive and hypoxic tumors, could simultaneously drive fibroblasts to become protumoral and macrophages to become immunosuppressive, creating a vicious cycle that supports tumor progression and hinders immune clearance. Our in vivo experiment with LDH inhibition (FX-11) underscores the therapeutic potential of disrupting this cycle. Inhibiting lactate production in LUAD not only slowed tumor growth but also reprogrammed the stroma: CAF markers went down and macrophages shifted away from M2. This suggests that targeting tumor metabolism (e.g., glycolysis/LDH) might have immunotherapeutic benefits by modulating the microenvironment, on top of direct effects on cancer cells.

When interpreting these results, it should be recognized that our in vivo experimental approach has some limitations. First, FX-11 can inhibit lactate production in all cell types within the tumor microenvironment, including the cancer cells themselves. Given that LDHA is critical for the proliferation and survival of cancer cells [36]. This phenomenon is significant. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that the observed tumor growth inhibition after FX-11 treatment may, at least in part, be due to its direct anti-proliferative effect on LUAD cells, rather than solely through modulation of the CAF–macrophage axis. Second, it has been reported that lactate can directly affect macrophage polarization independently of CAF [37]. Therefore, the reduced M2 macrophage infiltration observed in tumors treated with FX-11 may also reflect the direct effect of lactate deprivation on macrophages, a mechanism that bypasses the CAF-mediated pathway proposed in this study. Despite these limitations, the complementary evidence provided by our in vitro co-culture and exosome transfer experiments strongly supports the causal role of the lactate-CAF-exosomal miR-214-5p axis in promoting M2 polarization of macrophages.

It is also worth noting that miR-214-5p could be explored as a biomarker or a therapeutic target. If CAF-derived exosomal miR-214-5p is found in the circulation of cancer patients, it might serve as an indicator of an immunosuppressive TME. Therapeutically, one could consider using miR-214-5p inhibitors (antagomiRs) to prevent TAM polarization. Alternatively, blocking exosome release or uptake is a strategy being considered to disrupt the “communication network” in tumors.

One limitation of our study is that we focused primarily on the LUAD fibroblast-macrophage interaction; other cell types in the microenvironment might also be influenced by this pathway. For example, miR-214-5p-containing exosomes might affect T cells or endothelial cells or even cancer cells themselves, as CAF-derived exosomes have been shown to be taken up by cancer cells in other tumor types [38]. Additionally, while we demonstrated the importance of BHLHE41 in macrophages, the full transcriptional network by which it modulates polarization remains to be fully mapped. It will be interesting to see if BHLHE41 directly represses certain M2 signature genes or if it works by feedback on key signaling nodes like HIF-1α or others.

In conclusion, our work delineates a comprehensive mechanism whereby lactate, as a metabolic byproduct of cancer cells, acts as a catalyst for immune evasion: it epigenetically reprograms CAFs to produce a pro-tumoral miRNA (miR-214-5p) and to efficiently deliver it to macrophages via exosomes, thereby driving those macrophages into an M2 immunosuppressive state by targeting BHLHE41. This lactate-CAF-macrophage axis underscores the multifaceted role of tumor metabolism in shaping the immune microenvironment and opens up new avenues for therapeutic intervention, such as metabolic modulation and interruption of exosomal signaling, in lung cancer and potentially other malignancies with lactate-rich TMEs.
