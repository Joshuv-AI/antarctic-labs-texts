Joint segmentation and classification of cervical cells using U-Net and CNN ensemble for early detection of cervical cancer
Authors: Akinbowale Nathaniel Babatunde, Oluwasegun Julius Aroba, Abidemi Emmanuel Adeniyi, Salman Olatunji Isiaka, Tosho A Abdulrahman, Akeem Femi Kadri, Shuaib Babatunde Mohammed, Michael Rudolph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer remains a leading cause of mortality among women, particularly in resource-limited settings where cytological screening is hindered by manual analysis challenges. This study presents a joint segmentation and classification framework integrating U-Net and a CNN ensemble to automate and enhance the accuracy of cervical cell analysis. The U-Net architecture is employed to delineate nuclei and cytoplasmic boundaries from Pap smear images, leveraging skip connections to preserve spatial resolution in complex cellular environments. Following segmentation, an ensemble classifier combining ResNet-50, VGG-16, and InceptionV3 is utilised to differentiate cervical cell types with high precision. The proposed method was evaluated on benchmark datasets (APACS-23, Cx22, SIPaKMeD) using stratified five-fold cross-validation and external validation cohorts. Experimental results achieved a mean Dice coefficient of 0.934 for segmentation and a classification accuracy of 94.6%, outperforming single-model baselines by 5–8% in F1-score and AUC metrics. Grad-CAM visualisations demonstrated that the model’s focus corresponded to diagnostically relevant regions, reinforcing clinical interpretability. Ablation studies confirmed that the joint learning architecture substantially enhanced feature localisation and classification robustness. This research contributes a deployable, interpretable AI-based pipeline suitable for real-time cervical cancer screening, offering potential deployment in both centralised laboratories and low-resource healthcare environments. Future directions include expanding dataset diversity, integrating transformer-based models, and developing lightweight implementations for edge devices.Keywords: Cervical Cancer Screening, Joint Segmentation and Classification, U-Net, CNN Ensemble, Medical Image Analysis, Explainable AI

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to regular cytological screening is limited. Conventional Pap smear screening relies heavily on manual microscopic examination by trained cytopathologists, a process that is time-consuming, labour-intensive, and prone to inter-observer variability and diagnostic inconsistency (Adler et. al., 2025; Pacal, 2025; Sang’udi et al., 2025). These challenges are exacerbated by the intrinsic complexity of cervical cytology images, which often exhibit staining variability, overlapping cells, irregular nuclear morphology, and poorly defined cytoplasmic boundaries (Garbach et al., 2025; Gruber et al., 2025). As screening volumes increase, these limitations pose a significant barrier to timely and accurate early detection (Li, Liu, & Xie, 2023).

Recent advances in deep learning have substantially transformed medical image analysis, enabling automated and robust feature learning directly from raw image data. In cervical cytology, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated strong performance in both segmentation and classification tasks, outperforming traditional image processing and classical machine learning approaches ([27]; Adler et al., 2024; Shaik et al., 2025). Encoder–decoder architectures such as U-Net have become the de facto standard for biomedical image segmentation due to their ability to preserve fine-grained spatial details through skip connections, even when training data are limited (Zhang et al., 2023). Accurate segmentation of nuclei and cytoplasm is particularly critical in cervical cancer screening, as diagnostic decisions rely heavily on morphological cues such as nuclear size, shape, chromatin texture, and nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio (Babatunde et al., 2025a; Babatunde et al., 2025b).

Parallel to segmentation advances, CNN-based classification models have shown high diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing cervical cell types. Architectures such as ResNet-50, VGG-16, and InceptionV3 have been widely adopted due to their hierarchical and multi-scale feature extraction capabilities (Kadri et al., 2025a; Kadri et al., 2025b) [25]. However, single-network classifiers often suffer from limited generalization, especially when applied across heterogeneous datasets. Ensemble learning strategies mitigate this limitation by combining complementary models, thereby reducing variance and improving robustness [22]. Ensemble CNNs have consistently reported improved F1-score and AUC metrics on benchmark cervical cytology datasets such as SIPaKMeD and Herlev (Heidari et al., 2024; Kamzayeva, 2025).

Despite these advances, many existing studies treat segmentation and classification as independent or loosely coupled stages, resulting in fragmented pipelines that limit interpretability and clinical trust. More recent joint-learning frameworks attempt to integrate segmentation and classification, but several gaps remain. These include limited evaluation across diverse datasets, insufficient coupling between spatial localisation and diagnostic inference, and a lack of explainability mechanisms that clearly link predictions to clinically meaningful cellular structures (Zhang et al., 2023; Shaik et al., 2025). Addressing these gaps is essential for transitioning deep learning systems from experimental settings to real-world cervical cancer screening workflows.

This study proposes an interpretable, end-to-end joint segmentation and classification framework for automated cervical cell analysis. The framework integrates a U-Net–based segmentation module with a soft-voting CNN ensemble comprising ResNet-50, VGG-16, and InceptionV3. Segmentation outputs are used not merely as a preprocessing step but as spatial priors that directly guide classification, ensuring that diagnostic decisions are grounded in anatomically relevant regions (Lin et al., 2018; Wubineh, Rusiecki, Halawa, 2024; Tomaziu-Todosia et al., 2025). The framework is evaluated across three complementary public datasets, APACS-23, Cx22, and SIPaKMeD, covering pixel-level segmentation, overlapping-cell scenarios, and multi-class diagnostic classification (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Furthermore, segmentation-guided Grad-CAM visualizations are incorporated to enhance interpretability, allowing clinicians to verify that model attention aligns with diagnostically relevant nuclei and cytoplasmic regions ([27]; Shaik et al., 2025).

The main contributions of this work are threefold. First, the study introduces a tightly coupled joint-learning architecture that explicitly aligns segmentation and ensemble classification through joint optimization, improving both localization accuracy and diagnostic robustness. Second, comprehensive multi-dataset validation is conducted to demonstrate generalizability across diverse cytological imaging conditions, addressing a key limitation of many prior studies that rely on single-dataset evaluation (Heidari et al., 2024). Third, the framework embeds clinically grounded explainability by linking segmentation outputs with Grad-CAM–based visual interpretation, thereby enhancing transparency and supporting clinician trust. Collectively, these contributions advance the state of the art by delivering a unified, interpretable, and generalizable deep learning pipeline suitable for deployment in real-world cervical cancer screening environments.

Literature review

Recent progress in automated cervical cancer screening has been driven by advances in machine learning and deep learning applied to cytological image analysis. Traditional Pap smear interpretation relies on expert visual assessment of cellular morphology, a process that is time-consuming and prone to inter- and intra-observer variability, particularly in resource-constrained settings (Chauhan, Singh, & Kumar, 2023; Chauhan, Singh, & Mehta, 2025). Early computational approaches sought to mitigate these challenges through handcrafted feature extraction combined with conventional machine learning classifiers. Such methods focused on morphological descriptors, texture statistics, and colour features, achieving moderate success but exhibiting limited robustness to staining variability, overlapping cells, and heterogeneous acquisition conditions [12, 24]. A pivotal transition toward data-driven representation learning occurred with the adoption of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which enabled end-to-end optimisation directly from raw cytology images. Comparative studies by Chauhan and Singh [5] provided early empirical evidence that deep learning architectures consistently outperform conventional machine learning pipelines in cervical cell classification, particularly in complex multi-class scenarios. Subsequent investigations reinforced these findings, demonstrating that CNNs are more effective at capturing subtle morphological variations associated with dysplastic progression than manually engineered features [25, 26]. However, classification-focused CNN models frequently lack explicit spatial grounding, limiting interpretability and clinical trust (Chauhan, Singh, & Verma, 2023).

Parallel to classification research, segmentation-based deep learning methods emerged to address the need for precise localisation of diagnostically relevant structures such as nuclei and cytoplasm. Encoder–decoder architectures, particularly U-Net and its variants, became the dominant paradigm for biomedical image segmentation due to their ability to preserve spatial detail through skip connections ([27]; Zhang et al., 2023). High segmentation accuracy has been reported across diverse cervical cytology datasets, including challenging overlapping-cell scenarios (Heidari et al., 2024; [23]). Nevertheless, most segmentation-oriented studies remain diagnostically incomplete, as localisation outputs are not explicitly integrated into downstream classification or clinical decision-making processes. To bridge the gap between localisation and diagnosis, hybrid learning strategies have been increasingly explored. These approaches combine handcrafted descriptors with deep features or integrate multiple deep representations to exploit complementary information. For example, Chauhan and Singh , [7] demonstrated that selective feature-based machine learning classifiers can improve stability under constrained data conditions, while Chauhan and Singh [6] analysed the influence of CNN channel variation on feature discrimination in cervical cytology images. More recent work introduced hybrid deep feature concatenation networks that fuse representations from multiple CNN backbones, yielding improved classification accuracy (Chauhan & Singh, 2023; [28]). Although effective, these hybrid models typically adopt modular pipelines in which segmentation, feature extraction, and classification are loosely coupled, increasing susceptibility to error propagation and limiting end-to-end optimisation.

Transfer learning and fine-tuning strategies have further advanced cervical cytology classification by adapting large-scale pretrained networks to domain-specific data [18, 25]. Chauhan and Singh (2023) reported that fine-tuned CNNs outperform training-from-scratch approaches, particularly when annotated cytology datasets are limited. Building on this, Chauhan and Singh (2025) proposed hybrid learning frameworks incorporating dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) and progressive resizing to improve computational efficiency and convergence stability. Despite these methodological refinements, most existing frameworks continue to treat segmentation as an optional preprocessing step rather than an integral component of diagnostic inference (Zhang, Zhu, & Luo, 2025) . Recent studies have begun exploring joint or multi-task learning paradigms that integrate segmentation and classification within a unified framework. Joint learning has been shown to enhance feature localisation and reduce overfitting by enforcing shared representations across tasks (Chen et al., 2024; Shaik et al., 2025). However, current joint-learning implementations exhibit several limitations. Many are evaluated on a single dataset, limiting evidence of generalisability [14, 18]. Others prioritise performance metrics without embedding explainability mechanisms that clearly associate predictions with clinically meaningful cellular regions [22, 29]. As a result, the translation of such systems into real-world screening workflows remains constrained.

Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has therefore become an essential research direction in cervical cancer imaging. Visual explanation techniques such as Grad-CAM enable post hoc inspection of CNN decision regions, supporting clinician validation of automated predictions [27, 34]. While promising, explainability is often applied only at the classification stage, without explicit linkage to segmentation outputs or morphological structures. This disconnect reduces the clinical interpretability of hybrid and joint-learning systems. In contrast to existing hybrid and joint-learning approaches, the present study introduces a tightly integrated segmentation–classification pipeline that unifies pixel-level localisation, ensemble-based diagnostic inference, and explainability within a single deployable architecture. By explicitly using segmentation outputs as spatial priors for ensemble CNN classification, the framework ensures that diagnostic decisions are grounded in anatomically relevant regions. The incorporation of Grad-CAM visualisation aligned with segmentation masks further strengthens interpretability by linking model attention to nuclei and cytoplasmic features that are central to cytopathological assessment. Through multi-dataset validation and joint optimisation, the proposed approach advances beyond prior hybrid and joint-learning methodologies, addressing both performance and clinical trust requirements in automated cervical cancer screening.

Materials and methods

A comprehensive methodological pipeline was developed to support accurate segmentation and classification of cervical cells for early detection of cervical cancer. The approach incorporates carefully curated data sources, standardised preprocessing routines, and a dual-stage deep learning architecture comprising a U-Net for cell segmentation and a CNN ensemble for diagnostic classification. Training protocols and evaluation metrics were systematically designed to ensure clinical relevance, robustness, and reproducibility across diverse cytological datasets.

Dataset description

Reliable training and evaluation of deep learning models for cervical cancer screening require not only diverse and well-annotated datasets but also carefully designed data-partitioning strategies that prevent information leakage and ensure unbiased performance assessment. In this study, three publicly available datasets APACS-23, Cx22, and SIPaKMeD were employed to support pixel-level segmentation, overlapping-cell analysis, and multi-class classification, respectively. Representative samples and annotations from APACS-23, Cx22, and SIPaKMeD datasets

The APACS-23 dataset, released in 2023, comprises 3,565 Pap smear images containing approximately 37,000 cervical cells annotated at the pixel level for nuclei and cytoplasm. This dataset was primarily utilised to train and evaluate the U-Net segmentation module. Images were randomly divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets. Where patient or slide identifiers were available, images originating from the same patient or slide were assigned exclusively to a single subset to enforce patient-level separation. This strategy ensured that correlated samples did not appear across different splits, thereby reducing the risk of data leakage during segmentation training and evaluation. The Cx22 dataset consists of 1,320 images with nearly 15,000 labelled cell instances and is specifically designed to capture challenging overlapping cell scenarios under realistic staining and acquisition conditions. Individual images may contain multiple partially overlapping cells, increasing segmentation complexity. For this dataset, a stratified split of 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing was applied at the image level, ensuring that all cell instances derived from a single image remained within the same subset. This approach prevented cross-contamination between training and evaluation data and enabled a realistic assessment of segmentation robustness in dense cellular environments.

The SIPaKMeD dataset contains 4,049 cropped cervical cell images distributed across five diagnostic categories: superficial–intermediate, parabasal, metaplastic, koilocytotic, and dyskeratotic. This dataset was used exclusively for training and evaluating the classification component of the framework. A stratified split of 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing was adopted to preserve class balance across all subsets. Since SIPaKMeD images are provided as independent, pre-cropped single-cell samples without patient-level metadata, separation was enforced at the image level, in accordance with standard benchmarking practice for this dataset. To prevent data leakage within the joint segmentation–classification pipeline, strict isolation was maintained between data partitions throughout all stages of processing. Segmentation masks, cropped cell patches, and any augmented variants derived from a given original image were restricted to the same subset (training, validation, or testing). Data augmentation was applied only after dataset partitioning and exclusively to the training data, ensuring that no correlated samples were introduced into the validation or test sets. Overall, the dataset handling strategy described above, together with the dataset characteristics summarised in Table 2 and the visual examples presented in Fig. 2, ensures a fair, reproducible, and clinically realistic evaluation of the proposed framework. This design supports both cross-validation and independent testing while closely reflecting real-world deployment scenarios in which models encounter previously unseen patient data.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing is essential for preparing cervical cell images for deep learning, particularly in the presence of staining inconsistencies, noise, and resolution disparities. The preprocessing pipeline was structured to normalize image characteristics, enhance diagnostic features, and introduce variability through augmentation techniques that improve model generalization. Images were resized to a uniform dimension of 256 × 256 pixels, enabling a consistent input shape across segmentation and classification networks. This standardization improves computational efficiency while maintaining critical morphological cues such as nuclear contours and cytoplasmic structure. Pixel intensity values were normalized to the range [0,1], facilitating stable gradient propagation during training and preventing feature dominance due to input scale variations. Contrast enhancement techniques, including histogram equalization and CLAHE, improved visual clarity of cellular boundaries, particularly in samples with weak staining. Gaussian smoothing with a 3 × 3 kernel was applied to reduce background noise while preserving edge integrity. Data augmentation involved random rotations, flips, elastic deformations, and contrast perturbations, all implemented probabilistically to diversify the dataset and mitigate class imbalance. Model architecture

The proposed framework integrates a U-Net architecture for segmentation and an ensemble of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for classification. This joint approach is designed to simultaneously delineate cervical cell boundaries and assign diagnostic labels with high accuracy. Each architectural component is optimised for a specific function within the pipeline, but interacts in a unified manner to enhance overall performance.

U-Net-based segmentation module

The segmentation component is responsible for accurately extracting cellular structures such as nuclei and cytoplasm from whole-slide cervical cell images. A U-Net architecture was adopted due to its encoder-decoder structure, which enables the capture of high-resolution spatial features while preserving contextual information. The encoder compresses the input image through a series of convolutional and max-pooling layers, extracting hierarchical features at varying depths. The decoder restores the image resolution through upsampling and transposed convolutions, incorporating skip connections from corresponding encoder layers to preserve localisation precision. Each convolution block includes two 3 × 3 convolution layers followed by batch normalisation and ReLU activation. Skip connections bridge the encoder and decoder layers, allowing for detailed spatial recovery. The final segmentation output is passed through a sigmoid activation function to produce binary masks for cell regions. CNN ensemble for cell classification

The classification module receives segmented cell regions and categorises them into diagnostic classes such as normal, low-grade lesion, or high-grade lesion. This module incorporates an ensemble strategy that combines three state-of-the-art CNN architectures: ResNet-50, VGG-16, and InceptionV3, each contributing distinct feature extraction capabilities. ResNet-50 introduces residual connections, which facilitate deeper learning by preventing vanishing gradients and improving convergence. VGG-16 relies on uniform 3 × 3 convolutions and is known for learning fine-grained textural features in biomedical images. InceptionV3 captures multi-scale spatial patterns using its modular structure composed of parallel convolutional paths with varying kernel sizes. Each model was initialised with pre-trained ImageNet weights and fine-tuned on the cervical cell dataset. Feature vectors from the penultimate layers of the three networks were concatenated and passed through a global average pooling layer followed by a fully connected classification head. The final decision was computed using soft-voting among the three models, enhancing robustness and minimising individual model bias. Integration and workflow

The proposed framework adopts a segmentation-to-classification workflow in which spatially precise segmentation outputs directly guide diagnostic inference. Integration between the U-Net segmentation module and the CNN ensemble ensures that classification decisions are grounded in anatomically meaningful cellular regions while reducing background interference. The U-Net generates pixel-level binary masks delineating nuclei and cytoplasm, which are used as spatial priors for patch extraction. Connected component analysis identifies individual nuclei, and centroid-based cropping extracts fixed-size patches centred on diagnostically relevant nuclear regions. Patch dimensions are empirically selected to capture the nucleus and immediate cytoplasmic context, preserving key morphological features. All patches are resized to 224 × 224 pixels to ensure compatibility with ResNet-50, VGG-16, and InceptionV3. Segmentation-guided patch extraction limits the influence of background noise, staining artefacts, and neighbouring cells. To mitigate error propagation from imperfect segmentation, the framework employs joint fine-tuning with a weighted multi-task loss, aligning segmentation and classification objectives. In addition, ensemble learning reduces sensitivity to localised segmentation errors through soft-voting aggregation of complementary feature representations, improving robustness compared with single-model classifiers. The workflow also supports interpretability and clinical validation. Segmentation masks provide explicit spatial evidence for predictions, while Grad-CAM visualisations highlight discriminative regions within segmentation-guided patches. This alignment between spatial localisation and diagnostic output enhances transparency and supports clinician trust. Overall, the integrated workflow forms a tightly coupled and error-resilient pipeline in which segmentation informs classification, joint optimisation promotes feature consistency, and ensemble learning mitigates residual uncertainty, enabling stable performance and clinical relevance in cervical cancer screening.

Training procedure

The training procedure for the proposed joint segmentation and classification framework was structured with the objective of ensuring robust learning, fair evaluation, and reproducibility. Training followed a two-stage process comprising independent task-specific optimization and subsequent joint fine-tuning, while evaluation relied on stratified cross-validation and independent external validation, as outlined in Sect. "Validation strategy".

Segmentation model training

The U-Net segmentation model was trained using images from the APACS-23 and Cx22 datasets with pixel-level annotations. Training followed a stratified five-fold cross-validation protocol, in which the data were divided into five mutually exclusive folds. Within each fold, approximately 80% of the data served as the training set and 20% as the validation set, with fold assignments rotated such that each sample functioned as validation data exactly once. Stratification preserved the distribution of cellular structures and overlapping instances across folds. Optimization of the segmentation task employed a composite loss function combining Binary Cross-Entropy and Dice loss. The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, a batch size of 16, and a maximum of 50 epochs per fold. Early stopping and learning-rate reduction on plateau were applied to mitigate overfitting. For each fold, performance evaluation relied on the Dice coefficient and Intersection over Union (IoU), and results were aggregated across folds to report mean ± standard deviation, thereby quantifying performance variability.

CNN ensemble training

The classification module was trained using segmented and cropped cervical cell images derived from the SIPaKMeD dataset. Class imbalance, which is characteristic of cervical cytology data due to the lower prevalence of abnormal cell types, was explicitly addressed during model training to prevent bias toward dominant classes. A stratified five-fold cross-validation strategy was adopted to preserve the original class distribution within each fold, ensuring proportional representation of all diagnostic categories during training and validation. This approach is particularly suitable for cytology datasets, where minority classes often carry greater clinical significance despite limited sample size. In addition, data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training subsets, with increased augmentation frequency for underrepresented classes. Augmentation operations included rotations, flips, elastic deformations, and contrast variations, enhancing morphological diversity while preserving diagnostic structure. Class imbalance was further mitigated through the use of class-weighted categorical cross-entropy loss. Class weights were computed inversely proportional to class frequencies, increasing the penalty for misclassification of rare cell types such as koilocytes and dyskeratotic cells. This weighting scheme encouraged the ensemble models to learn discriminative features across all classes rather than optimising primarily for majority-class accuracy. Each base model in the ensemble, ResNet-50, VGG-16, and InceptionV3, was initialised with ImageNet pre-trained weights and fine-tuned under identical imbalance-aware training settings. Optimisation employed stochastic gradient descent with momentum, and performance was monitored using macro-averaged validation metrics. Final classification results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across folds, as summarised in Table 5, providing a robust estimate of performance stability under imbalanced conditions.

Joint fine-tuning

Following task-specific optimisation, the segmentation and classification modules underwent joint end-to-end fine-tuning using the training splits defined within each cross-validation fold. A weighted multi-task loss function combined segmentation and classification objectives. Joint fine-tuning employed a reduced learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁵ and was conducted over 20 epochs. This stage facilitated alignment between spatial localisation and diagnostic inference while preserving consistent evaluation boundaries within each fold.

Computational cost

Computational efficiency and deployment feasibility were evaluated by analysing hardware requirements, model size, training duration, and inference latency of the proposed joint segmentation–classification framework. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU (10 GB VRAM), an Intel Core i9 CPU, and 32 GB RAM. Training and inference were implemented using PyTorch with CUDA acceleration. The U-Net segmentation model contains approximately 7.8 million trainable parameters, while the classification ensemble comprises ResNet-50 (25.6 million parameters), VGG-16 (138 million parameters), and InceptionV3 (23.8 million parameters). The total parameter count of the full framework is therefore approximately 195 million, with only the classification heads fine-tuned during joint training to reduce computational overhead. Training time for the segmentation module averaged 2.6 h per fold under the five-fold cross-validation protocol described in Sect. "Segmentation model training", while ensemble classification training required approximately 3.1 h per fold as outlined in Sect. "CNN ensemble training". Joint end-to-end fine-tuning, described in Sect. "Joint fine-tuning", required an additional 45 min per fold due to reduced learning rates and partial parameter updates. Inference efficiency was assessed using the final trained models on the test sets. Average segmentation inference time was 38 ms per image, while classification of a single segmentation-guided patch required approximately 24 ms. End-to-end inference, including segmentation, patch extraction, and ensemble classification, averaged 62 ms per image, enabling near real-time performance. These results indicate that the proposed framework is suitable for practical deployment in clinical screening workflows, particularly in batch-processing or assisted diagnostic settings. Overall, the reported computational cost demonstrates a balanced trade-off between diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. The modular design allows lighter-weight backbones or model pruning strategies to be integrated in future work without altering the core pipeline, further supporting scalability and real-world applicability.

Evaluation metrics

Evaluation of the proposed framework was designed to provide a balanced and clinically meaningful assessment under class-imbalanced conditions commonly observed in cervical cytology datasets. Performance analysis therefore combined overlap-based segmentation metrics with class-aware classification measures, ensuring that both majority and minority cell types were appropriately represented (Zhong, Liu, & Chen, 2024). Class imbalance was explicitly considered during evaluation by prioritising class-wise and macro-averaged metrics rather than relying solely on overall accuracy. Macro-averaging assigns equal importance to each diagnostic category, which is particularly appropriate for cytology screening where abnormal cells are less frequent but clinically critical. This evaluation strategy complements the imbalance-aware training procedures described in Sect. "CNN ensemble training" and enables fair comparison across all classes. Segmentation performance was assessed using Dice Similarity Coefficient, Intersection over Union, and pixel accuracy, as detailed in Sect. "Segmentation evaluation". Classification performance was evaluated using precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, and ROC–AUC metrics, as formally defined in Sect. "Classification evaluation". Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of localisation accuracy, diagnostic discrimination, and robustness across validation folds.

Validation strategy

A two-tier validation strategy was adopted to rigorously assess generalization performance, consisting of stratified five-fold cross-validation and independent external validation on unseen data sources.

Stratified five-fold cross-validation

Stratified five-fold cross-validation served as the primary internal validation strategy for both segmentation and classification tasks. Under this protocol, the dataset was divided into five non-overlapping folds, with stratification ensuring balanced representation of diagnostic categories and morphological patterns within each fold. For each iteration, four folds were allocated for training and one fold for validation. Performance metrics were calculated independently for each fold and aggregated to report mean ± standard deviation, as presented in the Results section (e.g., Table 6 for segmentation). Reporting fold-wise variability provides insight into model stability and reduces the likelihood of performance overestimation. Low standard deviations observed for key metrics, including Dice coefficient, IoU, accuracy, and F1-score, indicate consistent model behaviour across folds. Where relevant, confidence intervals may be derived from fold-level statistics to further quantify uncertainty.

Independent external validation

Independent external validation was conducted using test data that were not involved in training or cross-validation. This evaluation included held-out samples from APACS-23 and an external cohort from Cx22, both of which exhibit variations in staining protocols, image resolution, and cellular density. Evaluation on external data relied on final models obtained after cross-validation training. Segmentation performance assessment employed Dice coefficient and IoU, while classification performance evaluation included accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, confusion matrix analysis, and ROC–AUC. This validation protocol offers a realistic estimate of model performance under deployment conditions, where previously unseen data from heterogeneous acquisition settings are encountered. The combined use of stratified cross-validation and independent external validation provides a comprehensive and unbiased assessment of the robustness and generalisability of the proposed joint segmentation–classification framework.

Results and discussion

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness, stability, and robustness of the proposed joint segmentation–classification framework across multiple datasets and validation protocols. Evaluation using stratified five-fold cross-validation and independent external validation confirms consistent performance under realistic data variability. Segmentation results exhibit high and stable performance across folds, with mean Dice coefficient and Intersection over Union values showing low variance. This consistency indicates reliable generalisation to unseen cytology samples rather than performance driven by favourable data splits. Qualitative inspection further supports accurate boundary delineation in both isolated and overlapping cell scenarios, reinforcing the robustness of the segmentation module. Classification performance of the CNN ensemble follows a similar trend, with narrow fold-wise variability observed for accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, and AUC. Macro-averaging ensures equitable contribution from minority but clinically relevant classes. Ablation analysis confirms that segmentation guidance and ensemble aggregation jointly enhance diagnostic stability, rather than yielding isolated improvements in specific validation folds. From a clinical standpoint, the findings support the framework’s role as an assistive decision-support system. Accurate segmentation provides spatial context for classification and interpretability, while Grad-CAM visualisations offer transparent insight into model reasoning. Residual misclassifications between morphologically similar classes reflect known inter-observer variability in cytopathology, underscoring the continued importance of expert oversight. Overall, the results demonstrate a balanced integration of accuracy, stability, and explainability. Consistent performance across validation folds highlights robustness, while the clinical discussion emphasises safe deployment within AI-assisted cervical cancer screening workflows rather than autonomous diagnosis.

Segmentation and interpretability performance

Performance consistency of the U-Net segmentation model was thoroughly validated across five-fold cross-validation. Metrics such as Dice Coefficient, Intersection over Union (IoU), and Pixel Accuracy all maintained high values, confirming the model’s robustness in delineating cellular structures under varying cytological conditions. Mean Dice coefficient reached 0.934, IoU averaged 0.879, and pixel-level accuracy exceeded 96% in all folds. The segmentation outcomes demonstrated minimal variability, as shown in Table 6, which presents individual fold metrics alongside their mean and standard deviation.

Component contribution and diagnostic value

Model performance was further evaluated through ablation analysis. As seen in Table 8 above, removing the segmentation stage led to a drop in classification accuracy from 94.6% to 86.2%, while excluding ensemble aggregation reduced the F1-score from 0.938 to 0.882. These results validate the architectural synergy between segmentation and ensemble classification, reinforcing the hypothesis that segmentation enhances diagnostic feature localisation and ensemble learning promotes robustness. Visual and statistical interpretability features, such as Grad-CAM heatmaps, affirm that model predictions are explainable and clinically aligned. Nuclear contours, chromatin patterns, and cytoplasmic regions, hallmarks of cervical dysplasia, were consistently highlighted during classification, thereby increasing clinical trust. This unified segmentation-classification system not only outperforms existing baselines but also demonstrates strong alignment with cytological diagnostic principles. It offers a reproducible, interpretable, and high-performing pipeline that is well-suited for integration into real-world cervical cancer screening workflows.

Comparison with recent and related works

Recent advances in deep learning for cervical cytology have typically focused on either segmentation or classification, but rarely both in a unified diagnostic framework. Segmentation-based works, such as Rasheed et al., [27] and Mosquera-Zamudio et al., [23], achieved strong boundary delineation (Dice ≈ 0.93 and IoU > 0.88, respectively), yet stopped short of providing diagnostic classification. Optimisation strategies, including Khozaimi et al., [19], further enhanced segmentation accuracy (Dice = 0.962, IoU = 0.91) but still lacked end-to-end diagnostic integration.

Classification-only approaches demonstrate high predictive accuracy but remain limited in interpretability. Qian et al., [25] reported AUC > 0.96 using an ensemble CNN, while Jin et al., [14] leveraged attention mechanisms for accuracy exceeding 95%. Liu et al., [22] combined Swin Transformers with CNNs, gaining 6.7% over CNN baselines, though at the cost of computational efficiency. Karadag et al., [18] achieved 93.5% accuracy on SIPaKMeD, but class imbalance reduced robustness. These frameworks underscore the predictive potential of CNN-based classifiers but fail to provide spatial grounding for clinical decision-making. Efforts to enhance explainability are emerging. Zhang et al., [34] introduced SegX, a segmentation-augmented interpretability framework, which improves the transparency of clinical AI models but remains exploratory for cervical cytology.

As summarised in Table 9, these studies collectively highlight a fragmented landscape: segmentation pipelines excel in boundary detection but omit diagnosis, while classification pipelines achieve accuracy but lack interpretability. In contrast, the proposed U-Net and CNN ensemble framework integrates segmentation, classification, and explainability within a single pipeline. It achieves competitive segmentation (Dice = 0.934, IoU = 0.879) and classification (Accuracy = 94.6%, F1 = 0.938) while incorporating Grad-CAM for transparent decision support. This positions the framework as a clinically relevant, end-to-end solution that bridges the gap between algorithmic performance and practical deployment.

Conclusion and future work

This study presented an interpretable deep learning framework that jointly performs segmentation and classification of cervical cells for early cervical cancer screening. Integration of a U-Net–based segmentation module with an ensemble of convolutional neural networks enables tight coupling between spatial localisation and diagnostic inference within a unified end-to-end pipeline. Evaluation on multiple publicly available datasets demonstrated strong generalisation performance, achieving a mean Dice coefficient of 0.934 and a classification accuracy of 94.6%. Grad-CAM visualisations further confirmed that model predictions are grounded in clinically meaningful cellular structures, supporting transparency and interpretability.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations remain. First, model evaluation was conducted on publicly available datasets, which, although diverse, do not fully capture the heterogeneity encountered in real-world multi-institutional deployments. Variations in staining protocols, scanner characteristics, image resolution, and population-specific morphology may introduce domain shifts that affect performance. Addressing this limitation will require training and validation on larger, more heterogeneous datasets, alongside domain-adaptive preprocessing, multi-domain learning strategies, and domain-aware normalisation techniques. The joint segmentation–classification design partially mitigates dataset-specific bias by enforcing spatial grounding, but further robustness improvements are necessary for large-scale clinical adoption.

Second, segmentation accuracy in densely overlapping cell regions remains a challenging aspect, particularly when cell boundaries are ambiguous or partially occluded. Although the current framework demonstrates stable performance in such scenarios, complex overlaps may still lead to boundary uncertainty and error propagation. Future work will explore transformer-based encoders and attention mechanisms to enhance spatial discrimination and contextual reasoning. The modular structure of the framework also supports incremental and continual learning paradigms, where the segmentation module can function as a stable morphological feature extractor while the classification component is progressively updated using regularisation- or replay-based strategies to mitigate catastrophic forgetting.

Third, the present study focuses exclusively on cervical cytology, limiting direct conclusions regarding generalisation to other cancer domains. While the proposed architecture is disease-agnostic, extension to additional cancer types will require domain-specific annotations, revised class definitions, and task-aware loss weighting. Differences in tissue organisation and imaging modalities may further necessitate multi-scale representations or instance-aware segmentation, although the core principles of joint learning and explainability remain transferable.

Finally, the framework currently operates at the image and cell levels, without explicit modelling of patient-level disease progression. Translational deployment would benefit from integration of longitudinal cytology data and clinical metadata to support progression-aware risk modelling. Future work will investigate temporal aggregation of repeated predictions using probabilistic or survival-based models, enabling patient-level risk estimation within a decision support context. Explainability can be embedded by linking segmentation outputs and attention maps to longitudinal risk trajectories, ensuring that risk predictions remain transparent, interpretable, and clinically actionable.
