Two-step grain boundary diffusion mechanism of a dopant accompanied by structural transformation
Authors: Chuchu Yang, Bin Feng, Toshihiro Futazuka, Naoya Shibata, Yuichi Ikuhara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dopant diffusion along grain boundaries (GBs) plays a critical role in modulating the GB chemistry, which further governs the microstructures and properties of polycrystalline materials. Here, we investigate atomistic GB dopant diffusion behaviors by directly tracing GB structures and chemistries in a Ti-diffused Al2O3 GB, using atomic resolution electron microscopy, spectroscopy and theoretical calculations. Our observations unveil that dopant diffusion introduces a GB structural transformation. Furthermore, such structural transformation leads to an unexpected dramatic variation of GB diffusion coefficients for Ti diffusion, which differ by one order of magnitude between the two different GB structures. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the dopant diffusion and segregation phenomena in GBs, providing fundamental understanding towards the intricate nature of GB diffusion processes.

Introduction

Grain boundaries (GBs), the interfaces between adjacent grains, are characteristic structures of polycrystalline materials and play a crucial role in controlling their overall performance1–6. Due to the peculiar atomic arrangement, GBs usually exhibit distinctive phenomena that are not present in the grain interior regions2,7–10. Dopant diffusion along GBs is such a case. It has been generally accepted that dopants tend to diffuse faster along GBs and segregate locally at the GB core during the material fabrication processes11. Since GB segregation of dopants significantly affects the microstructures and the resultant macroscopic properties of polycrystals2,12–14, doping has been one of the most popular ways for material design and fabrication. To precisely and effectively control the GB diffusion and segregation, a fundamental understanding of the dopant GB diffusion process is indispensable.

To date, many efforts have been devoted to the investigations of dopant diffusion along GBs11,15–19. Most of the experimental studies focused on the quantification of macroscopic GB diffusivity, while the GB structure itself is usually simplified and is not taken into account in detail. Moreover, recent studies demonstrate that GB structural transformation could frequently occur in various situations, such as during GB migration20,21, at different temperature range17,22,23, and after component change, including GB segregation23–26. These results suggest that the dopant diffusion process might be coupled with GB structural change, which challenges the classical GB diffusion theory so far. However, whether and how GB structural changes influence diffusivity remain unclear, and an atomistic description of this process is essential for a comprehensive understanding of GB diffusion phenomena.

In this work, we present an atomistic experimental framework tailored explicitly for directly tracing the atomistic structures and chemical changes along a Ti-diffused Al2O3 GB, using atomic resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). This approach not only enables us to detect the Ti concentration at different positions for the quantification of dopant diffusion coefficients, but also allows a direct observation of corresponding atomistic segregation structures. We find that Ti first diffuses along the GB and tends to segregate at some specific atomic sites. When the Ti concentration reaches to a critical value, the GB structural transformation occurs, which allows further diffusion and segregation of dopant atoms. Furthermore, the GB diffusion coefficient unexpectedly increases by one order of magnitude after the structural transformation. These results suggest that dopant diffusion and segregation would induce GB structural transformation, which in turn facilitates the further diffusion of dopants in a much faster way.

At the positions after the transition point (Fig. 2d–g), all the GBs exhibit an asymmetric structural unit, composed of a six-membered ring and a four-membered ring of Al columns (Fig. 2i). Such asymmetric structural feature agrees well with the GB structure without any dopants25,27, but differs from those symmetric ones before the transition point (Fig. 2a–c). Furthermore, atomic-resolution STEM EDS maps in Fig. 2d–f show the presence of a small amount of Ti segregation at these GBs. Preferential GB segregation sites were observed in Fig. 2d, while such a tendency becomes less obvious in Fig. 2e and f due to the low Ti concentration. Such a low concentration of Ti could also explain the absence of Ti detection in the low-magnification STEM EDS shown in Fig. 1c. No obvious Ti signal was detected at the depth position of Fig. 2g, indicating that the actual Ti diffusion front is in the depth position between Fig. 2f, g. These results suggest that when Ti diffuses into the GB, it first diffuses along the initial asymmetric structure without any GB structural change. As diffusion proceeds and the Ti concentration increases, GB transforms from the asymmetric structure into the symmetric one. This change is likely attributed to the alteration of the GB stability after Ti segregation, which will be discussed later. After the structural transformation, Ti continues to diffuse along the GB in both structures simultaneously.

The mechanism of Ti segregation and GB structural transformation

We first evaluated the GB energy of the pristine GB by DFT calculations. In the pristine case, the GB energy of the asymmetric GB (2.138 Jm−2) is lower than that of the symmetric GB (2.533 Jm−2), which agrees well with the previous study28. Although the calculations were performed at 0 K, our experimental evidence suggests that the GB structures remain unchanged at the elevated temperature of 1300 °C (see Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that our calculated results can be representative of the actual GB states under experimental conditions. Ti segregation energies at different atomic sites were then calculated for both asymmetric and symmetric GBs, where the valence state of Ti was considered to be 3 + based on the core-loss electron energy-loss spectroscopy results (see Supplementary Fig. 3). The segregation energy for each column was averaged over the Al sites along [0001] axis (Fig. 3a and c). GB atomic sites with negative segregation energy indicate the segregation is preferable. In the asymmetric GB (Fig. 3a), segregation energies scatter at different atomic sites. Only some specific atomic sites show negative segregation energy, and these preferential segregation sites predicted by calculations agree very well with the experimental observations (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, in the symmetric GB (Fig. 3c), all the atomic sites at the GB show negative GB segregation energies, suggesting that Ti segregation is energetically favorable at all these atomic sites. The lowest segregation energy appears at site 1, which agrees well with the experimental observations shown in Fig. 3d, where Ti mainly segregated at the center of the seven-membered ring (site 1). Such a tendency becomes less obvious when Ti concentration becomes higher (Fig. 2a and b), as Ti can segregate at all the GB atomic sites. Overall, the segregation energies are generally lower in the symmetric GB than those in the asymmetric GB, indicating that segregation is more preferable in the symmetric GB.

It is worth mentioning that in macroscopic diffusion experiments, such as radiotracer measurements and secondary ion mass spectrometry, the diffusion profile also shows a two-slope features11. However, this occurs because the obtained macroscopic diffusion profiles usually include contributions from both the GB and the bulk material in the near-surface region. This near-surface region is often disregarded due to the complexity of building suitable mathematic models and other unpredictable factors such as surface conditions. The GB signals are only predominantly found far from the surface region, which are usually used for quantification of GB diffusivity (see Supplementary Fig. 4 for a detailed discussion). In contrast, our present approach provides localized measurements directly along the GB. This ensures that the data collected here is specific to the GB region without interference from bulk signals, allowing for accurate capture of intrinsic diffusion behavior and structural transformations at any location, including the near-surface region (see Supplementary Fig. 4 for a detailed discussion). Thus, the two slopes in our diffusion profiles represent distinct diffusion behaviors in different GB structures, reflecting the intrinsic nature of the GB. Therefore, the two-slopes feature has a completely different meaning between Supplementary Fig. 4b and d.

On the basis of all these results, we propose an atomistic model for Ti diffusion in Al2O3 GB, as shown in Fig. 6. For clarity, only cations are shown in the illustration, where the red and blue atoms represent Al and Ti, respectively. Initially, when low concentrations of Ti diffuse into the pristine GB, the GB structure remains unchanged, and Ti prefers to segregate at specific atomic sites (from Fig 6a to Fig 6b). As the amount of Ti increases to a certain critical concentration, the GB transformed into a symmetric structure (Fig. 6c), accompanied with the formation of a GB phase junction. This structural transformation enables Ti to diffuse further with a much larger diffusion coefficient (one order of magnitude higher in this study) and results in an increase in both Ti concentration and segregation width (Fig. 6d). With further diffusion of Ti, it begins to diffuse and segregate radially towards the bulk region adjacent to the GB core (Fig. 6e). To fully complete our proposed model, further analysis is necessary in future. For example, investigating the effects of temperature and annealing time, as well as conducting precise theoretical calculations at high temperatures to understand how structural transitions occur, how they influence Ti diffusion and segregation, and the detailed atomistic mechanism of GB diffusivity difference. Our present results renew the fundamental understanding of GB diffusion phenomena. First of all, we demonstrate that even for one certain GB, the dopant GB diffusion process is complex, involving GB structural transformation. Moreover, such diffusion-induced structural transformation would lead to a drastic variation of GB diffusion coefficients, which highlight the importance of GB atomic structures when describing the GB diffusion17,19. Our experimental design, coupled with the application of STEM, provides the capability to directly trace the atomistic structure of the diffusion front. This aspect has long been neglected and cannot be imaged by other conventional methods like secondary ion mass spectrometry due to their lack of spatial resolution. Furthermore, as recent studies show that GB segregation-induced GB structural transformation is universal in many material systems23,25,26,33–35, our present conclusions are expected to be generally applicable for a wide range of materials, though the quantitative effects may vary depending on material and GB structure. Lastly, our results provide important implications towards future materials design via doping. It is shown that the slow diffusion front, although much faster than that of the bulk, would be the bottleneck for the whole GB diffusion process. Therefore, it might be possible to efficiently accelerate GB diffusion, or vice versa, by GB structural engineering.

Since the bicrystal surface is flat and polished to a mirror finish, Ti diffusion is expected to be uniform along the [0001] direction. According to previous studies36–38, the solubility of Ti under the present experimental conditions is between 500 and 5164 ppm. The diffusion depth under these experimental conditions corresponds to the grain size range of conventional high-performance, fully dense polycrystalline ceramics, which ranges from several hundred nanometers to several micrometers. The TEM sample of the Al2O3 lamella with the Ti-diffused GB was prepared using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) system (Helios G4 UX, FEI).

TEM and STEM characterization

Bright-field TEM and SAED analyses were performed in a TEM (JEM-2100HC, JEOL Co. Ltd.) at 200 kV. The atomic-resolution HAADF STEM images and EDS mapping were characterized using a probe forming aberration-corrected STEM (JEM-ARM200CF, JEOL Co. Ltd.) operated at 200 kV. The collection semi-angle of 90 to 200 mrad was set to obtain HAADF STEM images. Structural observations were also performed at 1300 °C as follows. TEM sample prepared by FIB was assembled on the MEMS nano-chip (through-hole type, DENSsolutions). In-situ heating and observation were performed using an in-situ heating holder (Lighting, DENSsolutions). Atomic-resolution ADF STEM images were acquired at a temperature of 1300 °C with JEM-ARM300CF (JEOL Co. Ltd.) operated at 300 kV.

The EDS elemental maps were acquired using the EDS system equipped with dual silicon drift detectors with a solid angle of about 1.7 sr. The GB Ti concentration was obtained by averaging the Ti signals within the GB region, as described in Supplementary Note 3 and 6. Such an approach enables accurate determination of GB chemistry when using an atomic aberration-corrected electron probe39.

Theoretical calculations

DFT calculations were performed using the projector-augmented-wave (PAW) method40 implemented in the Vienna Ab-Initio Simulation Package (VASP)41. We used the generalized gradient approximation functional parameterized by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE) with an energy cutoff of 300 eV and the 2 × 1 × 1 k-point mesh generated by the Monkhorst-Pack scheme. In this study, we adopted the most stable and metastable Σ7 GB models reported by Heuer et al.28. consisting of 560 atoms. The GB supercells comprise two equivalent GBs. Structural optimizations were performed until the norms of all the forces are smaller than 0.01 eV Å−1.
