Effect of multisensory stimuli on neonatal pain during blood sample collection: a network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chunlan Tao, Li Zhang, Jinghua Tang, Chuan He, Qin Liu, Yongli Li, Yuqin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultisensory stimulation (MSS) effectively alleviates procedural pain in neonates during blood sample collection. However, quantitative evidence on the comparative effectiveness of different sensory stimulation types remains limited. This network meta-analysis (NMA) systematically evaluated the efficacy of MSS interventions in reducing neonatal pain during blood sampling. The results aim to provide an evidence-based foundation for selecting optimal clinical pain-management protocols. A NMA of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines. Studies using MSS interventions were included. A systematic search was performed in the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, CNKI, CBM, Wanfang, and VIP databases from inception to May 14, 2025. Pain scores served as the primary outcome measure and were analyzed as continuous variables. The effect size was calculated using standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (Crl). Bayesian NMA was performed using Stata 18.0 software. A total of 46 RCTs involving 5836 neonates were included. Among these, 11 studies focused on arteriovenous blood collection, examining 7 different MSS types, and 35 studies focused on heel blood collection, examining 8 MSS types. For arteriovenous blood sampling, three interventions showed significantly better pain relief compared to the routine method, with the tactile-taste-kinesthetic intervention demonstrating the greatest effect (SMD = −3.29; 95% credible interval [Crl], −5.47 to −1.12). For heel blood sampling, five interventions were more effective than the routine method, with the tactile-taste-auditory intervention exhibiting the best analgesic effect (SMD = −5.45; 95% credible interval [Crl], −7.44 to −3.47). MSS significantly reduces procedural pain during neonatal blood sampling. Future research should investigate the impact of intervention duration, frequency, timing, and choice of pain-assessment tools to further optimize pain-management strategies in neonates. Introduction

Blood sampling is a routine clinical procedure performed frequently in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Primary sampling methods include arteriovenous blood collection for assessing critical indicators such as blood gas analysis and hepatic and renal function [1, 2] and heel blood collection for genetic metabolic disease screening and blood glucose monitoring [3]. These procedures are unavoidable in neonatal diagnosis and treatment. Research indicates that blood sampling accounts for 52.3% of procedural pain in neonates [4] and may induce severe pain responses [5]. Since neonates cannot express pain verbally, inadequate assessment or delayed interventions commonly occur in clinical practice. Repeated painful stimuli may trigger stress-related physiological responses (e.g., increased heart rate and decreased oxygen saturation) and long-term neurobehavioral abnormalities (e.g., attention deficits and learning disabilities) [6].

Due to potential adverse effects of pharmacological analgesia, such as hepatic impairment and allergic reactions, non-pharmacological interventions have attracted increasing research attention. Several studies have explored the efficacy of non-pharmacological methods for managing pain during blood sampling. For example, Zhang et al. investigated the effects of hammock positioning on heel-stick pain in preterm infants [7]. Song et al. evaluated olfactory stimulation using breast milk to relieve heel-stick pain in full-term newborns [8]. However, the optimal sensory stimulation for analgesic effect remains unclear, and single-modality interventions demonstrate limited analgesic effectiveness.

Multisensory stimulation (MSS) therapy provides two or more simultaneous sensory inputs, primarily involving visual, auditory, olfactory, tactile, and taste modalities [9]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that MSS can effectively alleviate pain [6, 10, 11]. Some researchers have investigated MSS in neonatal blood sampling procedures, reporting significant pain reduction without adverse effects [12, 13]. Moreover, Liu et al. and Wei et al. have conducted meta-analyses on MSS [3, 14]. However, existing studies typically use traditional single-group comparisons and lack rigorous comparative analyses of various combinations of sensory stimuli.

Network meta-analysis (NMA), an advanced form of traditional meta-analysis, allows simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions through direct and indirect comparisons. It quantifies efficacy differences among interventions via probability rankings [15]. Therefore, this study applied NMA to systematically compare the effects of different MSS interventions on neonatal procedural pain during blood sampling, aiming to provide an evidence-based basis for selecting the optimal pain management strategy in clinical practice.

Methods

This study was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251066400) and followed the PRISMA reporting guidelines.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in The Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Biology Medicine disc (CBM), Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform (Wanfang), and Weipu Information Chinese Scientific Journal Database (VIP) databases up to May 14, 2025. The search strategy utilized both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. Search terms included the following: “Infant, Newborn,” “Newborns,” “Newborn Infant,” “Neonate,” “Multi-sensory interventions,” “Multisensory,” “Non-nutritive sucking,” “Kangaroo mother care,” “maternal voice,” “sucrose,” “Blood Collection,” “Blood Sampling,” “Heel Stick,” “Heel Lance,” “Arterial Puncture,” “Radial artery puncture,” “Pain,” “Ache,” “Discomfor,” and “Procedural Pain.” The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included according to the following criteria: (1) Population: Neonates requiring blood sampling. (2) Intervention: Multisensory stimulation involving two or more sensory modalities (e.g., non-nutritive sucking combined with oral sucrose). (3) Control: Routine intervention groups that received no intervention (e.g., routine nursing care, waitlist), and single-sensory intervention groups receiving only one sensory modality (e.g., non-nutritive sucking). (4) Outcomes: The primary outcome was pain scores measured by validated tools, including the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS), Premature Infant Pain Profile (PIPP), Premature Infant Pain Profile-Revised (PIPP-R), Douleur Aiguë du Nouveau-né (DAN), Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS), ALPS-neo Pain and Stress Assessment Scale (ALPS-neo), and Bernese Pain Scale for Neonates (BPSN). (5) Study design: RCTs. Interventions were classified into five categories according to sensory modality: tactile interventions: non-nutritive sucking, skin-to-skin contact, kangaroo care; auditory interventions: white noise, music, maternal voice; taste interventions: sucrose, glucose; olfactory interventions: breast milk scent stimulation; visual interventions: light modulation.

Strategies combining two or more sensory stimuli were uniformly classified as a distinct “Multimodal Intervention,” defined by their sensory modality combinations (e.g., non-nutritive sucking [tactile] with sucrose [taste] as a tactile-taste intervention). This categorization facilitated comparative analyses of analgesic effectiveness across sensory stimuli combinations in subsequent network meta-analyses.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies published in languages other than Chinese or English; (2) sensory stimulation combined with pharmacological interventions; (3) reviews, protocols, conference proceedings, or case reports; and (4) studies with incomplete or unusable data.

Data extraction

Two graduate students trained in systematic reviews independently conducted the screening process. Duplicate records were removed using NoteExpress reference management software. Initially, studies were screened by reviewing titles and abstracts. Studies irrelevant to the inclusion criteria were excluded. Next, full texts were reviewed, and study quality was assessed. Relevant data extracted included the first author, year of publication, country/region, blood collection type, sample size, gestational age, birth weight, pain assessment tool, pain scoring time, pain score, intervention, and control measures. Data extraction results were cross-checked by the two graduate students. Disagreements were resolved by a third investigator.

Risk of bias assessment

The revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool (RoB 2) was applied to assess bias at the study level [16]. The assessment included domains such as the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer to ensure rigorous and unbiased evaluation.

Data synthesis

NMA was performed within a Bayesian statistical framework using Stata 18.0 software. Pain scores were analyzed as continuous variables. Due to heterogeneity in pain assessment scales across studies, SMD with 95% credible intervals (CrI) were calculated as pooled effect sizes. The following analyses were performed: (1) A network plot was generated to illustrate direct and indirect comparisons among sensory stimulation interventions. (2) Global inconsistency was assessed using the node-splitting method. If significant differences (P < 0.05) were identified between direct and indirect evidence, sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore sources of inconsistency. (3) The surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated to rank the efficacy of various sensory interventions. (4) Egger’s test was used to detect publication bias, with P < 0.05 suggesting potential bias.

Results

Search results

The initial database search identified 3884 records. After duplicates were removed using NoteExpress software, 2799 articles remained. Screening titles and abstracts based on inclusion and exclusion criteria identified 160 potentially eligible articles. After full-text assessment, 54 studies were excluded due to incomplete or unusable data, 40 involved different pain-causing procedures, and 20 were non-RCTs. Ultimately, 46 studies were included [2, 17–61]. The screening process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of the literature search process

Characteristics of included studies

The 46 included studies were published between 2012 and 2025. Most originated from Asia (35/46, 76.09%), followed by Europe (9/46, 19.57%), South America (1/46, 2.17%), and Oceania (1/46, 2.17%). A total of 5836 neonates participated, with sample sizes ranging from 15 to 145 and gestational ages between 24 and 42 weeks. Blood samples were collected via arteriovenous (11/46, 23.91%) and heel (35/46, 76.09%) methods.

Seven types of multisensory stimuli were applied in arteriovenous blood sampling: tactile-taste (3/46, 6.52%), tactile-auditory (2/46, 4.35%), tactile-tactile (2/46, 4.35%), tactile-kinesthetic (1/46, 2.17%), auditory-visual (1/46, 2.17%), taste-kinesthetic (1/46, 2.17%), and tactile-taste-kinesthetic (1/46, 2.17%).

Eight types of multisensory stimuli were used for heel blood sampling: tactile-taste (13/46, 28.26%), tactile-auditory (8/46, 17.39%), tactile-tactile (6/46, 13.04%), auditory-taste (4/46, 8.70%), tactile-taste-auditory (3/46, 6.52%), tactile-kinesthetic (1/46, 2.17%), tactile-olfactory (1/46, 2.17%), and tactile-auditory-visual-vestibular (1/46, 2.17%).

Pain assessment tools included NIPS (24/46, 52.17%), PIPP (11/46, 23.91%), DAN (4/46, 8.70%), NFCS (4/46, 8.70%), ALPS-neo (1/46, 2.17%), BPSN (1/46, 2.17%), and PIPP-R (1/46, 2.17%). Most assessments were conducted at the time of blood sampling (27/46, 58.70%). Detailed characteristics are summarized in Supplementary File 2.

Risk of bias

In this analysis, 13 studies were rated as “Low risk” (13/46, 28.26%), and 41 studies adequately reported random sequence generation (41/46, 89.13%). None of the included studies was rated as high risk; studies were categorized only as low risk or having some concerns. Further details are presented in Supplementary File 3.

(2) Efficacy analysis

The SUCRA ranking showed efficacy as follows: tactile-taste-auditory (93.9%) > tactile-kinesthetic (89.7%) > tactile-taste (68.8%) > tactile-auditory (47.8%) > tactile-tactile (44.7%) > auditory-taste (36%) > tactile-auditory-visual-vestibular (30.5%) > tactile-olfactory (27.7%) > routine intervention (10.9%) (Fig. 3b).

The league table showed that tactile-taste-auditory (−5.45 [−7.44, –3.47]), tactile-kinesthetic (−5.27 [−9.31, –1.23]), tactile-taste (−2.42 [−3.31, –1.53]), tactile-auditory (−1.55 [−2.44, –0.66]), and tactile-tactile (−1.42 [−2.83, –0.01]) interventions provided better pain relief compared with the routine intervention. With the exception of tactile-kinesthetic, tactile-taste-auditory intervention was superior to all other modalities (Table 1 (2)). Egger’s test indicated publication bias (P < 0.01). Further details are provided in Supplementary File 6.2.

Consistency test

Inconsistency tests indicated significant inconsistency (P < 0.05) in studies involving venipuncture (P = 0.003) and heel-stick (P = 0.000) procedures. Node-splitting analysis revealed significant inconsistencies (P < 0.05) between the tactile-taste-auditory and tactile-taste interventions, as well as between routine and tactile-taste interventions during heel-stick procedures. Further details are provided in Supplementary File 5.2.

Discussion

This comprehensive NMA synthesized evidence from 46 RCTs, involving 5836 neonates, to evaluate the analgesic effectiveness of various MSS interventions during blood sampling. First, for heel-stick procedures, the tactile-taste-auditory intervention was the most effective, achieving the highest probability ranking across evaluated modalities. Second, the tactile-taste-kinesthetic intervention demonstrated superior efficacy in alleviating pain associated with arteriovenous blood sampling. Among the MSS combinations evaluated, this tri-modal approach consistently ranked first in effectiveness, surpassing routine care and other dual- or tri-sensory interventions. Notably, tactile-taste interventions showed consistent effectiveness in both procedures, whereas tactile-kinesthetic interventions performed effectively in heel-stick procedures but showed no significant difference from routine care in arteriovenous blood sampling. These findings emphasize the benefits of combining multiple sensory modalities to enhance neonatal pain relief. The superior performance of tri-sensory interventions suggests a synergistic effect that may engage endogenous pain-modulation pathways more effectively. Thus, multisensory interventions offer a non-pharmacologic, developmentally appropriate, and clinically applicable strategy for neonatal pain management.

Hospitalized neonates frequently undergo multiple painful procedures for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Studies in China indicate that neonates experience an average of over five painful stimuli per day during hospitalization [5], with heel-stick procedures accounting for 56% of painful events [62]. Repeated painful stimuli may induce adverse effects, including altered pain sensitivity and damage to the nervous, immune, and endocrine systems [63]. This study demonstrated that tactile-taste-auditory intervention provided the most effective pain relief for heel-stick procedures, followed by tactile-kinesthetic and tactile-taste interventions. This finding aligns with previous studies by Huang et al. [18] and Zhao et al. [29], which also reported superior effectiveness of tactile-taste-auditory intervention compared with routine care during heel-stick sampling. However, Lu et al. [64] reported tactile-auditory interventions as most effective in preterm infants. Differences in these results may stem from variations in intervention definitions and group classifications. Specifically, our study explicitly defined multisensory interventions as involving two or more sensory modalities, whereas Lu et al. explored alternative therapeutic approaches (e.g., positional interventions). Additionally, our study categorized interventions according to sensory modality, while Lu et al. classified interventions based on therapeutic approach (e.g., kangaroo care and music therapy). The superior effectiveness of tactile-taste-auditory intervention may result from activation of unimodal sensory and associated brain areas through sensory stimulation. This intervention modulates multiple biological pathways, enhances cortical excitability, and utilizes intact sensory channels to compensate for deficits such as vision and touch [65]. Furthermore, the three-sensory saturation method (3TSS), comprising touch, taste, and auditory modalities, may block pain-signal transmission pathways to the cerebral cortex, thereby reducing pain perception [66, 67].

Although heel-stick sampling is common, arteriovenous blood sampling is equally essential in clinical diagnosis and treatment, inevitably causing neonatal discomfort and pain. Arteriovenous blood collection is commonly used to evaluate neonatal respiratory, physiological, and metabolic functions [68]. Among the 11 RCTs examining arteriovenous blood sampling, the tactile-taste-kinesthetic intervention demonstrated the highest efficacy. However, this finding primarily relies on a single RCT conducted by Li et al. [53], and similar results are not yet reported in systematic reviews or meta-analyses. Due to the limited evidence, this finding should be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, indicating directions for future research rather than firm clinical recommendations. Tactile interventions, such as non-nutritive sucking, stimulate oral tactile receptors, raise pain thresholds, and promote analgesic effects through serotonin (5-HT) release [69]. Kangaroo care activates neonatal touch, vestibular, and motor sensations through skin-to-skin contact and thermoregulation, engaging the vagus nerve pathway to inhibit pain-related neurotransmitters [70]. Oral sweeteners administered via syringes, droppers, or pacifiers stimulate taste receptors on the tongue, triggering endogenous opioid release and alleviating pain [71]. Kinesthetic interventions, such as swaddling or flexed positioning, mimic intrauterine environments, thereby enhancing neonatal comfort and coping capacity during painful procedures [53]. However, the World Health Organization’s Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative advises against pacifier use due to potential risks, including nipple confusion and infections resulting from inadequate sterilization [72]. Furthermore, repeated administration of sweeteners may negatively affect neonatal blood glucose regulation, gastrointestinal function, and neurodevelopment, posing particular risks for preterm infants due to brain vulnerability [73]. Consequently, kinesthetic interventions must consider infants’ developmental maturity, pain tolerance thresholds, and clinical conditions [1]. Clinical implementation thus requires individualized protocols balancing risks and benefits, preferably administered by experienced nursing staff. At the same time, notably, Mei-di Shen [74] reported optimal efficacy of the tactile-kinesthetic intervention. However, our analysis indicated that the tactile-kinesthetic intervention was not statistically superior to standard care for arteriovenous blood collection but showed better effectiveness in heel sampling. Potential reasons for this discrepancy include: (1) Differences in pain stimuli: Mei-di Shen’s study involved various painful procedures, including venipuncture, retinopathy of prematurity screening, and heel lance, whereas our study focused exclusively on blood sample collection. (2) Differences in study populations: Mei-di Shen studied preterm infants, whereas our study included neonates of varying gestational ages. (3) Methodological variations: Mei-di Shen’s meta-analysis included 18 studies with randomized and non-randomized designs, whereas our analysis included 46 RCTs exclusively. Tactile-kinesthetic interventions, such as massage combined with kinesthetic techniques, provide calming effects, stress relief, and improved pain management [75].

This study’s strength lies in its detailed investigation of commonly used neonatal procedures (heel-stick and arteriovenous blood collection) and its rigorous NMA evaluating various sensory-stimulation combinations. The quality of evidence is high, derived exclusively from RCTs and adhering to evidence-based guidelines, including the PRISMA statement. A comprehensive literature search and quality assessment were conducted, enhancing standardization and providing strong support for developing evidence-based neonatal multisensory pain management protocols. However, the study has several limitations: (1) Limited evidence for specific interventions may introduce bias. For example, the inclusion of only one trial assessing tactile-taste-kinesthetic intervention for arteriovenous blood sampling could overestimate its efficacy. (2) Substantial heterogeneity may result from variability in intervention duration, frequency, and differences in pain assessment tools and timing across included studies. (3) Significant inconsistency was identified by the node-splitting method (P < 0.05), particularly for heel-stick procedures. Specifically, inconsistencies occurred between tactile-taste-auditory and tactile-taste interventions and between routine care and tactile-taste interventions. Such inconsistencies may result from heterogeneous intervention protocols, pain-assessment methods, or assessment timing. While this does not negate the overall effectiveness of multisensory interventions, it suggests interpreting the relative rankings of certain interventions cautiously. Therefore, further research exploring factors such as gestational age, illness severity, or intervention specifics remains necessary. This study establishes foundational evidence for neonatal multisensory pain management and highlights areas warranting future investigation.

The results of this NMA have direct clinical implications. Firstly, they enable individualized care through a hierarchy of effective MSS interventions, allowing clinicians to tailor pain management strategies based on procedural type. Secondly, successful implementation requires careful consideration of clinical feasibility and safety. While multimodal interventions show superior efficacy, their complexity may present challenges in busy clinical settings. Moreover, clinicians should remain vigilant regarding potential risks, such as nipple confusion from pacifiers or hyperglycemia associated with repeated sweetener administration. Thus, standardized protocols and targeted staff education are essential to safely integrate these evidence-based MSS strategies into routine neonatal care.

Conclusion

This NMA compared the efficacy of various MSS combinations for pain relief during neonatal blood collection. Results indicated MSS significantly alleviated procedural pain. Specifically, tactile-taste-kinesthetic interventions provided optimal efficacy for arteriovenous blood sampling, whereas tactile-taste-auditory approaches showed superior outcomes for heel-stick procedures.
