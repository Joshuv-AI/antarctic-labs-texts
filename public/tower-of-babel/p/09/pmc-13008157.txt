Beyond the Usual Suspects: Gastrointestinal Histoplasmosis as a Marker of Undiagnosed Common Variable Immunodeficiency
Authors: Jorge Pacheco Oliva, Ninoska Meléndez Rodríguez, Abel Sánchez, José Luis López Chacón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 28-year-old woman presented with chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and recurrent gastrointestinal infections. Intestinal biopsies unexpectedly revealed Histoplasma capsulatum, prompting immunologic evaluation that demonstrated severe hypogammaglobulinemia and reduced B-cell counts, consistent with common variable immunodeficiency. She improved with intravenous immunoglobulin and antifungal therapy. This case highlights gastrointestinal histoplasmosis as a potential early marker of undiagnosed common variable immunodeficiency and underscores the importance of considering primary immunodeficiency in adults with persistent gastrointestinal symptoms.

INTRODUCTION

Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is the most prevalent symptomatic primary immunodeficiency in adults and is characterized by hypogammaglobulinemia, impaired antibody production, and recurrent infections. Its clinical spectrum is heterogeneous, frequently involving sinopulmonary infections, autoimmune disorders, and gastrointestinal manifestations. Up to 20%–60% of patients develop chronic diarrhea, malabsorption, or inflammatory bowel disease–like enteropathy, making the gastrointestinal tract a key site of morbidity in CVID.1

Histoplasma capsulatum is a dimorphic fungal pathogen classically endemic to the Ohio, Mississippi, and Missouri River valleys, although cases are increasingly recognized outside these regions due to travel, migration, and environmental exposure.2 Histoplasmosis is generally rare in nonendemic regions, and most infections are asymptomatic or self-limited; however, a minority of patients develop acute pulmonary, chronic cavitary, or disseminated disease, particularly in the setting of impaired cellular immunity.3 Infection typically begins via inhalation of microconidia, followed by transformation into the yeast form at body temperature. Yeasts are phagocytosed by macrophages and disseminate through the lymphatic and reticuloendothelial systems, where they may remain dormant for years.3 Symptomatic disease most commonly occurs in immunocompromised hosts, including individuals with HIV, solid-organ transplants, or chronic immunosuppressive therapy.4

Gastrointestinal involvement occurs in up to 70%–90% of disseminated histoplasmosis cases, although isolated gastrointestinal disease is uncommon and often mimics other conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal tuberculosis, or malignancy.5–10 Reported manifestations include ulceration, mucosal nodules, strictures, bleeding, and mass-like lesions, frequently leading to diagnostic delays. Histologic evaluation with Periodic Acid–Schiff and Gomori Methenamine Silver staining remains essential for identifying intracellular yeast forms and distinguishing histoplasmosis from other granulomatous processes.11,12

Opportunistic infections such as gastrointestinal histoplasmosis are rarely the initial clue to an underlying primary immunodeficiency. In adults without known immunosuppression, the identification of H. capsulatum in intestinal biopsies should prompt evaluation for occult immune disorders, including CVID. This case highlights gastrointestinal histoplasmosis as a potential early marker of undiagnosed CVID and underscores the importance of considering primary immunodeficiency in adults with persistent gastrointestinal symptoms and unusual infectious findings.

CASE REPORT

A 28-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented with a 6-month history of chronic diarrhea, abdominal pain, and progressive weight loss. She appeared severely malnourished, with a body mass index of 16.6 kg/m2, a total weight loss of approximately 60 pounds, and hypoalbuminemia of 2.3 mg/dL, consistent with severe protein-calorie malnutrition. She also described early satiety, fatigue, and recurrent episodes of presumed gastrointestinal infections treated empirically with antibiotics, with only transient improvement.

On examination, she appeared cachectic with temporal wasting and reduced muscle mass. Vital signs were stable. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated normocytic anemia, hypoalbuminemia (2.3 mg/dL), and markedly elevated inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, consistent with significant systemic inflammation. Electrolytes were normal. Stool cultures were intermittently positive for Escherichia coli and Candida species.

Upper endoscopy showed mild duodenal erythema without ulceration. Colonoscopy revealed patchy mucosal erythema and friability in the ascending colon, without ulceration or mass-like lesions. By contrast, the terminal ileum demonstrated multiple polypoid elevations, several of which appeared ulcerated with overlying fibrin (Figures 1 and 2). Capsule endoscopy additionally showed numerous polypoid lesions scattered throughout the small intestine (Figure 1). Targeted biopsies were obtained directly from the ulcerated polypoid lesions in the terminal ileum, rather than from the surrounding erythematous mucosa.

Retrograde enteroscopy. (A–C) Ileal mucosa with multiple polypoid lesions and small ulcers with fibrin.

Histologic examination showed moderate to marked active inflammation, with a macrophage-rich infiltrate and intracellular yeast-like organisms measuring 2–5 µm, surrounded by a clear halo. Periodic acid–Schiff and Grocott methenamine silver stains highlighted numerous intracellular fungal forms consistent with Histoplasma capsulatum (Figure 3). No necrotizing granulomas were identified.

Histopathologic features of ileal biopsy. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin stain (10×) showing mixed inflammatory infiltrate with increased macrophages in the lamina propria. (B) PAS stain (40×) demonstrating intracellular yeast-like organisms measuring 2–5 µm with narrow-based budding, consistent with Histoplasma capsulatum. (C) Grocott–Gomori methenamine silver stain (40×) highlighting fungal cell walls in dark brown to black.

Given the unexpected diagnosis of gastrointestinal histoplasmosis in a patient without known immunosuppression, an immunologic evaluation was pursued. Serum immunoglobulin levels revealed marked hypogammaglobulinemia: IgG 230 mg/dL (reference 700–1,600 mg/dL), IgA <7 mg/dL, and IgM <5 mg/dL. Flow cytometry demonstrated reduced CD19+ B-cell counts with preserved T-cell subsets, consistent with CVID. HIV testing was negative.

The patient was started on oral itraconazole and monthly intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) replacement therapy. Over the following months, she experienced progressive clinical improvement, including resolution of diarrhea, weight gain, and normalization of albumin levels. Inflammatory markers decreased significantly, and her functional status improved. She continues on IVIG therapy with close gastroenterology and immunology follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Gastrointestinal involvement in disseminated histoplasmosis is well documented, occurring in up to 70%–90% of cases, although isolated gastrointestinal disease remains uncommon and frequently mimics other inflammatory or infectious conditions.5–10 The ileum is the most commonly affected site, often presenting with ulceration, mucosal nodularity, or mass-like lesions that may resemble Crohn disease, intestinal tuberculosis, or malignancy. In this patient, the presence of multiple polypoid and ulcerated lesions in the terminal ileum, contrasted with a completely normal colonic mucosa, contributed to the initial diagnostic uncertainty.

Histologic evaluation remains the cornerstone for diagnosing gastrointestinal histoplasmosis. The identification of intracellular yeast forms measuring 2–5 µm, highlighted by PAS and GMS stains, is characteristic of Histoplasma capsulatum and helps distinguish it from other granulomatous processes.11,12 In this case, the absence of necrotizing granulomas and the predominance of macrophage-rich infiltrates further supported the diagnosis. Importantly, gastrointestinal histoplasmosis may occur without pulmonary involvement, as seen in several published reports, underscoring the need for a high index of suspicion when encountering atypical ileal lesions.5,8

A key feature of this case is that gastrointestinal histoplasmosis served as the initial clue to an underlying primary immunodeficiency. While disseminated histoplasmosis is classically associated with HIV, solid-organ transplantation, or chronic immunosuppression, it is rarely reported as the presenting manifestation of CVID; however, this case illustrates that CVID may first manifest through opportunistic gastrointestinal infections.4 CVID is characterized by hypogammaglobulinemia, impaired antibody production, and recurrent infections, with gastrointestinal involvement occurring in up to 60% of patients.1 Opportunistic fungal infections are unusual in CVID, making this presentation particularly noteworthy.

The patient's profound hypogammaglobulinemia, reduced B-cell counts, severe malnutrition, and recurrent gastrointestinal infections were consistent with advanced CVID. The identification of H. capsulatum in ileal biopsies prompted the immunologic evaluation that ultimately established the diagnosis. This highlights the importance of considering primary immunodeficiency in adults with persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, unexplained weight loss, or opportunistic infections, even in the absence of traditional risk factors.

Management of gastrointestinal histoplasmosis typically involves antifungal therapy with itraconazole, with amphotericin B reserved for severe disease. In patients with CVID, concurrent initiation of IVIG is essential to restore humoral immunity and prevent recurrent infections. In this case, the combination of antifungal therapy and IVIG resulted in marked clinical improvement, including resolution of diarrhea, weight gain, and normalization of albumin levels.

This case reinforces several important clinical lessons: gastrointestinal histoplasmosis can present without pulmonary disease; ileal lesions may mimic inflammatory bowel disease; and opportunistic infections in adults without known immunosuppression should prompt evaluation for underlying primary immunodeficiency. Early recognition and treatment are critical to prevent complications and improve long-term outcomes.

Gastrointestinal histoplasmosis is an uncommon presentation of Histoplasma capsulatum infection and may closely mimic inflammatory, infectious, or neoplastic conditions, particularly when isolated to the small intestine. In this case, the identification of intracellular fungal organisms in ileal biopsies served as the critical diagnostic clue that prompted evaluation for an underlying immune disorder, ultimately revealing previously undiagnosed CVID. This case underscores the importance of considering primary immunodeficiency in adults with chronic gastrointestinal symptoms, severe malnutrition, or opportunistic infections in the absence of known immunosuppression. Early recognition and appropriate treatment with antifungal therapy and immunoglobulin replacement are essential to improving outcomes and preventing further complications.
