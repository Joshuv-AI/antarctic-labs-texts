Qualitative investigation of academic routines and mental health among medical students following coronavirus vaccination
Authors: Soraia Nogueira Felix, Anna Paula Lima Costa, Juliana Matsumoto Andrade, Aristides Tadeu Correia, Fernanda Magalhães Arantes Costa, Renata Kobayasi, Maria Lúcia Bueno Garcia, Denise Ballester, Milton de Arruda Martins, Patrícia Zen Tempski, Iolanda de Fátima Lopes Calvo Tibério, Beatriz Mangueira Saraiva Romanholo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite being essential for protecting public health, the restrictions imposed during the coronavirus pandemic have negatively affected the physical and mental well-being of medical students. Furthermore, most students had negative perceptions of full-time online courses. The COVID-19 vaccine plays a vital role in preventing the collapse of healthcare systems and mitigating stress-related illnesses. The objective of this study was to explore medical students’ self-reported feelings, symptoms, and daily routines across two key phases: the period of social isolation and the subsequent return to face-to-face learning after coronavirus vaccination.MethodsA prospective qualitative study was conducted online with medical students in their 1st to 6th years at a university in São Paulo, Brazil, from 2020 to 2021 before and after in-person activities and coronavirus vaccination; focus groups and content analysis were used.ResultsIn total, 200 students participated in the initial focus groups, whereas 60 participated in the follow-up sessions in groups of five to ten students. The discussions highlighted recurring negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, loneliness, sleep disorders and time management losses. Coping strategies were explored, and a return to in-person activities and vaccination were significant factors for improvement, particularly with the support of peers and faculty. However, challenges related to time management and sleep quality persist.ConclusionsNegative feelings recurred in medical students’ reports during social isolation. However, coping strategies have been cited. The return to in-person activities and coronavirus vaccination were positive factors in students’ personal and academic routines. Despite this, most students reported changes in their physical, mental, or behavioral state.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03499-x.

Introduction

To protect public health during the pandemic, strict restrictions were enforced. While necessary, these measures also contribute to physical and emotional difficulties, affecting not only healthcare professionals but also undergraduate students, especially those in health-related fields such as medicine [1–5].

These challenges add to an already well-documented vulnerability among medical students, who have long been recognized as particularly prone to psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, burnout, and reduced quality of life, compared with both the general population and students in nonmedical disciplines [6–8]. This susceptibility arises from several factors, including the demanding academic environment, prolonged study hours, continuous exposure to human suffering, and persistent pressure to excel academically [9, 10]. Recent meta-analyses have confirmed that rates of depression and anxiety among medical students are considerably higher than those in the general population, with pooled prevalence estimates exceeding 25–30% for depressive symptoms and approximately 33% for anxiety disorders [11, 12]. These findings underscore that medical students represent a population at enduring risk whose vulnerability was further amplified during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The mental well-being of medical students has been adversely affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, a phenomenon widely documented across multiple studies [4, 13–15]. Many reported dissatisfaction with full-time online courses, mainly due to the absence of clinical practice, concerns about knowledge acquisition and assessment, and a preference for face-to-face learning [14, 16, 17].

Data worldwide suggest that social isolation, the rapid transition to distance learning, academic stressors, and anxiety related to coronavirus infection have considerable effects on medical students’ mental health [18–23]. The disruption of clinical placements, limited access to peer and faculty support, and blurring of boundaries between academic and personal life in home settings have been identified as major contributors to academic dissatisfaction and psychological strain during lockdowns [24, 25]. In addition, prolonged social isolation has been consistently linked to increased loneliness and perceived stress, factors strongly associated with poorer mental health outcomes in student populations [26].

Some studies have compared online and in-person educational activities and their effects on the lives of medical students [27–39]. Given their crucial role in public health, enhancing the mental well-being and time management skills of this population is of paramount importance [40]. Although the negative consequences of lockdowns and distance education are well documented, recent studies indicate that certain public health measures—particularly widespread COVID-19 vaccination campaigns and the gradual resumption of onsite learning—have been linked to improvements in students’ mental well-being and academic engagement. The return to in-person classes, in particular, has been associated with reduced loneliness, better perceived learning quality, and enhanced well-being, largely due to the restoration of social interactions and hands-on clinical practice [41–43].

In addition to their epidemiological benefits, COVID-19 vaccination campaigns have also been associated with psychological improvements, including reduced perceived risk of infection and lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms in vaccinated populations [44, 45]. For students in health sciences, vaccination may support the resumption of essential practical and clinical training and contribute to a perceived sense of continuity in academic progress [46]. These psychosocial effects are increasingly recognized as potential protective factors, likely arising from a combination of vaccination, social interactions, and broader adaptive processes during the recovery phase of the pandemic.

Nevertheless, few studies have examined how vaccination and the resumption of face-to-face learning jointly affect the psychological well-being and academic functioning of medical students. Addressing this gap is particularly relevant for guiding institutional policies and mental health interventions aimed at future health professionals. In this context, the present study explored medical students’ self-reports of their emotional states, symptoms, and daily routines during social isolation and after their return to in-person learning postvaccination. The findings were intended to strengthen the evidence base on potential protective factors, likely arising from a combination of vaccinations, the resumption of social interactions, accumulated coping strategies, and broader adaptive processes, that may support mental health and academic adaptation in times of crisis.

Methods

Study design

This prospective qualitative study was conducted between August 2021 and November 2022 and included medical students from all years (first to sixth) at a private university in São Paulo, Brazil. Participants were recruited via an online convenience sampling strategy utilizing Google Forms and Google Meet for outreach. Ethical approval was granted by the university’s Research Ethics Committee (protocol number 4,467,869), all participants provided electronic informed consent, and the identities of the participants were preserved. The eligibility criteria included current enrolment in the medical program and voluntary participation. In the second phase, students who had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine were included.

Among the 500 students invited through email, WhatsApp, or phone, 200 joined the first phase’s virtual focus groups, and 60 participated in the second phase. The 60 participants in the second phase also took part in the first phase of the study. Focus groups included 5–10 students, a sample size deemed sufficient to achieve thematic saturation in this specific qualitative design, which is consistent with recommendations for small-to-moderate focus group sizes in in-depth explorations of lived experiences [47–49]. Psychological support referrals were available through the university, and participants could address questions to the research team at any stage.

Measurements

Demographic characteristics

Demographic information was collected through a questionnaire structured by the authors (e.g., sex, age and year of study) (Additional file 1).

Focus groups

Student involvement occurred through online focus groups hosted on Google Meet. The discussion guide questions for these groups were developed to explore how the pandemic influenced students’ academic pursuits and personal well-being (Additional file 2). The focus groups were structured following the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP, 2013) methodology (Additional file 3) [50]. The participants were assured of the privacy of their identities and responses, and consent was secured for recording and transcribing the sessions.

Before initiating the group discussions, the interviewers received training sessions with student volunteers who were explicitly excluded from the main study population. This preparatory phase was crucial for optimizing the research process, maintaining alignment with the study aims, and maximizing time utilization. A pilot test involving eight undergraduate medical students was conducted to assess the questions, leading to modifications of the survey instrument. Each focus group session ranged from 20 to 40 min and involved two investigators—a primary facilitator and a co-facilitator (BMSR, APLC, JMA, and LRSF). The examiners’ training was provided by three experienced qualitative researchers from the faculty (FMAC, RK, and MAM). The participants were encouraged to verbalize their views openly, without intervention from the moderators.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to assess sociodemographic data. The recorded interviews were transcribed, and the text was read by two independent examiners to begin the qualitative analysis. The data were analysed via thematic analysis following the six-phase framework proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006). The thematic analysis adopted a mixed approach, with a predominance of the inductive method.

Although the focus group script was guided by the study objectives, the thematic analysis was primarily inductive, which was consistent with an experiential, phenomenological approach. This approach allows themes to emerge from participants’ narratives without imposing preexisting categories while acknowledging that the study objectives provide a loose analytical orientation [51].

The analysis involved the following steps: (1) familiarization with the data by reading transcripts multiple times; (2) generating initial codes manually; (3) searching for patterns among codes to create potential themes; (4) reviewing and refining themes against the coded data and entire dataset; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the report with selected extracts. Coding and theme development were independently conducted by two investigators. A third examiner audited the coding process and theme structure to enhance credibility and reduce potential bias. This process ensures the rigor, transparency, and trustworthiness of the qualitative findings [52–55].

Data saturation was achieved when no new themes or relevant information emerged from successive focus groups. In the first phase of the study (during social isolation), student participation was greater due to greater availability of time and interest in sharing experiences, a circumstance that could have occurred because of widespread dissemination among peers through messages and online networks. In the second phase (after vaccination and return to face-to-face activities), data saturation was reached relatively quickly due to the recurring nature of the participants’ narratives. The smaller sample size in this phase was methodologically appropriate for capturing rich, in-depth accounts while considering students’ reduced availability, as they adapted to in-person academic schedules, in line with qualitative research principles prioritizing depth over breadth [56, 57]. The overlap between the two participant groups facilitated comparative analysis of narrative evolution, although no individual tracking was conducted due to anonymization procedures.

The recorded interviews were transcribed and analysed via thematic analysis on the basis of Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework (2006) [55]. Data coding was supported by Taguette (open-source qualitative analysis software), which allowed collaborative coding and organization of emergent themes. Independent coding was performed by two investigators, with auditing by a third examiner to strengthen credibility and reduce potential bias.

All the data were stored on secure devices and servers. Transcripts and recordings were saved on password-protected personal computers and on the Google Drive of the research team, with access restricted only to the investigators directly involved in the study. Google Drive access was secured with two-factor authentication. Participant anonymity was ensured by assigning unique codes to each transcript, and no personally identifiable information was included in the analysis. All the quotations used in the manuscript were anonymized.”

Only the research team had access to the data for coding and analysis. A third investigator audited the coding process without accessing any identifying information to ensure reliability while maintaining confidentiality. These procedures were in accordance with the ethical approval granted by the university’s Research Ethics Committee (protocol number 4,467,869), ensuring confidentiality and secure handling of all participant information.

To enhance credibility, multiple procedures were adopted. Two researchers independently performed the initial coding and theme development, with a third investigator auditing both the codes and the thematic structure to reduce bias. Additionally, the focus group script was refined through a pretest with volunteer students not included in the study results. This process ensured the clarity and appropriateness of the questions, contributing to the robustness of the survey instrument. Although triangulation through additional data sources such as diaries or surveys was not employed, we implemented analytical triangulation by involving multiple coders and auditors in the analysis process. This approach strengthened the reliability and trustworthiness of the findings.

The findings from this phase illuminate the profound phenomenological experience of medical students during the sudden and prolonged period of social isolation. The themes and categories identified reflect a complex interplay of internal and external stressors, where personal narratives of psychological distress and academic challenges emerged alongside instances of positive adaptation and resilience. These subjective accounts highlight how the pandemic was not merely a set of events but also a series of deeply personal crises that necessitated significant psychological and behavioural adjustments.

Perceived quality of life, sleep, psychological well-being and physical health

During the social isolation period, a significant proportion of the students reported a decline in their quality of life. The narratives consistently pointed to a profound disruption of their usual routines and social connections, which profoundly affected their well-being.

‘It was impaired! My day is not being very profitable and in general, also not being good because of the social issue of social interaction.’

‘It was impaired! My day is not being very profitable and in general, also not being good because of the social issue of social interaction.’

The participants frequently reported significant disruptions to their sleep patterns, characterized by periods of both hypersomnia and insomnia, and many stated that even extended sleep did not result in a sense of restfulness. This finding underscores the intimate connection between external stressors and the disruption of fundamental physiological processes, reflecting a state of psychological dysregulation.

I perceived that my sleep was totally unregulated; had days that I slept too much and days that I slept less. I was an insomniac or sleepy, even when sleeping at night I wanted to sleep all day.

I perceived that my sleep was totally unregulated; had days that I slept too much and days that I slept less. I was an insomniac or sleepy, even when sleeping at night I wanted to sleep all day.

The participants frequently reported a deterioration in both their physical and mental well-being, often alongside an escalation in self-imposed demands, which they commonly attributed to an excessive activity burden.

I realized that I had sudden mood changes, was irritated with anything and often, anything made me go into a crisis of crying and anxiety and panic.Mental well-being was certainly shaken, and physical wellness was greatly impaired.

I realized that I had sudden mood changes, was irritated with anything and often, anything made me go into a crisis of crying and anxiety and panic.

Mental well-being was certainly shaken, and physical wellness was greatly impaired.

Self-demand, self-esteem and interpersonal relationships

The strengthening of prepandemic friendships was reported by some students. Conversely, relationships with educators and newly formed peer connections were perceived as distant, contributing to declines in academic achievement due to family-related distractions.

I perceived that my relationship with my friends was strengthened, because we united… one needed to support the other; we were going through the same things. About teachers, we had more contact when it was face-to-face; online, it made it difficult.

I perceived that my relationship with my friends was strengthened, because we united… one needed to support the other; we were going through the same things. About teachers, we had more contact when it was face-to-face; online, it made it difficult.

Nevertheless, some participants identified positive experiences, such as enhanced family bonds and the adoption of routines that supported self-esteem and self-management. This dual perspective highlights the complex and often contradictory nature of pandemic experiences, where personal bonds are both challenged and strengthened in new ways.

The relationship with my family at first was very stressful; we argued a lot in the beginning of the pandemic, then it improved a lot.

The relationship with my family at first was very stressful; we argued a lot in the beginning of the pandemic, then it improved a lot.

Emotional impact on personal and academic life

Students experienced an increase in emotional distress during the period of social isolation, which included widespread narratives of stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, solitude, panic, exhaustion, reduced productivity, and chronic irritability. These emotions, either triggered or intensified by the pandemic, demonstrably impaired students’ academic routines and scholastic outcomes. Moreover, a recurring theme in student feedback indicated a compromised observation of the university curriculum during this time. Students’ practical learning was frequently hindered by the absence of hands-on activities, exacerbated by an overwhelming workload and profound demotivation. Such circumstances often result in declarations of stress, disappointment, irritability, relationship problems, and a decline in general productivity.

I didn’t learn anything. Management got worse, and I became more lazy and tired.I was completely out of patience with everything, especially related to college; there came a moment when I could not even look at the computer, I couldn’t stand it anymore.

I didn’t learn anything. Management got worse, and I became more lazy and tired.

I was completely out of patience with everything, especially related to college; there came a moment when I could not even look at the computer, I couldn’t stand it anymore.

Self-perception amidst crisis and isolation

Negative perceptions were prevalent during the period of social isolation. Many students specifically highlighted the challenges of adapting their courses and daily routines. The majority of the participants’ accounts revealed adverse effects stemming from the medical course itself, as well as the broader context of the pandemic and social isolation. Nevertheless, several positive outcomes were also identified.

It’s good because you already wake up at home, don’t have to worry about waking up early or anything, but I think the lack of physical contact, having a person on your side makes a big difference… I believe it motivates much more than being alone in your home looking at a computer screen.

It’s good because you already wake up at home, don’t have to worry about waking up early or anything, but I think the lack of physical contact, having a person on your side makes a big difference… I believe it motivates much more than being alone in your home looking at a computer screen.

Affordability, new habits and religiosity

The participants noted that although limited internet access posed difficulties for some, the transition to remote learning was perceived as beneficial by others because of the elimination of commuting time.

I like the optimization of time, even more for those who live far away, I didn’t have to waste time with transport.

I like the optimization of time, even more for those who live far away, I didn’t have to waste time with transport.

The emergence of various negative coping behaviors, such as increased alcohol intake and tobacco use, was also noted. Counterbalancing these observations, however, were positive accounts concerning adaptive coping mechanisms, enhanced opportunities for focused study, and tactics that allowed successful engagement with remote learning platforms. Additionally, a segment of students reported initiating novel healthy routines during social isolation. In multiple interviews, religiosity was identified as offering a notable source of solace and support for some participants. The coexistence of both harmful and beneficial behavioral changes is a key finding, suggesting that students actively negotiate their well-being, with some successfully adopting new, positive strategies while others resort to maladaptive coping mechanisms.

I dedicated myself to cooking and got very close to God.

I dedicated myself to cooking and got very close to God.

Time management during social isolation

While many students indicated difficulties with time management, a smaller proportion identified benefits, particularly the additional time gained from not having to commute.

It’s gotten much worse, the day needs to have at least 72 h to be able to manage everything I did before and even if before I worked all day, now, at home, it seems that it’s much worse.

It’s gotten much worse, the day needs to have at least 72 h to be able to manage everything I did before and even if before I worked all day, now, at home, it seems that it’s much worse.

The themes that emerged in this phase speak to a dynamic process of psychological and behavioural adaptation. The narratives consistently reflect a shift in focus from the challenges of isolation to the complexities of reintegration, underscoring the students’ capacity for resilience and their subjective interpretation of events.

Discussion

The discussion is divided into two phases: during social isolation (before vaccination) and returning to face-to-face activities (after the COVID-19 vaccine). In analysing the qualitative data, this study drew upon phenomenological principles, emphasizing the lived experiences and subjective perceptions of medical students throughout the pandemic [58, 59]. While an inductive approach allowed themes to emerge from participants’ narratives, understanding these experiences through the lens of the transtheoretical model of change (TTM) [60] provided conceptual depth in interpreting behavioural and psychological adaptations. Specifically, TTM considers that change occurs across successive stages—precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance—which aligns with participants’ evolving coping strategies, adoption of healthier routines, adaptation to academic challenges during isolation and resumption of face-to-face learning.

During social isolation

The participants’ narratives reflect different stages of change as conceptualized by TTM, with many students oscillating between the contemplation and preparation phases. For example, some students reported initiating healthier behaviours, such as engaging in physical activity or establishing structured routines, indicating early stages of action, whereas others remained in a contemplative stage, recognizing the need for change but struggling to implement it. Phenomenologically, these experiences highlight the subjective tension between perceived vulnerability and attempts at adaptive coping, illustrating how personal meaning-making mediates the psychological impact of isolation [61, 62]. The observed duality—adoption of coping strategies alongside ongoing stress—can be understood through these frameworks, emphasizing that behavioural adaptation is gradual, nonlinear, and influenced by both internal perceptions and external circumstances.

During social isolation, students reported perceived impairments in physical and mental well-being, sleep quality, time management, and emotional well-being, all of which negatively affect academic achievement and interpersonal relationships. Personal circumstances and relationship disruptions appear to have further hindered their academic routines. The participants frequently reported experiencing tiredness, discouragement, self-doubt, worsening mental health, challenges with family readaptation, and an amplified academic workload. Conversely, a subset of participants indicated the integration of healthier practices, including increased physical activity, enhanced lifestyle routines, and reinforced religious engagement. This duality of reports—with some moving toward the action stage of the TTM while others remained in contemplation or regressed—highlights the highly individualized nature of adapting to a crisis.

Similarly, Khalil et al. [33] explored medical students’ practices in the period of social isolation and conducted focus groups, revealing dual perspectives on remote education. The participants frequently highlighted improved time allocation as a benefit derived from the cessation of commuting. Conversely, common obstacles cited were challenges in content comprehension, unreliable internet access, and difficulties in adjusting to the online learning modality [63].

Compared with the general population, medical students are known to exhibit higher rates of anxiety, depression, burnout, and perceived stress, coupled with a reduced quality of life [64–66]. This study posits that such issues intensified throughout the pandemic, with participants frequently articulating heightened psychological distress and a compromised quality of life. These results align with those of Yunus et al. [37], who reported that more than one-third of university students from various institutions experienced moderate to extremely severe levels of stress, anxiety, and depression during the pandemic [67].

Although most studies reported no significant effect on grades, our findings revealed that academic performance was impaired. Nonetheless, students adopted coping mechanisms, including engagement in physical activity, to manage the challenges of loneliness and family dynamics. Despite the presence of coping strategies, recurrent reports have indicated ongoing anxiety about future demands and the emergence of harmful behaviours, including elevated alcohol use and smoking.

In a parallel investigation, Ross et al. [38] examined the impact of the pandemic on fifth-year medical students through focus group analysis. This research revealed a high incidence of the development of coping mechanisms to mitigate academic pressures and psychological distress, while it also revealed sustained increases in reported stress and anxiety [68].

Numerous studies have confirmed the robust association between depression and anxiety, a pattern consistent with the self-reported co-occurrence of these conditions in our research. The self-reported data notably indicated the presence of suicidal ideation alongside feelings of distress, anxiety, and depression. To further corroborate these observations, Wathelet et al. [39] undertook a comprehensive study spanning diverse university faculties, revealing a high prevalence of suicidal ideation, psychological distress, depression, anxiety, and stress throughout the pandemic [69]. These outcomes resonate with the experiences articulated by the participants in the current study.

In line with the outcomes of this qualitative research, Mortazavi and Ghardashi [40], also utilizing focus groups with medical students, identified frequent concerns related to family well-being, personal health, stress, anxiety, fear of contamination, and the imperative for constant hygiene measures [70]. In parallel with these observations, the participants in the current study highlighted considerable reductions in both sleep quality and duration, coupled with concerns about COVID-19 exposure and a hesitance to access mental health services.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the pandemic undeniably intensified mental health challenges among medical students, a phenomenon strongly supported by the current study’s observations. The literature demonstrates that the sudden shift to online education, a reduction in clinical exposure, and ambiguity surrounding academic advancement substantially fuel elevated degrees of stress, anxiety, and depression [71–73]. Academic stressors, such as the perceived increase in workload, diminished interaction with peers and faculty, and concerns about competence acquisition, particularly in clinical skills, are key contributors [74]. Additionally, the inability to separate academic responsibilities from personal spaces due to home confinement amplified emotional exhaustion and reduced academic satisfaction [75]. The presented outcomes align with our participants’ reports of fatigue, demotivation, and persistent anxiety throughout the pandemic.

These findings are consistent with previous national studies that documented a high frequency of psychological distress among Brazilian medical students throughout the pandemic. Cardoso et al. (2022) reported elevated rates of common mental disorders within this group [76], whereas Mota et al. (2021) reported a clear relationship between pandemic-related stressors and the prevalence of generalized anxiety disorders [77]. Additionally, Silva et al. (2021) emphasized that the abrupt change to remote learning negatively impacts students’ perceptions of their academic achievement and quality of life [78]. Similarly, Mendonça et al. (2021) reported that the pandemic has had a profound negative influence on the mental health and overall well-being of this population [79]. This research revealed a significant increase in anxiety and depression associated with abrupt changes in the teaching format, uncertainty regarding academic progress, and feelings of loneliness. These findings corroborate and deepen the perceptions of the participants in this study, highlighting the need for institutional strategies for psychological support and curricular adaptation that consider the challenges faced by this population through periods of health crisis.

Thus, medical students may be subject to lifestyle changes and emotional well-being problems related to stress caused by catastrophic or challenging situations. Differences in mental state may influence students’ perceptions, coping, adaptation and behavioral changes caused by the period of isolation [80]. Subjectivity, perceptions and individual experiences are essential to understanding processes of change, development or adaptation. Knowing these factors can help us understand how students perceive themselves and why some change their behavior, according to psychological theories and models of behavior change, such as the transtheoretical model of change, psychological capital theory, or phenomenological approaches [81–83].

After the return of face-to-face learning and the COVID-19 vaccine

Following the resumption of in-person classes and vaccinations, many students’ narratives indicated progression to the action and maintenance stages of TTM, as evidenced by the sustained adoption of positive coping strategies, engagement in social activities, and adherence to academic responsibilities. Phenomenologically, this period represents a renegotiation of daily routines and social interactions, with participants actively reconstructing a sense of normalcy and self-efficacy. These theoretical perspectives complement inductive analysis by providing a structured lens to interpret the temporal and behavioural evolution described in students’ lived experiences [60, 84, 85]. Importantly, this approach highlights that improvements in mental well-being should not be interpreted as solely attributable to vaccination but rather as a product of the interplay between social reconnection, accumulated coping strategies, and individual adaptation over time. Despite this, fear of becoming sick and concerns about other people being vaccinated were recurring themes. Time management and sleep quality have reportedly been impaired during these two periods, but with different approaches.

Consistent with the student reports in the current study, challenges related to time management persisted even after the return to in-person classes, suggesting that this difficulty transcended the pandemic context and may be inherent to the rigorous demands of medical education, as described by Silva et al. (2021) in a national study [78]. The participants frequently mentioned procrastination, long commuting times, and pressure to meet high academic expectations as ongoing stressors. This persistence of challenges demonstrates that resilience is not an end-state but rather a continuous process of adaptation and negotiation with new demands, a central concept within the phenomenological approach.

Activities that have become face-to-face also influence self-demand, as students consider that with the return of in-person tasks, despite being an expressed preference, they feel obliged to perform well academically. Interpersonal relationships, which are extremely impaired during social isolation, improved significantly with face-to-face activities, especially after coronavirus vaccination, with respect to colleagues and teachers.

Notably, the improvements observed in students’ mental well-being after vaccination and the return to face-to-face activities should not be interpreted as direct causal effects of these events. Rather, these improvements likely reflect a complex and multifaceted adaptive process. According to psychological models such as the Transtheoretical Model of Change [86] and Psychological Capital Theory [87], behavioral and psychological adjustments occur through gradual stages of change, underpinned by the practice of personal resources such as hope, resilience, optimism, and self-efficacy [88].

Furthermore, a phenomenological perspective [89] emphasizes how individuals interpret, construct meaning, and integrate lived experiences into their coping mechanisms. These frameworks suggest that the positive changes reported by students may result from an interplay of factors, including the natural passage of time, the accumulation of coping strategies, enhanced social support, institutional adjustments, and broader shifts in personal meaning-making, rather than vaccination alone. This highlights the importance of acknowledging the dynamic, nonlinear, and multifactorial nature of psychological adaptation in the face of the pandemic.

Similarly, a mixed-methods study conducted on health students, including those in nursing, pharmacy, and other disciplines, by Salazar-Granizo et al. revealed that online learning during the pandemic changed lifestyles and generated stress. Additionally, students preferred face-to-face teaching, which is perceived as enabling more enriching interactions with their teachers and colleagues and the opportunity to develop essential practical skills in their health practice [90]. Esguerra et al. performed a qualitative analysis including 27 medical students and revealed that pandemic-related disruptions in learning negatively impacted multiple facets of their lives. These include increased time spent at home, increased flexibility in activities and greater chances of engaging with family members [91], findings that are consistent with those detected in the present study.

According to the reports of the participants in this survey, after vaccination, feelings of safety and greater confidence predominated. Despite the persistence of the charge concerning performance, face-to-face classes are mentioned as a facilitating factor for learning. Anxiety and stress, which seem to be constant among students, now seem to focus on good performance in studies.

Sancak et al. conducted a quantitative study involving physicians working throughout the pandemic and reported that more than half of the participants expressed positive attitudes towards vaccination, noting that vaccination beneficially affected their mental health [92]. Adolescents in Germany may also have experienced a positive impact on their well-being because of the imminent threat of vaccination. According to the authors, the decision to receive a vaccination can be viewed as a personal coping mechanism that has contributed to optimism and hope [93], comparable to our study. Educational activities on vaccine prevention directed at health students can be effective in improving the knowledge and awareness of future health professionals regarding vaccines, which will be the main source of information for the population [94]. Thus, we believe that the benefits of vaccination observed in this qualitative investigation should be the objective of continuing education for medical students.

The lockdown imposed during the pandemic profoundly disrupted the daily activities and academic lives of students, forcing them to rapidly adjust to a new routine and less effective learning methods. In a longitudinal study with undergraduate students, Kamback et al. reported that participants reported generally low to moderate levels of anxiety, stress, depression, and loneliness immediately before, during, and after returning to on-campus activities. These results highlight that social restrictions exert adverse psychological effects with significant implications for students’ mental health, which is consistent with the patterns identified in our study [29].

Interestingly, in the current research, even after returning to face-to-face activities, time management seemed to be an obstacle for students, with losses in this category in most responses related to either long travel times or procrastination. Many reported maintaining new positive habits, such as physical activity and greater care for hygiene and health, during the isolation period. Similarly, impaired time management emerged as a theme in a qualitative study including medical students conducted by Jiang et al. [95].

In accordance with most student reports, academic achievement was significantly greater in face-to-face activities, which had a positive effect on students’ return to social interactions, with students reporting feeling safer after vaccination. Thus, despite feelings of content loss during the isolation period, the impact of returning to face-to-face activities was positive among the students, with the vaccine often cited as a facilitator. The feelings of fear and anxiety in the students’ reports were related to concerns about other people’s vaccination status and to students feeling obliged to have good academic performance in the medical course before face-to-face activities.

Consistent with previous studies, our results suggest that the transition back to in-person learning, in combination with COVID-19 vaccination, was related to reductions in anxiety and stress among students [43, 92, 96]. Similarly, Lasheras et al. (2020) [72] and Fawaz & Samaha (2021) [71] reported that mental health improvements were linked to decreased uncertainty, renewed access to practical learning, and strengthened social connections. Despite these positive outcomes, ongoing challenges related to time management and academic workload persist, as widely documented in the literature [42, 73, 97] and reflected in the experiences of our participants.

The present investigation highlights how medical students perceived and experienced the psychosocial impacts of the pandemic, from the challenges of social isolation to adaptive processes related to the return to in-person activities and the vaccination rollout. The qualitative nature of this research provides a valuable lens into the subjective experiences of this population, emphasizing the multifaceted and dynamic nature of psychological adaptation. These findings align with national and international research on university and medical students throughout the pandemic, underscoring the importance of institutional support, mental health resources, and pedagogical adjustments to foster resilience and academic success in the face of catastrophic situations and unexpected challenges.

Although the data were analysed inductively, the thematic coding process was informed by phenomenological and behavioural change frameworks. This approach ensured that emergent themes were interpreted not only as isolated patterns but also in relation to participants’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioural adaptation processes, thereby enhancing the conceptual robustness of the findings. By integrating TTM and phenomenological perspectives, this study underscores the dynamic, stage-based, and subjective nature of adaptation in medical students during and after the pandemic, highlighting the need for tailored institutional strategies that support both psychological and behavioural adjustment.

Limitations, strengths and considerations

One limitation of this study is that it was conducted with students from a single institution, which may restrict the extent to which the findings can be generalized to other contexts with distinct educational structures, public health responses, or cultural backgrounds. Second, the qualitative design provides rich, in-depth data but inherently lacks statistical generalizability. Furthermore, data collection through digital focus groups may have constrained participants’ spontaneity and depth of sharing, particularly for sensitive mental health topics, due to a lack of nonverbal cues, face-to-face contact and potential discomfort with the virtual format.

Another significant limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the data, which were collected at two distinct time points but without longitudinal follow-up. This design precludes causal inference, making it difficult to attribute observed improvements exclusively to vaccination or the return to in-person learning. Instead, these improvements may reflect a complex interplay of factors, including natural psychological adaptation over time, increased peer support, institutional adjustments, and broader social recovery. Future research using longitudinal mixed-methods approaches with larger, diverse samples is necessary to better disentangle these variables and clarify the long-term impacts of pandemic-related disruptions on medical students’ psychological well-being. Thus, the interpretation of symptomatic manifestations on the basis of solely self-reports may be constrained by the lack of complementary clinical evaluations.

Additionally, as an observational study, the design does not allow for causal inferences. The improvements in mental well-being observed following vaccination and the return to in-person activities should be regarded as associative findings influenced by a range of contextual psychosocial factors. It is plausible that improvements are influenced by multiple concurrent factors, including the passage of time, the natural development of adaptive coping strategies, peer and institutional support, and psychosocial resilience mechanisms. Future longitudinal studies with appropriate controls are needed to disentangle these interrelated factors and clarify the specific contributions of vaccination, educational modalities, and other supportive interventions.

This study has notable strengths, particularly its qualitative design, which facilitated an in-depth exploration of medical students’ mental health, academic routines, and coping strategies across two critical phases of the pandemic: the period of social isolation and the postvaccination return to in-person learning. Such a comprehensive perspective remains relatively scarce in the literature [98]. The persistence of challenges such as impaired time management and sleep disturbances beyond the isolation period underscores the necessity for ongoing monitoring and targeted support. Educational institutions play a pivotal role in fostering safe spaces for dialogue, ensuring mental well-being surveillance, and implementing structured interventions to support student well-being [99–101].

Our results reinforce the importance of expanding access to healthcare, preventive actions, and strategies that enhance resilience, promote healthy habits, and improve time management skills, aiming to mitigate the long-term consequences of the pandemic for this vulnerable population. Furthermore, it is essential to develop and implement evidence-based strategies that enhance coping mechanisms during crises, promote healthier lifestyle behaviors, and foster effective time management skills to support the overall well-being of medical students.

Conclusions

Social isolation during the pandemic adversely affected both the mental well-being and academic functioning of medical students, with frequent reports of anxiety, depression, stress, impaired sleep, and time management challenges. Despite these obstacles, students have adopted various coping strategies, including engaging in physical activities, developing healthier behaviours, engaging in religious practices, and strengthening family ties.

The return to in-person activities combined with coronavirus vaccination was a positive factor, with students reporting improvements in various characteristics, including perceived stress, anxiety, depression, human interactions and better use of the medical course. Nonetheless, even with vaccination, many students reported physical or mental sequelae or even behavioral changes owing to the pandemic, principally related to sleep disturbances and persistent difficulties with time management — issues that appear to transcend the pandemic period and represent ongoing concerns in medical education.

Given these findings, educational institutions should prioritize the implementation of comprehensive mental health support strategies. These may include structured counselling services, peer support programs, workshops on time management and stress reduction, and more flexible academic policies to accommodate students’ diverse needs. Additionally, integrating mental health literacy into the medical curriculum may empower students to better recognize and manage psychological distress.

Policymakers and university administrators must consider creating sustainable wellness programs that go beyond pandemic-related interventions, acknowledging that the pressures of medical training continue to impact students’ mental health. Moreover, future research should adopt longitudinal designs to track the long-term psychological effects of the pandemic on medical students and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions over time.
