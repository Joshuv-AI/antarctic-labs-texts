Atypical gut microbiota composition in a mouse model of developmental stuttering
Authors: Sayan Nanda, Bryan Lamot, Nicole Guarino, Evan Usler, Diane C. Chugani, Aditya Dutta, Ho Ming Chow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Developmental stuttering is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by disfluent speech. It has been associated with mutations in genes involved in lysosomal enzyme trafficking. Mice with mutations in one such gene, Gnptab, exhibit atypical vocalizations analogous to stuttering in humans. This mouse model has enabled the study of various molecular mechanisms related to the disorder. Simultaneously, an increasing number of reports have suggested the role of gut microbiota in altered brain function and development in neurological disorders. In this study, we compared gut microbiota profiles from Gnptab mutant mice to wildtype control mice. Microbiome analysis demonstrated a distinct microbiota profile in Gnptab mutant mice. The most significant alteration was an increased relative abundance of Akkermansia, a genus of mucin degrading bacteria, which has previously been associated with multiple neurological disorders. Moreover, the altered microbiota profile of these mice was predicted to result in differences in abundance of several metabolic pathways, including short chain fatty acid and lipopolysaccharide synthesis. These pathways may play a role in the onset, progression and persistence of developmental stuttering. This is the first study to show a potential link between developmental stuttering and changes in the gut microbiota, laying the groundwork for a new research direction.

Introduction

Developmental stuttering is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by disfluencies in speech such as blocks, prolongations, and repetitions1. The etiology of stuttering is complex, involving interactions between genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors. Stuttering is highly heritable, and several genes associated with stuttering have been identified2,3. The first set of genes identified- GNPTAB, GNPTG, NAGPA, and AP4E1, are involved in lysosomal enzyme trafficking pathways4–6. Lysosomes, through signaling pathways, play important roles in energy metabolism and immune function7–9; these have previously been linked to stuttering10–15. The identification of mutations in genes associated with stuttering has enabled the development of genetic animal models of the disorder, which are valuable tools to interrogate molecular mechanisms underpinning developmental stuttering.

Knock-in mouse strains with GNPTAB mutations found in persons with stuttering (PWS) have been generated. Gnptab mutant mice exhibit reduced number of vocalizations and longer pauses between vocalizations, analogous to stuttering in humans — the speech of PWS is slower, and contains more and longer pauses, than the speech of people who do not stutter16–18. Moreover, Gnptab mutant mice exhibit corpus callosum abnormalities similar to that of PWS and morphological differences in astrocytes and microglia16,19. However, the mechanisms leading to these cellular differences remain unknown.

While neurological disorders have traditionally been studied within the central nervous system (CNS), the importance of the gut in the pathophysiology of neurological disorders has gained recognition in recent years. The gut interacts with the brain via bidirectional signaling, known as the gut-brain axis20,21. The gut is colonized by a vast array of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi. These microbes are collectively referred to as gut microbiota, and they play important roles in complex host processes that include but are not limited to carbohydrate digestion, regulation of fat metabolism, amino acid and vitamin synthesis, maintenance of gut epithelium integrity, regulation of the immune system, and inhibition of pathogens22,23. Gut microbiota is considered an important facet of human health24, and dysbiosis, or the imbalance in the gut microbiota, is associated with a number of neurological conditions including those commonly comorbid with stuttering such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and anxiety, as well as disorders with related neural bases such as Parkinson’s disease and Tourette syndrome25–29. These dysbiosis-related diseases underscore the crucial role a healthy functioning gut microbiota plays in metabolism, the immune system, as well as CNS function and development.

Gastrointestinal (GI) issues are not typically comorbid with stuttering; however, lysosomal enzyme trafficking genes associated with stuttering are expressed in tissues throughout the body, including the gut. Mutations in these genes may lead to lysosomal dysfunction linked to gut microbiome dysbiosis30–32. Additionally, a recent study has shown that stuttering is associated with a mutation in SSUH2, a gene primarily expressed in the duodenum and small intestine2.

The relationship between the gut microbiota and stuttering has yet to be explored. To study the potential link between gut microbiota and a genetic predisposition to stuttering, we compared gut microbiota in Gnptab mutant mice to wildtype control mice. Here, we report that the Gnptab mutation is associated with alterations in composition of gut microbiota as compared to wildtype mice.

Results

Microbiota alterations in mutant mice

Bacterial 16S rRNA sequences from fecal samples collected from Gnptab mutant mice and wildtype control mice were first analyzed to assess alpha diversity, which reflects bacterial diversity within a single sample. It was examined using Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) and the Shannon’s index (Fig. 1). Faith’s PD considers phylogenetic relatedness and counts the number of branches, while Shannon’s index is an abundance-based measure which accounts for evenness. For Faith’s PD, no significant difference was found between genotypes in males, females, or when combining males and females (adjusted p-values = 0.113, 0.236, and 0.467 respectively) (Fig. 1a). In contrast, mutant mice showed a significant decrease in Shannon’s alpha diversity compared to wildtype mice (adjusted p-value < 0.001) when analyzing males and females together (Fig. 1b). When separated based on sex, the genotype difference was only found in females (adjusted p-value < 0.001). Taken together, these results reflect a possible reduction of taxonomic complexity in the mutant mice microbiome.

We used two beta diversity metrics, unweighted UniFrac and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity to identify differences in gut microbiota composition33,34. Both metrics reflect dissimilarity between microbial communities, with unweighted UniFrac considering phylogenetic-relatedness and the number of unique branches, while Bray-Curtis dissimilarity is an abundance-based approach which measures the fraction of overabundant counts. Both beta diversity metrics differed significantly between mutant and wildtype mice. The difference in microbiota composition between genotypes is illustrated using principal coordinate analyses (PCoA) of the relative similarity among samples. The two-dimensional PCoA plots of UniFrac and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity show a distinct difference between mutant and wildtype mice in microbiota composition (Fig. 2a-b). The between-group difference was confirmed by a PERMANOVA (Fig. 2c-d). Furthermore, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance was used to quantify differences in microbiota composition explained by different variables35. Genotype, sex, timepoint (two timepoints considered: 3–4 weeks and 6–7 weeks), genotype-sex interaction, and genotype by timepoint interaction were included in the model (Table 1). Based on R2 values, strong contributions were made by genotype and sex. While still significant, we found relatively weaker contributions from timepoint and genotype-sex. Genotype-timepoint was not significant, which indicates time-related changes did not differ between genotypes. Together, this data suggests Gnptab mutant mice display a unique microbiota composition compared to wildtype mice and there was a significant genotype by sex interaction.

Differences in relative microbial abundance

An analysis of compositions of microbiomes (ANCOM and ANCOM-BC) was conducted in male and female mice separately to identify bacteria with differences in relative abundance between genotypes at the genus level36,37. The relative abundances were significantly different (adjusted p-value < 0.001 and W-value > 100) between the Gnptab mutant and wildtype mice in both males and females in three genera of bacteria – Akkermansia, Ruminococcaceae, and Anaeroplasma (Table 2; Fig. 3a-c). Akkermansia and Anaeroplasma showed an increase in relative abundance in Gnptab mutant mice, while Ruminococcaceae decreased in relative abundance in Gnptab mutant mice. Four genera of bacteria (Ruminococcus, Ruminococcus gnavus group, Blautia and Eubacterium coprostanoligenes group) showed differences between the genotypes in only males (Table 2; Fig. 3d-g). All these four genera showed an increase in relative abundance. We further evaluated the effects of sex and sex by genotype interactions in these bacteria genera using the same method (ANCOM-BC, adjusted p-value < 0.001) as above. Dysregulation of metabolic pathways

To determine the potential functional effects associated with the differences in microbiota composition, we used bacterial abundance profiles of mutant and wildtype mice to infer the abundance of metabolic pathways38,39. We identified several pathways that were predicted to be significantly altered in mutant mice relative to wildtype mice. Pathways related to microbial energy metabolism, vitamin synthesis, amino acid metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, heme synthesis, short chain fatty acid (SCFA) synthesis, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) synthesis were inferred to be dysregulated (Table 3).

Discussion

This study is the first to show a potential link between stuttering-related mutations in Gnptab and changes in gut microbiota composition. While the species richness between mutant and wildtype mice did not consistently differ in the two alpha diversity metrics, our results showed a robust difference between Gnptab mutant mice and their wildtype counterparts in beta diversity. This difference in beta diversity was consistent across both males and females as well as both age groups studied (3–4 weeks and 6–7 weeks). These diversity measures suggest that Gnptab mutant mice develop a distinct gut bacteria composition early in development. These differences in gut bacteria composition may contribute to behavioral and cellular anomalies previously described in Gnptab mutant mice16,17. However, the pathophysiology linking the Gnptab mutations, beta diversity, and “stutter-like” murine vocalization remains largely unclear. There are a number of predicted biological pathways that may link atypical microbiota composition to stutter-like vocalization including bacterial dysregulation influencing short chain fatty acid (SCFA) production and increased risk of inflammation due to atypical lipopolysaccharide (LPS) synthesis. These mechanisms may alter the neural efficiency necessary for fluent vocalization, particularly in stress-inducing environments.

Bacteria from the genus Akkermansia, Anaeroplasma, and multiple genera of bacteria from the class Clostridia were found to be dysregulated in Gnptab mutant mice. Notably, all these bacterial genera have been associated with anxiety, chronic stress, and depression, though conflicting results have been reported regarding how changes in their relative abundance relate to these symptoms40–42. The most significant difference was observed in the genus Akkermansia from the phylum Verrucomicrobiota. The relative abundance of Akkermansia increased more than 3.14 log-fold in male mutant mice and 7.04 log-fold in female mutant mice. Akkermansia is a relatively well-studied bacterium. It plays beneficial roles in gut health and has been proposed to be used as a probiotic for certain metabolic and neurological disorders43–45. However, the mechanisms by which Akkermansia influences host physiology are not yet fully understood.

Akkermansia specializes in degrading mucin in the intestines, a process that releases metabolites crucial for modulating energy and lipid metabolism as well as immune activities in the host46,47. However, high levels of Akkermansia may lead to excessive degradation of mucin, compromising mucosal barrier integrity. Disruption of the intestinal barrier can increase the entry of toxins and bacterial metabolites into the blood stream, thereby adversely impacting brain function. Akkermansia has been associated with several neurological conditions, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), depression, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), multiple sclerosis (MS), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and Parkinson’s disease (PD)48–51. However, the effects of the alteration of Akkermansia in these conditions are not fully understood. For example, in PD patients, increased Akkermansia relative abundance contributes to disease progression52,53, whereas it has been hypothesized to play a protective role in MS43,54,55. In the current study, we found increased relative abundance in Akkermansia in both male and female mutant mice. However, the increase was significantly higher in female mice. Since stuttering is more prevalent in males, with a male to female ratio of 4:1, the disproportional increase of Akkermansia in female mice may suggest a potential protective role for this genus in stuttering56–58. A significant sex effect and a sex by genotype interaction were found in the Ruminococcus gnavus group and the Eubacterium coprostanoligenes group, respectively. In contrast to Akkermansia, these effects appear to be driven by an increase in abundance in male mutant mice. Further studies are needed to better understand how the effects of sex in coordination with genotype, may contribute to gut microbiome alterations.

The analysis of predicted microbial functional pathways revealed upregulation or downregulation of bacterial energy metabolism and nucleotide metabolism, as well as vitamin, amino acid, nitrogen, heme, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) synthesis in mutant mice (Table 3). These pathways are integral for bacterial survival and homeostasis, and their dysregulation may exacerbate gut dysbiosis which in turn may contribute to CNS alterations via the gut-brain axis. These pathways may provide therapeutic targets to regulate gut health in PWS. Additionally, these pathways also release and consume metabolites which may have an effect on host metabolism.

Within the predicted pathways of microbial energy metabolism, TCA cycle VII (acetate-producers) and chondroitin sulfate degradation I (bacterial), may be relevant to the host. TCA cycle VII (acetate-producers) describes a TCA cycle variant used by certain bacteria to break down acetate. Acetate is a prominent SCFA which will be discussed in more depth in a later section. Chondroitin sulfate is a component of intestinal mucin and is thought to aid gut barrier integrity59. Downregulation of chondroitin sulfate degradation may suggest a dysregulation of mucin production, which has been associated with neurological disorders60.

Several pathways related to the synthesis of vitamin K were predicted to be upregulated in mutant mice. Two major forms of vitamin K exist in the body, phylloquinone (K1) and menaquinone (K2). Menaquinone in turn consists of different subtypes which differ based on the number of carbon side chains, the two most studied subtypes are menaquinone-4 (MK-4) and menaquinone-7 (MK-7). In this study, an upregulation of longer chained menaquinones (MK-7 and longer) was predicted. While it is known that MK-4 is the predominant form of vitamin K in the brain, other forms of vitamin K also likely get converted to MK-4 in the body61,62. An important caveat is that although bacteria are known to be the main producers of menaquinone, the contribution of gut-derived vitamin K to the body is still not universally accepted63–65. MK-7 has been used as a therapeutic in clinical trials of Parkinson’s disease66. It has also been found to reverse structural and cognitive effects of natural aging in rats67. Vitamin K also has important contributions to coagulation, bone health through the carboxylation of bone-related proteins, and the synthesis of sphingolipids which are an important structural and functional component of the nervous system68,69.

The predicted pathways related to nucleotide and nitrogen metabolism do not produce or consume metabolites with apparent relevance to host metabolism. Pathways related to amino acid metabolism modulate the amino acid pool in the gut, altering host absorption of amino acids and CNS-ENS nutrient sensing70. While a relationship exists between iron homeostasis and the gut microbiome, it is unclear how pathways associated with microbiota heme synthesis may be related to alterations in iron homeostasis. Interestingly, a recent neuroimaging study shows increased iron deposition in PWS71.

We identified a pathway related to the synthesis of acetate, a SCFA (purine nucleobases degradation I (anaerobic)). Acetate along with the other major SCFAs, propionate and butyrate, account for about 90% of SCFAs produced by gut microbiota72. While most bacteria-derived SCFAs will be used by the gut and liver, a small portion of them, predominantly acetate, can enter circulation, cross the brain blood barrier and may subsequently be taken up by the brain73. In the brain, acetate is primary metabolized in astrocytes, serving as an alternative energy source to glucose74,75. This pathway was predicted to be downregulated in both male and female Gnptab mutant mice, and since acetate is preferentially used by astrocytes over neurons, the downregulation of the pathway may contribute to astrocytic morphological anomalies associated with Gnptab mutant mice16,19.

Additionally, a pathway related to the production of butyrate (succinate fermentation to butanoate) was predicted to be upregulated in male mutants. Butyrate is another major SCFA produced by gut microbiota. However, a large proportion of butyrate is consumed by colonic epithelium cells which use it as a fuel, and only ~ 2% of butyrate reaches systemic circulation and other tissues76. Beneficial effects of butyrate on gut and brain health have been reported77,78. However, an increase in butyrate concentrations was detected in a study with young children with ASD79. The direct effects of increased butyrate production on the CNS with relevance to stuttering are still not clear.

We also identified predicted pathways related to LPS synthesis. LPS is a component of the cell membrane of gram-negative bacteria and is recognized as a pro-inflammatory molecule. CMP-legionaminate biosynthesis I was inferred to be upregulated in male mutant mice and ADP-L-glycero-β-D-manno-heptose biosynthesis was inferred to be downregulated in female mutant mice. CMP-legionaminate I is related to the biosynthesis of O-antigens, which are highly variable structures on the outermost portion of LPS80. ADP-L-glycero-β-D-manno-heptose biosynthesis describes the synthesis of an oligosaccharide component of the inner core of LPS81. LPS molecules interact with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) which stimulate inflammatory cytokine production and can induce tight junction permeability82,83. These interactions can lead to reduced integrity of both the gut epithelium and blood brain barrier, facilitating entry of immune factors and microbial metabolites into the brain84–86. Entry of immune and microbial factors can lead to an array of detrimental effects on the brain, such as altered activation of astrocytes and microglia, and subsequent accumulation of damaging reactive oxygen and nitrogen species87. Similarly, a proposed mechanism of stuttering describes an immune response to group A streptococcal infection which degrades the blood brain barrier and allows entry of auto-reactive antibodies to the brain10. In turn, immune cells and their factors may disturb the underlying cell function of the speech-motor circuitry leading to stuttering. In this study, the predicted enrichment of LPS-related metabolism in mutant males implicates higher inflammatory stimulation of the immune system making them susceptible to mechanisms of neuroinflammation. In contrast, downregulation of LPS synthesis in mutant females may be indicative of reduced inflammatory activity.

The findings in this study suggest that the gut microbiota may be an important microenvironmental factor influencing stuttering. More specifically, alterations in gut microbiota were observed in mice with a genetic predisposition to stuttering-like behaviors. We discuss how these microbiome alterations may affect CNS physiology. It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the methods used in this study. 16S amplicon sequencing of select hyper-variable regions is unable to provide species and strain level resolution, as a result, we are limited to a genus level analysis. To better characterize microbiota composition, future experiments using full-length 16S sequencing or whole genome sequencing (WGS) should be considered. In addition to offering superior taxonomic resolution, WGS would also allow functional annotation through direct counting of genes in the microbiome. In this study, we are limited to inferring functional pathways through predicted metagenomic profile, and therefore, the metabolic pathway results remain speculative. In the future, metabolomic and metabolic analyses are needed to verify these findings and identify potential therapeutic targets. A future experiment analyzing fecal samples from younger pups to identify when microbiome alterations first occur would allow for a precise timeline for intervention strategies. Moreover, vocalization testing following manipulation of microbiome composition through reintroduction of bacteria or fecal transplants would confirm the contribution of the gut microbiome to the development of stuttering. A major unknown of this research is whether these findings will be replicated in humans. Research in human participants in autism spectrum disorder, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease suggest that gut microbiome abnormalities may be found in PWS as well. In the future, therapies targeting microbial dysbiosis may alleviate symptoms of the disorder and microbiome characteristics may be used as an early marker for the development of stuttering.

Materials and methods

Animal Care. Homozygous mutant Gnptab Glu1179Lys mice (BALB/c background) were received from the National Institute of Health (NIH). Two genotypes were used for this study, Gnptab mut/mut mice and wildtype control mice. Each breeding pair was comprised of mice from the same genotype. This breeding strategy was utilized to avoid co-habitation of mice from different genotypes. After 3 weeks, pups were weaned and separated into cages with 1 to 5 littermates of the same sex. Individual housing is a known stressor to mice; hence, only socially housed mice (cages with two or more mice) were used for this experiment. Mice were housed in standard plastic cages in the same room on a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and were performed in accordance with the approved regulations and as per ARRIVE guidelines.

Fecal sample collection. Fecal samples were collected from 48 wildtype (24 from each sex) and 48 mutant (24 from each sex) mice. For each mouse, samples were collected at two time points, between 3–4 and 6–7 weeks of age. These timepoints roughly correspond to early childhood and late adolescence in humans. Hence, 24 samples were collected from each sex, timepoint and genotype combination, resulting in a total of 192 samples. However, 7 samples were removed and excluded from the study due to insufficient sequencing depth after quality control (sampling depth threshold- 4250 reads) during data processing. Fecal samples were collected at the beginning of the dark cycle of the day. For each sample, the mouse was scruffed without anesthesia and allowed to defecate on to a sterile filter paper. This would typically take 1–2 min; with a mouse being held for a maximum of 5 minutes. After 5 minutes, the stress from handling the mouse was considered excessive, excluding the mouse from this study. We ensured that there was no urine contamination or contact from the mouse on the filter paper. Typically, 1–2 fecal pellets were collected for each sample. They were dropped into sterile pre-labelled cryovials and immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. New gloves and a fresh filter paper were used for each sample collected. The samples were then stored in a -80 °C freezer before sequencing. After fecal collection, mice were sacrificed using CO2 followed by cervical dislocation in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis. DNA extraction, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing library preparation and sequencing using MiSeq were done at the University of Delaware DNA Sequencing and Genotyping Center (UD-SGC). DNA was extracted from fecal samples using a Mag-Bind®Stool DNA 96 kit. The 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing libraries were prepared using the V3/V4 primers (341F and 806R) in a two-step PCR protocol88,89. Single-end 300 cycle runs were performed using V3 × 600 sequencing kits (Illumina, United States). The data were collected in FASTQ format for bioinformatics analysis.

Bioinformatics analysis of bacterial 16S rRNA amplicon data was conducted using the QIIME2.2023.7 software pipeline90. The adapters were trimmed, followed by a quality control step. Sequences were denoised using the Deblur method91. Representative sequences were then aligned to a SILVA reference database (release 128)92. Alpha and beta diversity metrics were calculated and taxonomic tables were generated. Adonis was used to calculate variance explained by variables after beta diversity measurement. In order to identify differentially expressed bacteria, an ANCOM (and ANCOM-BC) analysis was conducted36,37. The Holm-Bonferroni method was used for multiple comparison correction in ANCOM-BC36. To identify potential microbial functional signatures and functional pathways associated with the differences in microbiota composition, we used PICRUSt2 and ALDEx2 to infer metabolic pathways38,39. PICRUSt2 uses a hidden-state prediction approach to infer metagenome of samples sequences. The predicted metagenome was then used to predict gene family and pathway abundances. To select significant predicted pathways, a threshold of (|median diff. in centered log ratio (clr) between mutant and wildtype mice| > 0.5 and adjusted p value < 0.005) was used. Benjamini-Hochberg method was used for multiple comparison correction in ALDEx239. BioCyc was used to retrieve metabolite input/output and descriptive information regarding the inferred pathways93.
