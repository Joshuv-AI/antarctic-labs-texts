EnhancerDB 2.0: a comprehensive cross-species enhancer atlas
Authors: Junnuo Zheng, Yukun Li, Xingyi Du, Xinyu Zhao, Weijie Chen, Jiawei Chen, Xudong Huang, Chaoyang Pang, Zhiyun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhancers are evolutionarily conserved cis-regulatory elements that drive spatiotemporal gene expression programs underlying development, adaptation, and phenotypic diversity. To clarify the function and regulatory network of enhancers and driven by the rapid growth of large-scale omics data, we developed EnhancerDB 2.0 as an updated version of EnhancerDB 1.0 to facilitate comprehensive profiling of enhancer regulatory networks and multi-omics interpretation of gene regulation. EnhancerDB 2.0 has been expanded from a single human species to five species (human, mouse, zebrafish, fruit fly, and yeast), with the total number of enhancer entries reaching 1,605,856. The database also now provides substantially enriched annotations, including transcription factor binding sites, target genes/miRNAs/lncRNAs, SNPs, CRISPR-Cas9 target sites, chromatin accessibility regions, repeat regions, methylation sites, and GWAS entries, with nearly 100 million annotated entries in total. Furthermore, EnhancerDB 2.0 also features an improved search function and a new analysis module capable of generating publication-quality charts. In summary, EnhancerDB 2.0 provides a user-friendly platform for exploring enhancer-mediated regulation and serves as a valuable resource for researchers in biology, medicine, genetics, and related fields.

Introduction

Enhancers are cis-acting elements on DNA that contain multiple transcription factor binding sites and play crucial roles in differentiation and the maintenance of organismal homeostasis (Ong and Corces 2011). Accordingly, the establishment of dedicated databases is critical for elucidating their biological functions and associated regulatory networks. Currently, several enhancer databases have been developed, such as EnhancerAtlas (Gao and Qian 2019), ENDB (Bai et al. 2019), dbSUPER (Khan and Zhang 2016), DiseaseEnhancer (Zhang et al. 2018), SEA (Keiser et al. 2007), HeRA (Zhang et al. 2021), and HACER (Wang et al. 2019). These databases serve as crucial platforms for exploring enhancer regulatory relationships (Ashoor et al. 2015; Fishilevich et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2018b; Cai et al. 2019; Kang et al. 2019; Zeng et al. 2019; Kumar et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2023). Although these databases are useful, there are still some shortcomings. ENDB (Bai et al. 2019) mainly focuses on experimentally validated enhancers and currently contains a limited number of entries, including 713 enhancers and 376 target genes, with no substantial updates in recent years. Moreover, some core functionalities of ENDB (Bai et al. 2019), such as the Browse module, are no longer fully accessible, which restricts its practical use. In contrast, EnhancerDB 2.0 addresses these limitations by providing rich candidate enhancer information and rich annotation information, as shown in Table 1. RAEdb (Cai et al. 2019) primarily provides basic browsing and querying of enhancers, but offers relatively limited annotation content and lacks systematic integration of enhancer–target gene relationships and downstream analysis modules. EnhancerAtlas (Gao and Qian 2019) emphasizes enhancer identification and prediction based on multi-omics data and has advantages in enhancer discovery; however, its annotation categories and database-level analysis functions remain limited and do not support comprehensive enhancer-centered analyses. By comparison, EnhancerDB 2.0 offers more comprehensive annotation content, including nine types of annotations such as SNPs, TFBS, and GWAS, together with eight enhancer-related analysis functions. In addition, several earlier databases, such as HEDD (Wang et al. 2018b) and HACER (Wang et al. 2019) have not been continuously maintained and are currently inaccessible, while EnhancerDB 2.0 is designed to be systematically updated on a three-month cycle to ensure sustained data availability and long-term usability. Therefore, it is important to update databases by integrating newly emerged enhancer-related data from recent years, in order to meet the growing demands of researchers working with large-scale omics datasets.

In 2019, we developed EnhancerDB (Kang et al. 2019), a database that compiles enhancer sequences and associated annotations widely recognized and cited by researchers in the field. However, to meet the evolving research needs, EnhancerDB requires significant updates, including support for additional species, expansion of enhancer entries, and integration of comprehensive annotation and analysis tools. To address these limitations, we recently launched EnhancerDB 2.0, which incorporates an expanded dataset derived from increasing amounts of multi-omics data. Compared with EnhancerDB 1.0, we have made the following improvements (Table 1): (i) Species: EnhancerDB 2.0 has been updated from one human species to five species: human, mouse, zebrafish, fruit fly, and yeast. (ii) Enhancer data: The total number of enhancer entries has increased by approximately 1.1 million, reaching 1,605,856. (iii) Annotation data: There are now almost 100 million additional annotated entries. EnhancerDB 2.0 now includes 134,849,893 transcription factor binding sites, 84,262 genes, 21,279,386 SNPs, 4,051 miRNAs, 497,081 lncRNAs, 24,780,006 CRISPR-Cas9 target sites, 225,648 ATAC-seq peaks, 14,028,754 repeat regions, 11,123,001 methylation sites, and 757,982 GWAS entries. It also offers 1,546,068 gene expression datasets and 8,569 miRNA expression datasets. (iv) User interface and functions: We have included an analysis page where users can create and publish high-quality analysis charts by uploading custom files. To enhance the database, a new data submission page has been added, and the search function has been improved. Users can now submit their own datasets. In summary, EnhancerDB 2.0 is a user-friendly and comprehensive platform for managing large-scale enhancer data from five species, providing a wide range of annotation and analysis tools to facilitate enhancer functional interpretation. Results

We developed a concise front-end web interface to assist users in browsing, searching, analyzing, downloading, and submitting enhancer data. The front-end user interface is divided into the following sections: (i) Home, (ii) Browse, (iii) Search, (iv) Analysis, (v) Statistics, (vi) Submit, and (vii) Help (Fig. 2a).

Browse

The Browse page is divided into two sections: a directory panel on the left and a data table on the right. To help users more precisely select enhancer data for the tissues or cell lines they are interested in, we categorized the directory by species: human, mouse, zebrafish, fruit fly, and yeast. Each species, except for yeast, is further divided by disease or normal conditions, or by tissue or cell line. Users can select the corresponding samples as needed. The data table on the right displays all basic information related to the enhancers of the selected samples. This includes enhancer location, length, the tissue or cell line where the enhancer is found, disease or health status, the number of tissues harboring the enhancer, the genome version, and the data source. Additionally, by clicking on the Enhancer ID, users can access the detailed page of that enhancer to view its comprehensive information (Fig. 2b).

Search

In the Search module, users can explore seven distinct methods for querying enhancer data: (i) “Search enhancer by Tissue/Cell line”. First, select the species, then choose the tissue or cell line, and finally select the specific sample. Click “Search” to obtain all enhancer information for the selected sample. (ii) “Search by TF”. Users first select the species, and then enter the TF name to view detailed information. (iii) “Search enhancer by genomic region”. Users first select the species, and then enter the desired genomic range to view all enhancer information within that range. (iv) “Search by SNP”. Users first select the species; then enter the SNP ID they wish to query. Click “Query” to view detailed information. (v) “Search by miRNA”. Users first select the species, and then enter the miRNA ID they wish to query. Click “Query” to view detailed information. (vi) “Search by human eQTL”. Users enter the eQTL name they wish to query and click “Query” to view detailed information. (vii) “Select tissue/cell-specific or ubiquitous enhancers”. Users can select tissue-specific enhancers using a slider (Fig. 2c).

Analysis

The Analysis module provides eight analysis functions: (i) Comparative analysis of enhancer between two samples. In this function, users can compare the enhancer differences between two samples of the same species. First, users select the species, then choose two different samples, and click “Query” to find out the similarities and differences in enhancers between these two samples. (ii) Analysis of enhancer target genes. Here, users enter some gene names, select the corresponding species, and click “Query” to view information about these genes and the enhancers that regulate them. (iii) Analysis of enhancer target miRNAs. Users input some miRNA names, select the corresponding species, and click “Query” to access information about these miRNAs and the enhancers that regulate them. (iv) Generate random enhancer regions. In this function, users can upload a BED file of enhancers. The BED file should contain three columns: chrom, start, and end. After uploading, clicking “Submit” will generate information about some enhancers that have the same length as the uploaded enhancer but do not overlap with it. (v) Enhancer overlap analysis. Users first select the species, then upload a BED file containing enhancer coordinates, and finally choose the overlap percentage. Submitting the information will generate enhancer information that meets the selected overlap percentage. The larger the selected overlap percentage, the higher the similarity between the generated enhancers and the enhancers in the provided BED file. (vi) Enhancer chromosome distribution. Users can upload a BED file. Clicking “Submit” will generate a chromosome map that shows the locations of the uploaded enhancers. (vii) Enhancer GC content analysis. By uploading a BED file and clicking “Submit”, users will obtain a GC content graph of the BED file. (viii) Length distribution and cumulative frequency of enhancers. Uploading a BED file and clicking “Submit” will generate graphs for the length distribution and enrichment analysis of these enhancers (Fig. 2d).

Download, statistics and submit

All of the datasets that are integrated into EnhancerDB 2.0 are available for download in the Download interface, along with a synopsis of each data source (Fig. 2e). Additionally, users can contribute to the ongoing development and growth of EnhancerDB 2.0 by uploading their own enhancer data using the “Submit” interface (Fig. 2f). An overview of the volume of data used to create the database is given by the “Statistics” interface. This includes statistical data like the quantity of annotation data and the number of enhancers on each chromosome of various species (Fig. 2g).

A case study

We take the enhancer “enh10005” as an example to demonstrate how to use EnhancerDB 2.0. Users can begin by browsing an overview of enhancers across various tissues and cell lines on the Browse page. To explore enhancers related to nonsmall cell lung cancer, users can simply click on “Nonsmall cell lung cancer” to display the relevant enhancers (Fig. 2b). Then, clicking on “enh10005” will lead to a detailed information page, which is divided into three sections: basic information, annotation details, and a heatmap of the expression levels of genes that the enhancer may regulate (Fig. 2h).

In the basic information section, users can view the enhancer's genomic location, length, fasta sequence, species, and the cell lines or tissues in which it is active. For example, “enh10005” is located on chromosome 7 (chr7:127304805–127311775) in the human genome, with a length of 6,971 bp, and is active in 12 tissues/cell lines, including Keratinocyte, PC-3, HeLa-S3, etc. The annotation section provides detailed regulatory network information, including SNPs that may influence the enhancer, genes that the enhancer may regulate, expression profiles of potentially regulated TFs, and associated miRNAs, lncRNAs, methylation sites, CRISPR-Cas9 target sites, ATAC sites and GWAS variants present on the enhancer. For instance, “enh10005” is annotated with 68 SNPs, 1,095 TFs, experimentally validated enhancer target genes, five GWAS variants, 42 CRISPR-Cas9 sites, and 94 ATAC-seq sites. The gene expression heatmap displays the expression levels of genes potentially regulated by the enhancer across various tissues or conditions. Overall, EnhancerDB 2.0 serves as a comprehensive and valuable resource for exploring enhancers and their associated regulatory networks.

Discussion

In 2019, we developed EnhancerDB, a database that compiles enhancer sequences and their associated annotations. We are pleased that EnhancerDB has been widely recognized and cited by researchers in the field. Nevertheless, the original version of EnhancerDB has certain limitations, such as containing enhancers data from only one species (human), limited annotation information, and a lack of integrated analysis modules. To address these issues, we launched EnhancerDB 2.0, an upgraded version that incorporates an expanded dataset derived from the growing volume of multi-omics data. Although several enhancer-related resources are currently available, they still have notable limitations. For example, EnhancerAtlas and RAEdb primarily focus on enhancer identification with limited downstream functional annotation, while other resources such as dbSUPER and HeRA are restricted to specific domains, which limits their broader applicability. Furthermore, the lack of continuous maintenance has rendered several earlier platforms (e.g. HACER, HEDD, and SEA) either static or no longer accessible. These limitations highlight the need for more integrative and sustainably maintained resources. Compared with existing resources, EnhancerDB 2.0 provides significant added value by shifting from a basic data repository to a comprehensive, analysis-oriented platform. In addition, its three-month update cycle ensures sustained data relevance and usability. Overall, EnhancerDB 2.0 effectively bridges the gap between basic enhancer identification and downstream functional interpretation, offering a more comprehensive platform compared to earlier resources.

EnhancerDB 2.0 now includes enhancer information from five species: humans, mice, zebrafish, fruit flies, and yeast. Using ChIP-seq data of H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and H3K4me3, along with DNase-seq data, EnhancerDB 2.0 has identified nearly 1,100,000 additional enhancers compared to the original version, bringing the total number of enhancers to 1,605,856. Furthermore, EnhancerDB 2.0 offers substantially richer annotation information than EnhancerDB, including details about TFs, SNPs, eQTLs, genes, miRNAs, lncRNAs, methylation sites, chromatin accessibility, Crispr-Cas9 sites and GWAS data. In addition, EnhancerDB 2.0 features optimized query functions, enabling users to search for tissue-specific enhancers, query transcription factors, search by genomic location, and retrieve information related to SNPs, miRNAs, and eQTLs. EnhancerDB 2.0 also introduces new analysis functions, enabling users to conveniently conduct various analyses on enhancer data and generate professional bioinformatics visualizations.

In the future, ongoing advances in sequencing technologies will generate increasingly rich omics datasets, offering deeper insights into enhancers. We will continue to utilize these data to identify additional enhancers and their annotations. To ensure data timeliness and consistency, newly generated omics data will be incorporated using a unified update strategy, in which newly released datasets are systematically processed, integrated, and functionally annotated at three-month intervals. Moreover, we plan to integrate more experimentally validated enhancers reported in the literature into EnhancerDB 2.0, and further expand its visualization and analysis modules. We also welcome researchers to contribute their own enhancer and annotation data to further enrich EnhancerDB2.0. We are confident that EnhancerDB 2.0 will serve as a valuable resource for studying enhancers and their roles in gene regulation, and we are committed to making it widely accessible to the scientific community.

Material and methods

Data collection/sources

To construct the enhancer dataset in human and mouse for EnhancerDB 2.0, we collected 863 datasets (Table S1) from ENCODE (ENCODE Project Consortium 2012) and GEO (Barrett et al. 2013). All reference genomes were converted to the latest versions using liftOver, with genome versions being human genome (hg38), mouse genome (mm39), fruit fly genome (dm6), yeast genome (sacCer3), and zebrafish genome (danRer11). In terms of annotation data, a total of 134,758,622 transcription factor binding site (TFBS) entries were integrated from multiple databases, including UCSC (Lee et al. 2022), GTRD (Kolmykov et al. 2021), REMAP (Hammal et al. 2022), JASPAR (Rauluseviciute et al. 2024), and TRANSFAC (Wingender 2000). Gene annotations, totaling 84,262 entries, were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser. A total of 21,279,446 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were retrieved from dbSNP (Phan et al. 2025). Additionally, 5,868,536 eQTL entries were retrieved from GTEx (Yang et al. 2017), PancanQTL (Chen et al. 2024), and SeeQTL (Xia et al. 2012). Additional annotation resources included 4,937 microRNAs (miRNAs) from miRbase (Kozomara et al. 2019), 497,061 long noncoding RNAs from NONCODE (Zhao et al. 2021), 225,648 ATAC sites from ATACdb (Wang et al. 2021), and 11,123,001 DNA methylation sites from GREAP (Yang et al. 2022). Furthermore, 418,782 experimentally derived chromatin interaction records (e.g. Hi-C, ChIA-PET, Capture-C) supporting physical enhancer–promoter interactions were integrated from Expresso (Cai et al. 2025) and the 3D Genome Browser (Wang et al. 2018a), and 757,982 GWAS entries were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (Cerezo et al. 2025).

Processing and quality control of ChIP-seq and DNase-seq data

Histone ChIP-seq and DNase-seq data were processed and quality-controlled using the ENCODE uniform processing pipelines. For histone ChIP-seq data, strand cross-correlation analysis was performed on deduplicated BAM files, and datasets meeting ENCODE thresholds (NSC ≥ 1.05 and RSC ≥ 0.8) were retained. Peaks were identified using the ENCODE ChIP-seq pipeline, and datasets with fraction of reads in peaks (FRiP) ≥ 0.01 were selected. DNase-seq data were uniformly processed using the ENCODE DNase-seq pipeline. Samples with mitochondrial read proportions below 20% and signal portion of tags (SPOT score) ≥ 0.4 were retained. These quality control procedures ensured the reliability and reproducibility of the histone ChIP-seq and DNase-seq datasets included in EnhancerDB 2.0.

Quality control of user-submitted data

To ensure the reliability and consistency of user-contributed data, EnhancerDB 2.0 applies a standardized quality control pipeline to all submitted entries. Specifically, the following steps are performed: (i) Format validation: submitted files are checked for compliance with BED coordinate format requirements, including chromosome naming conventions and genome build information; (ii) coordinate standardization: if the submitted genome version differs from the reference genome used in EnhancerDB 2.0, genomic coordinates are converted accordingly using liftOver and CrossMap; (iii) redundancy check: submitted records are compared against existing enhancer entries to identify and remove duplicates; (iv) source verification: the associated data sources and supporting literature provided by the submitter are reviewed for credibility; and (v) integration: only entries that pass all of the above checks are incorporated into the database.

Identification of enhancers

The methods for identification of enhancers are as follows: (i) Normalize the signals of DNase-seq and ChIP-seq datasets for H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and H3K4me1. (ii) Expand each DNase-hypersensitive site (DHS) region by 2 kb both upstream and downstream to generate candidate enhancer regions. (iii) Screen these candidate enhancer regions, retaining those with high H3K27ac and H3K4me1 signals, and low H3K4me3 signals, as described in previous studies (Creyghton et al. 2010; Rada-Iglesias et al. 2011; Pradeepa 2017). (iv) If the predicted enhancer regions overlap, merge them and treat them as a single enhancer. (v) Filter out enhancer regions that overlap with gene bodies. (vi) Use deepTools (Ramírez et al. 2016) to generate enhancer signal maps for the predicted regions, ensuring that they exhibit high H3K27ac and H3K4me1 signals, and low H3K4me3 signals. Due to the lack of corresponding multi-omics data for zebrafish, fruit fly, and yeast, enhancer regions for these species were directly obtained from the EnhancerAtlas database and the REDfly database (Keränen et al. 2022), totaling 323,427 active enhancers.

Regulatory annotation criteria

Regulatory relationships were defined based on the following criteria. If a TFBS overlaps with an enhancer region, the corresponding TF is assumed to potentially regulate the enhancer. The regulatory mechanisms between TFs and genes, as well as between TFs and miRNAs, are similar (Afshar et al. 2014; Li et al. 2020). According to previous studies, the region spanning 5 kb upstream and 1 kb downstream of the transcription start site (TSS) of a gene or miRNA is defined as its promoter region (McLean et al. 2010). Consequently, if the TFBS bound by a TF overlaps with the promoter region of a gene or miRNA, the TF is considered to potentially regulate that gene or miRNA. For genes, enhancer–target gene pairs were identified using experimentally derived chromatin interaction data (e.g. Hi-C, ChIA-PET, Capture-C). When such data were unavailable for certain cell lines and tissues, a distance-based approach reported in previous studies (Chepelev et al. 2012) was employed instead. If an enhancer is located within ±100 kb of the TSS of a gene, it is assumed to potentially regulate that gene. For each enhancer, we also recorded the number and identity of overlapping GWAS variants, along with associated traits or diseases, to provide insights into how noncoding variants may influence transcriptional regulation through enhancers. All annotation data were converted to the consistent genome version using UCSC LiftOver.

System design and implementation

EnhancerDB 2.0 runs on an Nginx Web server (https://nginx.org/). The database is managed using sqlite3, and the server-side functionality is implemented with PHP7.3. The front-end is designed using bootstrap V3.3.7 and Jquery V2.1.1. Interaction between the front-end and back-end is achieved through JavaScript. Analysis functions are implemented using both R Shiny and PHP. Statistical graphs on the user interface are designed using ECharts v3.0. To ensure optimal performance and compatibility, we recommend using the latest versions of Firefox, Chrome, or Safari browsers.
