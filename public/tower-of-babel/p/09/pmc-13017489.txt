Serological Survey in African Nonhuman Primate Species in Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of the Congo Reveals Low Overall Seroprevalence to Mpox Virus
Authors: Neraide Biai, Simon-Pierre Ndimbo-Kumugo, Severin Loul, Innocent Ndong Bass, Maëliss Champagne, Placide Mbala Kingebeni, Eric Delaporte, Jean-Jacques Muyembe-Tamfum, Charles Kouanfack, Ahidjo Ayouba, Steve Ahuka-Mundeke, Martine Peeters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mpox outbreak in August 2024 in central Africa, together with the mpox pandemic in 2022 associated with the emergence of new viral lineages in urban areas, highlighted that this historically neglected zoonotic tropical disease caused by mpox virus (MPXV) is of public health concern. The majority of mpox infections are of zoonotic origin, but knowledge on the animal reservoir of MPXV is still extremely limited.MethodsNonhuman primate (NHP) samples (n = 1571) from 29 different species, collected across Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), were tested for the presence of IgG antibodies with a peptide-based multiplex mpox serological assay.ResultsAccording to stringent or less-stringent cutoff used, 17 to 85 animals (1.1%–5.4%) had antibodies to at least 1 of the 3 peptides used. Seropositive samples were observed in 7 to 15 species, including Cercopithecus sp (C. ascanius, C. cephus, C. mitis, C. nictitans, C. neglectus, C. pogonias), Cercocebus sp (C. torquatus, C. agilis), Colobus sp (C. angolensis, C. guereza), Papio anubis, Lophocebus albigena, Mandrillus sphinx, Allenopithecus nigroviridis, and Pan troglodytes. Mpox seroprevalence was higher in Cameroon than in DRC (1.7%–8.5% vs 0.2%–0.9%; P < .01 to P < .001, respectively). Seroprevalence differed also according to sampling sites. In several sites, more than 1 NHP species was seropositive. Mpox seroprevalence differed, but not significantly, in arboreal (1.2%–5.4%; 15–69/1267), semiterrestrial (1.1%–4.3%; 1–4/92), and terrestrial species (0.5%–5.7%; 1–12/212).ConclusionsThe low overall seroprevalence suggests that NHPs are most likely intermediate hosts and may be infected by other species, including reservoirs. However, they remain a potential source of human infection.

Until 2022, mpox was considered as a neglected zoonotic tropical disease. Mpox is caused by monkeypox virus (MPXV), an orthopoxvirus of the Poxviridae family, which includes other well-known poxviruses such as variola virus, the cause of smallpox [1]. The first human case of mpox was detected in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in 1970 [2]. Since then, the disease has been endemic in DRC, and outbreaks have also been documented in other countries of Central and West Africa, mainly in forested areas [3]. There are 2 clades of MPXV: clade I (formerly the Congo Basin clade) and the less-virulent clade II (formerly the West African clade) [3]. Today, each clade can be further subdivided into subclades, with clade IIb responsible for the global mpox outbreak in 2022 and clade Ib, which has emerged recently in eastern DRC [4–6]. After its emergence at the end of 2023 and early 2024, clade Ib rapidly reached urban centers in DRC and neighboring countries in the east, such as Rwanda, Uganda, Kenya, and Burundi, and rare cases have been exported to other continents [7]. These observations led to the declaration of a Public Health Emergency for Continental Security for mpox on 13 August 2024, by the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the second declaration of a Public Health Emergency of International Security for mpox by the World Health Organization on 14 August 2024 [8, 9].

Overall, since the recognition of the first human case, DRC has experienced the highest burden of mpox cases. The number of suspected and confirmed mpox cases has increased significantly over recent years [10]. Since 2023, mpox cases have been reported from an increasing number of provinces, including new foci of sustained human-to-human transmission in large urban areas, especially with the new clade Ib variant but also with clade Ia, as recently observed in Kinshasa, the capital city [6, 11, 12]. These recent observations suggest that each zoonotic transmission could potentially lead to a large human outbreak.

Our recent study on genomic characterization of many MPXV strains from different areas in DRC showed that zoonotic transmission events represented the large majority (> 95%) of mpox infections between 2018 and early 2024 [11]. It is thus urgent to document the nature and extent of animal reservoirs, but current knowledge is still extremely limited. Rodents are considered a possible reservoir for MPXV in Africa, but the virus has only been molecularly characterized (near full-length genome) in a limited number of rodents in their natural habitat; that is, 1 Funisciurus anerythrus in 1985 from northern DRC and 1 Funisciurus pyrropus that was collected recently in the Tai National Park in Ivory Coast, each infected with a clade I or clade II virus, respectively [13, 14]. On the other hand, MPXV antibodies or polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-positive fragments, without sequence confirmation, have been reported in samples from various other rodents (eg, Cricetomys, Graphiurus, Mastomys, Oenomys, Funisciurus, Heliosciurus) and shrews (eg, Crocidura) [13, 15–20]. MPXV has also been characterized in nonhuman primates (NHPs) in Africa, particularly in symptomatic animals, that is, sooty mangabeys (Cercocebus atys atys) and western chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes verus) in the Taï National Park in Ivory Coast and central chimpanzees (P. t. troglodytes) in a sanctuary in Cameroon [21–23]. In addition, serological evidence has also been reported in other NHP species [24–30].

Apparently, MPXV can infect multiple hosts but most likely not all are reservoir species. Nevertheless, all potentially infected animals can represent a source of human infection. Although NHPs are assumed to be incidental hosts, it remains important to better understand their potential role in zoonotic spillover events, because interactions between humans and primates are frequent and can facilitate zoonotic transmission of viruses [31, 32]. In this study we analyzed samples from NHPs in 2 mpox endemic countries (Cameroon and DRC) for the presence of MPXV antibodies.

METHODS

Samples From Nonhuman Primates

NHP samples, collected during previous studies as whole blood or dried blood spot (DBS) from bushmeat and pet NHPs, were tested for the presence of antibodies to mpox [33–36]. The samples were collected between 1999 and 2016 at several sites in Cameroon and DRC (Figure 1). The study was approved by authorities of DRC (ESP/CE/009/2020; ESP/CE/235/2024) and Cameroon (No. 2018/09/1090/CE/CNERSH/SP). Briefly, whole-blood samples were collected from NHP bushmeat by intracardiac puncture or as DBS around the injury points caused by hunting. Whole-blood samples were stored at −20°C and after air drying DBS were stored with a desiccant in airtight bags at −20°C. Samples from pets were collected by venipuncture, after being tranquillized with ketamine (10 mg/kg). For each NHP sample, date and collection site were noted. Species were visually identified in the field and on a subset molecular analysis was done for confirmation, as previously described, by sequence analysis of a 450-bp mitochondrial DNA fragment spanning the 12SrRNA gene using primers 12S-L1091 and 12S-H1478 [37].

Screening for Mpox Antibodies

All samples were analyzed with our recently described peptide-based multiplex mpox serology using Luminex technology [38]. Three previously identified synthetic peptides from the right terminal region B (B21R.179/180 and B21R.185/186 for mpox and B22R.64/65 for variola major) [39] were synthesized as 31-mer peptides with an additional N-terminal cysteine residue (82%–95% purity) and conjugated to bovine serum albumin (Eurogentec). Reconstituted peptide conjugates (2 mg/mL in H2O) were stored at −80°C until use. Conjugated peptides were covalently coupled on carboxyl functionalized fluorescent magnetic beads (5 μg/1.25 × 106) (Luminex) with the BioPlex amine coupling kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Unreacted sites were blocked with blocking buffer from the amine coupling kit. In each well of 96-well flat-bottom plates (Greiner Bio-One), 100 µL of sample (1/200 final dilution) was incubated with 50 µL of beads (2000 beads/μL in assay buffer) for 16 hours at +4°C in the dark, under agitation at 400 rpm/min. After washing with assay buffer, 50 µL of biotinylated anti-human IgG (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated in obscurity for 30 minutes under agitation (400 rpm/min) at room temperature. After washing, 50 µL of streptavidin-R-phycoerythrin (1 mg/mL) (Fisher Scientific/Life Technologies) was added to each well and incubated for 10 minutes under agitation (400 rpm/min) at room temperature. Antigen-antibody reactions were read on MagPix equipment (Biotechne); at least 50 events were read for each bead and expressed as median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values.

In the absence of a positive control for NHPs, we applied 3 different methods to calculate the cutoff from the MFI values of the entire number of samples tested, as done in previous studies on seroprevalence of arboviruses or Ebola viruses in wildlife [40–44], that is change point analysis method, fitted univariate binomial, and exponential distributions to define the cutoff at a 0.001 risk for error. Analyses were performed with R version 4.0.2 software. For each method, we considered a sample reactive with an antigen if the MFI values were above the cutoff value shown in Supplementary Table 1. Similarly, as in previous studies, we defined the positivity of mpox antibodies as reactivity to at least 1 peptide [38, 39].

RESULTS

Nonhuman Primate Species Tested

A total of 1571 samples from 29 different NHP species were tested. Details on numbers and species per country and in total are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Among the 932 samples from Cameroon, collected at 24 different sites, 176 (18.9%) were from NHP pets. The remaining samples from Cameroon (n = 756) and DRC (n = 639) were derived from NHP bushmeat and in each sampling site the sampled species reflect the NHP distribution according to their geographic habitat range and represented those that were most frequently hunted at that time. In Cameroon, mustached monkeys (Cercopithecus cephus, 37.4%) and greater spot-nosed monkeys (Cercopithecus nictitans, 22.7%) represented 60% of the samples, followed by crowned guenons (Cercopithecus pogonias, 11.8%) and agile mangabeys (Cercocebus agilis, 7.7%); each of the remaining 15 species represented less than 5%. In DRC, the 639 samples were collected from 7 different sites. Among the 16 species sampled in DRC, red-tailed monkeys (Cercopithecus ascanius, 36.8%), Tshuapa red colobus (Piliocolobus tholloni, 13.5%) and Wolf's monkeys (Cercopithecus wolfi, 11.3%) predominated, followed by blue monkeys (Cercopithecus mitis, 8.0%), Allen swamp monkeys (Allenopithecus nigrovidis, 7.4%), and l’Hoest monkeys (Cercopithecus lhoesti, 5.6%); the 10 other species each represented less than 5%.

Low Prevalence of IgG Antibodies to Mpox in Nonhuman Primate Samples

In Cameroun, 2.3% to 5.7% (4–10/176) of the pet NHPs had antibodies versus 1.6% to 9.1% (12–69/756) of the bushmeat NHP (P = .5–.2, respectively). Overall, mpox seroprevalence was higher in Cameroon than in DRC, that is 1.7% to 8.5% (16–79/932) versus 0.2% to 0.9% (1–6/639) (P < .01 to P < .001), respectively. Seroprevalence also differed according to sampling sites as shown in Table 2. In Cameroon, seropositive samples were observed in 7 to 11 sites, with seroprevalences ranging from 0.5% to 14.3% and 4.0% to 35.7% for stringent and less-stringent cutoffs, respectively. In DRC, seropositive samples were observed in 1 to 4 of the 7 sites sampled, ranging from 0% to 2.2% and 1.5% to 4.4% (Table 2). Table 3 shows the species distribution of the sites where seropositive animals have been identified. Interestingly, positive C. cephus, C. nictitans, and C. pogonias in Cameroon were observed in almost all sites where they were collected. Similarly, agile mangabey (C. agilis) seropositive samples were also observed at multiple sites (Table 3). Moreover, in several sites more than 1 NHP species was seropositive (Table 3). Finally, seroprevalence differed, although not significantly, according to NHP habitat and was slightly higher in arboreal monkeys (1.2%–5.4%; 15–69/1267), than in semiterrestrial (1.1%–4.3%; 1–4/92) and terrestrial species (0.5%–5.7%; 1–12/212) (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 6).

DISCUSSION

Here we conducted the first large scale survey on the presence of mpox antibodies in wild NHPs in Cameroon and DRC where mpox is endemic. In more than 1500 samples from 29 different species, we identified between 17 and 85 (1.1%–5.4%) animals from 7 to 15 different species with mpox antibodies, including Cercopithecus sp, Cercocebus sp, Colobus sp, Papio anubis, Lophocebus albigena, Mandrillus sphinx, Allenopithecus nigroviridis, and Pan troglodytes. Previous studies also reported antibodies in different NHP species from Africa, but the number of samples tested per study was often low, and for some studies the numbers per species tested, as well as the exact species, were not always clearly defined. Moreover, a wide variety of antibody assays were used. Overall, almost 1700 wild or wild-captured captive NHP from Africa, from at least 20 different species, have been previously tested from West, Central, and East Africa between 1967 and 2011, and according to the assays used 45 (2.6%) to 74 (4.3%) were positive for antibodies, which is close to the values observed in our study on different species and with a different serological assay (Table 5) [13, 15, 24–30]. Similar to our study, antibodies have been reported from different Cercopithecus species (at least 6), in Colobus and Piliocolobus species, patas monkeys (Erythrocebus patas), and different baboon species (Papio sp), mainly from east and west Africa [24–30]. Importantly, only a few and no recent studies have been conducted in mpox endemic regions in central Africa. One study, conducted in 1988, tested 153 NHPs from 2 areas in Northern DRC endemic for mpox; 12 (7.8%) animals were seropositive, the majority were C. ascanius, but unfortunately no details on the species tested are provided and it is thus unclear whether other species were also positive [30].

Our study focused on 2 countries in central Africa where mpox is endemic and thus we extend the range of potential mpox infection to additional NHP species like Colobus (C. guereza and C. angolensis), Mandrillus (M. sphinx), Lophocebus (L. albigena), and other Cercopithecus species from Central Africa. In addition to the symptomatic sooty mangabey (Cercocebus atys) from Ivory Coast, in which mpox infection was confirmed by full-length genome sequencing [21], we observed antibodies in 2 additional species from the Cercocebus genus (C. torquatus and C. agilis) in Cameroon. Moreover, in our study, a high seroprevalence was seen for C. torquatus, although the number of samples tested was low and from a single site, and are therefore most likely not representative. Mpox infection has been confirmed by sequence analysis in a central chimpanzee (P. t. troglodytes) in Cameroon and in several western chimpanzees (P. t. verus) in Ivory Coast [22, 23]; in our study 2 samples tested positive but only with the less-stringent cutoff; however, the number of samples tested was low.

Mpox seroprevalence in our study is comparable to previous studies, despite the use of different serological assays. Nevertheless, we find similar seroprevalence and seropositive animals in similar NHP genera to those previously reported. Unfortunately, we cannot compare seroprevalence among species because previous studies were mainly conducted in West and East Africa, which are inhabited by different NHP species compared to those from our study in Cameroon and DRC. Mpox seroprevalence is higher in Cameroon than in DRC, but more samples have been tested from Cameroon, and the species distribution also differs between the 2 countries. Moreover, in Cameroon the geographic range of sampling was smaller, and samples were collected in similar ecological areas, that is forested areas. In Cameroon, the majority of mpox cases have been reported in forested areas of the southern part of the country where most NHP samples were collected. In contrast, in DRC, fewer samples were collected at fewer sites and over a wider geographic range, and in different ecological environments. Almost one-quarter of the samples were collected in eastern DRC, where almost no endemic mpox cases have been reported before the sustained human-to-human transmission outbreak that started at the end of 2023. It will thus be important to study more sites in the DRC to have a better picture of the seroprevalence of MPXV in NHPs. In addition to the wide diversity of antibody assays used in previous studies, another limitation to compare mpox seroprevalence is that we do not know whether antibodies also decline rapidly over time in wildlife, similar to that observed by certain studies in humans, which could also explain in part differences across study sites and species [45–47].

Interestingly, seropositive C. nictitans, C. cephus, and C. pogonias monkeys have been observed at different collection sites in southern Cameroon, suggesting that the infection is potentially widespread in these species. C. nictitans and C. pogonias monkeys are also present in northern DRC, but only 9 and 1, respectively, were tested in our study in DRC, explaining the absence of antibody detection. Similarly, the geographic range of C. agilis, C. guereza, and L. albigena species, in which antibodies have been observed in Cameroon, also spans northern DRC, but no antibodies were observed because only a few samples of these species were obtained in DRC; that is 3 C. agilis, 2 C. guereza, and 3 L. albigena. In Cameroon, we also observed that multiple species can be positive at the same site, suggesting potentially circulation of the virus among cohabiting NHP species. The NHPs that were sampled in Yaounde, the capital city of Cameroon, were from pet and bushmeat NHPs that were imported from other regions in the country. Overall, 2.6% to 5.9% (4–9/152) of animals sampled in Yaounde had antibodies, suggesting thus potential importation of viruses into densely populated areas and exposure to infected animals in urban sites.

The low overall seroprevalence observed in our and previous studies suggests that NHPs are most likely not a reservoir for mpox but can become infected through contact with other species, including reservoirs like rodents or squirrels. We therefore examined whether seroprevalence could differ among NHP habitats, but no significant differences were observed among, terrestrial, semiterrestrial, and arboreal species, which could suggest that NHPs can become infected from terrestrial and arboreal rodents. Mpox virus or antibodies have been detected in a wide diversity of rodent species in Africa, but among these it is not clear yet which are reservoir species or atypical hosts [24–30]. A recent study using ecological niche modeling concluded that the most probable MPXV reservoir is F. anerythrus but this needs to be confirmed on more data from field observations [20]. Interestingly, direct interspecies transmission of MPXV via predation of Cercocebus atys on Funisciurus pyrropus in Ivory Coast has been recently reported, reinforcing the role of squirrels as reservoir species .

Our study has several limitations. In the absence of positive and negative control samples from NHPs, we used 3 different cutoffs in our study, which could underestimate or overestimate the real prevalence. Our peptide-based multiplex assay has been validated on a panel of human samples with a sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 100% (unpublished results [38, 39]). It is possible that our assay is too specific and that the 3 antigens used are not sufficient to detect all mpox infections. The vast majority of NHP samples reacted only with 1 of the 3 peptides, similar to observations with the same antigens in an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay on human samples from the 2003 outbreak in the United States [39], and with the same multiplex assay on PCR-confirmed human samples in DRC [38]. Furthermore, cross reactivity between orthopoxviruses, together with rapid antibody waning, presents a challenge for the serological diagnosis of MPXV. We cannot exclude that our assay, similar to previous assays, may also detect cross-reactive antibodies to other orthopoxviruses and needs improvements for upcoming studies, for example by adding additional antigens as recently reported in other multiplex assays for detection of mpox antibodies in humans [48, 49]. Finally, samples derived from bushmeat and pets are potentially not representative of NHP in the wild. NHP pets are generally captured at a young age when the mothers are killed by hunters, and we cannot exclude that a proportion of bushmeat animals represent animals that have a weaker health status related to an infectious disease, including mpox, and thus more easily hunt. In the latter case, the observed prevalences could be an overestimation of prevalence in the wild.

Although NHPs are most likely not a reservoir species (as also indicated by the development of symptoms in some species), antibodies have been detected in a wide diversity of species. NHPs can be infected in the wild as illustrated by the sooty mangabey and chimpanzees in the Taï forest in Ivory Coast [21, 22]. NHPs can thus represent a source of human infection by zoonotic transmission through hunting or handling wild NHPs. Given the important number of ongoing zoonotic transmissions of mpox and their ability to adapt and spread widely in human populations, it is urgent to better understand which animal species can be infected with mpox, either as atypical host or natural hosts, and which species are the most likely source of human infection. Multidisciplinary studies to understand the ecology and extent of MPXV in wildlife, as well as the frequency and nature of contacts between humans and potential reservoir species, are urgently needed to implement strategies to reduce zoonotic transmission events.
