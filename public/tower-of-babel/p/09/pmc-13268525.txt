Winter sanitation and ground management practices for navel orangeworm (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) control in pistachios
Authors: Kent M Daane, Glenn Y Yokota, Rodrigo Krugner, Joel P Siegel, David R Haviland, Houston Wilson, Charles S Burks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe navel orangeworm, Amyelois transitella (Walker) (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae), is a key pest of California nut crops, whose control programs include the winter sanitation of unharvested nuts (mummies). A 5-yr survey of pistachio orchards showed a positive association between the number of mummies per tree and the number of A. transitella eggs deposited during the first flight in 3 of 5 yr, and a negative association in 1 of 5 yr. Factors that impacted overwintering A. transitella included the number of closed or empty nuts lacking a kernel (blanks), infestation levels (range 0.4% to 13.5%), and the number of mummies on the ground (range 11.2 to 90.6 mummies per m2). There was higher moth emergence from nuts on the berm than from the row middles. Mowing, discing, or rototilling the row middles reduced adult moth emergence, with raking nuts into the row middle being the most effective. Neonate A. transitella utilized both open pistachio mummies and insect damaged or split mummies; closed shell mummies and new crop undamaged nuts were not used. Oviposition varied by egg trap location, with the fewest eggs deposited on ground traps, and similar numbers deposited at mid-canopy and at upper canopy. These findings underscore the importance of winter sanitation that includes destroying ground mummies in pistachios to reduce the standing population of A. transitella and that a complete program includes both the removal of mummies from the canopy as well as their subsequent destruction on the ground.

Introduction

The navel orangeworm, Amyelois transitella (Walker) (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) is a key multivoltine pest of almond, pistachio, and walnut in California (Grant et al. 2016, Haviland et al. 2016, 2020a, 2020b). The larvae cause direct damage by feeding on the nut meat (Curtis et al. 1984, Wilson et al. 2020) and indirect damage by contaminating the hull with frass and facilitating the entry of Aspergillus fungi that produce carcinogenic aflatoxins (Doster and Michailides 1994, Picot et al. 2017). Amyelois transitella infests other crops, such as pomegranate and fig, but these 3 nut crops, grown on ∼970,000 ha, represent nearly $9 billion or 18% of California’s total annual crop value of $50 billion. The high-value and large export market for tree nuts often result in more intensive management practices to reduce damage. In-season controls are largely based on insecticide applications (Siegel et al. 2019a, 2019b, Haviland et al. 2020a, 2020b) although resistance to some toxicants, especially pyrethroids, has been documented (Demkovich et al. 2021). Additionally, spray drift presents another unintended impact that is a regulatory concern (Van Steenwyk et al. 2021). While mating disruption is an increasingly used tool (Burks and Thomson 2020, Haviland et al. 2021, Higbee and Burks 2021), its implementation is based on economics that vary annually and regionally.

Even with insecticide applications and/or mating disruption, orchard managers often undertake the additional cost of winter sanitation of unharvested “mummy” nuts. Mummies serve as both harborage and food for overwintering A. transitella larvae (Caltagirone et al. 1968). This moth does not diapause, therefore larvae develop in the mummies during winter, pupate and then emerge as adults, typically starting in late February with peak emergence in May (Siegel et al. 2008, Siegel and Gilcrease 2022). Research with A. transitella in almonds have shown that mummy removal—or sanitation—lowers A. transitella density and crop damage (eg Engle and Barnes 1983, Zalom et al. 1984, Higbee and Siegel 2009). Almond orchards are sanitized by mechanically shaking the tree with a harvester and/or using poles to manually dislodge mummy nuts, and once the mummies are on the ground they are either left to decompose, destroyed using a flail mower or tilled into the soil (Haviland et al. 2016, Wilson et al. 2020). The current recommendation for almond sanitation in California is no more than 2 mummies per tree, with a more stringent threshold of 0.2 to 1 mummy nuts per tree in the southern San Joaquin Valley. In pistachio, sanitation thresholds have not been developed, although A. transitella abundance has been attributed to large numbers of infested, overwintered mummies (Burks et al. 2008), and adult movement from pistachio to neighboring almond orchards was shown to increase insect damage (Higbee and Siegel 2009).

Studies in pistachio orchards are necessary to quantify the impact of sanitation on A. transitella. Implementing mummy sanitation in pistachio is challenging due to their smaller size and tougher shell compared with almond, making them harder to destroy. Here, we report the results of a multi-year study in commercial pistachio orchards that focused on 4 parameters. First, we investigated the relationship between the number of mummies in the tree canopy and on the ground and the A. transitella density in the subsequent season (2000 to 2004). Second, we compared pistachio sanitation programs that either left mummies where they fell under the tree (berm) or moved fallen mummies off the berm and into the row middles (drive row) (2002). Third, we examined the impact of winter ground management practices on A. transitella survival (2002 to 2004). Fourth, we assessed the impact of nut condition on the survival of neonate A. transitella, as well as the ability of A. transitella to deposit eggs on mummies on the ground (2009).

Materials and Methods

Orchard Survey

To initiate our assessment of pistachio sanitation practices, we chose 6 commercial pistachio blocks ranging from 15 to >40 ha, that were located in Fresno, Kings, and Tulare counties (2 each). The sites were selected to represent conventional, selective-use insecticides, and organic management practices (2 each), and all had a history of A. transitella damage. The conventional sites received pyrethroid and/or organophosphate insecticide applications for A. transitella and hemipteran pest control, the selective-use sites received pyrethroid and Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) insecticide applications, and the organic sites received multiple Bt applications for lepidopteran control. The conventional sites were relatively free of ground vegetation year-round, whereas the selective use and organic sites had either sown or resident ground vegetation during most of the season and employed a preharvest clearing or mowing of dried ground vegetation by late summer. Sampling at each site was conducted in a designated area of about 4 to 5 ha containing 1,000 trees. The plot shape depended on tree spacing and block size and was either 10 rows by 100 trees per row or 20 rows by 50 trees per row. The sampled area was located 10 to 15 rows from the orchard edge, and for each sample process, trees or pheromone trap locations were blocked by row and trees were randomly selected within each row. All orchards were sanitized in winter, although the number of mummies left in the tree canopy and on the ground varied among sites and years.

Multiple sampling techniques were used to determine mummy density and their level of infestation by A. transitella. In spring 2000, from April 5 to 20, the numbers of mummies per tree were recorded on 100 trees per site (25 trees in each of 4 rows, with every second or third tree per row sampled). To compare winter mummy collections and counts to the subsequent A. transitella densities, eggs were sampled using Pherocon IV NOW egg traps (Trécé Inc., Adair, Oklahoma) loaded with almond meal, with 5 traps per site and with traps replaced with fresh traps every 7 to 14 d (April through September). Data were transformed to “eggs per trap per week” and the sample date was the date that traps were returned to the laboratory, where the eggs were examined under a dissecting microscope and scored as live, hatched or dead. Mummy infestation levels were determined during a 2-wk period from March 23 through April 6 by collecting mummies from the canopy of 5 trees per site (long bamboo poles were used to dislodge nuts) and from the ground underneath the canopy (∼20 m2) of the same 5 trees. In this first year, the trees sampled were selected because of a large number of nuts in the canopy or on the ground. For each of the sampled trees, all nuts were collected and later dissected in the laboratory. The nut location (ground or canopy) was recorded for each nut, and its condition was categorized as closed or open, kernel present or absent (blank), and if the nut was infested the life stage (larvae or pupae) and survival (live or dead) of any A. transitella found was determined.

This methodology was followed over the subsequent 4 yr with some adjustments. Adult female A. transitella can oviposit on mummies that have an open suture and contain a kernel (filled nut) either in the tree canopy or on the orchard floor. Therefore, the number of overwintered nuts in the canopy and on the ground must be determined, as well as the percentage of those nuts that are open and filled. The ground sampling protocol was changed to improve the quantification of mummies by using a 1 m2 frame laid on the ground at 20 trees per orchard. All mummies inside the frame were counted and collected, and the position of the frame (berm or row middle) was recorded. In 2001, ground samples were collected 5 times: February 16, March 15, April 16, June 8, and August 14. In subsequent years (2002 to 2004) ground samples were taken before the first A. transitella flight in March and after that flight from June to August. The location of the sample was recorded as either berm or row middle. In the tree canopy, counts of mummies were made before (January to February) and after (March to April) winter sanitation occurred. Tree canopy collection and dissection of mummies ceased in 2002. In every year, egg traps were used to determine the relationship between mummy counts and subsequent A. transitella density, as described above.

Assessing Berm Management of Nuts

Three methods of winter berm management were assessed in 3 large commercial orchards, with each of the treatments fully replicated at each orchard. Treatments were blowing nuts off the berm, the combination of blowing, and raking nuts off the berm to the row middle where they were mowed or disced under, and an untreated control where nuts remained where they fell on the berm or row middle. The trials were in large (>20 ha) commercial orchards with mature trees (>15 yr old), located near Kettleman City (Kings Co., California), Shafter (Kern Co., California), and Madera (Madera Co., California). At each site, there were nine 4 to 5 ha plots and treatments were set in a randomized block design with 3 replicates, using adjacent blocks at each site to accommodate the large plot size. Nut and A. transitella density was determined by placing a 1 m2 frame on the ground and counting and collecting all mummies inside, as described previously, with 3 samples collected from each plot before treatment application on February 4-7, 2002 (sampled trees were evenly spaced on rows 17 (tree 17), 24 (tree 24), and 31 (tree 31). After treatment application, 10 samples were then taken per plot on March 5 to 11, May 30 to 31, and July 25 to 31 (sampled trees were evenly spaced on rows but staggered within each row to provide better separation on rows 7 (trees 10 and 13), 12 (trees 17 and 21), 17 (trees 24 and 27), 22 (trees 31 and 34), and 27 (trees 38 and 41). Collected nuts were dissected and the status of each nut was recorded as open or closed shell, filled or blank, and A. transitella larvae or pupae present and condition (live or dead).

Assessing Ground Management Practices

The effect of winter ground management practices on A. transitella survival was examined in a pistachio orchard at the University of California’s Kearney Agricultural Research and Extension Center (UC-KARE) in Parlier, California. There were 4 (2002 and 2003) or 5 (2004) field treatments: mummies were placed (i) on the berm with no vegetation, or in the row middle on (ii) previously mowed winter vegetation, (iii) winter vegetation that was then mowed (eg potential mortality from the mechanical process of mowing), (iv) winter vegetation that was then disced, and (v) winter vegetation that was then rototilled. There was a laboratory-held control that had nearly 100% emergence. Field treatments were set up using a randomized complete block design with 6 replicates per treatment. To initiate each annual trial, small (1 m2) treatment plots were established, initially demarcated using 1 m2 PVC pipe outlines and then marked at the corners with small posts that allowed a tractor to travel over the plot. Infested pistachio mummies were then collected from commercial orchards and used to seed the plots. A screen (0.15 × 1 × 1 m wooden frame, covered on top with window screening) was placed over each plot to trap the adults that emerged. Each plot was checked twice weekly and all adult A. transitella (typically beginning in late March and ending in late April) were collected and counted. The plots were searched, and all nuts were taken to the laboratory, including nuts buried down to 5 cm in the disced treatment, and dissected to record the presence of any remaining A. transitella, scored as live or dead.

In 2002, about 7,000 pistachio mummies were collected on February 19 at an orchard near Kettleman City, (Kings Co., California). A subsample of 200 nuts was dissected and found to be 33.5% infested with A. transitella, comprised of 77.4% larvae and 22.6% pupae. On February 21, these nuts were randomly divided into 34 bags of 200 each and assigned to one of the treatments described above and spread evenly inside each plot. Treatments were applied with the control plots left undisturbed (berm or row middle) or disced or mowed using a wheel disc or flail mower (row middle only). This was repeated in 2003 and 2004. In 2003, about 2,500 pistachio mummies were collected between January 21 to February 5 from a commercial pistachio orchard near Madera, (Madera Co., California); a subsample of 100 nuts was 13.0% infested. On February 25, these nuts were randomly divided into 24 bags containing 100 nuts each and assigned to 4 treatments (no laboratory held control). In 2004, about 3,000 pistachio mummies were collected from January 29 to February 4, from a commercial pistachio orchard near Madera, CA; a subsample of 150 nuts was 71.5% infested. On February 5, the remaining nuts were randomly divided into 30 bags of 95 nuts each and assigned to the treatments as previously described. All adult A. transitella were collected and recorded until emergence ceased.

Relationship Between Larval Survival and Nut Status

The effect of nut condition on the survival of neonate A. transitella was assessed in the laboratory using field-collected mummies and new crop nuts at 3 points in time. The first collection was on 30 April 2009 (open and closed mummies), the second on July 7 (open and closed new crop pistachios, closed but damaged new crop pistachios, and mummies) and the third on August 19 (open and closed new crop pistachios, closed but damaged new crop pistachios, and mummies). The new crop pistachios used were non-infested and non-damaged nuts that were either closed, or naturally “early” split nuts (those where the hull splits open prematurely, weeks before harvest), or closed non-infested and non-damaged nuts that were mechanically damaged to mimic feeding by stink bugs (Stahl et al. 2021) and leaffooted bugs (Daane et al. 2019), which could provide an entrance site for A. transitella larvae. The nuts were placed individually in a 30-ml diet cup with 12 replicates for each treatment (open and closed). Three neonate larvae were collected from a laboratory colony within 24 h of egg hatch and placed in each diet cup. Insects were held at 25 ± 1 °C, 60 ± 10% RH and a 16:8 photoperiod. After 2 wk, the nuts were examined for A. transitella survival as well as visible signs of feeding damage (eg feeding on the kernel) or feeding attempts (eg chewing on the nut epicarp). As a control for larval survival only, survival on treatment nuts was compared with survival on an artificial diet of red flakey wheat bran, honey, glycerin, water, Vanderzant modification vitamin mixture, and brewer’s yeast (Finney and Brinkman 1967).

A companion field trial was conducted at UC-KARE in 2009 using organdy sleeve cages. Three treatments (new crop nuts, open mummies, and closed shell mummies) were assessed on April 30, July 7, and August 20. Nuts in natural clusters were collected from trees, each cluster was trimmed to 3 nuts per cluster, and then hung by a small wire on a pistachio branch (without any other nuts present) to simulate mummies remaining in the tree. Each nut group was caged by an organdy sleeve to prevent other insects from entering, and 10 A. transitella neonate larvae were added directly onto the nuts in each cage. There were 12 replicates per treatment, set in a randomized block design using individual trees as a block. The cages were opened every 1 to 2 wk and the nuts were checked for feeding damage. After 6 to 8 wk, depending on accumulated degree days, the nuts were collected and dissected in the laboratory to determine A. transitella survival.

Oviposition at 3 Canopy Heights

Amyelois transitella oviposition preference at 3 heights in mature pistachio trees located near Madera (Madera Co., California) was tested from 19 June 2009 to 24 June 2009. The heights were ground (0.025 m above the ground), medium canopy (2.5 m above the ground), and high canopy (4.25 m above the ground). This pistachio orchard was located adjacent to a fig orchard that had a high incidence of A. transitella. Egg traps were filled with fresh bran diet and placed into the field to monitor oviposition. Ground traps were hung from a wire pushed into the ground and covered at its base with Tanglefoot (Grand Rapids, Michigan) to prevent ants from gaining access to the trap and eating the deposited eggs. Medium and high canopy traps were hung on the pistachio branch. Traps were placed in 2 rows of trees with a trap located every fifth tree, and with the selected trees staggered between the rows to increase the distance between the traps. There were 4 treatments: individual trees with either ground, medium canopy or high canopy trap placement, and trees with traps placed at all 3 heights. There were 12 replicates per treatment set in a randomized block design (48 trees, 84 traps).

Statistics

Results are presented as sample means ± SE. Analyses were performed using Systat Software Inc. (version 13, San Jose, California, United States). For the orchard survey study, the relationship between the number of mummies (canopy or ground) and the average number of A. transitella eggs per trap during the first flight was compared using linear regression; differences in mummy count among the 6 sites sampled were compared using the general linear model (GLM) function in Systat. When more than 2 treatments were tested, Tukey Pairwise comparison was used to separate means. Similarly, treatment differences in the berm management, ground management, and larval survival and oviposition height studies were compared using GLM, with treatments means separated using Tukey Pairwise comparison. Data were transformed to normalize the variance by using comparable units (eg eggs per trap per week) and log[x + 1] or square root(x + 0.5)], as needed.

Results

Orchard Surveys

Seasonal egg trap counts showed a typical pattern with peak egg deposition during the first flight and declining thereafter (Fig. 1), thought to be due to competition between the bait in egg traps and the large number of new crop nuts that become susceptible to A. transitella after hull split (Higbee and Burks 2011, Haviland et al. 2020a, 2020b). Across all orchards and years, 3,044 (range 320 to 760/year) clusters and 5,347 mummy nuts (range 654 to 1,295/year) were counted in the tree canopy post-sanitation (1.80 ± 0.13 mummies per cluster). Post-sanitation mummy counts were relatively similar from 2000 to 2003 at 2.10 ± 0.15, 1.40 ± 0.16, 1.69 ± 0.11, and 2.18 ± 0.28 mummies per tree, respectively, and higher in 2004 at 4.32 ± 0.40 mummies per tree. In each year, there was a greater difference among orchards with a 20.6-fold range in 2000 (0.25 to 5.15 mummies per tree; F = 39.67, df = 5, 594, P < 0.001), a 36.5-fold range in 2001 (0.06 to 2.19; F = 10.092, df = 5, 1194, P < 0.001), a 15.6 fold-range in 2002 (0.17 to 2.66; F = 34.883, df = 5, 594, P < 0.001), 99-fold range in 2003 (0.06 to 5.94; F = 18.135, df = 5, 294, P < 0.001), and a 9.6-fold range in 2004 (1.08 to 10.32; F = 19.486, df = 5, 294, P < 0.001). In 2001, canopy counts were taken before and twice after sanitation practices and recorded 6,279 clusters and 20,970 mummies over the 3 sample dates (2.07 ± 0.05 nuts per cluster) and showed a decrease from 48.4 ± 3.1 mummies per tree in January before sanitation practices were implemented to 1.40 ± 0.16 and 0.48 ± 0.05 mummies per tree in the post-sanitation samples in March and April, reductions of 97.1% and 99.0%, respectively. Using each orchard as a replicate, there was a significantly positive relationship between the number of overwintered mummies per tree and eggs per trap per week during the first peak flight in 2000 (Fig. 2A), 2002 (Fig. 2C) and 2003 (Fig. 2D), and a significant negative association in 2001 (Fig. 2B); there was no relationship in 2004 (y = 14.00 + 3.07x, F1,5 = 3.576, P = 0.131; R2 = 0.472). When the data were pooled across all years, there was no relationship between overwintered mummies per tree and eggs per trap per week during the first peak flight (y = 85.75–4.25x, F1,29 = 0.591, P = 0.448; R2 = 0.021).

To determine overwintered mummy infestation levels in 2000, 1,994 mummies were collected and dissected (633 and 1,361 from the canopy and ground, respectively), from which 82 A. transitella were recovered with 6.8% and 2.9% infestation of the canopy and ground nuts, respectively. Infestation level varied among sampled orchards, averaging 4.64 ± 1.66% (range: 0.4% to 10.2%). Of the total mummies collected, 67.3% were categorized as blank (aborted or empty) and these contained no A. transitella, therefore when the available nuts are considered, 12.6% were infested across all sites. A larger collection was made in February, March and April of 2001, with 45,145 mummies collected and dissected (1,890 and 43,255 from the canopy and ground, respectively). There were 86 live A. transitella recovered from the canopy (4.5% nut infestation, range 3.3% to 13.5%) and when available nuts were considered the infestation level was 17.2 ± 2.9%. There were 1,996 A. transitella recovered from the ground nuts (3.0% nut infestation, range 1.8% to 4.4%). There were 64.7 ± 2.6 mummies within ground samples each using a 1 m2 frame, which varied among orchards (F = 17.488, df = 5, 594, P < 0.001), but all orchards had a considerable number of ground mummies (range 40.4 to 90.6 per 1 m2 sample) compared with tree mummies. In 2002, 12,356 mummies were collected from the ground samples (no canopy mummies were collected), resulting in 21.4 ± 1.3 mummies per m2 per sample. There was variation among orchards (F = 8.454, df = 5, 572, P < 0.001), but all orchards had a considerable number of ground (range 11.2 to 35.6 per m2) compared with tree mummies. Mummies were not dissected in 2003 and 2004.

In years with ground samples using the 1 m2 frame (2001 to 2004), intact mummies declined from March to August (Fig. 3). There was no relationship between overwintered mummies per m2 per orchard and eggs per trap per week during the first peak flight for individual years (2001: P = 0.590, R2 = 0.078; 2002: P = 0.250, R2 = 0.31; 2003: P = 0.202, R2 = 0.37; and 2004: P = 0.875, R2 = 0.007) or combining all years (Fig. 4). From dissected ground samples (2000 to 2002), there was a dramatic decline (99%) in ground nut infestation after the February to March collections, from 8.2 larvae per m2 (0.078 ± 0.014 larvae per nut) to <0.9 larvae per m2 thereafter (April to August).

Assessing Berm Management of Nuts

Berm management practices affected the number of viable nuts on the orchard floor. A total of 46,135 nuts were counted, and 27,244 collected nuts were dissected over the 4 sample dates. When the mummies were pooled there were 56.9 ± 2.6 mummies per m2, with a 4.4-fold range among the 3 orchards from 21.0 ± 1.7 to 92.5 ± 5.2 mummies per m2. Before the treatments were applied there were similar number of nuts on the berm across all treatment plots in February (F = 0.473; df = 2, 69; P = 0.625; Fig. 5) while after treatment there were fewer nuts in the blown and blown-raked treatments than the control (F = 21.079; df = 2, 804; P < 0.001), with an impact of sample date (F = 28.919, df = 1, 805, P < 0.001), but no treatment × sample date interaction. When the post treatment (March, May, and July) samples were assessed there were 3.1-fold more nuts on the control berm (100.1 ± 5.4 a) than the blown berm (32.3 ± 2.7 b), and 6.6-fold fewer nuts on the blown-raked treatment compared with the control (15.2 ± 1.2 c) (F = 154.5; df = 2, 735; P < 0.001).

Overwintering mummies per square meter ground sample, averaged across 3 pistachio orchards (mean ± SE) before (February) and after (March, May, and July) nuts were blown or blown and raked off the berm and into the row middle.

Sanitation treatments did not affect pistachio nut conditions in terms of susceptibility to A. transitella damage. In February, there were similar percentages of susceptible nuts (open with an intact kernel) in the blown, blown-raked, and control treatments before treatment application (11.7 ± 2.3%, 10.2 ± 1.3%, and 11.2 ± 1.8%, respectively) and after treatment application the percentage of susceptible nuts was still similar (13.5 ± 2.0%, 12.1 ± 1.5%, and 10.7 ± 0.8%, respectively). Overall, the changes in nut condition from the winter to spring were an increase in the open-empty nut category and a decrease in the closed-empty and closed-with a kernel category. Similarly, infestation with A. transitella was not affected by the blown, blown-raked, and control treatments in February (6.1 ± 1.1%, 10.0 ± 1.4%, and 7.8 ± 1.7%, respectively). There was no pre-treatment (February) difference for total A. transitella nut infestation in the blown, blown-raked, and control treatments (10.3 ± 4.7%, 7.5 ± 1.2%, and 9.3 ± 2.5%, respectively) but there was a March to July effect (F = 6.095, df = 2, 389, P = 0.002), with fewer infested nuts in the blown-raked (0.84 ± 0.15% nuts per m2) than the control (4.1 ± 0.9% nuts per m2) but no difference in the blown treatment (2.4 ± 0.6% nuts per m2).

Assessing Ground Management Practices

In the 2002 trial, the laboratory-held control had nearly 100% survival with 65.7 ± 7.7 moths emerging per m2, which was higher than all field treatments (F = 52.113; df = 4, 23; P < 0.001). Excluding the control, adult emergence was higher in the berm treatment than in the middle row treatments (F = 12.013; df = 4, 20; P < 0.001; Fig. 6A). Year 2003 followed this same pattern with more adults recovered from the berm treatment than the mowed grass or rototilled treatment (F = 4.761; df = 3, 20; P = 0.012; Fig. 6B). In 2004, the mowed, disced and rototilled treatments had lower emergence than the grass treatment, and emergence from the disced and rototilled plots was lower than the berm (F = 9.852; df = 4, 25; P < 0.001; Fig. 6C).

Relationship Between Larval Survival and Nut Status

The treatments affected the percentage survivorship and feeding damage of A. transitella neonate larvae. In the first trial period, there was 100% survival in the control (artificial diet) but poor survival on open and closed mummies, as well as the closed new crop nuts collected on April 30 and July 7 (F = 312.06, df = 4,48, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7A). Feeding damage to or feeding attempts on the treatment nuts, as indicated by a larva’s successful entrance into the endocarp or chewing on the nut epicarp, indicated attempted feeding on open mummies and new nuts, but not closed shell mummies (F = 4.409, df = 2,57, P = 0.017). In the second trial period (23 July), there was a larger separation of treatments, with neonate survival on split nuts (eg open new crop nuts) higher than survival on both open or closed mummies and closed new crop nuts (F = 23.346, df = 5,66, P < 0.001; Fig. 7B). There was some survival on damaged nuts, suggesting that weakened areas on the nuts, or small cracks on the nut in or around the damaged area may have provided entrance sites for the neonates. Feeding attempts on split nuts were higher than open or closed mummies or damaged new nuts (F = 8.359, df = 4,52, P < 0.001). In the third trial (19 August), split nuts provided a superior host compared with open mummies or closed shell new crop nuts (F = 38.922, df = 5,66, P < 0.001; Fig. 7C). As in the earlier trial, damaged nuts provided a potential entry site, with survival on damaged nuts similar to that on open mummies. Damaged nuts, either through insect feeding or abrasions, provided entrance points for neonates. Feeding damage and attempts were greater on split nuts than closed mummies and closed new nuts, and higher on open mummies than closed (F = 6.041, df = 4,66, P < 0.001; Fig. 7C).

Results from the later field cage trial were similar, although the survival of the neonate larvae was low. In the first trial (29 April), none of the 120 first instar A. transitella survived on the new crop closed shell nuts. In the second trial (6 July), there was no survival on new crop closed nuts or closed mummies, and survival on open shell newly collected mummies was 2.5 ± 1.8%. This pattern was repeated in the third trial (19 August) with 5.0 ± 1.9% survival on open shell mummies and no survival on closed mummies or new crop closed shell nuts (F = 6.600, df = 2, 33, P = 0.004).

Oviposition at 3 Canopy Heights

Amyelois transitella eggs were laid at the ground, middle-canopy, and high-canopy positions. There was no difference in egg deposition when all 3 treatments were grouped on one tree or hung on individual trees. However, when these data were combined, there were significant differences in oviposition by height, with more eggs deposited at the medium (33.3 ± 5.8) and the high canopy (32.3 ± 4.0) than on the ground (20.1 ± 6.3 eggs per trap per week) (F = 6.243; df = 2,67; P = 0.003).

Discussion

Pistachio mummy sanitation recommendations are currently based on A. transitella biology combined with previous research in almonds that positively correlated increased population levels with crop damage. In almonds, winter sanitation has long been recognized as an important cultural practice to reduce overwintering A. transitella infestation levels (eg Caltagirone et al. 1964, Engle and Barnes 1983, Zalom et al. 1984, Higbee and Siegel 2009). In this study for pistachio, we demonstrated a positive relationship between first-generation egg deposition (as an indication of pest population density) and overwintered canopy mummies in 3 of 5 yr. The year 2001 was an outlier because of the negative association (Fig. 2B), and it had the lowest overall mummies per tree and some of the highest first flight egg depositions. This negative correlation might be because egg traps were more effective when competing with fewer mummies per tree. When the data for the 5 survey years were pooled there was no clear relationship between overwintered mummies and the subsequent A. transitella density. Similarly, we found no relationship between first generation egg deposition and the number of ground mummies (Fig. 4). A possible source of variability was our use of egg traps to estimate A. transitella population density. Although egg traps were the standard tool for measuring abundance during the time frame of this study, compared to other monitoring methods such as pheromone, egg traps capture more efficiently in the spring (the time of our comparison) and lose efficacy when they compete with new crop nuts when they become available (Higbee et al. 2014, Wilson et al. 2020). A previous study conducted by Higbee and Burks (2011) suggested that more egg traps per management block are necessary to infer differences at individual monitoring intervals, but that egg trap variability is reduced when data are pooled over weeks, as was done in this study.

In almonds, general winter sanitation guidelines are <2 mummies per tree (Haviland et al. 2020b), with a more stringent threshold of 0.2 mummies per tree in the southern San Joaquin Valley (Higbee and Siegel 2009). In this pistachio study, mummies per tree (post sanitation) ranged from 0.25 to 10.34 in the 6 orchards over the 5 yr survey, providing the presumptive variation in mummy density as compared to previous almond studies. We elected to use first flight egg trap densities as a measure of overwintering mummy impact on that year’s A. transitella population density and pest pressure rather than harvest damage assessment, as the growers’ spring and summer pesticide treatments would have altered this relationship. Still, the Higbee and Siegel (2009) multi-year data set in almonds, which also used sanitized fields, was larger with >50 orchards divided into 65-ha blocks, which were then subdivided into 16-ha plots and quartered into 4-ha sample units that resulted in >2,500,000 collected and dissected almond nuts. We suspect also that more pest pressure might have been recorded had we sampled orchards that were not sanitized and had counts of >100 overwintered mummies per tree, but this would have resulted in a survey of what was considered to be orchards with poor management practices.

The high proportion of blank and closed shell pistachio mummies collected may have interfered with our ability to demonstrate a clear positive relationship between the number of canopy mummies and moth density. Adults successfully oviposit on mummies that have an open suture and contain a kernel (filled nut), while nuts that are closed (closed shell pistachio) or lack a kernel (blank pistachio) cannot support A. transitella (Wilson et al. 2020). Therefore, not only must the number of overwintered nuts in the canopy be determined but also the percentage of those nuts that are blank or closed must also be known, and in this study, we noted a seasonal change in their prevalence. Another aspect that could be studied is the percentage of blanks left in the canopy after each shake, as it is likely that mummies left in the tree will be blanks, which have less weight and are then less likely to be shaken from the tree. Additional shakes might then have a decreasing benefit as a greater percentage of the canopy mummies might be blanks, but the cost of each post-harvest shake remains the same in terms of labor and equipment depreciation. This research suggests that pistachio growers should still embrace sanitation, adopted from the almond industry, but the “two mummies per tree” threshold may not be economically viable, especially when orchard managers must decide if their sanitation budget might be better utilized with one winter shake (to get the split nuts out of the tree and onto the ground where they can be destroyed) combined with destruction of the mummies on the ground that are more likely to contain kernels and, therefore, host for A. transitella.

Infestation rates were higher in canopy mummies (6.8% and 4.5% in 2000 and 2001, respectively) than in ground mummies (2.9% and 3.0% in 2000 and 2001, respectively), but since there were >100- fold more mummies on the ground than in the tree canopy, ground mummies represent a larger potential A. transitella population. Siegel et al. (2008) collected large numbers of mummies from the tree and berm and reported a sharp decline of infestation from December to February both in the canopy and on the berm, although infestation in February at both locations was 1.5% to 1.6%. During this same period, we report a consistent decline of infested nuts on the berm, but less during this same period on canopy nuts (3.7 to 1.6%). This suggests that while canopy mummies might be a better food source for A. transitella, ground mummies may be as or more important than canopy mummies in terms of numbers of potential host plant material before the new crop is susceptible. Similarly, we showed a consistent decline in the number of available ground mummies (Fig. 4), but in the critical May to June period, there were still >20 mummies per m2 under the tree canopy, indicating that ground mummies can serve as a source of A. transitella throughout the season.

We hypothesized that A. transitella in mummies on the raised berm have a higher survival than those in the row middles, where mummies are more easily destroyed. We then demonstrated that blowing or blowing-and-raking nuts from the berm reduced the availability of susceptible nuts (Fig. 5) and that more A. transitella adults emerged from mummies on the berm or row middle with vegetation than from the row middle that was mowed, disced, or rototilled (Fig. 6), as first reported by Siegel et al. (2008). However, because the seeded nuts were placed in each treatment in February, this study focused more on the effect of the mechanical aspects of mowing, discing, or rototilling rather than the fall to spring impact of moving mummies into the row middle where there is more moisture and winter degradation of the mummies. Wilson et al. (2022) showed that fall-spring cover crop mixtures of a “pollinator mix” and a “soil builder mix” resulted in fewer adult A. transitella emerging from almond mummies in the spring, suggesting increased overwintering mortality, and that spring egg deposition was reduced on mummies in the cover crops. Suggested changes to ground abiotic and physical conditions during the winter were not tested in our relatively short February to April studies.

The last trials focused on mummies that served as an oviposition and food source for the first and second generations during the critical spring and summer periods, and not as a source of overwintered moths. We showed that open mummies, split nuts of the new crop and damaged nuts of the new crop are food sources for A. transitella, whereas closed mummies and new crop nuts were not acceptable hosts. We also demonstrated that A. transitella oviposits at ground level, although mid- and high-canopy levels were more preferred. Researchers have long known that A. transitella needs a split or damaged nut for the larvae to enter and feed on the nut meat or kernel of almonds and walnuts (Wade 1961, Curtis and Barnes 1977, Soderstrom 1977). There is a period of time when mummies are the sole food source in nut crops, but this can vary; for example, different almond varieties have different phenological maturation periods and, thus, timing of hull split that can influence moth infestation (Wilson et al. 2020). Mummies certainly present a poorer food source than new crop nuts (Siegel et al. 2010), but there is also variability in the new crop seasonal availability and quality as a food source. For example, larvae developed faster on almond nuts from “Nonpareil” than “Carmel” variety (Siegel et al. 2010) and in pistachio both larval development and survival were dependent on pistachio nut stage, age, variety, and quality (Siegel and Kuenen 2011). We suggest that while susceptible new crop pistachio nuts and mummies in the canopy may be preferred, susceptible ground mummies present a poorer but acceptable food source and, therefore, must be considered as a key element of orchard sanitation. This demonstration that A. transitella oviposits at ground level in spring is important for outreach, since there is still a common perception to the contrary based on observations and studies of almonds at harvest (eg Curtis and Barnes 1977).

In almond orchards, sanitation of mummy nuts in the canopy has been an effective tool for A. transitella control (Zalom et al. 1984); however, in pistachio, we were unable to show a clear association between the number of canopy or ground mummies and first-generation A. transitella population density. Our results suggest that in pistachio, singularly removing mummies from the canopy may be an incomplete sanitation program because there is a considerable food source remaining in the form of ground mummy nuts that must be removed from the berm to the row middles to be destroyed. This is complicated further by the large number of pistachio mummies that are closed or blank, making simple counts of canopy or ground mummies difficult to correlate with the actual amount of food resource. Moreover, pistachios mummies are more difficult to destroy than almonds by tillage and more difficult to simply blow off the berms into the row middles for discing (Siegel et al. 2004, 2008). Researchers are currently investigating novel mechanisms to destroy ground mummies, such as grazing livestock (Wilson et al. unpublished data), and A. transitella survival in ground mummies should be lower than in the tree canopy because of predation by ants and other soil invertebrates. Prior to this work, Siegel et al. (2008) also stressed the importance of ground mummies, stating that it is “more economical to concentrate on removing or destroying mummies on the ground than to shake them from pistachio trees.” In this study, we demonstrated that the relatively simple ground management scheme of blowing pistachios from the berm into the mid-row and subsequently mowing the cover crop consistently reduced the standing population of A. transitella. Additional strategies such as raking the berm and pushing the nuts into the ground further reduce the standing population, as measured by oviposition. Ground management is now critical because of the dramatic expansion of pistachio plantings in California, from 37,651 bearing ha in 2004 to 197,785 bearing ha in 2025, a 5.3-fold increase (ACP 2025) combined with a 2.4-fold increase in bearing almonds (NASS 2025), and the subsequent movement between these 2 crops. The end result is monocultures extending dozens of kilometers. Simultaneously, there is increased regulatory and consumer demand to eliminate not only the broad-spectrum insecticides that are a mainstay in tree nut production but the use of all synthetic insecticides as well. The management challenge is to increase the impact of cultural control, and the strategies evaluated in this paper can serve as the foundation for current and future tree nut pest management.
