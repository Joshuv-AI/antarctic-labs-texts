Artificial intelligence-based prediction of claudin 18.2 expression and immune phenotype from routine histology to guide treatment decisions in patients with gastric cancer
Authors: H-D Kim, S Shin, W Hwang, J Shin, T Lee, J Hyung, J Park, S Pereira, C-Y Ock, A Puccini, G Carloni, YS Park, M-H Ryu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden, ranking as the fifth most commonly diagnosed malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality.1 First-line systemic therapy for advanced gastric cancer has evolved with the integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and targeted agents into chemotherapy regimens. In human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) positive disease, ICI plus chemotherapy has demonstrated survival benefits over chemotherapy alone,2, 3, 4, 5 while in HER2-positive and PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥1 tumors, the addition of pembrolizumab to trastuzumab-based chemotherapy further conferred overall survival (OS).6

Claudin 18.2 (CLDN18.2) has recently emerged as a promising therapeutic target. CLDN18.2 is a gastric mucosa-restricted tight-junction protein that becomes exposed on the tumor cell surface during malignant transformation and is enriched in diffuse-type and signet-ring cell gastric carcinomas.7 Two phase 3 trials showed that adding zolbetuximab, a monoclonal antibody against CLDN18.2, to chemotherapy improved outcomes in patients with CLDN18.2-positive gastric cancer.8,9 Consequently, zolbetuximab-based chemotherapy has emerged as a potential treatment option for this subgroup.

As CLDN18.2 testing is incorporated into routine clinical practice, the burden of biomarker assessment has increased substantially for pathologists and oncologists,10 including HER2, PD-L1 CPS, or tumor area positivity, mismatch repair (MMR)/microsatellite instability (MSI) status, and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) positivity.11, 12, 13 Moreover, ongoing clinical development of fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 isoform b (FGFR2b)-targeted therapies, such as bemarituzumab, has highlighted FGFR2b as a potential biomarker under investigation, further increasing biomarker testing complexity. The growing reliance on next-generation sequencing (NGS) further compounds these demands.12 In gastric cancer, where diagnostic material is frequently limited to endoscopic biopsy specimens, these requirements pose practical challenges related to tissue availability, costs, and turnaround times, underscoring the need for more efficient strategies to guide biomarker testing.

The expanding therapeutic landscape has complicated first-line treatment selections, particularly in patients with overlapping biomarkers relevant to both ICI- and CLDN18.2-targeted strategies.10 Although mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR)/MSI-high status is a well-established predictor of ICI response, other biomarkers such as PD-L1 and EBV also provide clinically relevant information, but their predictive value may be influenced by factors such as intratumoral heterogeneity, dynamic expression, and assay variability.11, 12, 13, 14 Additional tools are therefore needed to refine first-line treatment selection between ICI-based chemotherapy and CLDN18.2-targeted regimens.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches offer a potential solution to these challenges. By leveraging routinely acquired hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides without requiring additional tissue, AI models provide cost-efficient and standardized methods for patient stratification. AI-powered spatial analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and immune phenotypes (IPs) has been shown to predict outcomes after ICI therapy across multiple cancer types.15, 16, 17 In particular, an inflamed IP (IIP) identified by AI has been consistently associated with favorable responses to ICIs,16 highlighting its potential as a clinically actionable biomarker. However, whether AI can infer CLDN18.2 expression from routine H&E slides and integrate immune phenotyping to guide first-line treatment decisions remains unknown.

Therefore, we aimed to develop and validate an AI-based model to predict CLDN18.2 expression, along with tumor immune phenotype, from H&E-stained slides, with the goal of supporting efficient biomarker prioritization and informing first-line treatment decisions in gastric cancer.

Materials and methods

Study aim, design, and setting

This retrospective study aimed to develop and validate an AI-based model to predict CLDN18.2 expression from routine H&E-stained whole-slide images (WSIs) of gastric cancer and to examine the association between AI-predicted claudin 18.2 status, immune phenotype, and survival outcomes under different first-line treatment regimens. The study included three independent cohorts of patients with gastric cancer: a development cohort, an internal validation cohort consisting of patients treated with first-line nivolumab plus chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone, and an external validation cohort comprising patients from diverse ethnic backgrounds. The study was conducted using clinicopathologic and digital histology data obtained from a tertiary referral center [Asan Medical Center (AMC), Seoul, Republic of Korea] and an independent external cohort (Cureline, South San Francisco, CA).

Study population

This retrospective study included 1098 patients with gastric cancer from three independent cohorts: a development cohort (n = 622), an internal validation cohort (n = 378), and an external validation cohort (n = 98; Figure 1A).

The development cohort comprised patients with stage I-IV gastric cancer treated at AMC, derived from previously published retrospective studies.18,19 These included 299 patients with stage I-III resectable disease who underwent curative surgery between March 2018 and February 2019, 166 patients with metastatic disease treated with systemic chemotherapy from January 2012 to December 2022, and 162 additional patients treated at AMC from March 2024 to February 2025. Eligible cases required the availability of pretreatment H&E-stained slides and CLDN18.2 IHC. All specimens were obtained before systemic therapy and included biopsy or resection samples. After exclusions, 622 patients were included and randomly split into 497 and 125 patients for model training and model tuning, respectively, to preserve class balance.

The internal validation cohort comprised patients with advanced gastric cancer treated with first-line chemotherapy with or without nivolumab at AMC, derived from a previously reported retrospective study.20 Of 511 patients, 378 patients remained after applying identical exclusion criteria (196 treated with first-line nivolumab plus chemotherapy and 182 with chemotherapy alone). This cohort was independent of the development cohort, with no overlap.

The external validation cohort included 98 tumor samples collected between 2012 and 2018 from patients of diverse ethnic backgrounds, sourced independently from Cureline (Figure 1). Detailed clinical and demographic information was limited for this cohort; therefore, it was used exclusively for evaluating model generalizability.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of AMC (IRB No. 2023-1353) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because deidentified archival specimens were used. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, interpretation, or dissemination of this study.

Claudin 18.2 expression via immunohistochemistry

CLDN18.2 expression was evaluated by board-certified gastrointestinal pathologists using IHC on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections. Positivity was defined as moderate-to-strong linear membranous staining in ≥75% of tumor cells, consistent with the criteria used in phase 3 zolbetuximab trials.8,9 CLDN18.2 expression by IHC served as the primary outcome for model development and validation. All CLDN18.2 assessments across cohorts were centralized and performed by the same pathologists, who were blinded to all AI model outputs, to ensure consistency. Additional technical details are provided in Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Development of the CLDN18.2 prediction model

We developed the CLDN18.2 prediction model, Lunit SCOPE Genotype Predictor (SCOPE GP; Lunit, Seoul, Republic of Korea), employing a multiple-instance learning (MIL) framework—a weakly supervised approach suitable for WSI analysis using slide-level labels. Pretreatment H&E-stained WSIs were used as the sole model input and no clinical or treatment variables were incorporated.

The overall framework integrates WSI, preprocessing, deep feature extraction, and a robust ensemble of MIL classifiers to generate slide-level predictions (Figure 1B and C). WSIs were tessellated into patches and patches from the cancer area (CA) and cancer stroma (CS) were selected using automated tissue segmentation provided via Lunit SCOPE immuno-oncology (IO).15, 16, 17 High-dimensional features were extracted from these patches using a Vision Transformer (ViT)-based foundation model.21 These features were subsequently aggregated using multiple distinct MIL classifiers, and their predictions were integrated through a weighted ensemble optimized on the tuning cohort. The final ensemble prediction for each WSI was dichotomized into CLDN18.2-positive or -negative using a cohort-specific Youden index cut-off.

Detailed preprocessing steps, feature extraction, and model architecture are provided in the Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Immune phenotyping

Immune phenotyping was conducted using the Lunit SCOPE IO, a previously developed and validated model for cell detection and tissue segmentation on H&E-stained WSIs.15, 16, 17 The model was applied without retraining in this study. TIL densities were quantified within CA (intratumoral) and CS (stromal). Based on predefined TIL density thresholds, tumors were classified as IIP or non-IIP (Figure 1D). Further details on immune phenotyping are provided in the Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Evaluation of biomarkers and treatment

PD-L1, HER2, and MMR/MSI status, as well as EBV positivity, were assessed using standard clinical IHC and in situ hybridization protocols, as previously described.18 Assay procedures are summarized in Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Patients in the internal validation cohort received first-line nivolumab plus chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone, according to institutional practice. Tumor response was assessed using contrast-enhanced computed tomography and the RECIST version 1.1 at regular intervals. Additional treatment details are summarized in Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

CLDN18.2 prediction model interpretability

Model interpretability was assessed using attention maps generated from the MIL classifier, highlighting regions contributing most to the CLDN18.2 prediction. Details of attention score extraction, visualization, and qualitative review procedures are described in Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Statistical analyses

Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), with classification metrics summarized using the optimal cut-off determined by the Youden index separately within each cohort. Progression-free survival (PFS) and OS were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests, and hazard ratios were estimated with Cox proportional hazards models. Details of statistical procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Results

Patient characteristics

We analyzed three independent cohorts comprising 1098 patients with gastric cancer (Figure 1A; Supplementary Tables S1-S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021). In the development cohort (n = 622), the median age was 62 years, and stages I-IV disease accounted for 15.1%, 22.1%, 24.0%, and 38.8% of cases, respectively. Most specimens (98.7%) were primary gastric resections or biopsies, and CLDN18.2 positivity was 42.9%.

In the internal validation cohort (n = 378) of patients treated with first-line nivolumab plus chemotherapy (n = 196) or chemotherapy alone (n = 182), the median age was 63 years and most had metastatic disease at treatment initiation (78.6%). CLDN18.2 positivity was 36.8%.

In the external validation cohort (n = 98), the median age was 63.5 years and stage I-IV disease accounted for 28.6%, 46.9%, 18.4%, and 6.1% of cases, respectively. The majority of patients were Caucasian (95.9%), based on available demographic data. CLDN18.2 positivity was 25.5%.

Performance of the AI model for CLDN18.2 prediction

In the tuning cohort (n = 125), used for model selection and ensemble optimization, the model achieved an AUROC of 0.752 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.665-0.831], with sensitivity 0.778 (0.644-0.880) and specificity 0.676 (0.555-0.782) at the cohort-specific Youden index cut-off (Figure 2A). Exploratory stage-stratified analysis within the tuning set (n = 125) indicated comparable discriminatory performance across stages, with an AUC of 0.765 (0.652-0.867) in patients with stage I-III gastric cancer (n = 73) and 0.718 (0.573-0.855) in patients with stage IV gastric cancer (n = 51).

(A) Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and corresponding performance matrix for the CLDN18.2 prediction model in the tuning cohort (N = 125), including the area under the curve (AUC; area under the ROC curve [AUROC]), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), each reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). (B) ROC curves and performance metrics for the internal (N = 378) and external (N = 98) validation cohorts.

In the internal validation cohort (n = 378), AUROC was 0.752 (0.702-0.799) with sensitivity 0.763 (0.683-0.831) and specificity 0.661 (0.597-0.721). In the external validation cohort (n = 98), the performance remained consistent, with AUROC 0.856 (0.775-0.926), sensitivity 0.840 (0.639-0.955), and specificity 0.753 (0.639-0.847) (Figure 2B). The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were 0.567 (0.493-0.639) and 0.827 (0.766-0.878), respectively, internally, and 0.538 (0.372-0.699) and 0.932 (0.835-0.981), respectively, externally. The model exhibited consistent discriminatory performance across independent cohorts, with AUROC values of 0.752 in the development and internal validation sets and 0.856 in the external validation cohort, indicating its reproducibility and potential clinical utility.

Pathologist-evaluated proportions of tumor cells exhibiting moderate-to-strong membranous CLDN18.2 staining were available for 51.9% of internal cases (n = 196) and all external cases (n = 98). The proportion of positive tumor cells correlated significantly with the AI prediction score, with Spearman coefficients of 0.59 (P < 0.001) and 0.48 (P < 0.001), respectively.

Clinical outcomes based on AI-based immune phenotyping

In the internal validation cohort, 142 patients (37.6%) had IIP and 236 (62.4%) had non-IIP. PD-L1 CPS ≥1 was similarly distributed between IIP and non-IIP (47.5% versus 45.9%, P = 0.842), whereas CPS ≥5 was more frequent in IIP tumors (35.5% versus 25.1%, P = 0.044). Nivolumab plus chemotherapy was associated with improved PFS and OS compared with chemotherapy alone overall (Supplementary Figure S1A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021), with the survival benefit primarily observed in patients with IIP but not in those with non-IIP (Supplementary Figure S1B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021). Similar trends were observed across PD-L1 expression subgroups (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Clinical outcomes based on AI-based immune phenotyping and CLDN18.2 prediction

Patients were further stratified based on AI-predicted CLDN18.2 expression and immune phenotype. Among these groups, 28.0% (n = 106) were classified as CLDN18.2-positive/IIP, 21.4% (n = 81) as CLDN18.2-positive/non-IIP, 9.5% (n = 36) as CLDN18.2-negative/IIP, and 41.0% (n = 155) as CLDN18.2-negative/non-IIP (Figure 3A).

Among patients predicted to have CLDN18.2-positive/IIP tumors, nivolumab plus chemotherapy demonstrated a trend toward improved outcomes [PFS: hazard ratio (HR) 0.64, 95% CI 0.40-1.02, P = 0.059; OS: HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.43-1.08, P = 0.099], compared with chemotherapy alone. In contrast, CLDN18.2-positive/non-IIP tumors showed no significant advantage over chemotherapy alone (PFS: HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.67-2.02, P = 0.592; OS: HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.50-1.41, P = 0.505; Figure 3B).

In patients predicted to be CLDN18.2-negative/IIP, nivolumab plus chemotherapy was associated with significantly improved outcomes compared with chemotherapy alone (PFS: HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.15-0.82, P = 0.013; OS: HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.18-0.89, P = 0.021). Conversely, no significant survival benefit was observed in CLDN18.2-negative/non-IIP tumors (PFS: HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.51-1.03, P = 0.075; OS: HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.62-1.24, P = 0.459; Figure 3C). Similar patterns were observed when CLDN18.2 status was defined by pathologist IHC rather than AI prediction (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

We assessed patient classification and biomarker profiles relevant to ICI therapy. Cases lacking specific biomarker data were excluded (PD-L1, 1.6%; MMR/MSI, 16.1%; EBV, 9.8%). CLDN18.2-negative/IIP tumors exhibited the highest PD-L1 expression (CPS ≥5 in 60.0%), whereas CLDN18.2-positive/non-IIP tumors demonstrated CPS ≥1 and ≥5 in 35.0% and 16.3% of cases, respectively. Among CLDN18.2-positive/non-IIP tumors, 8.5% were dMMR and 2.7% were EBV-positive (Figure 4A–C).

Although MSI-high/dMMR tumors are generally associated with an inflamed immune microenvironment, a small proportion of dMMR tumors were classified as non-IIP. This may reflect intratumoral heterogeneity or immune microenvironmental features that are not fully captured by molecular MSI status alone. To further explore this observation, exploratory survival analyses were performed in patients with dMMR stratified by immune phenotype. Among patients with dMMR/non-IIP, no significant difference in PFS was observed between nivolumab plus chemotherapy and chemotherapy alone. Conversely, among patients with dMMR/IIP, ICI-containing therapy showed a trend toward improved PFS compared with chemotherapy alone (PFS: HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.048-1.24, P = 0.067; Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was further evaluated in the internal validation cohort with available sequencing data (n = 194, 51.3%), including 102 of 182 (56.0%) and 92 of 196 (46.9%) patients in the chemotherapy alone and nivolumab plus chemotherapy groups, respectively. Among MSI-h/dMMR tumors, TMB values were similarly elevated in the non-IIP and IIP groups (median 42.2 versus 41.4 mutations/Mb; P = 0.88). Similarly, no significant difference in TMB was observed between immune phenotypes in MSS/pMMR tumors (median 14.1 versus 12.5 mutations/Mb; P = 0.48; Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021). These findings suggest that TMB alone does not fully account for the immune phenotype defined by our AI model.

The proportion of CLDN18.2-negative/IIP tumors was higher in the nivolumab plus chemotherapy group than in the chemotherapy alone group (11.2% versus 7.7%) (Supplementary Figure S6A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021). Nevertheless, when stratified by treatment arm, the distribution of PD-L1, MMR/MSI, and EBV positivity across the four subgroups defined by CLDN18.2 status with immune phenotype was broadly similar, indicating comparable biomarker profiles irrespective of treatment regimen (Supplementary Figure S6B and C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

AI model interpretability via attention analysis

Attention maps revealed recurrent histological patterns associated with high prediction scores. The model frequently focused on poorly differentiated or poorly cohesive histology, corresponding to the diffuse subtype, and occasionally on adjacent collagen-rich stroma. In some cases, attention was concentrated at the CA–CS interface rather than the tumor center. Such observations are biologically plausible, as previous studies have reported that diffuse-type gastric cancers can exhibit higher CLDN18.2 expression,7,18,22,23 and that CLDN18.2 may mediate interactions between tumor cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts within collagen-rich stroma.24 However, reported associations with histological subtype remain inconsistent.25 As the model was trained on slide-level CLDN18.2 status (≥75% positivity) rather than region-specific labels, attention patterns should be interpreted as qualitative correlates rather than definitive evidence of spatial expression (Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated an AI-based model to predict CLDN18.2 expression from H&E slides using the same IHC criteria applied in pivotal trials of zolbetuximab.8,9 The model demonstrates consistent performance across Asian and Western cohorts, supporting generalizability. In addition, integration of AI-derived immune phenotyping enabled identification of subgroups with limited benefit from ICI-based chemotherapy who may be better suited for zolbetuximab-based regimens.

The model leveraged foundation model features with a ViT backbone and attention-based MIL to capture tumor heterogeneity, with predictions generated through an optimized ensemble. This study shows the use of state-of-the-art foundation model features with attention-based MIL to enhance predictive performance. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to demonstrate that an AI model can predict CLDN18.2 positivity, potentially providing guidance for first-line treatment in metastatic gastric cancer.

Although performance at the Youden index cut-off showed moderate sensitivity and specificity, the threshold can be flexibly adjusted for different clinical purposes. For example, when optimized for 90.0% sensitivity in the tuning cohort, the model achieved a sensitivity of 89.9% and a specificity of 47.3% in the internal validation cohort, and 100.0% sensitivity with 45.2% specificity in the external cohort, supporting its adaptability for screening-oriented applications. Conversely, when thresholds were optimized to maximize the PPV, the model achieved a PPV of up to 0.812 in the development cohort, with substantially increased specificity, albeit at the expense of sensitivity (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108021). These findings suggest that a dual cut-off strategy may be considered, depending on whether the intended application is screening or direct therapeutic decision support. Furthermore, the model is not intended to replace pathologic CLDN18.2 assessment, but rather to serve as a prescreening tool to guide confirmatory immunohistochemical evaluation.

Comparable performance between biopsy and resection specimens suggests applicability in routine workflows where tissue availability is limited. The model’s high NPV may reduce unnecessary CLDN18.2 testing and optimize assay allocation. As biomarker panels expand and NGS use increases, efficient use of tumor tissue remains a critical challenge. In this context, H&E-based AI approaches offer a standardized and reproducible complement to conventional IHC, which is subject to clone-dependent variability, technical variability, and interobserver inconsistency.

Exploratory analysis of attention maps suggested that regions contributing most to model prediction occasionally corresponded to diffuse-type histology or collagen-rich stromal interfaces, which have been variably associated with CLDN18.2 expression in previous reports.18,23,25 These findings should be regarded as qualitative observations rather than mechanistic explanations, but may provide insights into histological correlates of CLDN18.2 positivity and tumor–stroma interactions.

CLDN18.2 expression has been reported to change after chemotherapy,26,27 suggesting that the current 75% cut-off may represent a dynamic rather than fixed biomarker. AI-based approaches may therefore provide additional value through reproducible and quantitative assessments that can adapt to evolving clinical scenarios. Future studies integrating multiple biomarkers may further refine treatment selection and extend the applicability of AI-powered pathology models across tumor types.

A distinct feature of this study is the integrated use of AI-based CLDN18.2 prediction and immune phenotyping to inform response to ICI-based chemotherapy.15, 16, 17 We identified IIP subgroups with distinct clinical responses to nivolumab-based treatment. Notably, patients predicted as CLDN18.2-positive but exhibiting a non-IIP-derived limited benefit from nivolumab plus chemotherapy, indicating that alternative therapeutic approaches, such as zolbetuximab-based regimens, may be more appropriate for this subgroup. Approximately one-third of patients within the CLDN18.2-positive, non-IIP subgroup exhibited PD-L1 positivity, highlighting the limitations of PD-L1 as a biomarker in guiding treatment decisions.12, 13, 14 These findings underscore the clinical relevance of our model in refining patient selection.

The model used only histopathologic image features without demographic inputs; although no explicit fairness constraints were applied, consistent performance across Asian and Western cohorts supports population-level generalizability.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, this study was retrospective and the development and internal validation cohorts were derived from a single institution over an extended period. Retrospective sample collection, evolving treatment paradigms, and institutional practice patterns may introduce selection and temporal biases. In addition, the development cohort consisted predominantly of Asian patients, whereas the external validation cohort included mainly Caucasian patients. Although external validation supports cross-population applicability, larger prospective, multicenter cohorts with balanced ethnic representation are required to confirm robustness and clinical utility in contemporary settings. In addition, although discriminatory performance was comparable across disease stages, additional validation in larger cohorts enriched for advanced-stage disease is important to confirm robustness in metastatic populations, particularly regarding systemic treatment selection. Secondly, emerging CLDN18.2-targeted modalities, including antibody–drug conjugates and bispecific T-cell engagers, use different expression cut-offs, limiting the direct applicability of our model to these therapies.7,28, 29, 30 Although our findings suggest that CLDN18.2-positive/non-IIP tumors may derive limited benefit from ICI plus chemotherapy, evidence demonstrating improved outcomes with zolbetuximab-based chemotherapy in this subgroup is not yet available. Future real-world prospective studies are necessary to validate clinical utility in guiding zolbetuximab-based treatment. Thirdly, from an implementation perspective, the model operates on routine H&E slides without requiring additional user interaction or molecular inputs. Poor-quality or nondiagnostic slides may limit performance and should be excluded through pathology quality control procedures. Prospective real-world evaluation is necessary to assess workflow integration and robustness under variable slide quality conditions.

Conclusions

Our AI-based model predicted CLDN18.2 expression and immune phenotypes from H&E slides with encouraging performance, demonstrating potential clinical utility in stratifying patients for first-line therapy. These findings highlight the potential of AI-powered pathology to complement conventional biomarker assessments and guide optimal therapeutic strategies in gastric cancer. Further prospective validation in larger, independent cohorts is required to confirm its role in guiding therapeutic strategies in gastric cancer.
