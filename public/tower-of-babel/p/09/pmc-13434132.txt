Visualizing pain-processing networks with 7T resting-state functional MRI using pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling
Authors: Andreas Nowacki, Sarah Waber, Jan Rosner, Claudio Pollo, Andrea Federspiel, Roland Wiest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArterial spin labeling (ASL) at ultra‑high field MRI at 7 T provides enhanced signal‑to‑noise ratio and spatial resolution, enabling more sensitive functional imaging. The objective of this study was to investigate regional perfusion changes and their relationship to perceived pain during sustained capsaicin‑induced nociception. Twenty‑seven healthy right‑handed volunteers (mean age 31.9 ± 4.1 years; 12 female) underwent two MRI sessions: at baseline and during capsaicin-induced tonic pain. Participants rated pain and were classified as responders or weak responders. MRI included T1‑weighted structural imaging, resting BOLD‑fMRI and 3D turbo‑FLASH pCASL. Images were segmented, normalized to MNI space, and CBF maps calculated. Mean CBF was extracted from gray matter and 10 predefined pain‑matrix regions. Voxel‑wise linear regression analyzed correlations between CBF and pain ratings, with false discovery rate correction. Group differences between responders and weak responders were assessed voxel‑wise. Global gray matter CBF was unchanged between baseline and capsaicin conditions and did not differ by responder status. However, CBF increased significantly with pain in a network including the contralateral insular cortex, primary and secondary somatosensory cortices, supplementary motor area, bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and contralateral thalamus. Average perfusion in these regions rose by 8–15% and correlated strongly with pain scores (r ≈ 0.66; p < 0.001). Weak responders showed higher CBF in right fusiform and bilateral primary visual cortices and lower CBF in bilateral superior frontal gyrus compared with responders. In conclusion, pCASL at 7 T enables quantitative mapping of brain perfusion during sustained pain. Sustained capsaicin stimulation increases regional CBF in a distributed cortico‑subcortical network, with perfusion changes closely tracking perceived pain intensity. These findings demonstrate that tonic nociception engages broader associative networks and highlight the utility of ultra‑high field ASL for studying chronic pain mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49168-w.Subject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) at ultra-high field strengths (UHF) of 7 Tesla (7T) has been approved as a medical product by the by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Economic Area (CE mark) for human clinical assessments of the human brain (and knee) since 20171. Beyond structural imaging, 7T MRI, represents a significant advancement in the field of functional neuroimaging due to its markedly improved spatial and temporal resolution, enabling refined detection of neural activity2,3. Specifically, it facilitates advanced functional imaging techniques, including arterial spin labeling (ASL). Unlike conventional blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI, which is principally sensitive to relative changes in deoxyhemoglobin, ASL provides absolute measures of cerebral perfusion in in milliliters per 100 g of brain tissue per minute (ml/100 g/min)4. Thus, ASL constitutes an effective method to investigate altered brain function and physiology quantitatively and reliably, particularly in populations exhibiting altered cerebral perfusion, such as in patients with neurological disorders such as chronic pain5.

Neuroimaging investigations of pain have historically concentrated on brief, acute noxious stimuli in healthy individuals using 1.5T or 3T MRI and reliably demonstrated signal increases of a consistent neuronal “pain matrix” of activated brain regions6. Together, these areas shall be included to define the neuronal pain matrix in the present work. Among those areas most consistently identified are the primary and secondary somatosensory cortices (S1 and S2), insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), prefrontal cortical regions (including Brodmann areas 9 and 10), as well as subcortical structures like the thalamus and basal ganglia7,8. However, tonic pain models, which mimic chronic clinical pain conditions more accurately than acute stimuli, are increasingly employed and provide insights into the persistent neurophysiological mechanisms underlying chronic pain9,10.

A key advantage of ASL lies in the capability to measure slow and steady-state changes in brain perfusion, making it particularly effective for capturing prolonged stimuli, such as tonic pain induced by chemical agents like capsaicin11,12. Prolonged pain responses can be challenging to reliably capture using the BOLD method due to its high temporal resolution but relative sensitivity to transient rather than sustained changes13.

Investigating changes in the dynamic state that last for minutes to hours, and brain activations and deactivations as typically present during chronic pain conditions may remain challenging and elusive14. ASL has been shown to be sensitive in discovering changes in regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF)15 that may be harder to discover with conventional BOLD fMRI16.

This has been successfully demonstrated in chronic conditions as trigeminal neuralgia17, chronic knee pain18 or low back pain. In the present study, we aimed to identify the neural correlates of sustained tonic pain, reflecting clinical neuropathic pain conditions, using a prolonged painful stimulus in healthy volunteers at ultra-high field MRI (7 Tesla). Specifically, we employed high-dose capsaicin patches to induce a persistent burning sensation and investigated the associated evoked rCBF responses using pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling. We hypothesized that prolonged capsaicin-induced tonic pain would activate key structures of the pain matrix as defined above.

Results

Subject’s pain perception

Individual pain perception was assessed at baseline and a second time while a pain-inducing patch with capsaicin was attached to the subjects’ left arm. Subjects were dichotomized according to their individual rating of perceived pain with the patch (see supplement Figure S1). For this we set a cutoff of > 5 that defined the group of responders (N = 12) and the group of weak-responders (N = 15).

Discussion

The present study aimed to demonstrate the application of pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling pCASL at a clinical Ultra High Field (UHF) MRI. Upregulations of cerebral blood flow were observed during continuous nociceptive dermal stimulation with capsaicin. Key findings that emerged from our study, are (i) ASL can be reliably applied on a clinical UHF MRI and demonstrated cortical and deep gray matter upregulation of cerebral perfusion during sustained and unmodulated stimulation in healthy individuals and (ii) brain areas associated with cerebral processing of nociceptive stimuli encompass an extensive cortical network including somatosensory, insular and cingulate areas, the frontal and parietal lobe. These cortical areas, involved into the generation of a conscious percept of nociception (frequently referred to the so-called pain matrix) showed sustained upregulations in CBF that remained detectable throughout the MR acquisition period. We observed a robust correlation between subjective pain perception and increases in CBF, predominantly the sensory areas BA1–BA3. Interestingly, this observed pain-induced pattern within the brain is equally present when the linear regression analysis is computed in a two-fold way using (1) CBF measured during the pain condition alone and (2) CBF difference maps (i.e. CBF at pain-induced scan – CBF at baseline), which may stand as a consistency check.

Notably, the perceived intensity of pain did not differentiate between weak responders and strong responders after capsaicin application. Specifically, the processing of particular sensory information within the sensory environment of BA 1-BA 3 is in keeping with the hypothesis that prioritizing of salient events in the sensory environment is an important function to guarantee coherent behavior. The overall signature is distinguished from a general salience signal by its inclusion of somatic-specific regions, such as the secondary somatosensory cortex and the dorsal posterior insula, complemented by patterns of activity that are specific to physical pain within regions that are activated across various psychological processes (e.g. the anterior insula), previously identified as key elements of physical pain19. This neurologic signature is predominantly bilateral, with contralateral specificity in the primary and secondary somatosensory cortices, consistent with previous fMRI studies20,21.

Our study differed in the setup of the experimental design. While previous neuroimaging studies of pain have mainly used brief, acute noxious stimuli – both in healthy individuals and patients affected by chronic neuropathic pain disorders, we aimed to investigate tonic rather than event-related activation of brain areas involved into pain processing during persistent dermal stimulation. BOLD fMRI studies evaluating ongoing/slowly varying or constant pain are scarce and limited in the depiction of static conditions lasting minutes to hours, ASL, in contrast, is highly versatile, allowing for paradigms exploring pain states with more flexibility.

Time locked activation patterns, as e.g. elicited by high density laser stimulation, differs from aberrant cerebral processing during continuous experience of pain. Different chronic pain syndromes show diverse and sometimes condition-specific functional connectivity changes, in contrast to the relatively uniform acute pain pattern22–25. To address this knowledge gap, our investigation concentrated on sustained noxious stimulation that more closely mimic neuropathic pain conditions. CBF upregulations were consistently depicted in brain areas that showed a similar signature for pain in a machine-learning–based regression analysis with a 90% sensitivity and specificity at the level of the individual person. This pattern was consistently identified across 4 studies in 114 healthy participants and scanners26 from different vendors and field strengths. The brain areas reflect both the sensory-discriminative and affective dimensions of acute pain (contralateral sensory-motor cortices, the posterior insula, the anterior cingulate cortex, frontal lobe regions including premotor and prefrontal areas and subcortical regions within the basal ganglia). Additionally, we found increased perfusion in the ipsilateral (left) angular and superior temporal gyrus, the precuneus and lingual gyrus.

The angular gyrus constitutes a key hub connecting pain networks and is implicated in chronic pain. The superior temporal gyrus is involved in pain memory formation. The precuneus integrates sensory and emotional aspects of unpleasant stimuli and the lingual gyrus is part of the visual cortex and may be involved in pain-related imagery. These areas have not been consistently reported in event-related BOLD fMRI studies. A potential explanation is the variation of individual dynamics of endogenous pain experience. The latter may fluctuate over time and consist of periods of increasing, stable, and decreasing pain intensity, which may be missed during brief and high-intense stimulation, but are robust enough if pain is experienced during longer period lasting minutes to hours27.

ASL is particularly suited for investigating the neurobiology of pain, given that pain involves spontaneous fluctuations of its perceived intensity and quality28–30. Activated networks31–33 can be captured during sustained alterations in regional CBF, reflecting dynamic states of nociceptive or affective pain processing without requiring external stimulation. This is crucial specifically in the investigation of chronic pain14,34,35, where resting-state perfusion alterations may underpin subjective pain experiences.

A principal technical strength of ASL is its ability to acquire quantitative perfusion data at rest, offering a stable baseline measure of brain function that is not dependent on specifically designed activation studies and therefore better suited for populations where task compliance is limited or variable, such as in acute stroke36,37, epilepsy38–40, or psychiatric disorders41. Repetitive measurements over time enable the investigation of the organization and adoption of disease specific pathways and brain networks42,43 without concerns about radiation dose or contrast agent exposure. In stroke, ASL has proven useful in delineating regions of hypoperfusion and identifying tissue at risk even in the absence of visible structural lesions. The capability to track perfusion dynamics repeatedly allows for assessment of spontaneous recovery or the effects of interventions44. In epilepsy, ASL can detect interictal hypoperfusion or peri-ictal hyperperfusion, offering a valuable baseline measure when patients are not actively seizing. These baseline abnormalities can inform localization of seizure foci and may guide surgical planning. In psychiatry, where traditional biomarkers remain elusive, ASL provides a reproducible metric of brain function. Disorders such as depression45,46, schizophrenia47,48, and PTSD49 have been associated with altered baseline perfusion in key brain areas (e.g., anterior cingulate, insula, default mode network). Importantly, because ASL does not require task performance, it eliminates variability due to patient effort or understanding, offering a compliant and reliable window into underlying pathophysiology24,50,51. In many pathophysiological conditions, e.g. in chronic pain, psychiatric disorders, or interictal epilepsy, resting perfusion is not truly neutral but rather represents an altered state of baseline activation. Thus, ASL-derived baselines may themselves be functionally significant, revealing disease-relevant activity patterns without requiring task contrast paradigms42,52,53. This ability to capture intrinsic functional abnormalities at rest positions ASL as a uniquely informative tool in conditions where evoked responses may be blunted, unreliable, or ethically difficult to induce. Principal advantages of UHF MRI encompass a higher SNR, increased tissue and blood T1 values and subsequent more efficient labeling of blood water in ASL. Scans can be acquired continuously over several minutes, and compared to conventional task-based designs (e.g., pre-stimulus vs. sustained stimulus), they potentially facilitate the construction of a time-resolved map of tonic pain processing without the confounding variability that affects BOLD signals during prolonged acquisitions. The quantitative nature of ASL renders it ideal for within- and between-subject comparisons. In the context of chronic pain research or pharmacological testing, ASL can detect subtle changes in resting-state perfusion that may be exacerbated or alleviated by pharmacological interventions.

Some limitations of this study require discussion. The sample size of the present study is low. Moreover, the subjective pain index peaks at mid-values which could be the result of a potential response bias with a tendency towards the mean. Furthermore, this effect increases the risk of false statistical conclusions: as the majority of participants rated pain intensity near the cut-off threshold used for dichotomization, the assignment to responders and non-responders together with the associated perfusion measurements become unstable. Furthermore, two participants did not tolerate the capsaicin patch and could therefore not be included in the analysis. If their inclusion would have changed the results and led to a different picture remains speculative at this point. A conclusive statement about the robustness of the findings may therefore be lowered. Lastly, the group in the present study contains healthy subjects that were treated with capsaicin to evoke pain perception and no patients were included in the present study. Although we are optimistic, that the imaging modality used is well-suited to study clinical conditions of chronic pain, it remains speculative at this point.

In summary, we observed a robust activation of networks involved in both the perception and cognitive-evaluative processing of pain. Perfusion changes were observed in core network structures and in key hub connecting pain networks, pain memory formation and integration of perception and emotion, not consistently reported in event-related BOLD fMRI studies.

Methods

Study design and experimental setup

All experimental protocols were approved by our institutional committee (Kantonale Ethikkommission Bern, KEK-number 2020–02640). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The current study was conducted at the Swiss Institute for Translational and Entrepreneurial Medicine (SITEM) located at the University Hospital of Bern (Inselspital) on a 7T Siemens Magnetom Terra whole body MRI scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Deutschland) with a 32-channel Nova head coil (Nova Medical, Cambridge, MA, USA). Participants underwent two 7T MRI scans before (baseline) and approximately 20–30 min after local administration of a 6 cm2 8% capsaicin patch (Qutenza®, Averitas Pharma Inc.) to the left volar forearm. In order to assess quantitative and emotional aspects of pain participants were asked to rate their experienced pain intensity as well as the pain aversiveness according to the numeric rating scale (NRS 0–10, 0: no pain, 10: worst pain imaginable) before and after the second scan, in order to determine responders (defined as participants with pain intensity values of > 5 ) as well as weak-responders. As the pain aversiveness was rated the same as the subjective pain intensity by almost all participants, we focused our analysis on pain intensity. The neuroimaging data included a whole-brain anatomical t1-weighted scan (performed only at baseline), a t2*-weighted resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) (performed at baseline and after capsaicin-administration) and a resting cerebral blood flow (CBF) measurement using the technique of pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) (at baseline and after capsaicin-administration). During each of the recordings in the dark room of the scanner, participants were asked to close their eyes and to stay awake and were additionally instructed to lie still during whole registration.

Study cohort

For the present study 30 healthy, right-handed subjects were recruited from the University of Bern. Exclusion criteria for participation were a positive history of prior stroke, psychiatric disease, a known cerebral pathology, a documented allergy to capsaicin, or any active use of psychotropic drugs or analgesics. Three patients had to be excluded from the final analysis (wrong-side application of capsaicin patch in 1, intolerance to capsaicin in 2) leaving a final study cohort of 27 subjects (12 female/15 male; mean age = 31.95, SD = 4.11).

MRI data acquisition

The anatomical t1-weighted image acquisition was performed using a magnetization-prepared two rapid gradient echo (MP2RAGE) sequence with a time of repetition (TR) = 6000 ms; echo time (TE) 2.06 ms; inversion time 1 (TI1) = 800 ms; inversion time 2 (TI2) = 2700 ms; flip angle (FA) = 0 °; field of view (FOV) = 384 × 384 mm2; 256 sagittal slices of 0.66 mm slice thickness. With a matrix size of 256 × 256 this resulted in iso-voxel spatial resolution of 0.66 × 0.66 × 0.66 mm3. Acquisition time (TA) = 7 min 40 s. For the t2*-weighted fMRI scans we used multiband echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with TR = 300 ms; TE = 24 ms; bandwidth = 2422 Hz/pixel; FOV = 224 × 224 mm2; 36 transversal slices positioned at the line intersecting the points of anterior- and posterior commissure with 2.6 mm slice thickness; distance factor 20%; a total of 1000 volumes were collected within 5 min 08 s. Notably, these fMRIscancs were not further analysed in the present study. For pCASL we used a 3D turbo fast-low-angle-shot (TFL) readout as described elsewhere (16, 17). We used the following parameters: TR = 95.12 ms; TE = 1.55 ms; TReff = 8 s; bandwidth = 490 Hz/pixel, echo spacing (ES) = 3 ms; single post label delay (PLD) = 2.0 s; label duration (LD) = 1.0 s; 50 measurements, including an M0 image, were acquired in 11 min 42 s. For pCASL we additionally acquired a calibration measurement consisting of 2D-CAIPIRINHA sequence with TR = 190.18 ms; TE = 1.55 ms; and with equal other parameters as for the pCASL recordings; TA = 42 s. This calibration scan was acquired separately for estimation of GRAPPA weights. The pCASL acquisition we used, is not in line with the latest recommendations due to Swiss Law restrictions, only using single Tx mode and not full pTx mode54–56.

MRI and fMRI data preprocessing

All MRI and CBF-related fMRI data were processed with spm12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) and with ANTs 2.3.357. Moreover, CBF quantification was performed using a Matlab script (courtesy of Chenyang Zhao, University of Southern California, USA) implemented in Matlab. Anatomical data were first segmented using SPM12 into the three main brain tissue types: cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white-matter and gray-matter. During this step, anatomical data were transformed into a 2 mm X 2 mm X 2 mm standard MNI space.

ASL data preprocessing

Preprocessing and analysis of MR data were performed using SPM12 and custom Matlab code. We used SPM for registration of all image modalities, and we used “Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie algebra” [DARTEL] for accurate registration of functional- to anatomical images (see supplement Figure S2 and S3). Raw pCASL images were first realigned to remove movement artefacts during MRI acquisition. Cerebral blood flow (CBF) was quantified using the single- compartment kinetic model adapted for TFL-pCASL54,56

The timing constants PLD were set to 2 s, tagging duration (LD) = 1 s, blood/tissue water partition coefficient λ = 0.9 [g/ml] and tagging efficiency assumed to be α = 0.95. For 7.0T the decay time for labelled blood T1blood = 2087 ms, M0 are the equilibrium brain tissue magnetization images. DM was calculated by subtraction of all realigned label and control images. Quantified CBF flow time-series and average CBF maps for the entire acquisition were calculated. Then the CBF images were co-registered and normalized using the individual normalization parameters derived from segmentation (see above). CBF images were smoothed with a 4 mm Gaussian kernel.

Region of interest (ROI) analysis in the pain matrix

CBF values were extracted within 10 distinct predefined regions of interest (ROI) that were described to represent the ‘pain-matrix’(18). These 10 ROI’s were Brodman area (BA) 1, BA 2, BA 3, BA 9, BA 10, Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), Globus Pallidus (lateral and medial), Insula and Thalamus. From these 10 ROI’s we did extract regional CBF values for each subject and for each condition: i.e. baseline- and after pain patch. CBF values were averaged.

Statistics of neuroimaging data

In a first step the statistical analysis of neuroimaging data was conducted with both, the CBF maps from the baseline scan and from the second run (i.e. the run with the pain-induced patch) in order to produce distribution of CBF values for each condition (e.g.histograms). From the segmentation step of anatomical images, a mask that contained only gray matter was constructed. All CBF maps of all subjects were masked with this GM mask. From the masked CBF images we used all voxel containing CBF values to produce a histogram (see Fig. 1). For the test of baseline scan vs. pain-induced scan we used a paired sample t-Test.

In a second step we conducted a voxel-wise linear regression analysis using the CBF maps from the pain-induced scan and the subjective pain index to obtain the activation pattern correlated with the subjective pain intensity. With these CBF maps from the pain-induced scan we additionally calculated the statistical differences between “Responders to Weak Responders” using two-sample t-Test. In order to correct for multiple testing, we applied false-discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-values for multiple comparisons. An FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all models that we used. Identification of anatomical statistical significant brain region was based on the automated anatomical atlas AAL58 including version 3 of AAL3 that is included in ‘xjview’ (toolbox of SPM).

To check for consistency of the observed activations, we conducted an additional voxel-wise linear regression analysis using the difference in CBF maps from the pain-induced scan – baseline scan and the difference in subjective pain index recorded after pain-induced scan – baseline scan to identify potential brain regions that responded to pain-related changes. Therefore, for this within-subject analysis step a Robust Linear Regression (matlab library ‘fitlm’ with option ‘RobustOpts’) was used within the framework of a generalized linear model (i.e. GLM).
