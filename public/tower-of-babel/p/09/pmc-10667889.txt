Bilateral floating knee injury in a child with down syndrome: A case report
Authors: Reza Zandi, Farsad Biglari, Saber Barazandeh Rad, Amir Sabaghzadeh, Farshad Farzanegan, Meisam Jafari Kafiabadi
Published: 2023

Introduction

The first time description of the floating knee injury was by McBride and Blake in 1975 as a concurrent fracture of the femur and tibia. This injury is associated with high-energy trauma and is more common among young men. For this reason, this fracture is related to other injuries in orthopedics [1]. Although isolated femur or tibia fractures are relatively common in children, floating knee fractures are rare in this age group [2]. Lett et al introduced the most well-known classification of the floating knee, in which fractures are divided into 5 types. Type-A: simultaneous fracture of the diaphysis of tibia and femur, Type-B: fracture of the metaphysis of either tibia or femur plus fracture in the diaphysis of the other bone, Type-C: fracture of the epiphysis of either tibia or femur along with the diaphysis of the other bone, Type-D: an open fracture in any part of each femur or tibia bones, and Type-E: open fractures in both femur and tibia bones [3].

Trisomy 21 is the most common genetic disorder with characteristic features such as a flattened face, short neck, oblique palpebral fissures, and almond-shaped eyes and concomitant medical conditions including congenital heart disorder, deafness, and cataracts. Several musculoskeletal abnormalities are related to Down syndrome, associated with generalized ligamentous laxity, and joint hypermobility. These musculoskeletal disorders include atlantooccipital, atlantoaxial, hip, and patellar instability, scoliosis, spondylolisthesis, slipped capital femoral epiphysis(SCFE), pes planus, and hallux valgus [4].

Various systemic diseases in Down syndrome involving the musculoskeletal system, affect orthopedic surgeon decisions for proper diagnosis and management of fractures. Specifically, obesity, low bone mineral density, hypothyroidism, hematologic disorder, and arthropathy [4].

This case report highlights the rare occurrence of bilateral floating knee injury in a child with Down syndrome and emphasizes the need for thorough evaluation and appropriate management in such cases.

Case presentation

This case follows 2020 SCARE guidelines for reporting cases in surgery [5]. A 10-year-old boy presented to the emergency room after a car rolling accident while he was sitting in the back seat. On arrival, the patient was conscious and oriented. Initial management was performed based on the ATLS protocol. Trauma series radiographs were normal.

Due to the tenderness, visible ulceration, and swelling of both lower limbs, radiography was requested. The radiography on the right lower limb revealed a diaphysial fracture of the femoral bone (Fig. 1) and an open fracture (type 2 of Gustilo-Anderson classification) of the diaphysis of the tibia (Fig. 2), which was consistent with type D. The radiography of the left side showed a fracture of the growth plate of the distal femur (Salter-Harris type 2) and open fracture of the metaphyseal region of the proximal tibia (Fig. 3), which was consistent with type D.

A fracture of the right tibia fracture.

A Fracture of the left distal femur in the physeal region and a fracture of the tibia in the metaphyseal region.

In the emergency room, the patient's wounds were irrigated with 6 l of normal saline, the temporary splint was installed to reduce pain, and an ice packing and limb elevation were performed to prevent compartment syndrome. The patient was admitted to the ICU. The review of systems was normal except for a relatively large mass in the neck area, which was a laryngeal hemangioma according to past medical history (Fig. 4). The cardiac evaluation was normal.

A lump in the neck that was suspected to be a hemangioma.

The routine lab tests were significant for low hemoglobin (Hb = 8.9), and other tests including thyroid profile were either negative or normal. The neurosurgery and general surgery consultations were performed without any further investigation ordered from the aforementioned services.

On the fifth day of hospitalization, the child was transferred to the OR, and under general anesthesia, the physeal fracture of the left distal femur was repositioned and fixed under C-ARM control with distal femur anatomical plate by the lateral approach of the distal femur. Metaphyseal fracture of the left proximal tibia was reduced openly with an anteromedial approach and fixed with a 4.5 T- T-plate (Fig. 5).

Left lower limb post-op x-ray.

On the seventh day of hospitalization diaphyseal open fracture of the right tibia was fixed with LC-DCP 4.5 narrow The fracture was approached from the wound site and the patient's wound was sutured. Subsequently, the right diaphyseal femur fracture was fixed on the 10th day with a sub-vastus approach with two lag screws and a 4.5 LC-DCP plate (Fig. 6). Afterward, both lower extremities were immobilized with a long leg splint for a week.

Right lower limb post-op x-ray.

On the first day after the operation, we started the active range of motion of the knee. In the sixth week, the ROM of both knees was more than 90 degrees of flexion and full extension. Protective weight-bearing was started after six weeks, all four fractures were fused in the sixth month after the operation without any complications, and the union and ROM were complete (Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10).

The patient's parents were educated about the complications of fractures and possible surgical complications including damage to the growth plate, the possibility of non-union or misalignment, and infection. Parents were advised to bring the child for follow-up visits every three months in the first year. Then every six months to two years after the operation, and then annually until bone growth is complete.

Discussion

Floating knee fractures usually occur in accidents such as falling from a height or overturning a car or motorcycle [6]. In high-energy traumas, such as the floating knee, due to the release of a lot of energy, special attention should be paid to damage to internal organs and the brain [1]. It underscores the importance of multidisciplinary care involving orthopedic surgeons, neurosurgeons, and general surgeons to ensure optimal outcomes for patients with complex fractures, especially in the presence of underlying medical conditions such as Down syndrome.

There is no consensus regarding the optimal treatment of these types of fractures in children due to the small number of cases however, spica casting and traction were traditionally used in the past.

In the article published by Anari et al. in 2017, the rate of complications of non-surgical treatment, including nonunion, malunion, and limb length discrepancies was higher compared to surgical treatment [7]. Lett et al. recommend at least one fracture on each side to be rigid and fixed with a device [3]. In their study, Yue et al. reported a higher rate of malunion in patients who underwent non-surgical treatment they also suggested that at least one fracture should be fixed internally and the other fracture could be fixed externally or non-surgically [6].

Therefore, in our patient, due to the bilateral damage and the proximity of the fractures to the growth plate, we decided to perform internal fixation of all fractures.

Until now, no study had been conducted on a child suspected of having Down's syndrome who suffered from floating knee injuries, and according to previous studies [4], the necessary investigations were carried out to perform anesthesia and measures before, during, and after the operation. Down syndrome may help the patient recover, especially the ROM, due to various musculoskeletal conditions, such as generalized ligamentous laxity, joint hypermobility, and hypotonia.

The study's primary limitation lies in its sole reliance on a single case, and it is worth noting that all fractures were surgically managed. To address these constraints, it is strongly recommended to undertake additional research involving a larger cohort of cases to determine the prevalence of this injury among Down syndrome patients and explore potential relationships.

Conclusion

A floating knee is not a common fracture in children, especially in association with a chromosomal abnormality. The preferred treatment for this injury, especially in bilateral cases, maybe the internal fixation of all fractures in all age groups. It should be noted that comprehensive considerations are necessary for these patients before, during, and after surgery, especially in patients with Down syndrome.
