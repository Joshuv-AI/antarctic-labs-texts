Rabbits as preclinical animal models of cam morphology: proof of concept
Authors: Zhi Li, Pu Wan, Xuemiao Liu, Guichun Jiang, Jie Li, Dapeng Chen, Weiguo Zhang, Kang Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCam morphology is associated with acetabular cartilage lesions and early-onset osteoarthritis in patients with femoroacetabular impingement syndrome. Here, we propose a novel iterative rabbit model of cam morphology to characterize the pathophysiology of femoroacetabular impingement-related cartilage lesions.MethodsWe described a repeatable method for establishing a New Zealand white rabbit model of cam morphology by interfering with the epiphyseal tubercle and evaluated the histological outcomes of acetabular cartilage lesions. Cam morphology and subchondral bone were evaluated by micro-CT scanning. The expression of anabolic and catabolic biomarkers of acetabular cartilage was investigated via western blotting and immunohistochemical staining. Chondrocyte apoptosis was detected via TUNEL staining. Kinematics of the hip joint in the sagittal plane were evaluated by a passive optical motion capture system.ResultsThere were remarkable pathological similarities between this model and human diseases. The cam morphology rabbit model exhibited early cartilage degeneration and subsequent phenotypes of hip osteoarthritis, and this pathological process is not affected by surgical trauma. Hip motion in the cam morphology models resembled that in the healthy controls, and had only a slightly lower joint angle at all phases of the half-bounding gait cycle. This model preserved the natural and predictable movement pattern.ConclusionsThis rabbit model of cam morphology replicates the disease phenotype and allows for scientifically sound evaluation of disease mechanisms. It can be employed to study human cam-type femoroacetabular impingement syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09194-7.Keywords: Models, animal; Femoroacetabular impingement; Cartilage; Hip

Introduction

Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) is a motion-related clinical disorder of the hip characterized by a myriad of symptoms, clinical signs, and imaging findings that correspond with symptomatic morphological disorders and abnormal contact between the proximal femur and acetabulum [1]. FAIS patients with cam morphology have significantly high rates of acetabular cartilage lesions and early-onset osteoarthritis (OA) [2–4]. Cam morphology has typically been classified into following two classes [5]. Primary cam morphology is a cartilage or bony prominence of varying size at any location around the femoral head-neck junction, which likely due to load during skeletal maturation and its effect on the proximal femoral growth plate. Secondary cam morphology develops due to existing hip disease or acute trauma, including Perthes disease, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, healed proximal femoral fractures or acute fracture. Up to now, the underlying molecular mechanisms of the transition from focal cam impingement to global joint degeneration remain elusive [6]. The exploration of molecular mechanisms could lead to the development of nonsurgical therapies to prevent cartilage lesions and the progression of early-stage OA. However, the immorality of harvesting human acetabular cartilage samples at an early stage and the lack of preclinical animal models are considered hindrances to research.

Despite decades of research, merely two animal models of Cam-type femoroacetabular impingement have been established to date, both of which remain underutilized in biomedical investigations. Sheep have been considered reliable for establishing animal models of impingement between the proximal femur and acetabulum via intertrochanteric varus osteotomy and have been verified by histology and impingement simulation [7–10]. However, this large animal model presents several limitations, including complexity, high costs, time-consuming, technically demanding, and slow progression. Reports on clinical translation and basic research using this model were rare. A novel rabbit model of cam morphology was recently established by blocking the growth of the proximal femoral growth plate [11]. This rabbit model appears to more accurately recapitulate the pathophysiology of human primary cam morphology. However, the long-term disease outcomes associated with this model have not yet been documented in the literature.

The ideal cam-type FAIS animal model must be similar to humans in terms of disease pathology and able to reproduce the pattern and progression of cartilage lesions in a controlled manner, thereby allowing us to ascertain the course of events, as well as explore new disease-modifying agents or biological markers. Thus, we optimized and iterated the above rabbit model, chiefly in terms of the surgical approach and principle, and detailed the standardized process. Meanwhile, we aimed to provide evidence from histopathology, imaging, kinematics, and molecular biology to determine whether this iterated model is suitable for characterizing the pathology and course of cam-type FAIS.

Methods

Experimental animals and welfare

The Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines were used for animal experiment planning. A priori power calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to calculate the sample size (Supplementary material 1). All animal care and procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Dalian Medical University (protocol AEE23109). Seventy-five male laboratory New Zealand white rabbits were purchased at the age of five weeks from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and were housed individually in furnished metal cages with perforated metal walls and plastic footrests and were fed ad Libitum with a laboratory chow diet, timothy hay and water. The surface area of the floor of each single-housing system was 5100 cm2, and the maximum roof height was 70 cm. The holding rooms had adequate ventilation with a 12 h light/dark cycle. The environmental temperature ranged from 18 to 22 °C, and the relative humidity was approximately 60%. They were divided into small groups and placed in floor pens with bedding for at least four hours a day to assess social behaviors, encourage activity, and promote positive interaction daily. According to previous research and our pre-experiments, the cam morphology appeared within four weeks after model surgery, and the early stage of impingement was determined to be 8 weeks after surgery, with 20 weeks after surgery as the advanced stage [11]. The rabbits were randomly divided into five groups using random numbers when they were six-weeks-old: an early control group, an early model group, an advanced control group, an advanced model group, and a sham group, with 15 rabbits in each group. The early control group and early model group were euthanized at 14-weeks-old. The advanced control group, advanced model group, and sham group were euthanized at 26-weeks-old. Each rabbit’s pain, suffering and distress were assessed using the scheme reported by Hawkins [12]. A 20% reduction in body weight was used as a humane endpoint [13]. Sodium pentobarbitone (120 mg/kg i.v.) was used for euthanasia.

Surgical procedure in rabbits

The six-week-old rabbits in the model groups underwent surgery in the lateral decubitus position. Each rabbit was anesthetized with an intramuscular injection of 15 mg of ketamine and 1 mg of xylazine, and 1.5% inhalation isoflurane was used for anesthesia maintenance. The surgical procedure was randomly performed on either hindlimb in each rabbit to control for potential limb dominance effects. The contralateral limb was not used as a negative control due to anticipated alterations in limb kinematics and weight-bearing patterns. Healthy rabbits that did not undergo any surgical procedures were used as negative controls. Our three-step modeling method specifically involves sharp dissection of the superficial layer, blunt dissection of the deep layer, and direct drilling after external rotation. We made a skin incision from the iliac crest to the third trochanter (Fig. 1A1-A2). Following the incision, sharp dissection through the subcutaneous tissue, lateral fascia, tensor fasciae latae, and gluteus superficialis (gluteus maximus) exposed the gluteus medius. Using hemostatic forceps pointing toward the joint, blunt dissection through the gluteus medius directly exposed the hip capsule. No muscles were released from the insertion. The joint capsule appeared as a bright white area at this time (Fig. 1A3). Then, traction was applied with the leg externally rotated. The joint capsule was stretched and became thinner, allowing us to distinguish the femoral head–neck junction and acetabulum (Fig. 1A4-A5). We used a 1.5 mm orthopedic drill bit angled anterograde from the head–neck junction to destroy the ventral cranial part of the proximal femoral growth plate and epiphyseal tubercle directly without extensive capsulotomy (Fig. 1A6-B3). Using the femoral shaft as a reference, the drill bit was angled 30° on the sagittal and coronal planes and 45° on the transverse plane (Fig. 1B4-B6). A 4*1.5*5 mm defect was created in the growth plate. Debris was removed by flushing. Post-drilling capsular tissue proved inadequate for suturing due to excessive thinness and tension. The other structures were sutured layer by layer using 4 − 0 absorbable sutures. In the sham surgery, only part of the joint capsule was removed so that the epiphysis was not damaged. Each rabbit was administered 0.2 mg of meloxicam intramuscularly daily for three days after surgery.

Macroscopic and radiographic assessment of disease outcome

The acetabular cartilage lesions of rabbits in the advanced control group, advanced model group, and advanced sham group (n = 15, aged 26 weeks) were independently graded by two blinded observers (XML and PW) using the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) classification system after euthanasia. Cartilage lesions were localized using a clock face system (6 o’clock: transverse Ligament; 3 o’clock: cranial/anterior) (Fig. 2A, D). For femoral head mapping, we established an anatomical coordinate system in Mimics (Materialise, Belgium) by: (1) calculating a femoral neck centerline, (2) defining a perpendicular plane superior to the lesser trochanter, with the greater trochanter at 12 o’clock and cranial direction at 3 o’clock. This standardized approach remains unaffected by morphological differences. The presence of both cam morphology and acetabular cartilage lesions indicated successful establishment of the model.

The hip joints of the advanced control and model groups (n = 6) were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Then, the hips were scanned with a micro-CT scanner (NMC-200, NEMO®, China) with 35 μm resolution at 80 kV tube voltage and 40 mA current. The region of interest (ROI) was were localized to the 4 o’clock acetabular region and 3 o’clock femoral head region. The supporting analysis software, Avatar, provided automated analysis of bone microstructural parameters from volumetric ROI. The subchondral plate and trabecular bone were segmented manually using a previously described criterion based on the size of intracortical pores [14]. The remaining subchondral trabecular bone regions immediately beneath the subchondral plate were subjected to subsequent microstructural analysis. Forty serial slices were obtained for measuring bone mineral density (BMD), the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) ratio, trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), trabecular separation (Tb.Pf), connection density (Conn.D), degree of anisotropy (DA), fractal dimension (FD). The subchondral bone plate thickness (SBP.th) was then determined from the mean value of the segmentation results. Briefly, the outline of the subchondral bone plate was traced manually, and the thickness was measured at three evenly distributed locations on each slice. The average subchondral bone plate thickness from all these measurements was recorded for each rabbit. The radial alpha angle of the femoral head was measured [15].

Western blotting

Fresh acetabular cartilage tissue was harvested from the early control and model groups, advanced control and model groups, and sham group (n = 6), and the expression of Runx2, Col2, Sox9, and MMP13 was measured by western blotting (WB), with GAPDH used as the internal reference. Liquid nitrogen grinding methods and RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors were used to extract protein. Primary antibodies for Runx2 (1:1000, 20700-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Col2 (1:1000, M2139: sc-52658, Santa Cruz, USA), Sox9 (1:1000, 67439-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), MMP13 (1:1000, 18165-1-AP, Proteintech, China), and GAPDH (1:4000, GB15002-100 and GB15004-100, Servicebio, China) were used with matching secondary antibodies (1:10000, BK-0201 and BK-0202, AimokeBio, China). The intensity of each band was analyzed using ImageJ (NIH, USA) software.

Histological assessment and immunohistochemical analyses

The paraformaldehyde-fixed hips in all five groups (n = 6) were decalcified with 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, embedded in paraffin and cut into sections measuring 3 μm thick. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and safranin O/fast green (SO) and scored blindly (PW) according to the Mankin scoring principle. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, primary antibodies for Runx2 (1:100, PA5-82787, Invitrogen, The Netherlands), ACAN (1:30, 13880-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Sox9 (1:200, PA5-81966, Invitrogen, The Netherlands), and ADAMTS4 (1:30, 11865-1-AP, Proteintech, China) were used with matching secondary antibodies (1:3000, BK-0201 and BK-0202, AimokeBio, China). A TUNEL detection kit (PF00006, Proteintech, China) was used per the manufacturer’s instructions for a terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay of each section. The average number of cells with positive staining in each section was estimated in three high-power fields by a blinded observer (PW).

Nanoindentation

Acetabular cartilage samples collected from the early control and model groups (n = 15) were subjected to nanoindentation. The samples were obtained from the central area of acetabular 3 o’clock position and then maintained hydrated with PBS solution. All indentations were performed using a TriboIndenter (Hysitron Inc., USA) equipped with a conospherical diamond probe tip, and the microscopic geomorphology was captured using a microscanning apparatus The operation of indentations was force-controlled to a maximum indentation depth of 500 nm, and the nanoindentation test consisted of the loading segment (10 s), holding segment (2 s), and unloading segment (10 s). The load–displacement curves obtained in indentation. Then the parameters of elastic modulus and hardness in different groups were assessed and calculated according to the previous report [16].

Kinematics assessment

The rabbits in the advanced control and model groups (n = 15) were subjected to hip kinematics assessment at 12 weeks after surgery to obtain sagittal hip motion and joint angles. The Vicon Nexus system (Vicon, UK), a marker-based passive optical motion capture system, was used to collect kinematic data. The rabbits were trained to cross a customized transparent polyethylene channel on level ground, and eight infrared optical motion capture cameras were used at 1000 Hz to record data simultaneously. Fourteen reflective markers were placed on bilateral pelvic and hindlimb landmarks: iliac crest, ischiatic tuber, third trochanter, femoral condyle, midshaft fibula, os calcis, and metatarsals. A randomly positioned dorsal marker served for calibration. Force platform data (vertical ground reaction force peaks) identified gait transitions (lift-off/touch-down). Successful gait cycle captures were validated using established criteria [17]. The hip joint angle was defined by the long axis of the femur with respect to that of the pelvis, with squatting in the resting position defined as the static state. Each rabbit was recorded 10 times within a week; ultimately, 112 successful trials in the static state and 94 successful trials in the hopping state in each group were selected for analysis. All training sessions were conducted for a minimum of one week prior to the formal experiment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution in this study are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and those without a normal distribution are reported as the median with an interquartile range. GraphPad Prism 10.1 (GraphPad, USA) and SPSS 21.0 (IBM, USA) were used for statistical analysis. We used the Shapiro‒Wilk test to evaluate the normality of the data and Bartlett’s test to examine the homogeneity of variance. Statistical significance between two groups was calculated with Student’s t tests, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used for multiple group comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis test and Mann–Whitney U test were used when the data were not normally distributed. Categorical data were tested using the chi-square test, whereas ranked data were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test. In the figures, error bars were based on the 95% confidence intervals. All P values are two tailed, and a P value < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

Hips with cam morphology presented with cartilage lesions and OA phenotypes that were not attributed to surgical trauma

In terms of animal welfare, no complications, especially accidental death, incision infection, hip dislocation, joint stiffness, or severe pain, were observed throughout the experimental period. Postmortem examination revealed healed capsular tissues with limited fibrotic scarring in both model and sham groups, without significant adhesive pathology. The control group maintained normal articular integrity with no spontaneous osteoarthritic changes.

We determined the morphological features and disease outcomes of the cam model. Gross examination revealed that the femoral heads of all rabbits in the early and advanced model groups (30/30, 100%) had an abnormal rotation toward the front (1–5 o’clock), with the extended bone projecting anteriorly from the neck, which suggested cam morphology (Fig. 2B, C). The acetabular cartilage exhibited diffuse pathological thickening, roughening, and surface fibrillation in the early stage and thickening, erosion, peeling, or focal defects in the advanced stage (Fig. 2E). Compared with those in the control and sham groups, significantly more severe lesions were observed at the 2–5 o’clock position of the acetabular rim and cartilage in the model rabbits (Fig. 2F, G). Thus, we specified the 3–4 o’clock position as the ROI for subsequent research on the ICRS score.

As expected, increased subchondral bone remodeling was detected in the ROI in the hip of the advanced model (Fig. 3A, B). In the acetabulum, the subchondral bone plate was thinner and the BV/TV ratio was significantly lower in the advanced model group than in the control group. There were also significant differences in Tb.Th, Tb.N, and FD, indicating that the thinner trabecular architecture was more heterogeneous. In addition, the bone density was stable, and osteophytes were present in all the rabbit models but not in the control rabbits. Thus, the subchondral bone in the hips of the models exhibited changes similar to those observed in humans with early OA. We confirmed that the radial alpha angle at the 1–3 o’clock position on the femoral head increased significantly (Fig. 3C, D).

We further explored the pathological processes of cartilage lesions in a rabbit model and identified the potential impacts of surgical trauma on the cartilage. In terms of model groups, histological findings in the early stage included chondrocyte dedifferentiation, proliferation and clustering, as well as loss of tidemarks (Fig. 4A, B). And cartilage in advanced stage presented as a typical osteoarthritis phenotype, including chondrocyte clustering and loss of cellularity. The control groups did not present these changes, and the phenotype of sham group was more similar to the advanced control group. The Mankin scores are presented in Fig. 4C. The articular surfaces in early model specimens exhibited markedly increased surface irregularity and textural roughness compared to controls. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly reduced cartilage modulus and hardness in the model group relative to control specimens. Therefore, the biomechanical properties of the acetabular cartilage in the early model individuals had already deteriorated significantly (Fig. 4D).

We noted the downregulation of SOX9, an anabolism-related biomarker, and the upregulation of RUNX2, a catabolism-related biomarker, in the early-stage impingement (Fig. 5A); However, significant differences were not observed in the expression of MMP13 and COL2. In advanced stages, the model group displayed suppressed anabolism (SOX9, COL2) and enhanced catabolism (MMP13, RUNX2) versus controls. Sham groups showed mild, non-significant anabolic reductions, aligning closer to controls. These results indicated that homeostasis was not fully disrupted in the early stage and that homeostatic equilibrium shifted with continued impact, indicating damage. IHC staining confirmed that cartilage metabolism remained active in the early stage (Fig. 5B, D). We also confirmed that the apoptosis of the chondrocytes occurred during the progression of advanced OA rather than its onset (Fig. 5C, E).

In summary, we successfully created a rabbit model of cam morphology and acetabular cartilage lesions. Hips with cam morphology exhibited an osteoarthritis phenotype in the advanced stage, and these changes were not determined by surgical trauma.

The hip motion of cam morphology individuals resembles that of healthy controls, maintaining a predictable movement pattern

We first assessed the kinematic pattern of healthy hips in the sagittal plane (Fig. 6B). Healthy rabbits exhibited a half-bounding gait pattern, in which the hindlimbs push off and move together [17]. The arc of motion of the hip during half-bounding is very small, and its mobility is contributed mainly by spinal and pelvic movements. This finding is consistent with a previous report on Ochotona rufescens (Lagomorpha) [18]. This is because the spine is the primary segment responsible for propulsive movement in the half-bounding gait, pelvic movements and the protraction and retraction of the hindlimb (Supplementary material 2). Therefore, we did not distinguish or define the degree of flexion or extension of the hip but rather used the hip angle. The average curve of the hip angle was bimodal, and the hip angle was greatest at touch-down. At rest (after squatting), the pelvis was in an almost vertical position, and the femur was almost horizontal (Supplementary material 2). At this time, the actual weight-bearing area was the 1–5 o’clock part of the acetabulum and the femoral head.

Overall, the hip motion of the cam morphology rabbit models resembled that of the healthy controls (Fig. 6B). Although rabbits with cam morphology presented slightly reduced hip angles during all stages of movement, with significant reductions upon touch-down (95.13° (89.00°, 104.9°) vs. 88.24° (84.94°, 95.41°)), there was no significant difference in the arc of motion of the hip joint between the healthy controls and the cam morphology rabbit models (15.07° (10.92°, 20.17°) vs. 15.88° (11.76°, 19.22°)), nor in the static angle (84.64° (75.97°, 98.25°) vs. 79.46° (76.20°, 85.75°)) (Fig. 6C). The typical impingement was observed in the weight-bearing area (Fig. 6D). Therefore, despite potential fibrosis caused by surgical trauma, our model still preserved the natural and predictable movement pattern.

Discussion

The evidence presented in this study provides support for the utilization of a modified cam morphology rabbit model to advance biomedical research on human FAIS acetabular cartilage lesions. This model accurately represents the pathology of human cam morphology, and the slow progression of the disease allows us to identify key nodes to explore the underlying molecular mechanism between impingement and subsequent articular cartilage degeneration.

We disrupted the growth plate and the epiphyseal tubercle simultaneously. Preliminary evidence suggests that primary cam morphology represents physiological compensation for the supraphysiological load on the proximal femoral growth plate during skeletal maturation, reflecting epiphyseal extension and epiphyseal cupping overgrowth, to confer physeal stability [5, 19]. The interlocking mechanism between the epiphyseal tubercle and metaphyseal fossa is important for physical stability in the early stage [20]. However, in children, hips with cam morphology have a mismatched smaller epiphyseal tubercle and larger metaphyseal fossa, as well as a larger epiphyseal cupping, than those without FAIS [21]. There may be a causal link between the potential microinstability caused by mismatch and the progression of cam morphology [20–22]. We reproduce the instability of the physis and subsequent compensatory epiphyseal extension by disrupting the tubercle, similar to what occurs in the slipped capital femoral epiphysis [23–25]. Thus, our model successfully demonstrates the characteristics of both primary and secondary cam morphology.

Unlike models of joint instability-induced or chemical-induced OA, this rabbit model of cam morphology reflects developmental abnormalities and depends on normal joint function to ensure that motion-related impingement appears in the hip joint. Joint instability and long-term joint immobilization have been proven to induce OA in animals [26]. Once the soft tissue damage caused by surgical procedures permanently interferes with normal joint motion patterns, the true cause of cartilage lesions in our model becomes elusive. Our three-step surgical approach effectively avoids this potential interference. This study provides the first kinematic dataset of rabbit hip joints, confirming that rabbit models of cam morphology do not exhibit significant limb dysfunction. Moreover, these data suggested that the cam morphology and acetabular lesions were located in a weight-bearing area that could maintain contact despite a limited hip range of motion.

New evidence revealed a molecular link between mechanical impingement and cartilage lesions in cam-type FAIS. The OA phenotype appears in the early stage of FAIS, as the inflammation and degeneration of the synovium and labrum are much less severe [27]. Hashimoto et al. reported that, compared with that from end-stage OA patients and normal controls, cartilage from cam-type FAIS patients presented more active anabolism and catabolism and expressed significantly higher levels of IL-8, as well as CXCL3, CXCL6, and CCL3L1 [28]. Similarly, Haneda et al. reported increased molecular inflammation and metabolic activity in cam-type FAIS cartilage, with increased levels of IL-1β, MMP-13, ADAMTS-4, NITEGE, and COL2 and similar levels of cellular viability, compared with those in DDH OA and normal controls. The rabbit model enables the simulation of active metabolism and changes in key biomarkers, as well as the early OA phenotype, in human cartilage. Anabolism and catabolism were active and balanced in the early stage and then gradually became imbalanced, with catabolism dominating.

This study has multiple Limitations. First, the modeling procedures can be difficult for beginners to learn. Second, our experimental animal center provided sufficient housing space and was suitable for substantial physical activity for the rabbits. The rabbits exhibited unpredictable aggressive behaviors because of sexual development after approximately 12 weeks of age. As activity level could not be quantitatively assessed, it was only described as high. Third, rabbits differ from humans in terms of cartilage thickness and joint biomechanics; in particular, rabbits do not possess organized fibrocartilaginous labrum. Fourth, this study exclusively used male rabbits because fluctuating estrogen levels may induce confounding effects on cartilage pathology. Since cam morphology predominantly occurs in young active human males, employing young male rabbits better simulates this demographic. Furthermore, we did not collect biomechanics data during hopping and did not provide direct evidence to confirm the impact zone or contact stress. These steps will be conducted in subsequent finite element analysis.

Conclusion

The modified rabbit model of cam morphology successfully reflected, to a major extent, both the human disease phenotype and the underlying disease pathophysiology, shedding new light for biomedical research on the progression of cam-type FAIS.
