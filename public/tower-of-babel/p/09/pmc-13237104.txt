Direct synthesis of amorphous metal–organic frameworks from nanoclusters
Authors: Nanna L Baun, Sabina S Mortensen, Heloisa N Bordallo, Luke Keenan, Kirsten M Ø Jensen, Adam F Sapnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe direct synthesis of amorphous metal–organic frameworks (aMOFs) is an appealing yet often avoided approach due to the perceived unpredictability of amorphous network formation. Here, we develop a strategy for aMOF synthesis using pre-formed nanoclusters and rigid organic linkers, providing enhanced control over disorder and defect chemistry while bypassing the traditional crystallise–amorphise approach. By systematically comparing this approach to other direct synthesis routes and using X-ray pair distribution function, thermogravimetric, and statistical analyses, we establish key design principles governing aMOF formation. We demonstrate that kinetic control—fast reactions under basic conditions at room temperature—suppresses crystallisation and drives amorphous network formation. The nanocluster approach consistently yields highly disordered frameworks with the shortest coherence lengths among direct synthesis methods. Additionally, we show that tuning metal composition through doping with kinetically inert cations restricts coordination reversibility, significantly increasing defect density and structural disorder. Structural analysis reveals that while aMOFs share motifs with crystalline polymorphs, they cannot be directly mapped onto known structures, highlighting the importance of more nuanced characterisation approaches. By developing a systematic approach to aMOF design, this work provides a foundation for tailoring structural disorder, expanding their potential for catalysis, adsorption, and transport applications.Subject terms: Structural materials, Metal-organic frameworks

Introduction

Crystal engineering has played a pivotal role in the rapid development of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), leading to a diverse range of materials with applications in gas storage, catalysis, and drug delivery1. Crystalline frameworks have been the primary focus due to their well-defined structure–property relationships, which enable rational material design2–4. In contrast, amorphous MOFs (aMOFs) are gaining attention for their enhanced processability, catalytic properties, and greater mechanical integrity when formed as monoliths5–11. However, their discovery and characterisation remain a significant challenge9.

Most aMOFs are produced through a crystallise–amorphise approach, in which a crystalline MOF is first synthesised and then amorphised by heat or pressure6,9. Depending on the system and conditions, such treatments can also yield glassy MOFs that exhibit a glass transition, representing one subset within the broader family of non-crystalline MOF materials10. These methods pose a fundamental bottleneck in aMOF discovery, limiting their structural diversity by restricting them to derivatives of crystalline frameworks. As a result, aMOFs are often perceived merely as disordered analogues of crystalline MOFs6. To fully realise their potential as functional materials, synthetic strategies that bypass the need for a crystalline MOF are essential.

Direct synthesis offers a promising alternative, as it avoids the need for a crystalline MOF precursor12. However, it is often overlooked due to challenges in controlling the formation of amorphous networks. The kinetic nature of aMOFs and their relatively flat energy landscapes can lead to unpredictable structures, making rational design of direct synthesis difficult. Furthermore, polymorphism—a well-known challenge in MOFs—is even more complicated in aMOFs, where distinguishing between different polyamorphic phases is notoriously difficult yet can have a significant impact on material properties. For example, computational studies on the Fe‑BTC (BTC: 1,3,5-benzetricarboxylate) aMOFs have shown that variations in local order and defect concentrations independently influence properties such as pore size and surface area13,14. While a small number of studies have demonstrated that direct synthesis of aMOFs is possible by kinetically suppressing crystallisation, these examples remain comparatively rare and system-specific, underscoring that generalisable design rules for the rational, reproducible synthesis of aMOFs are still largely lacking12,15.

To address this, we focus on the related Fe‑BDC family, a system known for its extensive crystalline polymorphism16. By reacting an Fe salt with 1,4‑benzenedicarboxylic acid (H2BDC), multiple crystalline polymorphs can be formed, including MIL‑101 (MIL; Material Institute Lavoisier), MIL‑88, MIL‑68, MIL‑53, MIL‑235, and Fe(BDC)(DMF)16–21. These phases exhibit distinct local structures, topologies, and responsive behaviour. In 2022, an amorphous Fe‑BDC material with promising catalytic properties was reported and proposed as the amorphous analogue of the crystalline MIL‑101 framework17,22. However, given the extensive polymorphism within Fe‑BDC materials and the limited structural characterisation provided, it remains unclear whether this aMOF can be definitively assigned as the amorphous counterpart to MIL‑101. This raises the broader question: what defines the amorphous counterpart to a crystalline material? It is a question that has long been posed across families of important materials, such as oxide glasses, metallic glasses, and amorphous semiconductors23–25. This issue becomes especially significant when amorphous materials are synthesised directly, and therefore, no obvious parent crystalline material is present. Addressing this question requires a systematic approach to direct aMOF synthesis.

One strategy that successfully expanded the crystalline MOF field—particularly for complex, hierarchically structured frameworks—was the use of pre-synthesised secondary building units (SBUs)26–29. By directly reacting discrete SBUs or nanoclusters with organic linkers, greater control over framework connectivity and topology can be achieved. This strategy has already enabled the synthesis of crystalline Fe-BDC polymorphs such as MIL-8818. While direct synthesis of aMOFs from nanoclusters has been demonstrated using highly flexible linkers to kinetically suppress crystallisation, the combination of nanoclusters with rigid organic linkers to generate aMOFs remains essentially unexplored30.

In this work, we challenge the crystallise–amorphise paradigm by developing a nanocluster-based approach for the direct synthesis of aMOFs, specifically non-crystalline MOFs without a glass transition. We synthesise a series of M-BDC materials (M = Fe, Fe₂Cr, FeCr₂, and Cr) to investigate how synthesis conditions and chemical composition influence aMOF structure. Through a systematic study comparing nanocluster-based synthesis to one-pot and stepwise approaches using X-ray pair distribution function, thermogravimetric, and statistical analyses, we establish fundamental design principles for aMOF formation. Our goal is simple yet unconventional: To produce aMOFs with (i) well‑defined local structure, (ii) minimal long‑range order, and (iii) tuneable defect concentrations. By targeting highly disordered frameworks, we seek to maximise sample isotropy, a property that has the potential to enhance mass transport, while controlling defect concentrations can be leveraged for improved catalytic performance15,31. This study expands the structural diversity of aMOFs, moving beyond current limitations and providing a rational framework for their design.

Results and discussion

Overview

We begin by providing an overview of our approach and outlining the key sections of this study. Our primary focus is to develop the nanocluster (NC) approach for aMOF synthesis and compare it with one‑pot (OP) and stepwise (SW) methods [Fig. 1a]. We thus compare three different synthesis methods: In the NC approach, a pre‑synthesised nanocluster is used as a reagent that is reacted directly with an organic linker in solution. In the OP approach, the metal salt is directly combined with the organic linker in solution. The SW approach is an intermediate between the NC and OP methods: the metal salt is first reacted with acetate ions to form the nanocluster in solution, which is then reacted with the organic linker.

For this study, we selected the M₃O trimer as our nanocluster due to its role as an archetypal SBU in the MOF field [Fig. 1b]. The M₃O trimer is a fundamental building block of many canonical MOFs, such as MIL‑100, MIL‑101, and MIL‑5316,17,26,32. Importantly, it can be readily synthesised as a discrete nanocluster ([M3O(CH3CO2)6(H2O)3]NO3) using acetate groups as capping ligands, making it well‑suited for the NC approach26. The M₃O trimer has been extensively studied, and its ability to incorporate multiple metal types enables us to investigate both the chemical composition and synthetic method in the direct synthesis of aMOFs33–38. We specifically selected Fe and Cr for their similar ionic radii (0.645 and 0.615 Å for Fe3+ and Cr3+, respectively), enabling the complete compositional series to be synthesised, and their different chemical lability39.

This study is ordered as follows: (i) nanocluster synthesis, including formation kinetics and atomic structure, (ii) optimisation of the NC approach for aMOF synthesis, (iii) characterisation of aMOFs, (iv) investigation of local structure and defects, (v) characterising the extended structure, and (vi) establishing design principles for aMOF synthesis. In turn, we evaluate the NC, OP, and SW approaches while exploring the impact of composition and methodology on aMOF structure.

Nanocluster synthesis and formation

Before developing the NC approach, we first investigated the atomic structure and formation kinetics of the M3O nanocluster itself. Examining the kinetics of M3O formation is particularly important for understanding the OP and SW synthesis methods later. The M3O nanocluster is synthesised by combining concentrated solutions of a metal nitrate salt and sodium acetate, followed by heating and crystallisation [see “Methods”].

To study this reaction, we employed in situ pair distribution function (PDF) analysis using X‑ray total scattering measurements in a custom flow‑cell setup (MAX IV, Sweden), where sodium acetate is injected into the metal salt solution [Supplementary Fig. 1]. This allowed us to monitor the nanocluster formation in real time as the reagents are combined. Specifically, the emergence of the metal–metal (M–M) distance (~3.3 Å) in the PDF provided a direct means to track nanocluster formation in solution [Supplementary Fig. 2].

The results revealed that the rate of nanocluster formation is highly dependent on metal chemistry. Fe3O forms almost instantaneously upon the addition of sodium acetate, whereas Cr3O forms significantly more slowly. By fitting the temporal evolution of the M–M PDF peak intensity to a simple empirical model, we extracted pseudo‑rate constants for the reactions [Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. 2]. The analysis showed that Fe3O forms almost 18 times faster than Cr3O. These differences are attributed to the metals’ electronic configurations and ligand exchange rates. Fe3+, with its high‑spin d5 configuration, undergoes rapid ligand exchange, whereas Cr3+, with a d3 configuration, exhibits much slower ligand exchange, the slowest among first‑row transition metals40. These substantial differences in reaction speeds were anticipated to have significant consequences for the formation of M‑BDC systems later on.

Next, large single crystals of the M3O nanoclusters were synthesised from solutions with nominal compositions of Fe3, Fe2Cr, FeCr2, and Cr3 [see “Methods”]26. X‑ray fluorescence (XRF) confirmed the bimetallic composition of the Fe2Cr and FeCr2 nanoclusters [Supplementary Fig. 3]. Semi-quantitative analysis suggested a slightly lower proportion of Cr than in the nominal ratios [Supplementary Table 1]. This is unsurprising, given the significantly slower reaction rate of Cr3+ compared to Fe3+ observed in our in situ PDF studies. Nonetheless, single‑crystal X‑ray diffraction confirmed that the M3O nanocluster structure was present across all four samples [Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table 2]. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that the structure of M3O nanoclusters is minimally affected by the inclusion of different metal cations41,42.

aMOF synthesis & optimisation

Extensive research has identified the synthetic conditions that optimise MOF crystallinity43,44. Building on this knowledge, we focused on conditions opposite to those typically used to produce highly crystalline materials. For crystalline Fe‑BDC systems, high temperatures, long reaction times, and acidic conditions are known to favour crystallinity, which is consistent with thermodynamic control16. In contrast, to achieve aMOFs, we hypothesised that rapid, room‑temperature syntheses under basic conditions would promote disorder. This approach deliberately deviates from standard practices aimed at attaining crystallinity.

To test this hypothesis, we systematically studied Fe‑BDC and Cr‑BDC, aiming to produce materials whose diffraction patterns contained no Bragg peaks. Starting with a synthesis optimised for a highly crystalline Fe‑BDC polymorph, we systematically varied key parameters, including temperature, reaction time, reagent concentration, modulator (acetic acid) volume, and pH16,26. Details of these studies are provided in Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Figs. 4, 5 and Supplementary Table 3.

In summary, room‑temperature reactions using high reactant concentrations, low modulator volumes, and the addition of base were optimal for forming aMOFs from both Fe3O and Cr3O nanoclusters. Navigating the ligand exchange rates of Fe3+ and Cr3+ was crucial in determining the optimal synthesis conditions for aMOF formation40. The significantly slower ligand exchange kinetics of Cr3+ required reaction conditions that facilitated sufficient metal–linker interactions to enable Cr‑BDC to form an amorphous network. At the same time, the faster ligand exchange of Fe3+ meant that conditions had to be carefully tuned to suppress coordination reversibility, which would otherwise favour the formation of crystalline Fe‑BDC polymorphs. Balancing these competing factors was essential for achieving an amorphous product across different metal compositions.

Having established suitable conditions for directly synthesising aMOFs, we synthesised our complete series of samples across various compositions and methods. We synthesised M‑BDC using three synthetic methods (NC, OP, and SW) and four compositions (Fe, Fe2Cr, FeCr2, and Cr), yielding a total of 12 aMOF samples [see “Methods”]. This systematic approach allowed us to compare the influence of both composition and synthesis methods on the resulting aMOFs. M-BDC samples were obtained as a viscous gel-like sample upon mixing and washing. Upon drying in air, these gels formed large monolithic pieces that took the shape of their container [Supplementary Fig. 6]. Then, upon drying in an oven, macroscopic pieces were obtained. For measurement, the monoliths were ground into a fine powder.

Preliminary characterisation of the 12 aMOF samples was performed using X‑ray total scattering and powder diffraction measurements [Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 7]. The scattering patterns for all samples exhibited broad and poorly defined regions of diffuse scattering. One sample, FeCr2‑BDC (OP), contained a minor crystalline impurity phase identified as unreacted linker material [Supplementary Fig. 8]. Comparison between the scattering patterns revealed that the regions of diffuse scattering were largely consistent across all samples. However, slight variations were observed: Fe‑BDC (SW) showed slightly more defined scattering regions, while Cr‑BDC (NC) exhibited less well‑defined regions. XRF spectroscopy confirmed the presence of both Fe and Cr in all six mixed‑metal samples, and semi-quantitative analysis indicated higher Fe:Cr for Fe2Cr‑BDC samples than FeCr2‑BDC, as expected [Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 4]. Scanning electron microscopy images from the three Fe-BDC samples revealed inhomogeneous morphologies for each synthesis method [Supplementary Fig. 10].

Evolved gas analysis (EGA) was conducted to evaluate the decomposition temperatures of the M‑BDC samples [Fig. 2a, Table 1]. For comparison, EGA measurements were also performed on the Fe3O and Cr3O nanoclusters and the H2BDC linker [Supplementary Fig. 11]. As previously reported, Cr3O exhibited a higher decomposition temperature than Fe3O45,46. For H2BDC, a rapid mass loss was observed at 310 °C due to sublimation of the linker47.

The thermal decomposition of M‑BDC occurred in two distinct stages: (1) desolvation and dehydroxylation, followed by (2) decomposition of the organic linker. The first stage accounted for 14–22% of the total mass loss in most samples, except for Fe‑BDC (SW), which exhibited a 38% mass loss [Table 1]. We note that stage (2) often proceeded via a complex, multi-stage decomposition route, which has previously been observed for other highly defective aMOF systems48.

TG‑MS analysis showed that the initial mass loss in most samples was due to the loss of DMF [Supplementary Fig. 12]. This is consistent with previous reports showing that DMF can coordinate as the terminal (axial) ligand on M3O clusters21. Cr‑BDC (NC) was an exception, in which no DMF release was detected. Instead, the mass loss in this sample was attributed almost entirely to dehydroxylation [Supplementary Fig. 13]. The mass loss associated with this process (~17%) was comparable to the loss of DMF from the other M-BDC samples, suggesting that Cr‑BDC (NC) contains a much higher degree of hydroxylation. In this case, the terminal hydroxo/aqua ligands from the aqueous nanocluster synthesis likely remain coordinated, as the slow ligand-exchange kinetics of Cr3+ hinder substitution by DMF upon aMOF synthesis in DMF. This, in turn, implies a lower proportion of Cr sites bound to the organic linker, indicating a greater degree of metal–linker defects. The second stage, corresponding to linker decomposition, resulted in a mass loss of 41–56% in most samples [Table 1]. However, Cr‑BDC (NC) exhibited a significantly lower mass loss of only 27%, suggesting reduced organic linker content.

The decomposition temperature of each sample was determined as the point at which the highest rate of mass loss occurred, as identified from the derivative thermogram (DTG) [Table 1]. Across all M-BDC samples, decomposition temperatures ranged from 288 to 447 °C, with the most significant variations primarily attributed to differences in composition rather than the specific synthesis method used. A clear trend was observed: as the Cr content increased, the decomposition temperature systematically decreased. This behaviour contrasts with that of the isolated M3O nanoclusters, for which Cr3O exhibits greater intrinsic thermal stability than Fe3O. The decrease in decomposition temperature with increasing Cr content, therefore, suggests a loss of structural integrity of the Cr3O cluster within the amorphous network, implying that additional structural motifs are present beyond intact M3O SBUs. Further discussion of this structural instability is provided later. Additionally, some samples exhibited multiple peaks in the DTG, indicating that decomposition occurred in distinct stages, likely due to stepwise breakdown of the framework. This behaviour aligns with previous reports on aMOFs, further reinforcing the complex nature of their thermal decomposition48.

TGA also allowed us to estimate the degree of saturation of the M‑BDC samples [Table 2]. A fully coordinated sample (i.e., one without metal–linker defects) corresponds to 100% saturation. This was achieved by assuming that the residual mass after decomposition corresponds solely to the metal oxide (M2O3), following the method outlined in ref. 49. [Supplementary Note 3]. These saturation values are semi-quantitative but internally self-consistent and are therefore most meaningful when considered comparatively rather than absolutely. Additionally, the composition of each sample was also estimated [Supplementary Table 5].

No strong correlation was observed between the chemical composition of M‑BDC and the degree of saturation. However, the NC approach generally produced samples with lower degrees of saturation. This suggests that using pre‑formed SBUs leads to a higher concentration of metal–linker defects, likely due to ligand exchange between the capping acetate anions and the BDC linker.

Several systems exhibited high degrees of saturation (>90%), indicating very few metal–linker defects. Conversely, Cr‑BDC (NC) exhibited an exceptionally low degree of saturation of 31%, consistent with its reduced linker content. These results reinforce the idea that Cr‑BDC (NC) has a significant proportion of metal–linker defects, aligning with the greater hydroxylation observed in this sample.

We used Fourier transform infra-red (FT‑IR) spectroscopy to probe the binding modes present in M-BDC [Fig. 2b]. FT‑IR spectra were recorded in the range of 400 to 4000 cm−1 [Supplementary Fig. 14]. Key bands at 1560 and 1380 cm−1 were observed in all samples, corresponding to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the carboxylate group, respectively41,50. These bands confirm the metal–linker interaction, with their positions indicating that the BDC linker coordinates to the metal centres in a bidentate fashion. However, the asymmetric stretch at 1560 cm−1 was notably broad in most samples, suggesting a degree of linker unsaturation, consistent with the metal–linker defects inferred from TGA51. Broadening in FT-IR spectra is also indicative of increased structural disorder, which is further reflected in the complex and sample-dependent features observed in the fingerprint region (~1250–750 cm−1), where variations in aromatic C–H bending and carboxylate deformation modes are expected to be particularly sensitive to local coordination heterogeneity and disorder.

The M–O stretch, observed around 520 cm−1, was similar for Fe‑BDC and Fe₂Cr‑BDC52. However, the peak showed noticeable shifts, broadening, and asymmetry at higher Cr content. These effects were most pronounced in Cr‑BDC (NC), where significant peak broadening and shifts indicate inhomogeneity in Cr–O interactions, likely due to local structural variations. Other features in the FT‑IR spectrum of Cr‑BDC (NC) align with the TGA findings, supporting the conclusions of increased hydroxylation and reduced linker content in this sample. For example, the reduced intensity and presence of a prominent shoulder on the symmetric carboxylate stretch, as well as the presence of an additional band at 2900 and 3400 cm−1, which correspond to the C–H stretch of a methyl group and hydroxyl groups, respectively [Supplementary Fig. 14]53. These observations may indicate that Cr‑BDC (NC) exhibits distinct structural characteristics relative to other M‑BDC materials and may retain some acetate capping groups, further highlighting the influence of composition and synthesis methods on aMOF structure.

Nitrogen adsorption isotherms were measured at 77 K for the three Fe-BDC members of the family [Supplementary Fig. 15]. Both Fe-BDC (OP) and (SW) were non-porous, with maximum adsorbed values of only 8.8 and 0.3 cm3 g−1, respectively. For Fe-BDC (NC), a type III isotherm was observed with a maximal uptake of 135.8 cm3 g−1. We note that under the drying conditions employed here, low-porosity materials were expected to form54.

Our results indicate that we have successfully formed amorphous M‑BDC materials and have revealed varying degrees of metal–linker defects, with trends linked to composition and synthesis method. Collectively, these results emphasise the critical role of Cr and the NC approach in driving the formation of highly defective frameworks.

Local structure and node defects

A primary goal of our study was to obtain aMOFs with a well‑defined local structure. We used pair distribution function (PDF) analysis of the total scattering data to investigate the atomic structure of the M‑BDC materials. Visual comparison of the PDFs for all 12 samples revealed striking similarities, with key peaks observed at ca. 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, and 3.4 Å [Fig. 3a]. Moreover, we evaluated the reproducibility of the synthesis methods and found that almost identical PDFs were obtained in each case [Supplementary Fig. 16].

We first consider the PDFs of the nanoclusters themselves. PDFs calculated from our experimentally derived single‑crystal structures of the M3O nanocluster were nearly identical across metal chemistries, highlighting the negligible impact of metal composition on the nanocluster’s core structure in the solid state [Fig. 3b]. The positions of the characteristic peaks in the calculated PDFs closely matched those in the experimental PDFs, corresponding to (i) M–O, (ii) O–O, and (iii) M–M atomic distances.

Moving on to the experimental aMOF PDFs, the primary distinction between the M3O nanocluster and the M‑BDC PDFs was the relative intensity of the M–M (ca. 3.3 Å) to the M–O (2.0 Å) peaks, which varied with sample composition. For Fe‑BDC, the relative peak intensity aligned with the expected ratio from the nanocluster. However, as the proportion of Cr increased, the relative intensity of the M–M peak decreased, suggesting a lower degree of condensation in the local structure, that is, fewer M–M correlations for a given number of M–O bonds.

To explain this, we explored alternative local structural motifs, including octahedra, dimers, and chains, as potential causes of the observed decrease in M–M intensity. The rationale for these motifs is as follows: (i) octahedra and dimers have been noted as intermediates in the formation of the Cr3O nanocluster and (ii) chains are a natural choice arising from the known polymorphism in the M‑BDC crystalline systems16,55,56. Support for the presence of such defect nodes also comes from the thermal analysis: Cr-BDC (NC) exhibits the lowest decomposition temperature in the series, despite Cr3O clusters being more thermally stable than Fe3O clusters in isolation. This suggests that, within the amorphous network, the Cr3O motif is not fully preserved and likely undergoes partial dissociation, consistent with incorporation of defect nodes such as dimers or octahedra. We also note that the high degree of hydroxylation observed for Cr-BDC (NC) is compatible with such partially dissociated motifs. Alternative defect nodes would require extensive reorganisation or ligand redistribution, which is unlikely given the slow ligand-exchange kinetics of Cr3+.

Given that the M–M peak remains observable at all compositions, albeit at lower intensity, this rules out the possibility of purely octahedral or dimeric structures (since neither possesses the characteristic M–M peak). Conversely, although the chain structure contains the M–M peak, its PDF is almost identical to that of the trimer itself, eliminating the possibility of a solely chain-based structure.

Thus, given the EGA and FT‑IR results, which indicated varying degrees of unsaturation (i.e., metal–linker defects) in all samples, we hypothesised that the local structure consisted of trimers alongside defective nodes such as octahedra, dimers, or chains. By calculating linear combinations of the PDF for trimers and these defect motifs, we could simulate what different populations of node/defect-node might look like and see if these can capture the experimentally observed variation [Fig. 3c]. The linear combination with the chain motif failed to induce sufficient changes in the relative intensity of the M–M peak and was not considered further. On the other hand, both the trimer–octahedra and trimer–dimer combinations were able to augment the M–M peak successfully. The trimer–octahedra combination also showed a reduction in the O–O peak at 4.5 Å, while the trimer–dimer combination introduced shifts in the M–O and O–O peaks and a new peak around 3.7 Å.

This approach allowed us to estimate the proportion of defect nodes from the relative intensities of the M–O and M–M peaks [Supplementary Note 4]. As it was challenging to definitively distinguish between octahedral and dimeric defects (or a combination of the two), the proportion of defects was quantified for both scenarios [Table 3]. The defect proportion varied more with composition than with the synthesis method. The NC approach consistently yielded fewer defects among synthetic methods, while the OP approach produced the highest defect concentrations. Mixed‑metal samples exhibited more significant defect concentration variability than Fe‑BDC or Cr‑BDC.

To complement the PDF analysis, we also performed X-ray absorption spectroscopy at both Fe and Cr K-edges for all M-BDC materials. In the XANES region of the spectra, all Fe and Cr features were very similar [Supplementary Fig. 17, Supplementary Table 6]. In all Fe-containing samples, the Fe edge position was ~7125 eV, and the expected pre-edge feature at 7113 eV was observed, both consistent with analogous Fe-BTC materials containing Fe3+ in an octahedral environment57,58. In all Cr-containing samples, the Cr edge position was 6001 eV, and the expected pre-edge feature at 5990 eV was observed, both consistent with a related, crystalline material, MIL-100 (Cr), containing Cr3+ in an octahedral environment59. The EXAFS region of the spectra was then transformed from energy to k-space up to 15 Å−1, and the Fourier transform was calculated to obtain a real-space map of the Fe and Cr scattering path lengths [Supplementary Figs. 18, 19]. The uncorrected Fourier transform of χ(k) was dominated by a prominent peak between 1.47 and 1.56 Å for Fe and 1.50 to 1.57 Å for Cr, corresponding to the M–O peaks. The slight variations in this peak indicate some degree of inhomogeneity in the local structure, as commonly expected for an aMOF, particularly given the defective nature of these systems. Similar variations are observed in the higher-R region of the Fourier transform, again consistent with the presence of a superposition of varying defect structures.

Ultimately, the key finding is that Cr content is the primary factor influencing the proportion of node defects in the M‑BDC samples, with the synthesis method playing a secondary role. We previously highlighted the slower ligand-exchange rate of Cr3+ compared to Fe3+, which directly results in slower assembly of the Cr3O nanocluster than Fe3O. Given that aMOFs represent metastable states, these kinetic effects also strongly influence the formation of Cr‑containing M‑BDC frameworks. Under kinetic control, incorporating Cr results in a greater degree of defects as reversible ligand exchange becomes increasingly hindered, preventing the exchange necessary to ‘heal’ misconnections. This emphasises the significant role of composition in influencing local structure in aMOFs.

Extended structure

While the local structure of M-BDC frameworks is primarily influenced by chemistry, both composition and synthetic approach play significant roles in determining the extended structure. The PDFs reveal four main clusters of overlapping peaks in the medium range (centred around 7, 9, 11, and 13 Å) and three distinct shapes in the long range [Fig. 4a]. Most samples exhibit a broad hump around ~22 Å, while Fe-BDC (SW) and FeCr2-BDC (OP) display weak peaks, and Cr-BDC (NC) is entirely flat. Beyond 30 Å, all samples are featureless, reflecting their amorphous nature.

The NC approach consistently yields the least-ordered frameworks among the three synthesis methods, as evidenced by shorter coherence lengths (i.e., the maximum real-space distance over which atomic pair correlations persist, reflecting the size of structurally correlated domains in the material). While NC samples exhibit the lowest coherence lengths, neither OP nor SW consistently produces the most extended order, suggesting a more stochastic influence of these syntheses on structural coherence. We note that coherence length and node defect concentration are related but not directly correlated descriptors of disorder: coherence length reflects the spatial extent of network correlations, whereas node defects describe local deviations in node geometry, and the two can therefore vary independently.

Even in the absence of a structural model, statistical analyses of PDFs can provide robust insights. To quantify structural similarities among the experimental PDFs, we used the Pearson correlation coefficient, which ranges from +1 (complete correlation) to −1 (complete anti-correlation)60,61. Then, hierarchical clustering was used to organise these similarities into a dendrogram, where closely related PDFs cluster at shorter branch heights, that is, towards the bottom of the dendrogram, indicating greater similarity [Fig. 4b]62. This approach offers an alternative to the full Pearson correlation matrix, effectively grouping structurally similar materials [Supplementary Fig. 20].

This analysis immediately identifies five separate groups of samples, with Cr-BDC (NC) identified as the most dissimilar sample: (i) Cr-BDC (NC), (ii) Fe-BDC (SW), (iii) Fe-BDC (NC), Fe2Cr-BDC (NC), FeCr2-BDC (NC), and Fe-BDC (OP), (iv) Cr-BDC (OP, SW) and (v) Fe2Cr-BDC (OP, SW) and FeCr2-BDC (OP, SW). By plotting these five main groups of PDFs, we can see that the analysis successfully clusters similar PDFs together [Supplementary Fig. 21]. This naturally leads to the question: How do their atomic structures differ?

To gain insight into the underlying atomic structure, we compared the M-BDC aMOFs to known crystalline polymorphs: MIL-101, MIL-88B, MOF-235, MIL-53, and MIL-68 [Fig. 4c]16. While we do not expect perfect agreement between our highly disordered aMOFs and these ordered crystalline structures, these comparisons provide a reference for interpreting the medium-range order in our samples. MIL-101, MIL-88B, and MOF-235 are trimer-based frameworks [Fig. 5a]. MIL-101 features nanoclusters assembled into supertetrahedral SBUs, while MIL-88B and MOF-235 have simpler networks formed simply by bridged nanoclusters. MIL-53 and MIL-68, in contrast, consist of chains of octahedra linked by BDC, and given the varying degrees of defect sites in M-BDC, these motifs may also be present [Fig. 5b]. Another key difference is the relative orientation of the trimer or chains with respect to the linker: in MIL-101 and MIL-68, they adopt a cis-configuration, whereas MIL-88B, MOF-235, and MIL-53 adopt a trans-configuration.

The calculated PDFs for these crystalline polymorphs display sharp peaks extending beyond 20 Å, in stark contrast to the broad features of the aMOFs [Fig. 4c]. However, certain peak positions align with experimental data. To account for the structural disorder in the aMOFs, we considered how to artificially introduce disorder into the crystalline structures. We chose a simple approach: increasing the thermal displacement parameter (Biso), which broadens the peaks and simulates the effect of structural disorder.

Subtle differences were observed in the medium-range order (5 to 15 Å), while more pronounced variations appeared in the long range (>15 Å). However, as most experimental features fall within the medium range, definitive classification of the aMOFs remains challenging. Nevertheless, statistical analysis provided further insight by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between each experimental PDF and the five crystalline polymorphs.

Notably, instead of different PDF groups aligning with different polymorphs, nearly all M-BDC samples—except Cr-BDC (NC)—correlated most strongly with MOF-235 [Supplementary Fig. 22]. This suggests a preference for a trans-orientation of trimers relative to the linker. Cr-BDC (NC) did not correlate with any structure, which is expected given the absence of extended structural features in its PDF. When the order of experimental PDFs in the correlation matrix is rearranged to match the hierarchical clustering groups, a clear trend emerges [Fig. 5c]. Each group of PDFs exhibits similar correlation values with the crystalline polymorphs, reinforcing that the structural motifs within each group are closely related.

Rather than trying to define aMOFs as direct amorphous analogues of crystalline phases, this analysis reveals a more nuanced picture: no single polymorph fully describes any sample, as indicated by the highest Pearson correlation coefficient reaching only 0.76. Our findings challenge the common assumption that aMOFs can be directly mapped onto known crystalline polymorphs. Unlike amorphous materials derived from crystalline counterparts, where a clear structural relationship exists, these aMOFs were synthesised directly under amorphous-favouring conditions. This raises a broader question: What defines a unique amorphous phase? Must both short- and medium-range order align with a crystalline reference, or must the framework topology be partially retained? This remains an open question. These results emphasise that aMOFs cannot simply be viewed as ‘disordered versions’ of known crystalline structures. Instead, they represent a distinct, structurally complex class of materials with unique atomic arrangements that emerge from their specific synthesis conditions.

Two key trends emerge by examining the relationship between node defect concentration and coherence length across the explored phases: chemistry primarily dictates defect density, while the synthesis method strongly influences coherence length [Fig. 6]. These findings provide a fundamental understanding of how disorder can be controlled in aMOFs and serve as the foundation for broader design principles.

aMOF design principles

Building on these insights, we propose three system-informed design principles for the direct synthesis of aMOFs. Rather than being universally applicable, these principles reflect trends observed across the materials studied here and are intended to highlight conditions that favour amorphous network formation over crystallisation in nanocluster-derived systems. They provide a conceptual framework for rationally targeting aMOFs and for understanding how synthesis parameters influence disorder and defect chemistry.

(1) Kinetic Bias Towards Amorphisation: In the systems studied here, fast reactions at room temperature using high reagent concentrations under basic conditions bias formation toward amorphous networks by suppressing ordered assembly. Under these conditions, limited ligand exchange and reduced coordination reversibility disfavour crystallisation pathways. While similar conditions may also support crystalline MOF formation in other systems, our results show that kinetically dominated regimes can be exploited to access aMOFs when combined with appropriate building units, with fine-tuning of reaction rate and reversibility remaining critical.

(2) Nanocluster Approach as a Route to Enhanced Disorder: Using pre-formed SBUs promotes higher structural disorder compared to direct mixing of metal salts and linkers, likely due to the additional ligand exchange and network reorganisation required during assembly. In the present system, this results in aMOFs with shorter coherence lengths. Although nanocluster-based strategies are not yet widespread in aMOF synthesis, our results indicate that they represent a powerful, though currently underexplored, pathway to controllable amorphous networks.

(3) Defect Engineering via Compositional Tuning: Incorporation of more kinetically inert metals increases defect concentration and structural disorder by limiting structural rearrangement. For example, Cr incorporation leads to higher defect densities than in Fe-based analogues, reflecting slower ligand-exchange kinetics. This demonstrates that metal composition can be used as a design handle to modulate disorder and defect landscapes, with direct implications for properties such as reactivity and host–guest interactions, though the specific outcomes will depend on the metal–ligand chemistry involved.

Taken together, these guidelines outline how kinetic control, choice of building unit, and metal chemistry can be combined to bias synthesis toward amorphous rather than crystalline materials. They are not intended as universal rules, but as a transferable design framework that can inform future efforts to deliberately access and exploit disorder in aMOFs beyond conventional crystallisation-driven approaches.

In this study, we have demonstrated the direct synthesis of amorphous metal–organic frameworks (aMOFs) using pre-formed nanoclusters and rigid organic linkers. By systematically investigating the interplay between composition, synthesis conditions, and structural disorder, we have established design principles that govern aMOF formation. We find that using nanoclusters as synthetic precursors enables the formation of highly disordered frameworks. Additionally, our findings show that kinetic control, particularly fast reactions under basic conditions at room temperature, is crucial for suppressing crystallinity and promoting amorphous network formation.

A key outcome of our study is the role of metal composition in defect engineering. Incorporating kinetically less labile metals, such as Cr, significantly increases defect concentration by limiting structural rearrangement, thereby providing a powerful tool for tuning aMOF properties. Our analysis of local and extended structure highlights that while aMOFs exhibit clear structural motifs related to their crystalline counterparts, they cannot be directly mapped onto known crystalline polymorphs. Instead, these materials form unique amorphous networks dictated by both synthesis conditions and composition.

Importantly, our results reinforce that aMOFs are not merely disordered versions of crystalline frameworks. Attempts to correlate their structures with known crystalline polymorphs reveal that no single ordered framework fully describes their atomic arrangements. Instead, aMOFs possess distinct network architectures that demand new characterisation approaches beyond traditional crystallography. This highlights the necessity of multi-scale structural analysis: while the choice of SBUs primarily determines local order, the synthesis conditions and composition strongly influence the extended disorder. Our work demonstrates the power of PDF analysis coupled with statistical clustering in resolving these complex structural relationships.

Moving beyond the conventional crystallise–amorphise paradigm, our bottom-up study provides a foundation for the rational design of next-generation aMOFs with controlled disorder and tailored functionality. These principles allow for aMOF design in applications requiring structural flexibility, defect engineering, and enhanced transport properties, marking a step toward the rational design of amorphous framework materials.

Methods

Nanocluster synthesis

Nanocluster synthesis was performed as described in ref. 26. M(NO3)3.9H2O (0.015 mol) [Table 5] was dissolved in 5 mL hot water (50 °C), and CH3COONa (0.03 mol) was dissolved separately in 5 mL hot water (50 °C). The two solutions were mixed and stirred for 10 min at 50 °C. The resulting solution was allowed to cool down to room temperature and left in the air for crystallisation. After several days, single crystals were obtained.

Single-crystal X‑ray diffraction

Single‑crystal X‑ray diffraction data were collected using a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer with a PHOTON 100 CMOS detector, a Mo Kα high brilliance IμS X‑ray tube (λ = 0.71073 Å), a multilayer X‑ray mirror, and the temperature kept at 100 K using an Oxford Cryo system. The structures were solved using Olex2 with the ShelXT programme using intrinsic phasing63,64. Refinements were performed using the ShelXL refinement package with least-squares minimisation. The extinction coefficient was refined. Hydrogen atoms were added automatically using a riding model. All other atoms were refined anisotropically.

X‑ray fluorescence spectroscopy

XRF measurements were carried out using a Malvern Panalytical Epsilon 4 XRF spectrometer equipped with an Ag‑anode X‑ray tube operating at 50.000 kV/6 μA and a high‑resolution silicon drift detector. Samples were prepared in 35 mm disposable plastic cups on 3.6 μm PETP polyester film. Before measurement, the sample environment was filled with He gas to avoid detecting elements in the air.

M‑BDC syntheses

All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (99%), Chromium (III) nitrate nonahydrate (99%), terephthalic acid (98%), sodium acetate (99%), sodium hydroxide pellets (98%), and N, N-dimethylformamide (99.8%) were all purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

One-pot (OP)

M(NO3)3.9H2O (0.6 g, 1.5 mmol) and H2BDC (0.25 g, 1.5 mmol) were dissolved in 5 mL DMF. NaOH (0.06 g, 1.5 mmol) was slowly added, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min. The product was washed three times with DMF (5 mL) and three times with 60 °C deionised water (5 mL) and dried in an oven at 60 °C.

Stepwise (SW)

M(NO3)3.9H2O (0.6 g, 1.5 mmol) and NaCH3COO (0.37 g, 4.5 mmol) were dissolved in 5 mL DMF. H2BDC (0.25 g, 1.5 mmol) was slowly added to the mixture, and the mixture was stirred until dissolved. NaOH (0.06 g, 1.5 mmol) was then slowly added, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min. The product was washed three times with DMF (5 mL) and three times with 60 °C deionised water (5 mL) and dried in an oven at 60 °C.

Nanocluster (NC)

M3O cluster (100 mg, 0.15 mmol) and H2BDC (75 mg, 0.45 mmol) were dissolved in 5 mL DMF. NaOH (36 mg, 0.9 mmol) was then slowly added, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min. The product was washed three times with DMF (5 mL) and three times with 60 °C deionised water (5 mL) and dried in an oven at 60 °C.

Powder X-ray diffraction

Finely ground M-BDC samples were deposited onto a zero-background sample holder and mounted on the diffractometer. Measurements were performed at room temperature on a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) in Bragg-Brentano geometry. Data were collected for one hour between 12 and 70° 2θ with a step size of 0.02°, using an Fe fluorescence filter.

Scanning electron microscopy

SEM was measured with the lower electron detector of a JEOL 7800F-prime using an acceleration voltage of 5 kV and a working distance of 10 mm. Finely ground powder of Fe-BDC was dispersed in ethanol and drop-cast onto Si wafers before being adhered to the sample holder. The quality of the 100 k-magnification images was limited by the instrument’s resolution and the buildup of electric charge on the poorly conducting sample. This can cause particles to move during raster scanning and, in some cases, degrade the sample during data collection.

Fourier transform infra-red spectroscopy

The IR spectra were recorded using a Bruker ALPHA‑P FT‑IR spectrometer with a single-reflection ATR module. Data were collected in transmission mode between 375 and 4000 cm−1 on a powdered sample.

Thermogravimetric and mass spectrometry measurements

Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) was conducted using a Netzsch TG 209 F1 Libra (Netzsch, Germany) apparatus with an Aёolos QMS403C mass spectrometer. MS data acquisition was controlled by the Proteus software. The quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS) detected ion currents from the gaseous products over a range of 1–50 m/z. They were collected from room temperature to 500 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under an air atmosphere (40 mL min−1), and around 5 mg of sample was used for each measurement. To account for any balance drift in the instrument due to increasing heat, each TG measurement was initiated with a buoyancy correction run, conducted on an empty crucible. This correction mirrored the gas-flow and thermal-ramp conditions. Solvent and linker mass losses were determined from the plateau region of the TGA curve. Solvent mass loss was taken as the difference in mass between the initial plateau and the onset of linker decomposition, identified by a sharp mass drop and significant detection of mass 44 in the MS. Linker decomposition mass loss was determined from the mass change between the plateau before the sharp decomposition drop and the stable plateau after decomposition.

Nitrogen adsorption measurements

Adsorption isotherms of nitrogen were measured at 77 K using a Micromeritics TriStar II PLUS instrument. Approximately 40 mg of each sample was degassed at 60 °C for 18 h prior to measurement.

X‑ray total scattering and pair distribution function analysis

X‑ray total scattering data were collected at the DanMAX (MAX IV, Sweden) and P02.1 (DESY, Germany) beamlines using synchrotron radiation (λ = 0.354 Å and λ = 0.207 Å, respectively). A small amount of finely ground sample was loaded into a Kapton capillary (1 mm diameter). Capillaries were sealed and mounted onto the instrument. Data were collected under ambient conditions for each sample, empty capillary, and the blank instrument over the region ~0.2 < Q < ~ 18.4 Å−1. The raw total scattering data were corrected for background, multiple scattering, container scattering, and Compton scattering using xPDFsuite over the range 0.5 < Q < 17 Å−1 using an rpoly value of 1.0 and Qmax,inst of 18.4 Å−1 65.

In situ X-ray total scattering data were collected at the DanMAX beamline (MAX IV, Sweden) using synchrotron radiation (λ = 0.354 Å). A custom flow setup was used to perform the in situ measurement. Aqueous solutions of the metal salt were placed in a round-bottom flask and pumped through a fused silica capillary (0.85 mm diameter) placed in the path of the beam using a peristaltic pump. A separate syringe pump was then used to inject the sodium acetate solution and initiate the reaction. The reaction mixture was heated to 50 °C using a hot plate. Data were measured in the range ~0.85 < Q < 17.5 Å−1 using a DECTRIS PILATUS3 X CdTe 2 M area detector and a time resolution of 1 s. Aqueous solutions of the metal salt were used to facilitate background subtraction, and the PDFs were generated over the range 0.5 < Q < 16 Å−1 using an rpoly value of 1.0 and Qmax,inst of 18 Å−1. Fitting of the M–M peak intensity was performed in Python using the SciPy package66.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy

Spectroscopic measurements were performed at beamline B18 (core EXAFS) at Diamond Light Source (Didcot, United Kingdom)67. The synchrotron was operating with a ring energy of 3 GeV and at a current of 300 mA. The X-ray source was a bending magnet, and energy scanning was performed in continuous QEXAFS mode using a Si(111) dual-crystal monochromator (DCM). X-ray focusing to the sample position was achieved using Si mirrors coated with Pt, pitched at 2.4 mrad, yielding an X-ray spot size of 600 × 600 μm2 FWHM (h × v). Data were acquired in transmission mode using ion chambers and Stanford amplifiers. Acquisition times of 131.21 s (over the Fe K-edge) and 182.46 s per spectrum (over the Cr K-edge) were used with a 0.3 eV step size. The energy calibration of the DCM was achieved by measuring the spectra of Fe and Cr foils. Data were processed in Athena, wherein a flat background from the pre-edge slope and a fitted low-order polynomial to the post-edge region were both subtracted68. The Fourier transform of the normalised data was also performed in Athena using a k-weight of 2.
