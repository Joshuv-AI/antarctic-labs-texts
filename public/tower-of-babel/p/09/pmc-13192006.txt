Genetic susceptibility and HLA association in autoimmune hepatitis among Yemeni patients
Authors: Waleed Qassem Farie, Saleh Salem Bahaj, Anwar Kassem Al-Madhagi, Sarosh Sher Ali, Aref Noman, Waleed Yahya Alkassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic, immune-mediated liver disease characterized by the progressive destruction of hepatocytes, often leading to cirrhosis and liver failure. Genetic predisposition, particularly the involvement of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles, is well-documented in the pathogenesis of AIH. However, the specific HLA associations in the Yemeni population remain unexplored, despite increasing clinical evidence of AIH in this region.ObjectiveThis study aims to elucidate the distribution of HLA class alleles in Yemeni patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with AIH and assess their association with disease susceptibility, contributing to the growing body of immunogenetic research in Middle Eastern populations.MethodsThis case-control study included 93 AIH patients and 280 healthy controls (total N = 373), all of Yemeni origin. We conducted high-resolution HLA typing and compared the frequencies of HLA alleles with global data. Allelic distributions were analyzed for associations with AIH susceptibility, and findings were interpreted in the context of previously identified genetic markers of autoimmune liver disease.ResultsThe most frequent allele in Yemeni AIH patients was HLA-DRB1*03:01 (42.5% vs. 15.5% in controls, OR = 4.0, 95% CI = 2.8–5.8, p = 0.01), followed by HLA-DRB1*04:01 (21.5% vs. 16.9%, OR = 1.3, 95% CI = 1.1–2.0, p = 0.04) and HLA-A*02 (31.7% vs. 27.9%, OR = 1.2, 95% CI = 1.1–1.3, p = 0.001). Protective alleles such as HLA-DRB1*13:01 were significantly more frequent in controls (13.2% vs. 3.8%, OR = 0.3, 95% CI = 0.1–0.6, p = 0.001). Our results demonstrate a genetic predisposition to AIH in Yemen, primarily associated with the HLA-DRB1*03:01 and HLA-DRB1*04:01 alleles. The observed distribution aligns with international trends but also reveals distinct ethnic-specific patterns that warrant further investigation.ConclusionThis study highlights the critical role of HLA-DRB1* alleles, particularly DRB1*03:01 and DRB1*04:01, in the genetic susceptibility to AIH in the Yemeni population. These findings not only enhance the understanding of genetic factors in AIH but also establish a foundation for future immunogenetic research in the Middle East. The identification of region-specific HLA associations could inform both diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for AIH in Yemen and similar populations. This study was restricted to adults (≥ 18 years); therefore, findings may not apply to pediatric AIH.Keywords: Autoimmune hepatitis, HLA-DRB1 alleles, Genetic susceptibility, Human leukocyte antigen, Immunogenetics, Yemeni population

Introduction

Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic, immune-mediated liver disease that affects individuals across various age groups and ethnicities. Clinically, it presents with elevated aminotransferase levels, hypergammaglobulinemia, and circulating autoantibodies, along with interface hepatitis observed histologically. If left untreated, it may progress to cirrhosis, liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. Although AIH predominantly affects females, with a global female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1, it can also occur in males, especially in certain ethnic populations [2].mographic and Clinical Characteristics of AIH

Globally, AIH is considered a rare disorder with an estimated prevalence of 10–25 cases per 100,000 individuals in Europe and North America [3]. However, this prevalence is increasing due to enhanced awareness and improved diagnostic practices [4]. In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, limited epidemiological data exist, though studies suggest an upward trend in AIH diagnoses, particularly among pediatric and adolescent patients [5]. In Yemen, unpublished clinical observations and emerging case series indicate a rising incidence, with a notable deviation from the usual female predominance, as more males are being diagnosed compared to global norms [6].

The pathogenesis of AIH involves a multifactorial interplay between environmental triggers and host genetic susceptibility, particularly involving human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes. HLA class II molecules, especially those in the DRB1* locus, have been implicated as strong susceptibility markers in various populations. Studies have consistently shown associations between AIH and alleles such as HLA-DRB1*03:01 and DRB1*04:01 in European cohorts [7]. In contrast, HLA-DRB1*03:01 and HLA-DRB1*04:01 have been consistently associated with autoimmune hepatitis type 1 (AIH-1) in adult populations across European, North American, and Middle Eastern cohorts, highlighting their central role in disease susceptibility [8]. Protective alleles such as HLA-DRB1*15:01 and DRB1*13:02 have also been described [9].

Despite the well-established role of HLA alleles in AIH susceptibility, no prior genetic studies have been conducted among Yemeni patients. This knowledge gap is particularly important given the region’s unique demographic and clinical patterns. The present study aims to characterize the distribution of HLA class I and II alleles among Yemeni AIH patients and to identify those alleles associated with either increased susceptibility or protection. By establishing a foundational immunogenetic profile, this work seeks to enhance disease recognition, inform tailored therapeutic approaches, and contribute to the global understanding of AIH pathogenesis.

Methodology

Study design and population

This case-control study was conducted to investigate the association between HLA class alleles and autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) among Yemeni patients. 93 adult patients (≥ 18 years) clinically diagnosed with autoimmune hepatitis AIH-1 and 280 healthy, age- and sex-matched controls with no history of autoimmune or liver diseases. All participants were ethnic Yemeni and long-term residents of Yemen.

Study setting

The laboratory works were performed at the USTH Laboratories, Sana’a city, Yemen in the molecular biology, immunology, and biochemistry laboratories. This study was conducted during a period of three years starting in January 2022 and ended in December 2024.

Eligibility criteria

For AIH patients (cases)

Inclusion criteria required patients to be adults aged 18 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of autoimmune hepatitis based on the International Autoimmune Hepatitis Group (IAHG) scoring system. AIH-1 was defined by the presence of ANA and/or SMA, while AIH-2 was defined by anti-LKM1 and/or anti-LC1 positivity. During serological screening, 61.3% of AIH cases tested positive for both ANA and ASMA, 10.8% for ANA alone, 21.5% for ASMA alone, and 6.4% were seronegative. Importantly, no patients tested positive for anti-LKM1 or anti-LC1, indicating the absence of AIH type 2 in this study population. As such, all included cases were classified as AIH-1, and subgroup comparison between AIH types was not applicable. Therefore, all genetic associations reported herein pertain to AIH-1.

For healthy controls

Controls were selected from the general Yemeni population. Eligible individuals were those without a history of autoimmune diseases, liver disorders, or any serological evidence of viral hepatitis. Age and sex matching were ensured to minimize demographic confounding.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Science and Technology Hospital, Sana’a, Yemen. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. (Sanaa University)

Sample collection

Venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected aseptically from each participant into sterile EDTA-coated tubes. Samples were kept at 4 °C and transported promptly to the molecular biology laboratory for processing. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and stored at − 20 °C for serological testing, while buffy coats were used for DNA extraction.

Serological testing and exclusion of viral hepatitis

In this study, autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) type 1 was diagnosed based on the presence of antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-smooth muscle antibodies (ASMA), both of which are commonly used biomarkers for this condition. The sensitivity and specificity of these diagnostic tests were as follows: ANA demonstrated a sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 90%, while ASMA had a sensitivity of 92% and a specificity of 88%. These autoantibodies were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (e.g., BioTek Instruments, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. To further confirm the diagnosis and exclude AIH type 2, patients were tested for anti-LKM1 and anti-LC1 antibodies. No patients in our cohort tested positive for these markers, ensuring that all included cases were classified as AIH type 1. This serological approach supports the validity of the AIH classification in this study.

DNA extraction and quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from buffy coat leukocytes using a commercial silica membrane-based kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA purity and concentration were assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered of acceptable quality for downstream applications.

HLA typing procedure

HLA genotyping focused on loci HLA-A, HLA-B (Class I), and HLA-DRB1 (Class II). PCR-SSP (sequence-specific primers) was used for allelic discrimination, employing commercially prepared kits (e.g., Olerup SSP® or equivalent) validated for high-resolution typing. Amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were visualized on 2% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide. High-resolution typing was performed for the HLA-DRB1 locus, while HLA-A and HLA-B loci were typed at low-to-intermediate resolution (two-digit and four-digit where possible). This distinction is reflected in the reporting of allele results.

HLA allele assignment

Allele names were harmonized to current IMGT/HLA nomenclature, including expression suffixes (e.g., ‘N’ for null alleles). Frequencies were recorded for each allele, and specific attention was given to DRB1* alleles due to their known association with AIH susceptibility. Because DRB1 genotyping was performed by PCR-SSP without sequence-based typing, rare alleles and null-allele designations should be considered putative pending confirmatory sequencing. Null alleles (e.g., those with suffix ‘N’) were interpreted according to established IMGT/HLA nomenclature and published functional annotations.

Statistical analysis

Allelic frequencies were calculated using direct counting. The differences in allele frequencies between AIH patients and controls were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for small sample sizes. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to determine the strength of association between specific HLA alleles and AIH. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data was analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 373 individuals were enrolled in this case-control study conducted in Sana’a city between January 2022 and December 2024. The participants were divided into two groups: 93 autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) patients and 280 healthy controls, maintaining a case-to-control ratio of approximately 1:1. Participants were categorized into three age groups (≤ 20, 21–40, and > 40 years), with the majority of both patients (78.5%) and controls (70.7%) falling within the 21–40-year range. No significant association was observed between age distribution and disease status (p = 0.3).

In terms of gender distribution, males represented 72.0% of the AIH group and 66.8% of the control group, while females constituted 28.0% and 33.2%, respectively. The male-to-female ratio among patients was approximately 2.6:1, and no significant association was found between gender and AIH occurrence (p = 0.3). However, marital status showed a significant difference: 73.1% of AIH patients were married compared to 57.0% of controls (p = 0.006), suggesting a potential sociocultural or lifestyle-related factor in disease prevalence (Table 1).

HLA allele distribution and genetic associations with AIH

As all patients in this study were serologically confirmed to have AIH type 1, the following HLA associations are specific to the AIH-1 subtype: 186 HLA alleles in AIH cases and 560 alleles in the control group in each type. Within the HLA-DRB1 locus, the most significant susceptibility association with AIH was observed for HLA-DRB1*03 (42.5% in AIH cases vs. 15.5% in controls; OR = 4.0, 95% CI = 2.8–5.8; p = 0.001), followed by HLA-DRB1*04 (21.5% vs. 16.9%; OR = 1.3, 95% CI = 1.1–2.0; p = 0.01). Conversely, alleles such as HLA-DRB1*07:10 N (20.9% vs. 12.9%; OR = 1.8, 95% CI = 1.2–2.8; p = 0.007) and HLA-DRB1*13 (3.8% vs. 13.2%; OR = 0.3, 95% CI = 0.1–0.6; p = 0.001) were more common among controls, suggesting potential protective effects.

In the HLA-A locus, HLA-A*02 was the most frequent allele (31.7% in AIH vs. 27.9% in controls; OR = 1.2, 95% CI = 1.1–1.3; p = 0.001). While this modestly increased frequency may indicate a weak susceptibility trend, HLA-A*03 was less prevalent among AIH cases (2.7% vs. 6.9%; OR = 0.4, 95% CI = 0.1–0.9; p = 0.03), implying a possible protective association.

Among HLA-B alleles, a strong association was identified with HLA-B*08 (23.1% in AIH vs. 6.3% in controls; OR = 4.5, 95% CI = 2.8–7.3; p = 0.001), confirming its significant role as a susceptibility allele. In contrast, HLA-B*51 was markedly more frequent in controls (0.5% vs. 16.8%; OR = 0.03, 95% CI = 0.004–0.2; p = 0.001), indicating a strong protective effect against AIH development(Table 2).

Overall, these findings highlight that HLA-DRB1*03, HLA-DRB1*04, HLA-A*02, and HLA-B*08 are potential genetic susceptibility markers for AIH, while HLA-DRB1*01, HLA-DRB1*13, HLA-A*03, and HLA-B*51 appear to confer protective effects. This genetic pattern underscores the influence of both MHC class I and class II alleles in determining AIH susceptibility within the Yemeni population.

Based on the observed allele frequency of HLA-DRB1*07:10 N (20.9%), the expected frequency of homozygous individuals would be approximately 4% under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. However, genotype-level analysis to identify homozygous carriers was not performed in the present study. No specific clinical or immunological abnormalities attributable to homozygosity for this null allele were assessed. This represents a limitation, and future studies should investigate genotype distributions and potential phenotypic effects in homozygous individuals.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the immunogenetic landscape of autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) in the Yemeni population, highlighting both shared and population-specific associations with human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I alleles. Our findings underscore the strong association of HLA-DRB1*03 and HLA-DRB1*07 alleles with increased susceptibility to AIH, consistent with previous studies in European, North American, and Latin American populations [10–12]. These alleles have been recognized as key determinants in AIH type 1, contributing to the autoimmune targeting of hepatocytes via altered antigen presentation. The HLA-DRB107:10 N allele identified in this study is a well-characterized null allele with documented lack of cell-surface expression, as reported in the 18th International HLA and Immunogenetics Workshop [13]. Accordingly, its designation as a non-expressed allele should be explicitly recognized. The observed association in our cohort is therefore unlikely to reflect direct antigen presentation but may instead be attributable to linkage disequilibrium with nearby functional HLA variants or haplotypes. Given that HLA typing in this study was performed using PCR-SSP without sequence-based confirmation, future work will include targeted gene sequencing to confirm the presence of the DRB107:10 N allele and to verify its functional non-expression in this population.In addition to these alleles, our study identified HLA-A*01, HLA-B*08, and HLA-B*35 as class I alleles significantly associated with AIH, reaffirming findings from other cohorts [14–16]. HLA-B*08 has been extensively linked with AIH in Northern European populations and is often part of the ancestral 8.1 MHC haplotype, which is associated with several autoimmune disorders [17]. Interestingly, the prominence of HLA-A*01 and HLA-B*35 in our cohort may reflect unique regional immunogenetic patterns in Yemen, underscoring the need for localized genetic epidemiology studies [18].An unexpected finding in our study was the predominance of males (72.0%) among AIH patients, which contrasts with the established global female predominance (3:1) [2]. This divergence may reflect genetic or environmental influences specific to Yemen, possible referral or diagnostic biases, or under-recognition of female cases. Further studies are needed to explore sex-related differences in AIH susceptibility in Middle Eastern populations.

Importantly, no patients in this study exhibited serological markers of autoimmune hepatitis type 2 (anti-LKM1 or anti-LC1), confirming that the cohort exclusively represents adult AIH type 1. The HLA associations identified in this study are consistent with those reported in adult autoimmune hepatitis type 1 (AIH-1) populations. In particular, HLA-DRB103:01 and HLA-DRB104:01 have been widely recognized as प्रमुख susceptibility alleles in adult cohorts and are thought to contribute to disease pathogenesis through altered antigen presentation and CD4 + T-cell activation. Given that all patients in the present study were classified as AIH type 1, these findings should be interpreted within the context of adult AIH-1 and not extrapolated to pediatric or type 2 AIH.

Conversely, several alleles, including HLA-DRB1*15, DRB1*13, and HLA-B*51, were more frequent in healthy controls and, based on statistical comparison (OR < 1, p < 0.05), appear to confer a protective effect against AIH. Protective alleles in this study were strictly defined as those significantly more common in controls than in patients; rare or absent alleles without statistical support were not classified as protective. These findings align with prior reports where DRB1*15 has been associated with reduced AIH susceptibility in East Asian and European cohorts [19–21]. HLA-B*51 has also been implicated in protective roles in autoimmune liver conditions, although it is more commonly associated with other immune-mediated diseases such as Behçet’s disease [22].

The observed distribution of HLA-A* alleles revealed a high frequency of HLA-A*02 variants across both patients and controls, indicating their ubiquity rather than specific disease association. However, susceptibility in AIH may be modulated not by individual alleles alone but by haplotype combinations and their impact on T-cell repertoire and cytokine profiles [23]. The detection of HLA-A*03 and HLA-A*30 predominantly in controls further supports their role as protective markers in this population.

The significance of HLA class I molecules in AIH, although less frequently emphasized than class II, is increasingly recognized. Class I molecules may influence disease phenotype, severity, or treatment response by modulating CD8 + T cell responses [24]. These insights are particularly relevant in the Yemeni context, where no prior comprehensive HLA typing studies for AIH have been conducted, making this work foundational for future research and clinical stratification [25].

Published HLA frequency data in Yemenis are limited but available. For example, study reported baseline HLA-A, -B, -Cw and -DRB1 frequencies in a Yemeni cohort [26]. Where comparable allele groups are reported, the distribution observed in our control group was broadly consistent with these data, supporting that our controls are representative of the underlying Yemeni population.

All AIH cases were serologically classified as AIH type 1 based on ANA and/or ASMA positivity, with complete absence of anti-LKM1 or anti-LC1 antibodies. This serological profile is consistent with previously reported distributions in adult AIH populations from certain regions. The absence of AIH type 2 in our cohort precluded subgroup comparisons of HLA allele distributions between AIH-1 and AIH-2. We acknowledge this as a limitation in the scope of genetic analysis; however, our findings remain robust in delineating HLA associations specifically within AIH type 1.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a clear association between specific HLA alleles and susceptibility to autoimmune hepatitis in Yemeni patients. These findings provide strong evidence of population-specific genetic predispositions in the Yemeni population and reinforce the role of HLA polymorphism in modulating autoimmune hepatitis susceptibility and resistance. The identification of both risk and protective alleles reinforces the importance of HLA polymorphism in AIH and highlights the necessity of regional studies to inform diagnostic criteria and personalized immunotherapy approaches. Rare alleles such as HLA-A*02:68 and HLA-B*51:58 were detected in the study. Although reported here, their identification should be interpreted with caution as confirmatory sequencing was not performed in this study. Future studies with DNA sequencing validation are needed to confirm these findings. The relatively small sample size limits the power to detect associations with rare alleles, and larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings.
