Combination of PARP and KRASG12D inhibitors enhances therapeutic efficacy by exploiting vulnerabilities in PDAC
Authors: Xin Xu, Xin Chen, Rongli Xu, Zhenyu Huo, Changying Li, Somaira Nowsheen, Khaled Aziz, Fan Yao, Zhenkun Lou, Min Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive malignancy driven predominantly by KRAS mutations, with KRASG12D present in ~40 % of cases. Although the selective KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 shows promising activity, monotherapy responses are incomplete and resistance emerges rapidly. In this study, we show that KRASG12D blockade suppresses homologous-recombination (HR) repair by downregulating BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32, creating a state of HR deficiency that sensitizes PDAC cells to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition. Combined MRTX1133 and olaparib treatment produced synergistic cytotoxicity in vitro and durable tumor regression in vivo, even in MRTX1133-resistant models, and remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment with enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration. These findings demonstrate that co-targeting KRASG12D and PARP exploits an induced DNA-repair vulnerability to achieve synthetic lethality and immune activation in KRASG12D-driven PDAC.Subject terms: DNA damage and repair, Pancreatic cancer, Targeted therapies

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for over 90% of pancreatic cancers and is characterized by a poor prognosis, with a five-year survival rate of ~10% and median survival of 6–12 months1,2. Most patients present with advanced disease and are ineligible for curative resection. For these patients, standard treatments like chemotherapy and radiotherapy provide limited benefits, with diminishing efficacy over time3,4. This underscores the need for more effective therapeutic strategies, particularly those that address the resistance mechanisms inherent in PDAC’s complex genetic landscape.

KRAS mutations are present in over 95% of PDAC cases and serve as a critical oncogenic driver in this malignancy5. The KRAS protein functions as a molecular switch, alternating between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state6,7. Mutations in KRAS, such as the KRASG12D/C variant, lead to constitutive activation of oncogenic pathways, including the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascades8–10. These pathways drive uncontrolled cell proliferation and genomic instability, promoting the transition from early pancreatic lesions to aggressive, invasive cancer11. Therefore, targeting KRAS is a highly attractive therapeutic approach for PDAC. While significant progress has been made in KRAS-targeted therapies, including the development and FDA approval of KRASG12C inhibitors like sotorasib and adagrasib5,12, these therapies apply to only a small subset of PDAC patients, as KRASG12C mutations are present in only about 2% of cases13. In contrast, the KRASG12D mutation is the most prevalent, occurring in approximately 40% of PDAC patients14,15. Importantly, KRASG12D mutations are associated with poorer survival outcomes compared to other KRAS variants16,17, making KRASG12D an urgent therapeutic target. MRTX1133, a highly selective KRASG12D inhibitor, has shown promising preclinical efficacy18,19. However, as with other targeted therapies, the rapid development of resistance limits the long-term efficacy of KRASG12D inhibitors20–24. To address this challenge, combination therapy approaches are crucial. In particular, exploiting synthetic lethality—where the inhibition of KRASG12D induces vulnerabilities in DNA repair pathways—presents a distinct opportunity.

In this work, we demonstrate that KRASG12D inhibition leads to the downregulation of homologous recombination (HR)-related proteins, including BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32, inducing HR deficiency. This renders KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells more sensitive to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition. This synthetic lethal effect persists even in the context of resistance to KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133. The combination of MRTX1133 and the PARP inhibitor Olaparib shows synergistic anti-tumor effects in vitro and in vivo, leading to significant tumor regression and survival benefits. These findings establish co-targeting of KRASG12D and PARP as a mechanistically rational therapeutic approach for KRASG12D-driven PDAC.

Result

KRASG12D correlates with poor prognosis and homologous-recombination pathway activation

We first interrogated the TCGA-PDAC cohort to determine the clinical impact of specific KRAS variants. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients harboring KRASG12D mutations had significantly poorer survival compared to those bearing KRAS non-G12D allele or wild type (WT) (P = 0.0202 and <0.0001, respectively; Fig. 1a). Reactome gene-set enrichment of differentially expressed genes highlighted pronounced enrichment of homologous-recombination (HR) and DNA-damage-response (DDR) pathways in the KRASG12D subset (adjusted P < 0.01; Fig. 1b), suggesting a potential link between KRASG12D signaling and DNA-repair proficiency.

To functionally test this association, we treated KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cell lines (AsPC-1, PANC-1, and SW1990) with the KRASG12D-specific inhibitor MRTX1133 and exposed them to ionizing radiation. MRTX1133 did not affect the induction of γ-H2AX foci immediately after irradiation, but it markedly delayed foci clearance at 4 and 8 hours (Fig. 1c, d and Supp. Fig. 1a–e), indicating impaired double-strand break repair. Neutral comet assays confirmed higher tail DNA percentage in MRTX1133-treated cells, consistent with persistent DSBs (Supp. Fig. 1b–e). In contrast, KRAS-wild-type BxPC-3 cells displayed unchanged repair kinetics (Supp. Fig. 1f, g). Clonogenic survival assays further demonstrated that KRASG12D inhibition sensitized mutant PDAC cells to irradiation, resulting in reduced colony formation (Fig. 1e–g). Collectively, these findings indicate that KRASG12D inhibition compromises DNA-repair capacity and increases susceptibility to genotoxic stress.

KRASG12D inhibition downregulates homologous-recombination genes and induces functional HR deficiency

To elucidate the molecular basis of the DNA repair deficiency induced by KRASG12D inhibition, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells (AsPC-1, PANC-1, SW1990) treated with MRTX1133. Transcriptomic analysis revealed broad suppression of HR-related genes, including BRCA1, RPA2, and RAD51, in MRTX1133-treated cells (Supp. Fig. 2a). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that MRTX1133 treatment significantly decreased BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA2 transcript levels in AsPC-1, PANC-1, SW1990, and KPC1199 cells (Supp. Fig. 2b). Immunoblotting confirmed reduced BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32 protein across these lines, accompanied by decreased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and AKT, consistent with on‑target suppression of KRAS signaling (Fig. 2a–d). Consistent with expression changes, MRTX1133-treated cells displayed markedly fewer BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32 foci after irradiation, whereas markers of DNA-damage recognition (γ-H2AX, MDC1, FK2) remained unaffected (Fig.2e–h). As expected, KRAS-wild-type BxPC-3 cells showed no reduction in HR foci following MRTX1133 exposure (Supp. Fig. 2c). DR‑GFP reporter assays demonstrated a marked loss of HR efficiency in the KRASG12D‑mutant lines (Fig. 2i, j), establishing that KRASG12D inhibition induces HR deficiency through inhibition of HR gene expression.

To exclude off-target effects of MRTX1133, we knocked down KRASG12D via shRNA in three KRASG12D-mutant cells. Knockdown of KRAS recapitulated MRTX1133-induced HR deficiency. In Comet assays, KRAS knockdown cells showed significantly higher tail DNA percentage 4 h after ionizing radiation, indicating impaired double-strand break repair (Supp. Fig. 3a, b). Immunofluorescence staining revealed pronounced reductions in BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32 foci, while markers of DNA damage recognition (γ-H2AX, MDC1, FK2) remained unchanged (Supp. Fig. 3c–h). DR-GFP reporter assays further demonstrated a substantial loss of HR repair efficiency (Supp. Fig. 3i–k). Moreover, HR deficiency scores increased in all three knockdown models—AsPC-1 (LOH:5 → 6; TAI:11 → 13; LST:7 → 11), PANC-1 (LOH:3 → 9; TAI:10 → 14; LST:10 → 16), and SW1990 (LOH:5 → 9; TAI:6 → 14; LST:10 → 15)—closely mirroring the phenotype observed with shBRCA1 in PANC-1 cells (Supp. Fig. 3l–o). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that KRASG12D maintains HR gene expression and DNA-repair competence in PDAC cells.

Pharmacologic validation with the pan‑KRAS inhibitor BI‑2865 yielded concordant results. In KRASG12D-mutant cells, BI-2865 treatment downregulated BRCA1, RPA32, and RAD51 (Supp. Fig. 4a–c) and reduced their foci formation (Supp. Fig. 4d–j), accompanied by decreased ERK1/2 phosphorylation. DR-GFP assays further confirmed a decline in homologous recombination repair proficiency (Supp. Fig. 4k–m). Comparable effects were observed in KRASG12V and KRASG12C cells (Supp. Figure 4n–p), indicating a shared KRAS‑dependent mechanism of HR suppression across alleles.

Given that KRAS signals through the RAF–MEK–ERK (MAPK) cascade, we asked whether MAPK suppression is sufficient to repress HR genes. Treatment with the MEK inhibitor trametinib (0, 10, or 50 nM for 24 h) produced a dose‑dependent decrease in phospho‑ERK1/2 across KRASG12D‑mutant (AsPC‑1, PANC‑1, SW1990) and non‑G12D (BxPC‑3, CFPAC‑1, MIAPaCa‑2) lines (Supp. Fig. 5a–g), accompanied by downregulation of BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA2. Thus, MEK inhibition is sufficient to reduce HR‑gene expression, supporting a shared MAPK‑dependent mechanism by which KRAS activity maintains the HR gene program.

KRASG12D uniquely upregulates HR genes independent of cell‑cycle status

Given that homologous recombination occurs predominantly during the S/G2 phase25–28and that MRTX1133 alters cell-cycle distribution (Supp. Fig. 6a–e), we examined whether HR suppression by MRTX1133 is secondary to cell-cycle changes. To test this, we synchronized cells in S phase with hydroxyurea before exposing them to MRTX1133 (Supp. Fig. 7a). Even under S-phase synchronization, transcript and protein levels of BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA2 remained strongly reduced in KRASG12D‑mutant cells (Supp. Fig. 7b–f). Notably, ERK/AKT suppression mirrored that observed in synchronized conditions (Supp. Fig. 7c–f). Consistent with these expression changes, BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32 foci were reduced, whereas γ-H2AX, MDC1, and FK2 foci were unaffected (Supp. Figure 7g–l). DR‑GFP assays performed after synchronization confirmed a marked impairment of HR repair following MRTX1133 treatment (Supp. Fig. 7m). These findings demonstrate that KRASG12D inhibition suppresses HR gene expression and HR proficiency even when the cell cycle is held constant, indicating a cell-cycle-independent mechanism.

To determine whether the cell-cycle-independent HR regulation is specific to KRASG12D or shared among other KRAS variants, we ectopically introduced KRAS variants (G12V, G12C, or G12D) into KRAS-wild-type BxPC-3 cells via lentiviral transduction, generating stable cell lines expressing each mutant at comparable levels (Supp. Fig. 8a). In hydroxyurea-synchronized cells, quantitative RT-PCR analysis of selected HR genes revealed that RAD51, RPA2, BRCA2, and BRCA1 transcripts were significantly elevated in KRASG12D-expressing cells, but not in KRASG12V- or KRASG12C-expressing lines (Supp. Fig. 8b). Moreover, in synchronized non-G12D mutants treated with the pan-KRAS inhibitor BI-2865, HR-gene repression was abolished despite sustained ERK and AKT inhibition (Supp. Fig. 8c–f). These findings define KRASG12D as uniquely capable of sustaining HR-gene expression beyond MAPK or cell-cycle control. Collectively, our data support a two-arm model of KRAS-mediated control of homologous recombination: (1) a shared, MAPK and cell cycle–dependent arm engaged by multiple KRAS alleles and pan KRAS blockade, and (2) a KRASG12D-specific, cell cycle–independent arm that directly elevates HR gene expression.

PARP inhibitor synergies with MRTX1133 in vitro and in vivo

Given that HR deficiency confers sensitivity to PARP inhibitors, we tested whether KRASG12D blockade enhances olaparib efficacy. Across multiple KRASG12D-mutant cell lines, dual treatment with MRTX1133 and olaparib produced robust synergy, with cell-growth inhibition (CGI) ≥ 50 and Bliss synergy scores > 5 (Fig. 3a, b). Consistent synergy was also detected using HSA, Loewe, and ZIP models (Supp. Fig. 9a). Colony-formation assays confirmed a pronounced reduction in clonogenic survival under combination therapy (Fig. 3c). The synergistic effect of MRTX1133 plus olaparib was reproduced in an independent KRASG12D‑mutant murine cell line (Supp. Fig. 9b). Notably, MRTX1133 combined with olaparib induced only moderate growth inhibition in non-transformed epithelial cells (BEAS-2B and hTERT-HPNE, Supp. Fig. 9c, d), in contrast to the profound cytotoxicity caused by MEK and PI3K inhibition, underscoring the tumor-selective potential of KRASG12D-targeted therapy.

To enhance translational relevance, we next examined how MRTX1133 interacts with standard pancreatic-cancer chemotherapies. In four KRASG12D‑mutant cell lines (AsPC‑1, PANC‑1, SW1990, and KPC1199), MRTX1133 exhibited pronounced synergy with cisplatin, with Bliss scores consistently >15 and corroborating HSA/ZIP/Loewe metrics >10 (Fig. 3d; Supp. Fig. 9e). In contrast, combinations of MRTX1133 with gemcitabine (Supp. Fig. 9f, g) or paclitaxel (Supp. Fig. 9h, i) yielded minimal or no synergy (scores <10). Collectively, these findings suggest that MRTX1133 selectively sensitizes KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells to HR-dependent DNA-damaging agents, such as PARP inhibitors and platinum compounds, but not to cytotoxics that act via other pathways.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of combining KRASG12D and PARP inhibition in vivo, we established cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models using KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cell lines (PANC-1 and SW1990). Mice bearing established tumors were treated with vehicle, MRTX1133, Olaparib, or the combination of both drugs. Dual therapy produced the greatest reduction in tumor volume compared with either monotherapy (Fig. 4a–g, Supp. Fig. 10a, b). Histological analysis of excised tumors confirmed that combination therapy led to a decrease in RAD51 expression and increased markers of DNA damage (γ-H2AX) and apoptosis (cleaved caspase-3) compared to monotherapy or vehicle-treated groups (Fig. 4h–q). Western blot and RT-PCR analyses of tumor tissues further validated significant reductions in HR-related genes in the combination therapy group (Supp. Fig. 10c–e). We also tested this combination in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models from two KRASG12D-mutant PDAC tumors, where the combination of MRTX1133 and Olaparib again outperformed monotherapy, resulting in marked tumor regression and increased DNA damage markers (Fig. 5a–q, Supp. Fig. 11a–e). Transcriptomic profiling of PDX tumors showed HR-pathway enrichment in vehicle controls and suppression under combination treatment (Supp. Fig. 11f, g). These results underscore the robustness of dual KRASG12D and PARP inhibition, highlighting its potential as a durable therapeutic strategy for KRASG12D-driven pancreatic cancers.

KRASG12D inhibition suppresses HR and preserves PARP‑inhibitor synergy in MRTX1133‑resistant models

Resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies like MRTX1133 can develop rapidly through signaling bypass. Because MRTX1133 suppresses HR gene expression independently of the MAPK pathway, we wondered whether this HR suppression persists in MRTX1133-resistant cells. To test this, we generated MRTX1133-resistant derivatives from three KRASG12D-mutant pancreatic cancer lines: AsPC-1-R, PANC-1-R and SW1990-R. These sublines displayed high MRTX1133 IC₅₀ values yet retained baseline sensitivity to olaparib (Supp. Fig. 12a–f).

In the parental lines, MRTX1133 reduced ERK and AKT phosphorylation in a dose‑dependent manner, whereas the resistant sublines maintained high levels of phospho‑ERK and phospho‑AKT despite treatment (Fig. 6a–c). Nevertheless, MRTX1133 continued to downregulate BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA32 in both parental and resistant cells (Fig. 6a–c), indicating that HR gene repression is maintained even when MAPK–PI3K signaling remains active in the resistant context. Time-course experiments reinforced this decoupling: in parental cells treated with 50 nM MRTX1133, p-ERK, and p-AKT declined within 6 h and remained low up to 72 h, with HR gene expression decreasing in parallel; in resistant sublines, p-ERK and p-AKT remained high, yet HR-related transcripts steadily decreased throughout the observation period (Fig. 6d–f). Immunofluorescence showed impaired recruitment and foci formation of HR markers in resistant cells after MRTX1133 exposure (Fig. 6g, h; Supp. Fig. 12g–j). DR-GFP assays showed comparable loss of HR-repair efficiency in resistant and parental cells (Fig. 6i). Together, these data demonstrate that MRTX1133 continues to impose an HR-deficient phenotype in KRASG12D inhibitor-resistant cells, independent of MAPK signaling.

We next examined whether MRTX1133 maintains synergy with PARP inhibition in resistant models. Cotreatment of MRTX1133 and olaparib exhibited strong synergy in AsPC-1-R, PANC-1-R, and SW1990-R, with Bliss independence scores exceeding 15 and concordant synergy across HSA, ZIP, and Loewe models (Fig. 6j–l; Supp. Fig. 12k). In xenograft models derived from SW1990-R and PANC-1-R, MRTX1133 alone had little antitumor effect, whereas the combination of MRTX1133 and olaparib produced the most significant tumor growth inhibition (Fig. 7a–f). Histological analysis of tumors from mice receiving the combination therapy showed reduced RAD51 expression, increased γ-H2AX staining and elevated cleaved caspase-3, indicative of enhanced DNA damage and apoptosis (Fig. 7g–p). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MRTX1133–olaparib co-treatment retains potent anti‑tumor efficacy against KRASG12D inhibitor-resistant tumors.

Combination therapy remodels tumor immune environment and prolongs survival

Because KRASG12D inhibitors can modulate the tumor microenvironment, we assessed how simultaneous KRASG12D and PARP inhibition affects tumor-infiltrating immune populations. We performed single-cell RNA seq (scRNA seq) on KPC tumors treated with vehicle, MRTX1133, olaparib, or the combination (Supp. Fig. 13a–d). Integration of 115,664 cells across all groups followed by unsupervised shared-nearest-neighbor (SNN) clustering identified ~24 transcriptionally distinct clusters (Supp. Fig. 13e). Across immune compartments, T/NK and myeloid lineages were most abundant, and both expanded with combination therapy (Fig. 8a, b; Supp. Fig. 13f, g). Within the T/NK subset, dual therapy enriched cytotoxic CD8+ effector and CD4+ T cells (Fig. 8c, d; Supp. Fig. 13g–i). Gene-set activity analysis revealed an effector-dominant state, with the highest cytotoxicity, T cell–mediated immunity, and interferon-γ response signatures in combination-treated tumors (Fig. 8e). Flow cytometry corroborated these findings, showing increased CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell frequencies within the CD3+ compartment (Fig. 8f), a higher proportion of TNF-α effector CD8+ T cells, and a reduced fraction of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8g, h). Together, these data demonstrate that dual KRASG12D and PARP inhibition markedly reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment toward an activated effector phenotype.

We next evaluated the immune consequences of the combination using an orthotopic KPC-luciferase PDAC model in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. Mice were treated with vehicle, MRTX1133, Olaparib, or the combination for seven days. The combination therapy resulted in significantly greater tumor reduction compared to monotherapy (Fig. 9a, b and Supp. Fig. 14a) and extended overall survival (Fig. 9c). Consistent with our CDX and PDX results, MRTX1133 reduced HR related gene expression in vivo (Fig. 9d–i; Supp. Fig. 14b–g), and the combination further increased apoptosis (Fig. 9f–h; Supp. Fig. 14b). Histological and transcriptional analyses showed enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration (Fig. 9d) and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and interferon-stimulated genes (Supp. Fig. 14h–l), indicating robust immune activation within the TME. Body weight and organ histology were unchanged across treatment arms (Supp. Fig. 14m, n), indicating good tolerability. Notably, depletion of CD8+ T cells with anti-CD8α antibodies markedly diminished the combination’s antitumor effect (Fig. 9j–m), indicating a CD8+-dependent mechanism. These data indicate that synthetic lethality between KRASG12D inhibition and PARP inhibition is accompanied by immune activation that amplifies therapeutic efficacy in vivo.

Discussion

Despite recent advances in targeted therapies, inhibiting KRAS-driven signaling in PDAC has achieved limited clinical success, with most strategies showing suboptimal efficacy29,30. KRASG12C inhibitors have demonstrated promise in cancers such as non-small cell lung cancer13,31, but the more common KRASG12D mutation in PDAC poses unique challenges. KRASG12D mutations drive oncogenic signaling that fuels tumor progression and confers resistance to standard therapies. While selective KRASG12D inhibitors like MRTX1133 have shown potent preclinical activity20,21,32,33, rapid resistance limits their durability34–36, underscoring the need for mechanistically informed combination strategies.

Combinations targeting downstream RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK or PI3K–AKT–mTOR cascades have yielded efficacy across KRAS-mutant cancers regardless of allele type37–41, yet these approaches are often constrained by toxicity. Our findings reveal an alternative vulnerability: KRASG12D inhibition downregulates HR genes—BRCA1, RAD51, and RPA2—creating a state of HR deficiency that confers synthetic lethality with PARP inhibition. This mechanism distinguishes allele-specific KRASG12D blockade from broad downstream inhibition. Dual targeting with MRTX1133 and olaparib produced synergistic antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo without additional toxicity, highlighting its translational potential.

Clinically, the combination of KRASG12D and PARP inhibition represents a precision-medicine strategy for the ~40% of PDAC patients harboring this allele14,15. In our study, pan-KRAS or MEK pathway blockade in diverse KRAS-mutant contexts suppressed HR genes and HR repair activity through a cell-cycle–dependent mechanism, revealing a shared KRAS-driven regulatory axis that contributes to PARP sensitization across alleles. In contrast, KRASG12D inhibition uniquely engages an additional, allele-specific mechanism that downregulates HR gene expression independently of cell-cycle control. This two-arm model, comprising a general MAPK- and cell-cycle–dependent pathway common to multiple KRAS variants and a KRASG12D-specific, cell-cycle–independent arm, defines distinct layers of HR regulation. Even in MRTX1133-resistant cells where ERK and AKT signaling were reactivated, HR suppression and PARP responsiveness persisted, reflecting a decoupled regulatory mechanism that remains active under resistance. While the molecular basis of this KRASG12D-specific arm remains to be elucidated, future studies aimed at identifying the downstream effectors mediating this noncanonical HR regulation will be critical for refining allele-targeted therapeutic strategies. Together, these findings indicate that KRASG12D–PARP co-targeting exploits both shared and allele-specific vulnerabilities to induce synthetic lethality and overcome adaptive resistance in pancreatic cancer.

Beyond DNA-repair defects, this combination therapy reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment, adding another layer of therapeutic potential32,33,42,43. In our study, combination therapy enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, increased TNF-α⁺ effector populations, and reduced PD-1 expression, converting immune-cold PDAC tumors into inflamed, effector-dominant lesions. These immune effects, validated by scRNA-seq and flow cytometry, provide a rationale for integrating immune-checkpoint blockade with KRAS–PARP co-inhibition to further amplify therapeutic efficacy.

Despite these advances, the mechanism by which KRASG12D regulates HR-gene transcription remains incompletely understood. While cell-cycle redistribution contributes modestly, our data show that even under synchronized conditions, MRTX1133 markedly suppresses BRCA1 and RAD51, implicating additional, MAPK-independent regulatory factors. Future studies dissecting these pathways may identify predictive biomarkers and additional vulnerabilities in KRASG12D-driven tumors.

Although the clinical development of MRTX1133 has been terminated, its preclinical promise in inducing HRD and sensitizing tumors to PARP inhibition underscores opportunities for alternative applications. Moreover, these findings incentivize the advancement of next-generation KRASG12D inhibitors, such as RMC-9805 or emerging analogs44,45, which may overcome current limitations and translate into effective therapies for KRAS-driven PDAC.

In conclusion, our findings provide compelling preclinical evidence for the combination of KRASG12D and PARP inhibitors as a promising therapeutic strategy for PDAC. This approach exploits synthetic lethality, HR deficiencies, and immune modulation, offering a promising avenue for improving outcomes in patients with KRASG12D-driven pancreatic cancer.

Method

Animals and ethical regulations

All research was conducted in compliance with relevant ethical regulations and guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. All animal procedures and study protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Tumor Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Approval No. NCC2024A149).

Cell culture

Human PDAC cell lines (AsPC-1, BxPC-3, CFPAC-1, Mia PaCa-2, PANC-1, and SW1990) were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College. Murine PDAC cell lines KPC1199 and KPC1242 were derived from tumor-bearing KPC mice (KrasLSL-G12D, Trp53−/−, PDX-1-Cre) on a congenic C57bL/6 background. For orthotopic experiments, KPC1199 cells were transfected with enhanced firefly luciferase. Human normal tissue cell lines BEAS-2B and hTERT-HPNE were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College and National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, respectively. KPC1199, KPC-luc, KPC1242, BxPC-3, PANC-1, SW1990, BEAS-2B, and hTERT-HPNE were maintained in DMEM medium (11995500, Gibco) with 10% FBS (10437-028, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122, Gibco). AsPC-1 was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (11875500, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. CFPAC-1 was maintained in IMDM medium (31980030, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. Mia PaCa-2 was maintained in DMEM medium with 10% FBS, 2.5% horse serum (16050130, Gibco) and antibiotics. All cells were incubated in a humidified ThermoFisher incubator with 5% CO2.

Therapeutic agents

MRTX1133 (TargetMol, T9303), BI-2865 (TargetMol, T72062), Olaparib (TargetMol, T3015), Trametinib (TargetMol, T2125), Alpelisib (TargetMol, T1921) and Hydroxyurea (TargetMol, T0676) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)(Sigma-Aldrich, 41648) to generate stock solutions with concentrations between 5–50 mmol/L. The maximum final concentration of DMSO in all experimental wells was 1%. In vivo mAb anti-mouse CD8α antibody (Clone 53-6.7, BioXCell) was purchased from Bio X Cell.

Plasmid transfection and lentiviral infection

pLVX-EF1a-KRAS-G12C, pLVX-EF1a-KRAS-G12D, pLVX-EF1a-KRAS-G12V and pLVX-EF1a-IRES-Puro were constructed and validated with sequencing by Shenzhen Dingke Biotechnology. The following shRNA sequences were inserted into pLKO.1 vector. Human KRAS shRNAs: #1, 5′-GAGGGCTTTCTTTGTGTATTTCTCGAGAAATACACAAAGAAAGCCCTCTTTTTG-3′, #2, 5′-CCTATGGTCCTAGTAGGAAATCTCGAGATTTCCTACTAGGACCATAGGTTTTTG-3′. Human BRCA1 shRNAs: #1, 5′-GGCAGGTATTAGAAATGAA-3′, #2, 5′-TATAAGACCTCTGGCATGAAT-3′.

To generate lentivirus, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the target plasmid, psPAX2, and pMD2G at a 5:3.75:1.25 ratio. Viral supernatants were harvested 36-40 h post-transfection, clarified by centrifugation (800×g, 5 min), filtered through 0.2-μm membranes, aliquoted (1 ml), and stored at −80 °C. For lentiviral transduction, cells seeded in 6-well plates 16–18 h prior were infected with 1 ml viral supernatant supplemented with 10 μg/mL polybrene. For transient experiments, cells were analyzed 48 h post-infection. For stable cell lines, cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL) for ≥7 days.

Western blotting

Cells were seeded in 6 cm plates in complete culture medium. After overnight incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator, cells were treated with indicated concentrations of MRTX1133 or BI-2865; control cells were treated with 0.1% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, 41648). After 48 h of treatment, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, collected and lysed with NETN buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Nonidet P-40) containing 50 mM β-glycerophosphate, 10 mM NaF and 1 mg ml−1 each of pepstatin A and aprotinin. Whole-cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min. For protein extraction from tumor tissue, samples were cut into pieces and lysed with NETN buffer. After the samples were subjected to 2 min of ultrasonication, whole lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min. Then the total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE. Immunoblotting was performed according to standard procedures. Primary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNA extraction kit (NCM, M5105) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher). For cDNA synthesis, 200–1000 ng of total RNA samples were used for reverse transcription in 20 µl of reaction volume using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, RR037). RT–qPCR was performed with the Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara, RR601A). β-actin was used as a control for mRNA. Primers sequences used are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were cultured on coverslips and treated with 2 Gy IR. Depending on the foci to be stained, cells were then washed in PBS at 1 h after irradiation; pre-extracted with a solution of 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose and 0.5% NP-40 for 10 min on ice; incubated in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. For RAD51 and RPA32, cells were fixed at 6 h after 10 Gy IR. For RPA32, a mixture of acetone/ methanol (1:1) was used as a fixative at −20 °C. For the recovery time course, cells were fixed at the indicated time. Samples were then permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 solution for 10 min at room temperature; blocked with 5% goat serum and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Antibodies were obtained from the following sources: γ-H2AX (Millipore, 05-636), MDC1 (Millipore, 05-1572), FK2 (Millipore, 04-263), BRCA1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-6954), RPA32 (Santa Cruz, sc-56770) and RAD51 (GeneTex, GTX100469). Samples were then washed three times and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to visualize nuclear DNA. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides with anti-fade solution and visualized using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope.

Comet assay

Cells were irradiated (5 Gy IR), incubated for 4 hours, and embedded in agarose for neutral Comet assay (abcam, ab238544) and stained with SYBR™ Gold Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Invitrogen™,S11494). Tail DNA percentage was quantified using ImageJ with OpenComet plugins (nå 50 cells/sample).

HRD score calculation

Genomic DNA was extracted and subjected to whole-genome sequencing (DNBSEQ platform; >30x coverage). HRD scores (LOH, TAI, LST) were computed using scarHRD software (v1.0; thresholds per Myriad Genetics guidelines).

Cell cycle analysis by EdU incorporation

To assess cell cycle dynamics, cell lines were treated with 50 nM MRTX1133 or BI-2865 for the indicated time points (0–72 h). Cells were pulsed with 10 μM 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 2 h prior to harvest. EdU incorporation was detected using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 594 Imaging Kit (Invitrogen, C10339) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and incubated with the Click-iT reaction cocktail for 30 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides with anti-fade solution and visualized using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope with excitation at 594 nm for EdU and 405 nm for Hoechst 33342. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified by counting at least 100 cells per field from at least five random fields per sample, using ImageJ software.

Repair assays

HR repair efficiency was quantified using the DR-GFP reporter assay, as previously described46. Briefly, PDAC cells were seeded in 6-cm plates in triplicate and transfected 24 h later with a mixture containing 2 µL Polyetherimide (PEI), 1000 ng DR-GFP plasmid, 100 ng mCherry plasmid, and 1000 ng I-SceI plasmid to generate double-strand breaks (DSBs). After 8–16 h of transfection, cells were treated with the specified reagents. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry (Attune NxT Flow Cytometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific). HR repair efficiency was calculated as the ratio of GFP-positive cells or mCherry/GFP double-positive cells (Supp. Fig. 15A).

Colony formation assay

Cells with 2 days pretreated with MRTX1133 (50nM) were seeded at low density and treated with indicated concentrations of Olaparib or indicated dose of irradiation the next day; cells were then left for 10–12 days to allow colonies to form. Colonies were stained with staining solution (0.25% crystal violet, 25% methanol in 1× PBS) for colony visualization. Colonies were counted manually (colonies containing 50 or more cells were counted) or digitally using ImageJ software with customized parameters. Results were normalized to plating efficiencies. Unless otherwise stated, each value is relative to the value in the cells treated with vehicle control.

Cell viability assay and synergy score analysis

PDAC cell lines were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well in 100 µL media, respectively. Drugs were dispensed in each well. The next day, cells were treated with DMSO or the indicated concentrations of inhibitors. After 3 days, cell viability was measured by optical density using the CCK8 assay (40203ES80, Yeasen). Then the optical density was measured by a microplate reader (Biotek 800TS, USA, Santa Clara). After background subtraction, we calculated the cell viability as the percentage change relative to the control cells.

For combination treatments, cells were exposed to trametinib (MEK inhibitor), alpelisib (PI3K inhibitor), MRTX1133 (KRASG12D inhibitor), or olaparib (PARPi) at their respective IC50 concentrations, and growth curves were monitored over 96 hours using real-time cell analysis (IncuCyte system).

As for scoring of combination therapy, Synergyfinder47 was used to determine inhibition rate and calculate the Bliss, Loewe, zero interaction potency (ZIP) scores and highest single agent (HSA) synergy scores. Due to the fact that these models were developed under significantly different assumptions about the behavior of individual drugs, it is crucial to carefully interpret the identified synergy and antagonism patterns to avoid false-positive and false-negative results. Here, we outputted four synergy score to avoid multiple testing bias.

In vivo mouse models

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Tumor Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (NCC2024A149) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Experiments were conducted using only 6–8-week-old female mice. Mice were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-h light and dark cycle, a temperature range of 20–25 °C, and humidity maintained between 30% and 70%. The study did not include sex-based comparisons, as the focus was not on sex-specific effects. To avoid additional variables from mixed sex populations, all female mice were used in this study.

Mouse CDX models were established by transplanting indicated PANC-1 and SW1990 (parental and resistant) cells into 6-week-old BALB/c nude mice. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume = 0.5 × L × W × W. When tumors measured 80–120 mm3, mice were divided into groups for the indicated treatment. Tumor-bearing mice were treated daily with vehicle, MRTX1133 (30 mg/kg), olaparib (50 mg/kg), or the combination starting on Day 12 (upon tumors reaching ~100 mm³) through Day 18 (7 days total). Tumor volume was measured on Day 19, followed by euthanasia and tumor collection for downstream analyses.

For PDXs, BALB/c nude mice were used at the age of 6 weeks. PDX models (passage 3–5) were purchased from Nanchang Royo Biotech Co., Ltd. The sample collection was approved by the medical ethics committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Permit No. 2021012). Mice were monitored for tumor volume. When tumors reached 1 cm in diameter, tumors were aseptically collected and reimplanted into new mice. For each model, tumor fragments (4 × 4 × 4 mm3) were implanted on the right hind flanks of the mouse host and allowed to grow to an average volume of 80–120 mm3 as monitored by caliper measurements. Tumor-bearing mice were treated daily with vehicle, MRTX1133 (30 mg/kg), olaparib (50 mg/kg), or the combination starting on Day 15 (upon tumors reaching ~100 mm³) through Day 21 (7 days total). Tumor volume was measured on Day 22, followed by euthanasia and tumor collection for downstream analyses.

For the subcutaneous KPC1199 tumor models, 2 × 106 KPC cells were subcutaneously injected into the flank of C57BL/6 mice. Treatment was initiated when tumors reached an average volume of 80–120 mm3. Mice were treated with vehicle, MRTX1133 (30 mg/kg), olaparib (50 mg/kg), or the combination for 7 days. Tumor growth was measured every other day. Tumors were then harvested for single-cell sequencing and flow cytometry.

For the orthotopic model, KPC-luc (luciferase expressing), 1 × 105 cells, were injected with Matrigel (BD Bioscience) in the tail of the pancreas of C57BL/6 mice. One week after injection, tumors were detected by bioluminescent image, and mice were randomly assigned to groups for the indicated treatment. The tumor was monitored by bioluminescent imaging once a week for a total of three times, starting from when the signal was first detectable. Mice were treated with vehicle, MRTX1133 (30 mg/kg), olaparib (50 mg/kg), or the combination for 7 days.

For the orthotopic model for survival, KPC-luc (luciferase expressing), 1 × 105 cells, were injected with Matrigel (BD Bioscience) in the tail of the pancreas of C57BL/6 mice. One week after injection, mice were randomly assigned to groups for the indicated treatment. Mice were treated with vehicle, MRTX1133 (30 mg/kg), olaparib (50 mg/kg), or the combination for 7 days. Subsequently, mouse survival was monitored daily for up to 30 days.

For CD8+ T Cell Depletion, immunocompetent C57BL/6 J mice were subcutaneously inoculated with KPC1199 cells on day 0. CD8+ T cell depletion was initiated on day 6 with intraperitoneal administration of anti-CD8 antibodies (200 μg/dose, Clone 53-6.7, BioXCell) or isotype controls, repeated every 3 days throughout the study. Combination therapy with MRTX1133 (30 mg/kg intraperitoneal daily) and Olaparib (50 mg/kg intraperitoneal daily) commenced on day 7 and continued for 7 days (day 7–day 13). Vehicle controls were administered in parallel. Tumor volumes were monitored every day until the endpoint on day 14, at which point tumors were harvested for weight measurement and further analyses.

The maximal tumor size permitted by our Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee is 2000 mm³ in volume. We monitored tumor growth every day, and we confirmed that the maximal tumor size was not exceeded in any experiment. Animals were euthanized humanely before reaching these endpoints.

RNA sequencing

AsPC-1, PANC-1, and SW1990 cells were cultured in duplicate and treated with 50 nmol/L MRTX1133 for 24 h. Tumor tissue was harvested from PDX at the endpoint. RNA integrity and purity were verified after isolation, and then sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (Novogene Co., Ltd., Beijing) was performed. Differential expressions were analyzed with DESeq2. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) used the Reactome pathway database48 with default parameters.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of the KPC1199 tumor

KPC1199 tumors were harvested, dissociated into single-cell suspensions (viability ≥85%), and processed for scRNA-seq using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3 Kit (10× Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Single-cell libraries were prepared using the Chromium platform, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina Nova 6000 PE150. Raw reads were demultiplexed and aligned to the reference genome using Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) with default parameters. KPC1199 cells expressing <200 or >6000 genes, >5% mitochondrial genes, or >3% red blood cell genes were excluded. Genes with >100,000 counts per cell were also filtered out. After quality control, 115,664 cells and 27,121 genes were retained for analysis. Samples were normalized and integrated using Harmony. Dimensionality reduction was performed by PCA and visualized with UMAP. Unsupervised clustering at resolution 0.5 identified 24 clusters, which were annotated using SingleR (v1.4.1) and known markers as: cancer cells, T/NK cells, Myeloid cells, Neutrophil cells, CAF, endothelial cells, mast cells, pericytes, and lymphatic endothelial cells. T/NK cells were re-clustered (resolution 2.7) and classified as CD8 Teff, CD8 T_Prolif, CD8 Tex, CD8 Tnaive, CD4 T and CD4_Treg. Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was performed using GSVA (v1.46.0) with MSigDB Hallmark gene sets to calculate pathway enrichment scores for each sample.

Flow cytometry sample preparation and analysis

Fresh tumor tissues were minced with sterile scissors, mechanically dissociated by pressing through a 70-μm cell strainer using a 5 mL syringe plunger, and passed through a second 70-μm strainer. Cells were collected, centrifuged (500×g, 10 min, RT), and red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysis buffer (A1049201, Thermo Fisher) for 5 min at RT. After centrifugation and washing, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer. Cells were first incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 Fc block (clone 93, 101319, BioLegend) for 10 min at 4 °C to block nonspecific binding. Live/dead discrimination was performed using BD Horizon® Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Biosciences, 564406), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Samples were analyzed on a Cytek NL-CLC3000 flow cytometer. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1). Gate strategy was presented in Suppl. Fig. 15B.

Databases analysis

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma RNA-seq data sets: Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network49, TCGA with RNA-sequencing by expectation maximization (RSEM) estimated batch-normalized median expression values, mutation status and clinical data were downloaded from TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/). From these, KRASG12D and non-KRASG12D samples were selected based on the missense mutation. The fold-change difference in gene expression between KRASG12D and non-KRASG12D samples was calculated by computing the Log2 ratio of the average expression values in tumor samples. GSEA was conducted using the R-fgsea package. A cohort containing both mutation data and patient survival data was used for survival analysis. Kaplan–Meier analysis and the log-rank test were used to estimate patient prognosis.

Immunohistochemistry and hematoxylin-eosin staining

Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5-mm slices, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard procedures. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed using a citric acid buffer (pH 6.8) in a pressure cooker for 20 minutes. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 3% H2O2 in tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 20 min at room temperature. Tissue sections were then blocked with 200 µL of 10% goat serum and 2.5% horse serum. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with the primary anti-γ-H2AX (Millipore, 05-636), Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580), caspase3 (CST, #9664), RAD51 (GeneTex, GTX100469), and CD8 (Abcam, ab217344) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times in TBS with 1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 5 min each, the sections were incubated with Goat anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were washed again three times with TBST for 5 min each, then developed using DAB HRP Substrate. Finally, the slides were counterstained, dehydrated, and cover-slipped according to standard protocols. Histologic slides were captured by microscopy, and the analysis was processed in ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Graphical data representation and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10, with specific details provided in the respective figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, a one-way ANOVA was employed. For tumor growth curves, two-way ANOVA was used. For animal survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were performed using the log-rank test.
