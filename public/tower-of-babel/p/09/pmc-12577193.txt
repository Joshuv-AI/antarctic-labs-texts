The VigilanS-Prison programme: a pilot study of a brief contact intervention for reducing suicidal behaviours in prisons (2021–2024)
Authors: Thomas Fovet, Marion Estoup, Fabien D’Hondt, Vincent Jardon, Christophe Debien, Pierre Thomas, Guillaume Vaiva, Marielle Wathelet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSuicidal behaviour is highly prevalent in prisons. The VigilanS programme is a brief contact intervention that is now recognized as a pillar of France’s national suicide prevention strategy. This study aimed to evaluate the VigilanS-prison intervention, focusing on two aspects: its feasibility and its effectiveness in reducing the rates of suicide attempts (SAs) and suicides in correctional facilities.MethodsVigilanS-Prison was launched in June 2021 in two of the 16 correctional facilities for adults in the Hauts-de-France region: Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin. The programme expanded to Longuenesse and Vendin-le-Vieil in June 2023 and to Maubeuge in January 2024. The activity of the programme from June 2021 to April 2024 was described. To assess its impact, suicidal behaviours recorded by the prison administration between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023, were analysed. Interrupted time series (ITS) analyses compared the monthly rates of SAs and suicides before and after programme implementation in Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin. Joint time series analyses compared these trends to those of control facilities.ResultsA total of 384 individuals (43 women) were included in VigilanS-Prison due to either a history of SAs in the year prior to detention (n = 83) or following an SA in prison (n = 301). During the study, 1,343 postcards were distributed, with 78 participants (20.3%) responding at least once. Following implementation, the SA rates in Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin decreased significantly (-37.31 per 10,000), whereas the suicide rates showed a non-significant reduction (-2.91 per 10,000). Over time, declines in monthly SA (-0.69 per 10,000) and suicide (-0.09 per 10,000) rates were observed but remained non-significant. Comparisons with control facilities revealed no significant differences in immediate or long-term trends.ConclusionThe VigilanS-Prison programme has demonstrated feasibility and an immediate significant reduction in SA rates in intervention facilities. However, its effect on suicide rates was modest and non-significant. The long-term trends suggest potential benefits, although these effects remain non-significant. The specificity of the programme's impact could not be established, given the non-significant results in comparisons with control facilities. Further evaluation with larger datasets collected at the individual level and over a longer follow-up period is needed to confirm the effectiveness of the programme.Keywords: Suicide, Prison, Prevention, Brief contact intervention, Postcard

Introduction

Suicidal behaviour is a major concern in prisons and correctional facilities worldwide [1]. In France, suicide accounts for approximately half of all deaths in correctional facilities [2]. Compared with the general population, suicide is 7.4 times and 26.9 times more frequent among incarcerated men and women, respectively. The average suicide rate in French prisons and correctional facilities is 16.4 per 10,000 person-years, making it one of the highest worldwide [1]. Additionally, suicide attempts (SAs) are disproportionately common, with a pooled prevalence during incarceration of 8.6% (95% CI: 6.1–11.2) in men and 12.2% (95% CI: 7.1–17.2) in women [3]. Therefore, addressing suicide and self-harm prevention within correctional facilities is a critical challenge.

Although numerous risk factors have been identified [4], evidence that supports interventions for preventing suicidal behaviour in correctional settings remains limited [5]. Notably, the World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted the importance of integrating prison suicide prevention into national suicide prevention strategies and encouraging the development of innovative approaches tailored to the prison environment.

Brief contact interventions (BCIs), developed in the 1980 s, are cost-effective strategies that maintain contact with patients after a SA through crisis cards, phone calls, letters, or postcards, often during the high-risk period following an SA [6, 7]. BCIs typically involve brief messages expressing concern and offering help. Recent reviews suggest that BCIs reduce the risk of suicide reattempts, with postcards being particularly effective in maintaining connection and support [8]. The VigilanS programme is a national suicide prevention initiative in France that uses BCIs, such as crisis cards, phone calls, and postcards, to support individuals discharged after an SA. Compared with standard care, it has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing recurrent SAs, particularly among those with a history of prior attempts [9]. The VigilanS programme is now one of the pillars of France’s national suicide prevention strategy [10].

On the basis of the VigilanS programme, we designed a BCI that is appropriate for the prison environment (i.e., the VigilanS-Prison) [11, 12]. This programme has been progressively implemented within five correctional facilities in Northern France. This study aimed to evaluate the VigilanS-Prison intervention, focusing on two key aspects: (i) its feasibility, assessed through programme activity, and (ii) its effectiveness in reducing SA and suicide rates.

Methods

Description of the VigilanS-Prison programme

Figure 1 provides an overview of the VigilanS-Prison programme. The inclusion criteria were (i) an SA within the year prior to incarceration (i.e., Criterion 1) or (ii) while in detention (i.e., Criterion 2). Criterion 1 was systematically evaluated during the admission process (within the first 72 h of incarceration) using the following question: “In the past year, have you tried to kill yourself?”. Criterion 2 was fulfilled for any self-harming behaviour in prison, regardless of suicidal intent. This criterion makes it possible to begin the programme during the first days of incarceration, a high-risk period for suicidal behaviour [13].

The VigilanS-Prison programme algorithm

Both criteria were evaluated by a mental health professional. For individuals included in the programme, five postcards (Day 0, Day 8, Month 2, Month 3, and Month 4) were personally delivered. All postcards were handwritten and sent on behalf of the VigilanS-Prison team. The messages conveyed concern for the individual’s well-being (e.g., “We hope you are doing well”) and encouraged a response (e.g., “Don’t hesitate to write us”). If participants replied, the VigilanS-Prison team sent a personalized response, initiating correspondence. No materials were reviewed by prison staff. Upon release, each participant received a VigilanS-Prison emergency “green” card with a phone number providing access to community mental health services if needed.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants during the screening process, following the provision of an information letter. This study was reviewed and approved by the local ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord-Ouest IV) under reference number HP 23/06.

The French prison system is structured into three main types of facilities: jails (“maisons d’arrêt”, MAs) for individuals awaiting trial or serving short sentences (typically less than two years); detention centres (“centres de détention”, CDs) for individuals serving medium-length to long sentences with a lower risk profile; and high-security prisons (“maisons centrales”, MCs) for individuals serving long sentences for serious crimes. VigilanS-Prison was deployed in five of the 16 correctional facilities for adults in Northern France: Lille-Sequedin (programme implemented in June 2021), Lille-Annoeullin (June 2021), Longuenesse (June 2023), Vendin-le-Vieil (June 2023), and Maubeuge (January 2024). Activity of the VigilanS-Prison programme

Data collection

To describe the activity of VigilanS-Prison, we used the database maintained by the VigilanS centre in Lille and updated by the programme’s coordinators. For VigilanS-Prison, the dataset included the following:

Socio-demographic information: age (in years) and sex (male, female);

Socio-demographic information: age (in years) and sex (male, female);

Suicidal behaviours: the occurrence of an SA or completed suicide after inclusion (yes/no);

Suicidal behaviours: the occurrence of an SA or completed suicide after inclusion (yes/no);

Follow-up characteristics: the inclusion criteria for the programme (Criterion 1 or 2), the dates of the four postcard mailings, whether the individual responded (yes/no), and the incarcerated person’s status (in case of release or transfer).

Follow-up characteristics: the inclusion criteria for the programme (Criterion 1 or 2), the dates of the four postcard mailings, whether the individual responded (yes/no), and the incarcerated person’s status (in case of release or transfer).

The study period spanned June 2021 to April 2024.

Statistical analyses

Only individuals who received at least one postcard were included in the analyses, as non-receipt of a postcard was considered equivalent to not benefiting from VigilanS-Prison. All socio-demographic variables were categorical and are presented as counts and percentages. The number of postcards sent and correspondences initiated were recorded.

Impact of the VigilanS-Prison programme

Data collection

To assess the impact of VigilanS-Prison on suicidal behaviours, we used data from the prison administration (Direction Inter-régionale des Services Pénitentiaires, DISP Grand-Nord), which allowed us to calculate monthly SA (defined as any self-harming behaviour in prison, regardless of suicidal intent) and suicide rates for each facility between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023. The dataset included the following:

The number of available places and incarcerated people (annual data as of January 1);'

The number of available places and incarcerated people (annual data as of January 1);'

The distribution of incarcerated people by age group and sex;

The distribution of incarcerated people by age group and sex;

The number of suicides by sex (monthly data);

The number of suicides by sex (monthly data);

The number of SAs by sex (monthly data).

The number of SAs by sex (monthly data).

Statistical analyses

Monthly suicide and SA rates were calculated for each facility as the ratio of the number of suicides or SAs to the average incarcerated population, defined as the mean of the number of incarcerated people on January 1 of the current year and January 1 of the subsequent year.

The evolution of suicidal behaviours in Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin before and after the implementation of VigilanS-Prison was estimated using interrupted time series (ITS) analyses. These analyses model a time series of the outcome of interest (here, suicides and SAs in detention) to establish an underlying trend, which is then “interrupted” by an event at a known time point (in this case, the implementation of VigilanS-Prison in June 2021) [17]. The models used to study the effect of VigilanS-Prison on the monthly SA and suicide rates in these two facilities included three terms: (i) time (in months from June 2021 to December 2023), (ii) period (before or after the implementation of VigilanS-Prison), and (iii) time since the implementation of VigilanS-Prison. ITS analysis makes it possible to determine whether VigilanS-Prison led to a change in the level or trend of the time series of suicidal behaviours. We were unable to assess the impact of VigilanS-Prison implementation in the Longuenesse, Vendin-Le-Vieil and Maubeuge prisons because the programme was initiated in these prisons at the end of the study period.

Additionally, to compare the evolution of suicidal behaviours in Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin to that in the 14 other control facilities in the region over the same period, joint time series (JTS) analyses were conducted [18]. These models used a difference-in-differences (DID) approach, incorporating the following terms: (i) time (in months from June 2021 to December 2023), (ii) the period (before or after VigilanS-Prison implementation), (iii) group (VigilanS-Prison or control), (iv) the time since VigilanS-Prison implementation, and (v) interactions: time*group, period*group, and time since implementation*group. JTS analysis ensures that the observed changes are specific to the facilities where the programme was implemented and are not due to other factors—common across all facilities—that could influence the indicators. The Longuenesse, Vendin-Le-Vieil and Maubeuge prisons were considered control establishments because the programme began in these facilities at the very end of the study period (June 2023).

A significance threshold of 5% was used. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.0.3.

Results

Activity of the VigilanS-Prison programme

Characteristics of individuals

Between June 2021 and April 2024, 400 incarcerated individuals were included in the VigilanS-Prison programme. Sixteen individuals who did not receive any postcards were excluded from the analyses, resulting in a final sample of 384 individuals, accounting for 397 programme inclusions, as 13 individuals (3.4%) were included twice. Most participants were housed in Lille-Annoeullin (n = 197) and Lille-Sequedin (n = 150), the first facilities to deploy VigilanS-Prison. The remaining participants were included from Longuenesse (n = 25), Maubeuge (n = 7), and Vendin-Le-Vieil (n = 5). The majority were men (n = 341; 88.8%), while women accounted for 11.2% of the inclusions (n = 43). The most represented age group was 30–39 years, accounting for 35.2% of men and 39.5% of women.

A total of 83 individuals (21.6% of all inclusions), including 68 men (19.9% of men) and 15 women (34.9% of women), were included in the programme because of a suicide attempt (SA) within the year prior to their incarceration (i.e., Criterion 1). The remaining 301 individuals (78.4% of all inclusions), including 273 men (80.1% of men) and 28 women (65.1% of women), were included following an SA during their incarceration (i.e., Criterion 2).

Among the 384 individuals included in the programme, 106 (28%) made an SA after inclusion (i.e., recurrence of suicidal behaviour). Of these individuals, 69 (65.1%) made a single attempt in recurrence, and 37 (35.1% of them) made multiple attempts in recurrence. Among the individuals who had reattempted suicide, 12 were included on the basis of Criterion 1, and 94 were included on the basis of Criterion 2. This represents a recurrence rate of 14.5% (12/83) among individuals included based on Criterion 1, compared with 31.2% (94/301) among those included based on Criterion 2.

Postcards and correspondences initiated

A total of 1,343 postcards were distributed between June 2021 and April 2024, consisting of 397 postcards No. 1, 359 postcards No. 2, 316 postcards No. 3, and 271 postcards No. 4. Among the 384 participants, 266 (69.3%) received all 4 postcards, and 78 (20.3%) responded at least once, including 67 men (19.6% of all men), and 11 women (25.6% of all women).

Impact of the VigilanS-Prison programme

Efficacy of the programme in the facilities in which it was deployed – results of the ITS analysis

Suicide rates

The average monthly suicide rates before and after the intervention were 2.3 and 1.9 per 10,000, respectively, in the intervention group, and 1.4 and 1.7 per 10,000 in the control group. The evolution of the monthly suicide rate from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023, in the Lille-Annoeullin and Lille-Sequedin facilities is shown in Fig. 3. In the period following the deployment of VigilanS-Prison, compared with what was expected on the basis of the trend projected from the pre-deployment period, the suicide rate was lower and showed a slower increase. However, ITS analysis indicated that the immediate reduction in the monthly suicide rate following the implementation of VigilanS-Prison (−2.91; p = 0.089) and the evolution of the slope during the post-deployment period (−0.09; p = 0.267) were not statistically significant.

Evolution of the monthly suicide rate (in suicides per 10,000 individuals) before and after the deployment of VigilanS-Prison in the Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin facilities (ITS analysis results)

SA rates

The average monthly suicide rates before and after the intervention were 36.0 and 60.7 per 10,000, respectively, in the intervention group, and 37.8 and 55.0 per 10,000 in the control group. The evolution of the monthly SA rate from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023, in the Lille-Annoeullin and Lille-Sequedin facilities is shown in Fig. 4. In the period following the deployment of VigilanS-Prison, compared with what was expected on the basis of the trend projected from the pre-deployment period, the rate of SA was lower and showed slightly slower growth. However, according to the ITS analysis, only the result concerning the immediate reduction in the monthly SA rate following the implementation of VigilanS-Prison was significant (−37.31; p = 0.001). The slope in the post-deployment period was not significantly different from that before deployment (−0.69; p = 0.216).

Efficacy of the programme in the facilities in which it was deployed compared to other facilities – results of the JTS analysis

Suicide rates

The evolution of the monthly suicide rate from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023, in each of the two groups is illustrated in Fig. 5. According to the results of the JTS analysis, the monthly suicide rates in the two groups did not vary significantly before June 2021 (p = 0.235). The observed decrease in the suicide rate in the Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin facilities immediately after the deployment of VigilanS-Prison compared with the control facilities was not significant (immediate effect = −1.49 per 10,000 per month; p = 0.427). Similarly, the slope of the evolution of the monthly suicide rate after the deployment of VigilanS-Prison in the Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin facilities was less steep compared to the control facilities, but this result was not significant (effect on trend = −0.146 per 10,000 per month; p = 0.127).

SA rates

The evolution of the monthly SA rate from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023, in both groups is shown in Fig. 6. According to the results of the JTS analysis, the monthly SA rates in both groups did not vary significantly before June 2021 (p = 0.808). The observed decrease in the SA rate in the Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin facilities immediately after the deployment of VigilanS-Prison compared with the control facilities was not significant (immediate effect = −12.66 per 10,000 per month; p = 0.317). Conversely, the slope of the evolution of the monthly TS rate after the deployment of VigilanS-Prison in the Lille-Sequedin and Lille-Annoeullin facilities was steeper compared to the control facilities, but this result was not significant (effect on trend = 0.816 per 10,000 per month; p = 0.204).

Discussion

We successfully implemented a BCI, VigilanS-Prison, for suicide prevention in five correctional facilities in Northern France. A total of 384 individuals, including 43 women, participated in the programme, and 1,343 postcards were distributed, with 78 individuals (20.3%) responding at least once. Our findings indicate an immediate reduction in the monthly SA rates in the two facilities where VigilanS-Prison was initially deployed. However, the immediate effect on the monthly suicide rate was modest and statistically non-significant. Regarding long-term effects, we observed a decrease in the slope of the monthly suicide and self-harm rates, although the comparison with the slope before deployment of the VigilanS-Prison was not statistically significant. Compared with correctional facilities in the same region that did not benefit from the VigilanS-Prison programme, no significant differences were observed in either the immediate impact on suicide and SA rates or changes in their average rates.

The development of suicide prevention strategies in prisons is a real challenge [14]. These results demonstrate that BCIs can be successfully implemented in prison settings. However, several modifications were necessary to adapt the VigilanS programme to this context. In particular, phone contact, which is a key feature of the VigilanS system for the general population, was not feasible due to prison regulations. Consequently, the programme relied solely on written correspondence. All materials were distributed by mental health professionals, which offered several advantages, such as providing the opportunity for illiterate or non-French-speaking individuals to read postcards and fostering a therapeutic relationship through the delivery process.

An especially notable finding of our study is that over one-fifth of the incarcerated people who participated in the programme responded to the postcards at least once. This finding suggests that the VigilanS-Prison programme may serve as a valuable tool for alleviating feelings of isolation and lack of social support, both of which are well-established risk factors for self-harm in incarcerated individuals [3]. The overrepresentation of suicidal behaviour in prisons is often linked to high-risk factors such as solitary confinement, victimization, and inadequate social support. Notably, both social support—by fostering a sense of connection and the feeling of being heard—and enhanced suicide prevention literacy—which includes increasing knowledge about self-harm behaviours, available help, and how to access this help—have been identified as potential mechanisms driving the effectiveness of BCIs. However, despite these promising findings, we were unable to demonstrate the specific impact of the intervention on the evolution of SAs and suicides in this study.

These negative results could be explained by several limitations in the design of the study. Notably, the limited time since the implementation of the VigilanS-Prison programme and the small number of facilities involved reduce the statistical power of the study. This lack of power may have hindered our ability to detect the programme’s effect on suicidal behaviours. Furthermore, for practical reasons, the evaluation relied on aggregated data at the facility level rather than on individual-level data. Institutional-level analyses tend to dilute individual and contextual variations, potentially making them less sensitive for detecting intervention effects, particularly in smaller samples. This approach limits statistical power and the model’s ability to isolate specific effects at the individual level, such as prior SAs or detention conditions, which may influence the response to the programme. An individual longitudinal analysis would provide a more accurate understanding of the impact of VigilanS-Prison and enhance the robustness of conclusions regarding its effectiveness. Furthermore, two of the 14 control facilities began implementing the programme in June 2023. However, the likelihood that these two facilities, which received the programme only during the last six months of the study period, prevented us from demonstrating the effect of the programme is minimal. Finally, the penetration rate of the VigilanS programme cannot be calculated in this study. It may, in particular, be affected by under-reporting of previous suicide attempts in the year prior to imprisonment, especially due to shame, stigma, or fear of punishment in prison.

Future evaluations should use individualized data, include precise measurements of suicidal behaviours over an extended follow-up period, and incorporate additional dimensions, such as mental health indicators, to assess the programme’s broader effects. It will also be important to assess the impact of the VigilanS programme according to the type of institution (prison, jail or high-security facility). Although the post-implementation increase in suicide attempts was not statistically different from control facilities, future research should also assess whether VigilanS heightens awareness of suicidal behavior, facilitating its detection and reporting. Moreover, a quantitative approach alone cannot fully analyse the underlying mechanisms of VigilanS-Prison. Complementing it with a qualitative approach—through focus groups, interviews, and direct input from all stakeholders (health care providers, prison staff, administrators, and users)—would offer a holistic understanding of the programme’s impact and reveal how and why it works (or does not work) in different contexts. Directly surveying participants could clarify the postcards’ effectiveness and provide insights for tailoring the prevention program in prison and after release. This combined approach would help identify optimization strategies and generate nuanced, actionable recommendations to guide the national rollout of VigilanS in correctional facilities.

Future research should explore alternative time intervals for sending postcards, as well as complementary interventions, such as in-prison messaging or peer mentoring, to enhance the effectiveness of the program. Optimizations of the VigilanS-Prison algorithm could be considered, as previous research has emphasized the importance of tailoring BCIs to the specific needs of patients with varying levels of suicidality [15]. In particular, a number of studies investigating the impact of BCIs have shown the increased vulnerability of people suffering from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [15, 16]. Considering the high prevalence of PTSD and stress-related disorders in prisons [17–19], specific strategies could be developed for this sub-group.

Finally, the VigilanS-Prison programme is time consuming, requiring significant commitment from the health care providers and stakeholders involved in its implementation. This fact highlights the need for a comprehensive socio-economic evaluation to assess the programme’s cost-effectiveness and sustainability. Such an evaluation should analyse the balance between the resources invested, including time and personnel, and the potential benefits in terms of reducing suicidal behaviours and improving mental health outcomes in correctional facilities. Such an analysis will help determine whether the programme offers a viable solution for broader implementation.

Conclusion

BCIs to prevent suicidal behaviour can be implemented for people in prisons. Future studies should investigate the effectiveness of BCIs in preventing suicidal behaviour in the prison population. They should also consider the impact of the emergency “green” card provided upon release, as the post-release period is marked by a particularly high mortality rate. While promising, the VigilanS-Prison trial requires both a re-evaluation with a larger set of data collected at the individual level over an extended follow-up period to confirm its effectiveness and a qualitative assessment to thoroughly analyse the underlying processes of the programme.
