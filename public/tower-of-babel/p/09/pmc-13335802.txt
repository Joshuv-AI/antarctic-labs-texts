Sudden cardiac death in a child with low-risk hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: limitations of current pediatric risk stratification—a case report
Authors: Salma Bensalah, Amina Outahayou, Abdellah Sbai Sassi, Ibtissam Fellat, Mohamed Cherti, Andriana Anagnostopoulou, Karina Del Valle Zamora, Giulia Bruno, Ugur Canpolat, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is characterized by marked phenotypic heterogeneity and remains a leading cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young individuals. Risk stratification in children is particularly challenging, and current predictive models may underestimate arrhythmic risk in certain patients.Case summaryWe report a familial case of sarcomeric HCM. A 41-year-old man presented with sustained ventricular tachycardia revealing asymmetric HCM, requiring implantable cardioverter–defibrillator (ICD) implantation, and later died from end-stage heart failure. His 12-year-old son was diagnosed with asymmetric HCM and was classified as low risk according to the HCM Risk-Kids score. At the time of evaluation, the child did not meet current guideline based criteria for ICD implantation. Nevertheless, SCD occurred during moderate physical exertion 2 months after diagnosis.DiscussionThis familial case highlights the limitations of paediatric risk stratification scores at the individual level and underscores the need for a more individualized approach integrating family history, early phenotypic expression, advanced imaging findings, and genetic context beyond risk scores alone.Keywords: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Sudden cardiac death, Risk stratification, Implantable cardioverter–defibrillator, Case report

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiac disease and a leading cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young individuals. Diagnosis primarily relies on transthoracic echocardiography, while cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) plays an increasingly important role by providing additional prognostic information and aiding surgical planning. Therapeutic strategies have progressively evolved to include novel pharmacological agents, implantable cardioverter–defibrillators (ICDs), tailored septal myectomy, and mitral valve repair.

In this case-based familial report, we aim to highlight the arrhythmic risk associated with sarcomeric HCM through the history of a family carrying a TTN gene mutation and to discuss whether SCD could have been prevented in the affected child.

Case report

We report the case of a 41-year-old man (Patient X) with no known cardiovascular risk factors, admitted to our institution for syncope caused by sustained ventricular tachycardia (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed asymmetric HCM. A dual-chamber ICD was implanted. The patient underwent generator replacement in 2018 and subsequently experienced recurrent hospitalizations for decompensated heart failure due to severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction, ultimately leading to death at the age of 56 from end-stage heart failure due to progressive pump failure.

Regular wide-QRS tachycardia consistent with ventricular tachycardia.

As part of family screening, asymmetric sarcomeric HCM was diagnosed in his 12-year-old son. The electrocardiogram showed negative T waves in lateral and inferior leads. Echocardiography revealed predominant septal hypertrophy without left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction or systolic anterior motion (SAM) with maximal left ventricular wall thickness of 17 mm, left atrial diameter of 29 mm, left ventricular outflow tract gradient of 24 mmHg with preserved ejection ventricular fraction, and no history of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia or unexplained syncope (Figure 2). Holter monitoring showed rare premature ventricular contractions. Exercise testing was performed and revealed no arrhythmias, no abnormal blood pressure response, and no symptoms during exertion. Cardiac MRI confirmed asymmetric septal hypertrophy with no extensive late gadolinium enhancement. Genetic testing identified an heterozygous variant in TTN gene (exon 25). This variant had been reported as pathogenic in ClinVar. The paediatric SCD risk score (HCM Risk-Kids) calculated at 3.95% did not indicate ICD implantation. According to American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) paediatric risk markers, the patient had no major risk factors. The ESC HCM Risk SCD model was not applied because it is validated only for patients >16 years. However, 2 months after diagnosis, the child died suddenly during moderate physical exertion while running to catch a school bus.

Parasternal long-axis view showing asymmetric hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with septal predominance.

Among other family members, the brother of Patient X, aged 41 years and asymptomatic, showed electrocardiographic left ventricular hypertrophy with secondary repolarization abnormalities. Echocardiography revealed asymmetric HCM without subaortic obstruction or SAM. Holter monitoring did not show non-sustained ventricular tachycardia. Cardiac MRI confirmed the diagnosis. Risk stratification did not support ICD implantation.

One of his sons, aged 10 years, reported palpitations. His ECG demonstrated electrical left ventricular hypertrophy with repolarization abnormalities. Echocardiography showed asymmetric HCM with SAM with maximum left ventricular wall thickness of 20 mm (Figure 3). Holter monitoring was unremarkable, and cardiac MRI confirmed asymmetric hypertrophy. The paediatric risk score again did not support ICD implantation.

Parasternal long-axis view (A) and apical four-chamber view (B) showing asymmetric hypertrophic cardiomyopathy predominantly involving the septal wall.

Additionally, two sisters of Patient X were diagnosed with sarcomeric HCM without indication for ICD implantation (family pedigree shown in Figure 4). Family screening and genetic investigations are ongoing.

Discussion

Sarcomeric HCM, the most common inherited cardiac disease (prevalence approximately 1:500),1 is an autosomal dominant condition characterized by substantial clinical, phenotypic, prognostic, and genetic heterogeneity.

HCM is defined by a maximal left ventricular wall thickness of ≥15 mm on echocardiography or CMR in the absence of other causes of hypertrophy. Wall thicknesses of 13–14 mm may be diagnostic when associated with a positive genetic test or a strong family history. In children, wall thickness must be indexed to body surface area.

Before the era of molecular genetics, the pathophysiology of HCM remained largely unknown.2 In 1990, positional cloning identified the first disease-causing gene, MYH7, encoding the β-myosin heavy chain, followed in 1995 by MYBPC3, encoding cardiac myosin-binding protein C.3 To date, more than 20 genes have been implicated in HCM.

In our family, genetic testing identified a heterozygous variant in the TTN gene (exon25). This variant has been reported as pathogenic in ClinVar; however, no formal classification according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria was performed in our study. Furthermore, no segregation analysis was available within the family. Based on available literature, TTN mutations appear to play a relatively minor role in HCM. Several studies have described gain-of-function TTN variants in HCM patients. Satoh et al. identified a Z-line mutation increasing binding affinity to α-actinin.4 Other studies reported mutations in the cardiac-specific N2B exon enhancing interaction with DRAL/FHL2.5 Nevertheless, screening studies failed to demonstrate significant involvement of TTN mutations in HCM pathogenesis.6,7 In contrast, TTN mutations are more commonly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy.8 Therefore, the presence of a TTN variant in our case should be interpreted cautiously and cannot alone explain the malignant arrhythmic outcome observed in the child. The absence of a clearly pathogenic sarcomeric mutation represents major limitation of this case and restricts conclusions regarding the role of genotype in the malignant arrhythmic outcome observed.

Clinical presentation is non-specific and may include exertional angina, dyspnoea, palpitations, or syncope.

The ECG is almost always abnormal, with left ventricular hypertrophy observed in more than half of patients. Deep and narrow Q waves in lateral and inferior leads are present in approximately one-third of cases. Other abnormalities include atrial enlargement, bundle branch block, atrial fibrillation, and giant negative T waves (>10 mm) in apical forms.

Transthoracic echocardiography establishes the diagnosis by demonstrating asymmetric septal hypertrophy. Beyond diagnosis, echocardiography is essential for symptom evaluation and SCD risk stratification, including assessment of diastolic and systolic function, left atrial size, apical aneurysm, and presence of SAM.

Cardiac MRI is now routinely performed for diagnostic confirmation and risk assessment. Its main prognostic value lies in late gadolinium enhancement, reflecting myocardial fibrosis, typically with a patchy distribution in hypertrophied segments. The extent of fibrosis is an additional risk marker, although it may be absent in early disease stages.

Management of sarcomeric HCM focuses on relief of outflow tract obstruction, prevention of malignant arrhythmias, and treatment of heart failure. β-Blockers are first-line therapy, followed by verapamil, diltiazem, or disopyramide when needed. Selective myosin inhibitors, particularly mavacamten, represent a major therapeutic advance by significantly reducing gradients and potentially avoiding septal reduction therapy. In patients with persistent symptoms and a gradient ≥50 mmHg despite optimal medical therapy, septal reduction (surgical myectomy or alcohol septal ablation) should be considered in expert centres.

The indication for ICD implantation remains complex. Secondary prevention after resuscitated SCD carries a strong recommendation, as illustrated by Patient X. In primary prevention, decision-making relies on multiple parameters with lower levels of evidence. The ESC HCM Risk-SCD score estimates 5-year risk based on age, maximal wall thickness, LVOT gradient, family history of sudden death, non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, and unexplained syncope. ICD implantation is recommended when estimated risk is ≥4%–5%. Additional factors not included in the score—such as left ventricular ejection fraction <50%, extensive late gadolinium enhancement (>15%), or apical aneurysm—may support ICD implantation. This risk model could not be applied due to the patient’s age of 12 years.

In children under 16 years, the HCM Risk-Kids score is recommended. However, in our case, the child was classified as low risk (HCM Risk Kids model estimated a 5 year risk of SCD of 3.5% below the recommended threshold of 6% for ICD implantation, but within the intermediate risk range of 4%–6%) despite suffering SCD. Recent studies have shown that most cardiac arrests occur in patients categorized as low or intermediate risk by current scores.9 Although incorporation of novel risk markers such as myocardial fibrosis and genetic data may improve future risk stratification, the present case primarily illustrates the limitations of current clinical risk models in individual paediatric patients rather than providing direct evidence for genotype driven risk prediction.

This case does not aim to challenge the overall validity of existing risk stratification models, but rather to highlight their inherent limitations when applied to individual patients. While these models are derived from population-based data and provide valuable guidance, they may fail to capture the full complexity of risk in certain clinical scenarios.

Current practice suggests that physical activity recommendations in HCM should be individualized, taking into account risk factors for SCD, arrhythmias, and overall functional status. While low-to-moderate intensity exercise may be considered in selected patients, high-intensity or competitive sports are generally discouraged in individuals at high risk or with exercise-induced arrhythmias or significant obstruction.

Conclusion

Given the significant complications associated with ICD implantation in children including inappropriate shocks, lead failure, and multiple device replacements, the indication for ICD placement must be carefully individualized, balancing potential benefits against long-term risks.
