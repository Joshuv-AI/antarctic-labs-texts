Total Tau protein as a novel biomarker for detection of neural injury following cardiopulmonary bypass in pediatric congenital heart surgery patients
Authors: Marc J Lussier, Daniel J McKeone, Eric Scott Halstead, Allen R Kunselman, Jason R Imundo, John L Myers, Akif Ündar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association of Tau protein levels with postoperative evidence of neural injury following cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in pediatric cardiac surgery patients.MethodsOne hundred forty-four consecutive pediatric cardiac surgery patients were recruited. Whole blood (3 mL) was collected in EDTA tubes from an arterial line at 5 time points: pre-midline incision, at 3 to 5 minutes on CPB, just prior to weaning from CPB, at 1 hour post-CPB, and at 24 hours post-CPB. Plasma was analyzed via an automated immunoassay for total Tau, a protein linked to brain injury. Tau levels over time were compared between 2 patient groups: age (neonates/infants age <365 days vs children age ≥365 days) and type of heart disease (cyanotic vs acyanotic).ResultsThirty-six patients had postoperative evidence of neural injury. Tau levels over time differed between the 2 groups. At the end of CPB and at 1 hour post-CPB, there were significant differences between patients stratified by age (neonates and infants vs older children; P < .001) and by type of congenital heart disease (cyanotic vs acyanotic; P < .05). At 24 hours post-CPB, each 10-pg/mL increase in total Tau corresponded to a 12% increased risk of neural injury (odds ratio, 1.122; 95% confidence interval, 1.043-1.206; P = .002). When stratified by neural injury, age, and type of congenital heart disease, Tau remained persistently elevated in all patients post-CPB compared to respective baseline levels (P < .001).Conclusions24-hour post-CPB plasma Tau may be a reliable marker of neural injury in the pediatric congenital heart surgery population.Key Words: congenital heart surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass, protein biomarker, total Tau protein, neural injury, pediatrics, neonates and infants

Binary and multivariable logistic regression results for neural injury markers.

Central Message

Total Tau may be a reliable 24-hour postoperative marker of neural injury in the pediatric congenital heart surgery population.

Perspective

Total Tau is an understudied protein biomarker in the perioperative period for pediatric congenital heart surgery patients. To our knowledge, there has not been a study done relating this protein with neural injury. Total Tau may have utility in the postoperative period for evaluation of neural injury risk and to help guide recovery and surveillance for developmental delays.

For pediatric patients undergoing congenital heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), neurodevelopmental delays are a known long-term adverse outcome.1, 2, 3, 4 Protein biomarkers associated with neuronal and glial cell damage, such as S100B and GFAP, have been shown to increase following CPB in children5, 6, 7 and have been associated with developmental delays.6 However, limited evidence suggests the same of another protein biomarker, Total Tau.

Tau acts to stabilize tubulin comprising the axons of the central nervous system and protects against mechanical strain. When neurons are damaged, Tau phosphorylation inhibits its ability to bind to tubulin, destabilizing the axon and releasing Tau into the extracellular space. Tau aggregates are then released into systemic circulation following vascular endothelial dysfunction and disruption of the tight junctions comprising the blood-brain barrier, which can result from mechanical damage or other processes, such as changes in cerebral blood flow.8 Although its association with traumatic brain injury9 and with neural injury in patients with neonatal encephalopathy10,11 is known, few studies have investigated the relationship between postoperative Tau levels and impaired neurodevelopment.12

Current screening algorithms for neurodevelopmental delays and subsequent interventions include risk stratification and surveillance.1 Although patients undergoing open-heart surgery with CPB are known to be at an increased risk for developmental delays,1 a recent nationwide study revealed significant variations in outcomes for Society of Thoracic Surgeons — European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) category-stratified patients between upper-decile and lower-decile hospitals.13 This study showed that all patients, regardless of risk category, were susceptible to adverse outcomes across hospitals. These results suggest that further research focusing on both high- and low-risk patient groups is needed to improve such outcomes as neurodevelopmental delays following congenital heart surgery. Additionally, future studies should include a focus on patient characteristics, such as age and type of CHD, within those risk groups.

Although these factors are patient-specific and cannot be changed, modifiable risk factors, such as perfusion modality, also may play a role in patient outcomes. A recent randomized clinical trial from our Pediatric Cardiovascular Research Center showed no significant differences in short-term clinical outcomes, including Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction 2 (PELOD-2) score, Pediatric Risk of Mortality 3 (PRISM 3) score, Vasoactive-Inotropic Score (VIS), intubation time, pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) length of stay (LOS), hospital LOS, and 180-day mortality, between pulsatile and nonpulsatile perfusion patients when risk-stratified and divided into cyanotic CHD and acyanotic CHD groups, even though the pulsatility index in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) is closer to physiologic levels for pulsatile perfusion patients.14,15 An analysis of protein biomarkers and neural injury was not performed in our previous studies, however.

Beyond risk stratification, postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can detect white matter injury (WMI), which has been shown to precede poor neurodevelopment.16 However, these imaging techniques might not be sensitive enough to sufficiently detect lesions a few months after surgery17 and are not always performed. Given that developmental delays might not manifest until later in life, at which point MRI no longer would be helpful, additional screening methods are needed to aid the detection of neural injury.

Our present secondary subanalysis aimed to explore the levels of total Tau measured 24 hours after surgery in relation to neural injuries. This information may be valuable for guiding surveillance and management during the immediate postoperative period and in the years following congenital heart surgery. Our primary hypothesis is that the presence of total Tau following CPB will be associated with instances of adverse cerebral events, such as seizures and delayed cognitive development. Our secondary hypothesis is that because of the greater risk posed to patients with cyanotic heart disease and younger patients, total Tau levels will be higher in those patients compared to patients with acyanotic heart disease and older patients.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Randomization

Parental informed consent was obtained for patients at the Penn State Health Children's Hospital. Eligible subjects were then enrolled (Penn State College of Medicine IRB Study ID: PRAMS019299-A; last approval date September 20, 2024) based on the following criteria: age <18 years, heart surgery with CPB planned to repair a congenital heart defect, and only 8 Fr to 14 Fr arterial cannulas used during the operation. Additional enrollment criteria are described included in Appendix E1. Patients were then randomized into either the pulsatile group or the nonpulsatile group. Further details on the inclusion, exclusion, and randomization processes are provided in Figure E1. The presence of total Tau and incidence of cerebral events occurring after the surgery were evaluated for each group. Details of cerebral events and time to detection are provided in Table E1. This clinical trial was registered with the National Institutes of Health National ClinicalTrials.gov database (NCT00862407). All experimental protocols were approved by the Penn State College of Medicine Institutional Review Board.

CONSORT flow diagram.

Risk Stratification

Patients were divided into appropriate STAT mortality categories for further analysis based on an objective measure of analyzing mortality risk associated with the procedure performed, as developed by the agencies.18

Anesthesia, Perfusion, and PICU Protocols and Transcranial Doppler and Near-Infrared Spectroscopy

Details of anesthesia, perfusion, and PICU protocols, as well as transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasound and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) operation, can be found in our previously published work14,15,19, 20, 21 and in Appendix E1.

Biomarker Collection and Measurement

For each study patient, 3-mL samples of whole blood were collected from an arterial line at 5 specific time points: baseline (approximately 30 minutes before the midline incision), twice during CPB via an arterial port of the heart-lung machine—once 5 minutes after the initiation of bypass and again right before weaning off of bypass—and twice after CPB was discontinued (once 1 hour after and once 24 hours after). The samples were processed, centrifuged to collect 1.5 mL of plasma, aliquoted in 3 tubes of 0.5 mL each, and stored at −80 °C until assayed. Biomarkers were measured using the Ella Automated Immunoassay System (Catalog # 600-100; Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN). Plasma samples were centrifuged at 16,000 RCF for 4 minutes prior to 1:1 dilution with Sample Diluent 13 (Catalog # 896098; Bio-Techne). In total, 70 μL of diluted sample was loaded into each inlet. Cartridges were then analyzed using Simple Plex Runner software version 3.9.0.28.

PELOD-2 Score, PRISM 3 Score, and VIS

The PELOD-2 score, PRISM 3 score, and VIS were used postoperatively to measure vital organ injury, risk of mortality, and cardiovascular support, respectively. Further details of these scores are provided in our previously published work14,15 and in Appendix E1.

Postoperative Measurements and Neural Injury

Other outcomes evaluated include intubation time (in hours), PICU LOS (in days), hospital LOS, and 180-day mortality. Postoperative chart review was performed to look for evidence of neural injury, including postoperative instances of stroke, seizure, WMI, developmental delays, behavioral problems, memory loss, unexplained loss of consciousness, hearing loss, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADD/ADHD).

Statistical Methods

Demographic, perioperative, operative, and postoperative characteristics were compared between groups based on the patient's neural injury status (with neural injury, without neural injury), age (neonates/infants, older children), CHD status (cyanotic, acyanotic), and perfusion modality (pulsatile flow, nonpulsatile flow). The Fisher exact test was used to compare categorical characteristics between groups for data collected once per patient (eg, sex). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare continuous characteristics between groups for data collected once per patient (eg, hospital LOS).

Linear mixed models were used to compare characteristics between groups for data collected repeatedly over time per patient (eg, Tau levels). The mixed models used an unstructured variance-covariance matrix to account for the within-subject, as well as the between-subject, variability inherent in data collected at multiple time points per patent (ie, repeated measurements). Residual diagnostics were used to assess model fit and underlying parametric assumptions (eg, quantile-quantile plots to assess normality). Logarithmic transformation of an outcome was performed prior to analysis with mixed models as necessary (eg, Tau) to meet parametric assumptions, and back-transformation to the original scale resulted in data being reported as geometric means at each time point. A Box-Cox transformation was used for the outcomes of plasma-free hemoglobin (PFH) and gaseous microemboli counts (GME) prior to mixed model analysis because the logarithmic transformation did not help meet parametric modeling assumptions. Post hoc comparisons between groups at each time point were adjusted for multiple comparisons testing using the Tukey-Kramer method. Binary logistic regression assessed the association of 24-hour post-CPB Tau levels with neural injury. Furthermore, a multivariable logistic regression assessed the association of 24-hour post-CPB Tau level with neural injury adjusted for age status (neonate/infant vs children), CHD status (cyanotic vs acyanotic), and perfusion modality (pulsatile vs nonpulsatile). The effect size from logistic regression models is reported as an odds ratio.

For each subgroup, Spearman correlation coefficients (rS), using the Fisher Z-transformation to obtain 95% confidence intervals, were used to assess the strength of the correlation between 24-hour post-CPB Tau level and GME counts at the right MCA and the arterial line, as well as with operative and postoperative characteristics. All hypothesis tests were 2-sided, and all analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Demographics and Perioperative Characteristics

A total of 144 patients were included in this study. Patients were stratified based on their neural injury status (n = 36 with neural injury, n = 108 without neural injury), age (n = 66 neonates/infants, n = 78 older children), CHD status (n = 38 cyanotic, n = 106 acyanotic), and perfusion modality (n = 74 pulsatile flow, n = 70 nonpulsatile flow). There were differences between patient groups in demographic and perioperative characteristics. Notably, patients who experienced neural injury tended to be younger, weighed less, had decreased height, and had longer aortic clamp (XC) times (Table 1). Differences in demographic and perioperative characteristics between the 2 patient groups are detailed in Table 1 and Appendix E1.

Total Tau Levels

Total Tau protein levels for patients stratified by neural injury status, age, CHD status, and perfusion modality are presented in Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4. (The figures represent median Tau levels; comparisons were based on geometric means obtained from mixed models). In all stratified categories, Tau levels were significantly greater at the end of CPD and after CPB compared to baseline (P < .001; Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4). These levels tended to be greater in patients who experienced neural injury compared to patients who did not experience neural injury before, during, and after CPB, although the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 1).

Just prior to weaning from CPB, median Tau levels were significantly higher in neonates/infants compared to older children (Figure 2). Median Tau levels remained consistently higher at 1 hour after CPB (126.5 pg/mL in neonates/infants vs 73.3 pg/mL in older children; P < .001) and 24 hours after CPB (62.2 pg/mL vs 20.7 pg/mL; P < .001). Cyanotic patients had significantly higher median Tau levels at all 3 time points compared to acyanotic patients: just prior to weaning from CPB: 150.5 pg/mL versus 94.5 pg/mL (P < .001); at 1 hour after CPB: 119.0 pg/mL versus 85.0 pg/mL (P = .008); at 24 hours after CPB: 52.3 pg/mL versus 30.0 pg/mL (P = .036) (Figure 3). There was no significant difference in median Tau levels between pulsatile and nonpulsatile patients at any time point (P > .97; Figure 4).

Neural Injury

Thirty-six patients experienced a postoperative neural injury. In a binary logistic regression model, Tau level at 24 hours post-CPB was a significant marker for determining whether a patient will have a neural injury (Figure 5). For every 10-pg/mL increase in 24-hour post-CPB Tau, the odds of a neural injury increased by 12% (odds ratio [OR], 1.122, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.043-1.206; P = .002). In a multivariable logistic regression model, markers for neural injury included 24-hour post-CPB Tau level, age status (neonate/infant vs children), CHD status (cyanotic vs acyanotic), and perfusion modality (pulsatile vs nonpulsatile). Adjusting for age <365 days, acyanotic status, and perfusion modality, for every 10-pg/mL increase in 24-hour post-CPB Tau, the odds of a neural injury occurrence increased by 8% (adjusted OR [aOR], 1.086; 95% CI, 1.001-1.178; P = .046). The aOR for the other predictors were not statistically significant (age status: aOR, 1.812, 95% CI, 0.713-4.604, P = .21; CHD status: aOR, 1.240; 95% CI, 0.500-3.070, P = .64; perfusion modality: aOR, 1.302, 95% CI, 0.580-2.925, P = .52) (Figure 5).

Discussion

This study revealed multiple notable findings regarding the protein Tau and its trends and relationships in pediatric patients undergoing congenital heart surgery with CPB. First, 24-hour post-CPB total Tau was a significant predictor of the postoperative incidence of neural injury. Second, median Tau levels were significantly higher in neonate/infants compared to older children and in cyanotic patients compared to acyanotic patients, at 3 time points: just prior to weaning from CPB, 1 hour post-CPB, and 24 hours post-CPB. Finally, 24-hour Tau correlated significantly with postoperative outcomes.

The most significant finding of our study is the association of total Tau with neural injury. When measured at 24 hours after the cessation of CPB, every 10-pg/mL increase in Tau corresponded to a 12% increase in the risk of neural injury. When adjusted for age, type of CHD, and perfusion modality, every 10-pg/mL increase in 24-hour post-CPB Tau increased the risk of a neural injury by 8% (aOR, 1.086; 95% CI, 1.001-1.178; P = .046). With this increased risk, we sought to determine the perioperative factors contributing to potential neuron damage. Although we expected perioperative neuromonitoring techniques to aid the detection of modifiable risk factors for the emergence of postoperative neural injury, our findings suggest that cerebral oxygen saturation did not contribute meaningfully to its development. GME delivery also was not correlated with 24-hour Tau. However, because only 36 patients experienced neural injury, it is possible that our sample size was not large enough to reveal a correlation between GME counts and Tau levels. In addition, other nonmodifiable factors, such as poor delivery of neuronal growth factors as a sequalae of CHD in the prenatal environment, could play a role.22 Our data do show that patients with neural injury had significantly longer XC times (P = .04), so it is possible that more complex and longer surgeries could predispose a patient to greater GME delivery. Larger studies with this distinction in mind may prove useful in determining the true significance of microemboli delivery in neuropsychological development.

Another important finding of this study is the difference in Tau levels between patients separated by age and type of CHD. At the end of CPB and at 1 hour and 24 hours post-CPB, median Tau levels were significantly higher in neonates/infants compared to older and in cyanotic patients compared to acyanotic patients, respectively. Among all groups, Tau levels were highest at the end of CPB; however, the persistently higher protein levels in the day after CPB, combined with the known presence of Tau following brain injury, suggest that younger patients and patients with cyanotic CHD may be at greater risk of the pathologic effects of CPB. It is important to note, however, that older and acyanotic patients were not free of risk; up to 24 hours post-CPB, Tau levels were significantly greater compared to baseline measurements in these older patients as well. These findings suggest that patients from all risk groups are susceptible to axonal injury following CPB, and that further research focusing on both high-risk and low-risk groups is critical to reduce the neurologic sequelae associated with pediatric congenital heart surgery.

A final important finding is the significant positive correlations between 24-hour Tau and postoperative outcomes. Among neural injury patients, weak (0.25 < rS < 0.5) and moderate (0.5 < rS < 0.75) correlations were seen between Tau and measurements of vital organ injury (24-hour PELOD-2 and PRISM-3 scores), cardiovascular support (VIS), and short-term outcomes (hospital LOS). Importantly, while additional moderate correlations were seen among all patient groups, the only strong correlations (rS > 0.75) were seen in neural injury patients: intubation duration and PICU LOS.

This study has several limitations. The first is that our initial outcome measure was intended to be Tau levels in patients stratified by pulsatile or nonpulsatile flow. Although the finding regarding the significant predictive value of Tau is interesting, future longitudinal studies with larger patient cohorts focusing on Tau levels and monitoring for developmental delays or neural injuries are needed to allow more definitive conclusions. A second potential limitation involves the selection process of neural injury patients. Patients with stroke, seizure, WMI, developmental delays, behavioral problems, memory loss, loss of consciousness, hearing loss, or ADD/ADHD diagnoses after the surgery were selected for the “neural injury” group. Patients with chromosomal abnormality-induced (n = 21) or teratogen-induced developmental delays (n = 3), preexisting autism spectrum disorder (n = 1), cerebral palsy (n = 1), postsurgical traumatic accident causing neural injury (n = 1), and reported developmental delays unconfirmed by clinicians (n = 3) were placed in the “no neural injury” group. Considering the role of genetics in neurodevelopmental disorders such as ADD/ADHD, and the fact that many patients underwent surgery before exhibiting typical manifestations, we cannot be entirely certain that exposure to CPB led to these diagnoses. Additionally, the inclusion of patients with chromosomal abnormalities in the no neural injury group may have artificially elevated Tau values during and after CPB in that group, as patients with such conditions as Trisomy 21 tend to be more at risk during and after surgery.23 Therefore, this selection process could be a factor in the lack of a significant difference in total Tau levels between patients with and without neural injury during and after CPB.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified the trends of total Tau in the pediatric population following CPB. At 24 hours postsurgery, an increase in Tau levels corresponded to an increased risk of injury. Median Tau levels also were increased in younger patients and patients with cyanotic CHD at the end of CPB and after CPB. Although this study did not find a difference in Tau levels between patients with and without neural injury, its use as an independent predictor of neural injury cannot be ignored, and the implication that the cerebral environment during CPB may be similar to that seen after traumatic brain injury is important for postoperative monitoring. Since traditional imaging techniques such as CT and MRI are expensive and not routinely performed following surgery, 24-hour Tau has the potential to help refine screening and risk stratification methods for developmental disorders in patients in the years following surgery. Larger studies from other centers are needed to definitively determine the value of Tau and to continue improving surveillance methods for adverse outcomes following congenital heart surgery.

Methods

Patients and Randomization Protocol

Patients with cannula sizes smaller than 8 Fr (6 Fr) and larger than 14 Fr (18 Fr, 19 Fr, 20 Fr, 22 Fr) during CPB were excluded because either 0 patients or 1 patient in either pulsatile or nonpulsatile flow were in those groups, and thus a between-group comparison would have been skewed. The enrolled patients were selected at random to undergo either pulsatile or nonpulsatile perfusion during CPB for surgical repair of congenital heart defects (Figure E1). The randomization procedure was as follows. Pulsatile or nonpulsatile designation was written on a small card, which was then placed into a small envelope. Twenty small envelopes were then placed into a large envelope. This resulted in 10 cards with pulsatile perfusion and 10 cards with nonpulsatile perfusion in the large envelope. Before the midline incision, a clinical research nurse brought the large envelope to the operating room, where one of the small envelopes was removed. The card was opened, and the assigned perfusion type was shown to the operating surgeon, the anesthesiologist, and the perfusion staff. This process was performed until all 20 cards were removed from the large envelope. After the first 20 cards were removed, another large envelope was prepared using the same methods as described above. This process was repeated 9 times to accommodate the entire cohort of patients.

All participants involved in the care of the study patients were blinded to the perfusion modality the patients received in the operating room except the operating surgeon, anesthesiologist, and perfusion staff.

Anesthesia, Perfusion, and PICU Protocols

General anesthesia was achieved with isoflurane, pancuronium, and fentanyl. All surgeries were performed primarily via median sternotomy incision. The CPB circuit comprised a Maquet-HL-20 heart-lung machine (Maquet Cardiopulmonary, Getinge Group) capable of pulsatile or nonpulsatile flow, a Capiox hollow-fiber membrane oxygenator, a Capiox pediatric 32-mm arterial filter (Terumo Cardiovascular Systems), a cardioplegia set (66,483-01; Terumo), a Minntech hemoconcentrator (HemoCor HPH400TS; Medivators), PVC tubing (LivaNova Smart Perfusion Pack), a Stockert heater-cooler system (Sorin Group USA), and arterial cannulas from the DLP series (Medtronic), Sarns Tender Flow series (Terumo Cardiovascular Systems), or Fem-Flex II series (Edwards Lifesciences). The primer for the CPB circuit was composed of 400 mL of Plasmalyte-A, 50 mL of 25% human albumin, 15 mEq NaHCO3, and 1000 U of heparin. After the primer was prepared, 120 to 180 mL was removed to provide additional volume during the surgery as needed, and 250 mL of packed red blood cells were added. The hematocrit goal was ≥26% while on CPB. After CPB initiation, a 0.5 g/kg dose of mannitol was administered. Finally, before CPB discontinuation, all patients were subjected to modified ultrafiltration. The pulsatile perfusion group had the following settings on CPB: 10% of the base flow, 20% of the pump head start point, and 80% of the pump head stop target. The pump rate was determined using the patient's weight: >15 kg, 90 beats/minute; 7 to 15 kg, 100 beats/minute; <7 kg, 120 beats/minute.E1, E2, E3

In the PICU, most of the patients were sedated with fentanyl and/or dexmedetomidine. For mechanical ventilation, tidal volume was set to 10 mL/kg. Minute ventilation was adjusted to either a normal pH or, for shunted patients, a pH necessary to balance out Qp/Qs. To maintain an appropriate PO2 for the palliated or repaired defect, the oxygen and positive end-expiratory pressure were titrated. Additionally, patients who were at risk or who had pulmonary hypertension had an alkalotic pH and received nitric oxide. The vasoactive agents consisted of milrinone, epinephrine, and nitroprusside, and when initiated in the operating room, were continued postoperatively. Nitroprusside was often titrated to maintain an age-appropriate mean arterial pressure at the 50th percentile. All patients received continuous monitoring consisting of cerebral oximetry, electrocardiography, arterial blood pressure, central venous pressure, end-tidal CO2, and oxygen saturation. Based on the defect, serial lactate levels and mixed venous oxygen saturation values were obtained.

Clinical examination, perfusion, urine output, and hemodynamic and oximetric variables were monitored continuously or intermittently to determine the titration and weaning of inotropes and/or volume. Weaning of vasoactives usually occurred on the first postoperative day. Packed red blood cells were administered as needed to maintain a hematocrit >40% in cyanotic patients and >30% in acyanotic patients who were hypotensive, hypovolemic, or persistently acidotic. If a patient was actively bleeding, platelets and fresh frozen plasma were given.E2

TCD Ultrasound

TCD ultrasound (Pioneer TC8080; Nicolet Biomedical) was used to measure intraoperative cerebral flow velocities and detect any gaseous microemboli (GME) during CPB. Two ultrasound sensors were placed, 1 above the zygoma in the right temporal window to insonate the right MCA at a depth between 25 and 50 nm, and the second on the CPB circuit tubing before the aortic cannula. A 2-MHz pulse-wave transducer was used to generate simultaneous M-mode and spectrogram readings. Perioperative data were collected at the following time points: baseline (preincision), on bypass before aortic cross-clamp, and on bypass after cross-clamp at 5 minutes, 20 minutes, 40 minutes, and 60 minutes.E1,E2

NIRS

NIRS is a noninvasive method used to evaluate real-time changes in oxygen saturation, which is the ratio of oxyhemoglobin to total hemoglobin. This is accomplished with an INVOS 5100B NIRS monitor (Somanetics). The NIRS monitor uses 2 silicon photodiodes and a light-emitting diode to transmit 2 near-IR wavelengths of 730 nm and 805 nm. Two Pediatric SomaSensors (Somanetics) were attached to the right and left sides of the forehead below the hairline, and perioperative data were collected at the following time points: baseline (preincision), on CPB before cross-clamp, and on CPB after cross-clamp at 5 minutes, 20 minutes, 40 minutes, and 60 minutes.E1,E2

PELOD-2 Score

The PELOD-2 score is a measurement of vital organ injury using multiple variables from 5 different organ systems: mean blood pressure, lactatemia, Glasgow Coma Scale, pupillary reaction, ratio of PaO2 to fraction of inspired oxygen, PCO2, presence of mechanical ventilation, white blood cell count, platelet count, and serum creatinine.E5,E6 A higher score implies a greater amount of organ injury and is correlated with a higher mortality rate. Measurements of these variables and subsequent score calculation occurred at 24, 48, and 72 hours postsurgery for all patients remaining in the PICU at the measurement times.E1,E2

PRISM 3 Score

The PRISM 3 Score was used to measure the risk of mortality of patients admitted to the PICU. A higher score is associated with greater risk of mortality. Physiologic variables that contribute to the score are measured through the first 4 hours after admission, and laboratory variables are measured between 2 hours pre-admission through the first 4 hours after admission. The variables include systolic blood pressure, temperature, mental status based on the Glasgow Coma Scale, heart rate, number of pupils >3 mm and fixed, pH, total CO2, PCO2, arterial PaO2, glucose, potassium, creatinine, urea, white blood cell count, prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time, and platelet count.E1,E2,E7
