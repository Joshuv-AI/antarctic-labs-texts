Genome-wide characterization of the LAC gene family in five fern species reveals evolutionary diversification and putative roles in lignin biosynthesis in Alsophila spinulosa
Authors: Ruixuan Chen, Yingxue Ma, Yu Sui, Jiaxi Chen, Dexuan Xiong, Jiaxing Ding, Yingzhu Zhan, Peiyun Wang, Jiangtao Fan, Xiong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaccases (LACs) are multicopper oxidases that play vital roles in lignin polymerization, cell wall formation, and various stress responses in plants. Despite their importance, genome-wide investigations of the LAC gene family in ferns remain limited. As the earliest lineage of vascular plants, ferns offer a unique perspective for understanding the evolution of lignification and the development of vascular systems. Among them, the tree fern Alsophila spinulosa is the only extant woody fern with a distinct trunk structure, providing an ideal model for exploring the molecular basis of lignin biosynthesis and secondary growth in primitive vascular plants.ResultsIn this study, we performed a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the LAC gene family in five fern species. A total of 160 LAC genes were identified and classified into five phylogenetic subfamilies. Comparative analysis revealed marked variation in family size among the five species, with A. spinulosa possessing the largest repertoire. Integrated transcriptomic analysis, WGCNA, qRT-PCR validation, and subcellular localization assays identified several vascular tissue–enriched AspiLAC genes potentially associated with lignin deposition and secondary cell wall formation. Notably, AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29, and AspiLAC42 were localized to the plasma membrane.ConclusionThis study provides the first genome-wide characterization of the LAC gene family in ferns, revealing its evolutionary diversification, duplication patterns, and expression dynamics. The results indicate that gene duplication has driven the expansion and functional specialization of LAC genes in A. spinulosa. The identification of xylem-specific AspiLAC genes and their potential regulatory roles in lignin synthesis offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of lignification, vascular tissue evolution, and secondary metabolism in early land plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08655-y.Keywords: Fern, LAC family genes, AspiLAC genes, Lignin biosynthesis, Expression pattern

Background

Laccase (EC 1.10.3.2) is a copper-dependent oxidoreductase that belongs to the multicopper oxidase (MCO) family [1]. It utilizes molecular oxygen as the final electron acceptor to catalyze the oxidation of a wide range of phenolic compounds, aromatic amines, and other reductive organic substrates, concomitantly reducing oxygen to water [2]. A typical laccase protein usually consists of three conserved copper oxidase domains, designated as Cu-oxidase_1, Cu-oxidase_2, and Cu-oxidase_3, each containing specific copper-binding sites [3]. The catalytic center of laccase contains four copper ions, which are classified into three types based on their electron absorption properties and coordination environments: type 1, type 2, and a pair of type 3 copper ions [4]. The T1 copper site is typically located on the protein surface and is coordinated by one cysteine and two histidine residues, functioning as the primary site for direct electron transfer from the substrate [5]. In contrast, the T2/T3 trinuclear copper cluster (TNC) is buried within the protein core and coordinated by several histidine residues, mainly responsible for the reduction of molecular oxygen [6]. When the substrate is oxidized at the T1 site, the electrons are transferred through internal electron-transfer pathways to the TNC, where the T2 and T3 copper ions act cooperatively to reduce molecular oxygen stepwise to water [7]. Laccases are widely distributed in nature and are found in a variety of organisms, including fungi, plants, bacteria, and insects [8–10]. It was first identified in 1883 from the sap of the Japanese lacquer tree (Rhus vernicifera), which gave the enzyme its name [11]. As research has advanced, it has become evident that laccases participate not only in fungal processes such as lignin degradation, pigment biosynthesis, and the decomposition of organic pollutants, but also play diverse and essential roles in plants [12]. These include the oxidative polymerization of lignin precursors in the cell wall, the formation of anthocyanins, and the regulation of responses to various biotic and abiotic stresses [13–15]. Due to its unique structural characteristics and broad catalytic capability, laccase has emerged as a key focus of research in plant developmental regulation and environmental biotechnology [16, 17].

In land plant evolution, ferns are the closest sister group to seed plants within euphyllophytes and represent one of the most species-rich lineages of vascular plants. [18, 19]. The development of the vascular system was a crucial step in the successful terrestrialization and advanced evolution of plants, providing mechanical support and establishing channels for the long-distance transport of water and organic substances [20]. The vascular system of plants consists of xylem and phloem, with the xylem primarily responsible for water conduction and structural reinforcement [21, 22]. The xylem of ferns is mainly composed of tracheids, whose cell walls are rich in lignin, enabling the plants to maintain upright growth and stress resistance across diverse ecological environments [23]. LAC functions as a pivotal enzyme in the terminal phase of lignin polymerization, with its expression level exerting a direct impact on lignin deposition and cell wall lignification [24]. Overexpression of LAC genes in poplar has been shown to increase lignin content and result in the thickening of vascular cells [25]. In Arabidopsis, mutations in certain LAC genes lead to abnormalities in vessel wall formation and impaired lignification [26]. Moreover, the tissue- and development-specific expression patterns of different LAC members suggest a functional differentiation in regulating lignin monomer composition and spatial deposition patterns [27]. Although the LAC gene family has been systematically characterized in various plant species such as Brachypodium distachyon [28], cotton [29], Populus [25], flax [30], rice [31], sweet sorghum [32], research on this gene family in ferns remains largely unexplored. At present, the genomes of several representative fern species have been successfully sequenced [33–36], providing a foundation for exploring the evolution and functional characteristics of LAC genes in ferns. These species encompass diverse ecological types, including aquatic, terrestrial, and arborescent ferns, reflecting the remarkable evolutionary diversity of the fern lineage and providing an ideal system for comparative genomic and functional genetic studies [33, 34]. Such broad phylogenetic representation offers valuable opportunities to investigate the origin, expansion, and functional diversification of the LAC gene family within ferns. In particular, A. spinulosa, a representative tree fern characterized by a well-developed upright rhizome and pronounced secondary lignification, serves as an excellent model for exploring the relationship between LAC genes, vascular tissue development, and lignin formation in early vascular plants [35]. Therefore, systematic identification of LAC genes across five fern species and elucidation of their roles in lignin biosynthesis in A. spinulosa are of great significance for understanding the origin and evolution of vascular systems in ferns.

In the present work, the LAC gene family was systematically investigated in five fern species, resulting in the identification of 160 distinct LAC genes. Multiple analytical approaches were applied to characterize these genes, including subgroup classification, analysis of biochemical features, phylogenetic reconstruction, chromosomal localization, conserved motif identification, and prediction of protein interaction relationships. In addition, transcriptome profiling combined with quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to evaluate tissue-dependent expression patterns of LAC genes in stem tissues (xylem, pith, phloem, and sclerenchymatic belt) and leaf, and to screen for candidate transcription factors potentially associated with LAC regulation. Taken together, these results contribute to a clearer understanding of LAC gene family evolution in ferns and provide a useful framework for further investigation of LAC-mediated regulatory processes involved in lignin formation.

Material and methods

Identification and Classification of LAC Genes in five ferns

To identify LAC gene family members in five representative fern species, genome sequences, predicted protein sets, and corresponding annotation files were collected from publicly available genome resources. The genome assembly and annotation of Alsophila spinulosa were obtained from Figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.19075346; BioProject PRJCA006485). The genome assembly and annotation of Adiantum capillus were obtained from Figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.31570321). Genomic resources for Azolla filiculoides and Salvinia cucullata were accessed through FernBase (https://www.fernbase.org; BioProject numbers PRJNA430527 and PRJNA430459), and the S. cucullata genome version used in this study was v2. The reference genome of Ceratopteris richardii was retrieved from the Phytozome portal (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Crichardii_v2_1; version 2.1). Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles corresponding to conserved laccase domains (PF00394, PF07731, and PF07732) were downloaded from the Pfam database via the InterPro resource (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) and employed as queries to screen fern protein datasets using HMMER v3.0. Protein sequences meeting the significance threshold (E-value < 1e−10) were retained for further analysis. Candidate LAC proteins were subsequently verified using domain annotation tools available in the SMART database (https://smart.embl.de) and the Conserved Domain Search service at NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) to confirm the presence of conserved multicopper oxidase features, while sequences lacking LAC-specific motifs were excluded. Physicochemical attributes of the confirmed LAC proteins, including amino acid length, molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity, were calculated using the ProtParam program implemented on the ExPASy bioinformatics platform (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). In addition, the probable subcellular localization of each LAC protein was predicted using the online PSORT suite (https://psort.hgc.jp/).

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of LAC genes

According to previously published studies, a total of 17 LAC genes have been identified in the genome of the Arabidopsis thaliana [37]. The corresponding LAC protein sequences were obtained from the TAIR11 database (https://www.arabidopsis.org). Multiple sequence alignment of LAC proteins from five fern species and A. thaliana (67 from A. spinulosa, 25 from A. capillus, 38 from C. richardii, 10 from S. cucullata, 20 from A. filiculoides and 17 from A. thaliana) was performed using ClustalW with default parameters. After alignment, 573 amino acid positions were retained for phylogenetic tree construction. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method implemented in MEGA v7.0 (Kumar et al., 2016), with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch reliability. Classification of LAC proteins was performed based on bootstrap support values (bootstrap > 75) and by referring to the established subgroup designations from Arabidopsis and rice. The resulting phylogenetic tree was further annotated and visualized using the iTOL online tool (http://itol.embl.de/).

Analysis of gene architecture and conserved motifs

Conserved motif analysis of the LAC protein sequences was performed using the MEME online tool (http://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) (Bailey et al., 2009). For the LAC gene family, the number of target motifs was set to 20, with specific parameters defined for the minimum and maximum motif widths and the number of repetitions. In addition, gene structure information—including exons and introns—was extracted from the genome GFF annotation file using TBtools, and the results were visualized accordingly (Chen et al., 2020).

Analysis of Chromosomal locations, gene duplication and collinearity

Chromosomal positions of LAC genes were determined for A. spinulosa, A. capillus, and C. richardii. Because chromosome-level genome assemblies are not yet available for A. filiculoides and S. cucullata, corresponding chromosomal localization analyses were not conducted for these two species. Putative gene duplication events were detected using the MCScanX program with default settings [38]. Tandemly duplicated LAC genes were subsequently displayed using the gene distribution visualization functions implemented in TBtools [39]. In addition, pairwise comparisons of LAC protein sequences within A. spinulosa, A. capillus, and C. richardii were carried out using BLASTP, and the rates of nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitutions were estimated with KaKs Calculator [40] to evaluate evolutionary selection acting on LAC genes. To refine the analysis, the following steps were taken: (1) BLASTP was used to screen the optimal gene pairs; (2) the corresponding CDS sequences of these gene pairs were selected and aligned using MACSE [41] for codon-based alignments; (3) KaKs_Calculator with the YN model was then used to calculate the Ka/Ks values.

Promoter cis-regulatory element analysis of LAC genes

To explore potential cis-regulatory features of LAC genes in the five fern species, genomic fragments spanning 2 kb upstream of the translational start site (ATG) for each LAC locus were retrieved. These upstream sequences were analyzed using the PlantCARE platform to identify putative promoter elements and regulatory motifs. The distribution patterns of cis-acting elements among different LAC genes were subsequently summarized and visualized with TBtools [39].

Expression pattern analysis of AspiLAC genes

To investigate the expression profiles of AspiLAC genes across tissues, transcriptome datasets from five distinct tissues, including xylem (Xy), phloem (Ph), pith (Pi), sclerenchymatic belt (Sb), and leaf (Le) were obtained from our previously published study [35] and are publicly available under BioProject accession PRJCA006485. Data analysis and visualization were conducted using TBtools [39].

qRT-PCR analysis

Plant materials of A. spinulosa were obtained from Meishan City, Sichuan Province, China (29.90°N, 103.14°E). Young stem tissues of A. spinulosa were harvested and transversely sectioned. Using a scalpel, the Xy, Ph, and Sb belt were carefully dissected from the vascular bundles, and Pi samples were collected from the center of the stem. Young leaf samples were collected concurrently from the same individuals. Tissues from three independent trees were used as biological replicates. Immediately after harvesting, all samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and maintained at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from Xy, Ph, Pi, Sb, and Le tissues using a column-based plant RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including on-column DNase I treatment to remove genomic DNA. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver.2 (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., China; Code No. AG11728) with the all-in-one protocol, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reverse transcription reaction was performed in a 20 μL system under the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s, and then held at 4 °C. Primer specificity was verified by semi-quantitative PCR followed by agarose gel electrophoresis and further confirmed by melting-curve analysis. Semi-quantitative PCR was performed in a 20 μL reaction mixture containing 10 μL 2 × Flash PCR MasterMix (Dye), 0.8 μL of forward primer (10 μM), 0.8 μL of reverse primer (10 μM), template cDNA, and ddH2O to the final volume. Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., China; Code No. AG11701) in a 20 μL reaction system on a Bio-Rad CFX real-time PCR system. Melting curves were analyzed using Bio-Rad CFX Manager 3.1. Relative expression levels were estimated using the 2^-ΔCt method, where ΔCt = Ct_target gene − Ct_AspiActin. AspiActin was used as the internal reference gene, and the primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Statistical differences in gene expression among the five tissues were assessed by one-way ANOVA based on ΔCt values, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data processing and figure preparation were performed using Excel 2019, SPSS, and GraphPad Prism 5 [42].

WGCNA analysis, and functional annotation

To assess the involvement of AspiLAC genes across different tissues of A. spinulosa, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using transcriptomic datasets derived from 10 tissue types, including leaves and stems at three developmental stages, as well as xylem, phloem, pith, and thick-walled tissues. A total of 30 RNA-seq samples generated in a previous study [35] were incorporated into the analysis. Co-expression network construction was performed using the WGCNA package in R, with all expressed genes subjected to hierarchical clustering based on a topological overlap–derived dissimilarity metric. Gene modules were defined from the resulting dendrogram, which was generated using the Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Module eigengene values were subsequently calculated and correlated with tissue types to evaluate module–trait associations. Within the resulting network, gene–gene connections were assigned weights according to their topological overlap values (ranging from 0 to 1), and network structures were visualized using Cytoscape. To infer the biological functions represented by each module, genes within individual modules were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses, following previously established procedures [35].

Subcellular location analysis of AspiLAC genes

Subcellular targeting of AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29, and AspiLAC42 was examined by cloning their stop codon–deleted coding regions into the pMOA33 expression vector, driven by the CaMV 35S promoter and fused in-frame with eGFP. Primer sets used for construct generation were designed in SnapGene (v7.1.2) and are listed in Table S1. Recombinant plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 using a freeze–thaw transformation protocol [43]. The plasma membrane marker RCI2A-mCherry and the empty pMOA33-35S-GFP vector were included as reference controls. Agrobacterial suspensions harboring either control or recombinant constructs were co-infiltrated into leaves of 4-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana with infiltration solution containing 10 mM MES (pH 5.6), 10 mM MgCl₂, and 200 μM acetosyringone. Following a 48-h dark incubation, fluorescence signals were visualized using a Nikon confocal laser scanning microscope.

Results

Identification and classification of LAC genes in five ferns

The number of protein-coding genes in A. spinulosa and C. richardii significantly higher than that of the other three ferns, which may be caused by whole-genome duplication (WGD) [35, 36], After filtering out redundant and incomplete sequences, 160 distinct LAC genes were retained across the five fern species. Among them, 67 LAC members were identified in A. spinulosa, followed by 38 in C. richardii, 25 in A. capillus, 20 in A. filiculoides, and 10 in S. cucullata (Table 1). A. spinulosa contained the largest LAC repertoire, accounting for the highest proportion of genes among the examined species, whereas S. cucullata possessed the smallest number. In contrast, the LAC gene counts in C. richardii, A. capillus, and A. filiculoides were broadly comparable. Based on their chromosomal positions, LAC genes from all five species were systematically named. The predicted LAC proteins exhibited an average molecular mass of approximately 64.6 kDa, with theoretical isoelectric points ranging from 4.83 to 9.58. Subcellular localization prediction suggested that the 160 fern LAC proteins were distributed across multiple cellular compartments. Chloroplast localization was the most frequent prediction, accounting for 66 proteins, followed by the vacuolar membrane, extracellular space, cytosol, endoplasmic reticulum, and plasma membrane, with 27, 15, 14, 11, and 10 proteins, respectively. Only two proteins were predicted to localize exclusively to the nucleus, and one additional protein was predicted to localize to both the cytosol and nucleus. Detailed physicochemical parameters of individual LAC proteins are provided in Table S2.

Phylogenetic relationships and subfamily organization of LAC proteins in ferns

To examine the phylogenetic relationships between fern and angiosperm LAC proteins, a rooted Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree was constructed using 160 LAC sequences from five fern species together with 17 A. thaliana LAC proteins, with A. thaliana serving as the outgroup (Fig. 1). Phylogenetic analysis grouped the fern LAC proteins into five well-supported subfamilies, as summarized in Table S3. The number of LAC members varied markedly among subfamilies, with subfamily III representing the largest clade (48 genes), whereas subfamily I contained the smallest number (6 genes). Within individual subfamilies, substantial interspecific variation was evident. For example, subfamily V included 13 LAC genes in A. spinulosa, compared with 6 and 5 members in C. richardii and A. capillus, respectively, while only 1 and 5 genes were detected in S. cucullata and A. filiculoides. Comparable patterns of uneven gene distribution were also observed in other subfamilies. Overall, these results indicate pronounced diversification and evolutionary divergence of the LAC gene family among fern lineages, laying the groundwork for subsequent functional investigations.

Gene structure and protein motif diversity among LAC genes in five ferns

Examination of gene architectures uncovered substantial heterogeneity among the 160 LAC loci, particularly in exon–intron organization and overall structural composition (Fig. S1). While several LAC genes possessed well-defined untranslated regions, some annotated LAC genes lacked UTRs in the current gene models, which may cause by either genuine structural variation or inaccurate gene annotation [44]. Such architectural differences likely arose from lineage-specific evolutionary processes, including genomic rearrangements and post-duplication functional diversification. Despite the overall variability, LAC genes positioned within the same phylogenetic clade generally displayed comparable exon–intron patterns, with similar exon counts and gene lengths. To assess protein-level conservation, motif composition of LAC proteins from the five fern species was analyzed using the MEME platform. Ten conserved motifs were identified and designated as Motifs 1–10 (Table S4). Motif arrangements were largely conserved across species, particularly within individual subfamilies, where LAC proteins frequently exhibited highly similar motif architectures. This conservation indicates a strong association between motif organization and functional roles of LAC proteins. Functional annotation revealed that Motif 1 mapped to a core region of the catalytic domain and contained residues critical for electron transfer, whereas Motif 2 corresponded to the second α-helical segment, which is likely involved in maintaining protein conformational stability. Notably, both motifs were consistently present in all identified LAC proteins, underscoring their fundamental importance for enzymatic activity and structural integrity.

Cis-regulatory element diversity in the promoter regions of LAC genes in five ferns

Cis-regulatory elements (CREs) serve as binding sites for transcription factors (TFs). Predicted motifs within the 2 kb upstream promoter regions identified a total of 586 potential CREs in the promoters of LAC genes (Table S5-1). Based on their biological functions and response types, these regulatory elements can be classified into three major categories: (i) Abiotic and biotic stress, (ii) Phytohormone responsive, and (iii) plant growth and development (Table S5-2). The results showed that stress-related elements accounted for the largest proportion, suggesting that the expression of LAC genes may be strongly regulated by environmental factors. Notably, in A. spinulosa, MYB- and MYC-binding sites were far more abundant than other types of regulatory elements (Fig. 2), and this enrichment pattern was also observed in the other four fern species (Fig. S2). Given the pivotal roles of MYB and MYC transcription factors in phenylpropanoid metabolism and lignin biosynthesis, these findings suggest that they may play a central role in the transcriptional regulation of LAC genes in ferns, particularly in mediating responses to stress and developmental signals.

Chromosomal distribution patterns and evolutionary divergence of LAC genes in three ferns

As chromosome-level genome assemblies are currently unavailable for A. filiculoides and S. cucullata, chromosomal mapping of LAC genes was conducted exclusively in the remaining three fern species. Clear interspecific differences were observed in the genomic distribution of LAC loci. In A. spinulosa, which contains 69 chromosomes, AspiLAC genes were located on 24 chromosomes (Fig. 3). Chromosome 25 harbored the largest number of AspiLAC genes (13 members), whereas chromosomes 2, 6, 17, 19, 32, 35, 38, 42, 43, 47, 64, and 68 each carried only a single AspiLAC gene. In A. capillus, 30 chromosomes were identified, with AdcLAC genes distributed across 15 chromosomes; chromosome 10 exhibited the highest gene density. Similarly, C. richardii possesses 39 chromosomes, and CriLAC genes were mapped to 17 of them, with chromosome 2 containing the greatest number of CriLAC members. In Table S6, we calculated the Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks ratios for duplicated gene pairs in the LAC gene family of three fern species. All gene pairs exhibited Ka/Ks ratios below 1, indicating that these gene pairs are influenced by purifying selection, such as AdcLAC8&AdcLAC9 (Ka/Ks = 0.146868) and AspiLAC17&AspiLAC51 (Ka/Ks = 0.182497). This suggests that, during evolution, non-synonymous mutations have been strongly constrained by selection, preserving the functional integrity of the genes. The Ka/Ks value for AspiLAC15&AspiLAC16 is 0.0704334, further reflecting the influence of purifying selection and indicating that these gene pairs have also been maintained under strong selective pressure, ensuring functional stability. The P-values for the gene pairs are much smaller than 0.05, demonstrating statistical significance and supporting the role of purifying selection in shaping these genes.

Gene duplication and synteny analysis of AspiLACs

To clarify the expansion pattern of the AspiLAC gene family in A. spinulosa, duplication analysis identified 14 duplication events. These duplications generated multiple AspiLAC gene pairs that were unevenly distributed across chromosomes 4, 6, 9, 12, 14, 38, 42, 44, 63, and 68 (Fig. 4A). In contrast, no duplication signals were detected for LAC genes in C. richardii or A. capillus (Fig. 4B–C). Comparative synteny analyses were subsequently conducted to explore the evolutionary relationships of LAC genes among fern species. Collinearity was examined between A. spinulosa and four representative ferns, including two terrestrial species (C. richardii and A. capillus) and two aquatic species (A. filiculoides and S. cucullata). Eight collinear LAC gene pairs were identified between A. spinulosa and A. capillus, whereas only two collinear pairs were detected in C. richardii (Fig. 4D, Table S7), These genes were also classified into the same phylogenetic groups in the phylogenetic tree. Overall, species occupying similar habitats exhibited more syntenic conservation within the LAC gene family. Consistently, terrestrial ferns showed substantially higher numbers of collinear LAC gene pairs compared with aquatic ferns.

Expression patterns of AspiLAC genes across five tissues in A. spinulosa

To comprehensively investigate the biological functions and tissue-specific expression characteristics of the LAC gene family during development, this study analyzed the expression profiles of AspiLAC in A. spinulosa based on transcriptomic data from five distinct tissues: Xy, Ph, Sb, Pi, and Le (Table S8). A circular heatmap was generated from the expression data (Fig. 5A), revealing differential expression patterns of AspiLAC across tissues. Overall, the genes could be clustered into five expression groups, with several members exhibiting strong tissue-specific expression. For instance, AspiLAC1, AspiLAC9, AspiLAC12, AspiLAC33, AspiLAC50, and AspiLAC58 showed markedly high expression in the Sb (Fig. 5A), whereas AspiLAC3 and AspiLAC53 were highly expressed only in the Pi. Additionally, 21 genes, including AspiLAC2, AspiLAC5, and AspiLAC6, exhibited strong expression in Le. Notably, more than one-third of the AspiLAC displayed elevated expression levels in xylem, particularly AspiLAC7, AspiLAC25, AspiLAC28, AspiLAC29, AspiLAC42, and AspiLAC54, which were expressed significantly higher than in the other four tissues (Table S9). This suggests that these genes may play essential roles in xylem differentiation and function. Although most AspiLAC genes exhibited differential expression across tissues, indicating potential functional diversification, further experimental validation is required to substantiate these findings. To confirm the reliability of the transcriptomic data, 12 AspiLAC genes were randomly selected for qRT-PCR analysis. The expression patterns of these 12 genes across the five tissues were consistent with the RNA-Seq data (Fig. 5B–M), thereby confirming the accuracy and reliability of the transcriptomic results.

Identification of key AspiLACs and regulators in xylem development and lignin biosynthesis

Earlier analyses showed that a substantial number of AspiLAC genes exhibit elevated expression in xylem tissue. To explore their possible involvement in xylem formation and lignin biosynthesis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted based on RNA-seq data from six A. spinulosa tissues, leading to the identification of 12 distinct co-expression modules (Fig. 6A). Among them, the lightyellow module showed the strongest correlation with xylem tissues (Fig. 6B). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the genes within the lightyellow module indicated significant enrichment in pathways related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 6C–D, Tables S10–S11). Further examination revealed that AspiLAC7, AspiLAC25, AspiLAC28, AspiLAC29, AspiLAC42, AspiLAC54, and AspiLAC56 were all assigned to this module. In addition, these AspiLAC genes were co-expressed with several transcription factors, including AspiWRKY39, AspibHLH68, and AspiMYB20 (Fig. 6E), all of which also exhibited high expression levels in the xylem (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these results suggest that transcription factors such as AspiWRKY39, AspibHLH68, and AspiMYB20 may play potential regulatory roles in controlling AspiLAC gene expression during xylem development and lignin biosynthesis. Additional analysis of the promoter regions of these AspiLAC genes (Fig. S3) revealed the presence of potential cis-regulatory elements that are likely to be recognized by AspiWRKY39, AspibHLH68, and AspiMYB20. These findings suggest that these transcription factors may directly regulate the expression of AspiLAC genes during the process of xylem development and lignin biosynthesis. The identification of these cis-regulatory elements provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms controlling the regulation of AspiLAC genes and their potential role in lignin biosynthesis.

Subcellular localization of AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29 and AspiLAC42

Based on the expression analysis, AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29 and AspiLAC42 were selected for subcellular localization analysis. To determine their subcellular distribution, the coding sequences of AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29 and AspiLAC42 were fused to eGFP under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells together with the plasma membrane marker RCI2A–mCherry. Confocal laser scanning microscopy showed that free eGFP was distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus, whereas the fluorescence signals of AspiLAC7–eGFP, AspiLAC29–eGFP and AspiLAC42–eGFP were predominantly detected at the cell periphery. Notably, the green fluorescence of AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29 and AspiLAC42 largely overlapped with the red fluorescence of RCI2A–mCherry in the merged images, indicating strong co-localization with the plasma membrane marker (Fig. 7). These results suggest that AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29 and AspiLAC42 are mainly localized to the plasma membrane. Given that laccases play critical roles in monolignol oxidation and lignin polymerization during secondary cell wall formation, the plasma membrane localization of these AspiLAC proteins supports their potential involvement in lignin deposition and secondary cell wall formation.

Discussion

Ferns occupy a key phylogenetic position as some of the earliest diverging vascular plants, with origins dating back to approximately 360 million years ago [45, 46]. The emergence of specialized vascular tissues and the development of secondary cell walls, particularly through lignin deposition, represented major evolutionary innovations that enhanced mechanical stability and enabled efficient long-distance water transport in early land plants [47]. These evolutionary developments laid the structural foundation for terrestrial colonization and the emergence of arborescent growth forms, thereby promoting the diversification of subsequent plant lineages [48]. Among living ferns, A. spinulosa is unique in exhibiting a woody growth habit, supported by a well-developed and highly lignified vascular system within the main stem [35]. The appearance of such reinforced vascular tissues is widely regarded as a critical step in the evolutionary shift from aquatic to terrestrial environments [49]. As a dominant constituent of the secondary cell wall in xylem, lignin plays a central role in conferring mechanical strength and maintaining hydraulic efficiency in vascular plants [50]. Consequently, investigating lignin biosynthesis and its regulatory networks in A. spinulosa provides valuable insight into how early vascular plants adapted structurally to life on land. Laccases (LACs) constitute a key enzyme family involved in lignin formation by catalyzing the oxidative polymerization of monolignol precursors [51]. In addition to their roles in secondary cell wall construction, accumulating evidence indicates that LAC genes participate in diverse biological processes, including plant growth regulation, stress adaptation, and secondary metabolic pathways [52]. Studies in angiosperms have further linked LAC activity to secondary wall thickening, defense-related responses, and tolerance to abiotic stresses [8]. Despite these advances, comprehensive analyses of the LAC gene family in ferns have remained limited. The recent release of high-quality fern genome assemblies now enables systematic genome-wide characterization and evolutionary investigation of LAC genes in A. spinulosa and related species. Such analyses provide a valuable framework for elucidating the molecular basis of lignin biosynthesis and for understanding the evolutionary trajectory of vascular tissue development during the early diversification of land plants.

Our analysis (Table 1) revealed a clear association between LAC gene family size and the ecological lifestyles of the five examined fern species. Terrestrial ferns tended to harbor a larger complement of LAC genes than aquatic species, with A. spinulosa exhibiting the greatest expansion. These observations suggest that terrestrial ferns, especially the woody species A. spinulosa, tend to maintain larger LAC gene complements, which may be related to greater lignification demands and increased vascular complexity [35]. Compared with the relatively stable aquatic habitats, terrestrial ferns are exposed to greater climatic fluctuations, diverse soil conditions, and stronger mechanical stresses, while possessing more developed root systems and aerial structures [53]. These ecological and structural challenges may have driven the functional diversification and gene duplication of the LAC gene family to enhance adaptability and structural stability [54]. Similar evolutionary patterns have been observed in other plant lineages. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, LAC4, LAC11, and LAC17 have been shown to play essential roles in lignin polymerization and secondary cell wall thickening, and their disruption results in reduced stem strength and impaired vessel development [26]. In P. trichocarpa, expansion of the LAC gene family has been linked to lignin biosynthesis, salt stress responses, and pathogen resistance [55]. Moreover, in Oryza sativa, OsLAC10 was found to be involved in drought and oxidative stress tolerance by modulating ferroxidase activity [56]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the expansion and functional diversification of the LAC gene family represent a fundamental evolutionary strategy enabling terrestrial plants to cope with environmental complexity, reinforce structural integrity, and achieve ecological adaptation. The number and composition of cis-acting elements within promoter regions generally determine the expression level and tissue specificity of genes. In most cases, a higher abundance of cis-elements corresponds to stronger transcriptional activity [57]. Through systematic analyses of promoter architecture, researchers can infer the potential biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of genes [58]. In the present study, we found that MYB and MYC elements were the most abundant motifs within the promoter regions of LAC genes (Fig. S2), suggesting that these genes are likely co-regulated by the MYB–bHLH (MYC) transcriptional regulatory complex [59, 60]. Furthermore, hormone-responsive elements were also prevalent in the LAC promoters, with ABA-responsive elements (ABREs) being particularly enriched. Given that ABA plays a crucial role in mediating plant responses to drought, salinity, and other abiotic stresses [61], this finding implies that certain LAC genes may be induced by ABA signaling and participate in developmental regulation and stress adaptation in ferns. Similar transcriptional regulation mechanisms have been observed in other plant species; for instance, in Prunus sibirica, ABA-mediated transcriptional control has also been shown to be closely associated with lignin metabolism and stress responses [62].

The evolution of the LAC gene family in A. spinulosa appears to be primarily driven by gene duplication events, including both tandem and segmental duplications [63, 64]. Gene duplication provides essential genetic material for the emergence of novel functions, thereby enhancing the adaptive capacity of plants in changing environments [65]. Through collinearity analysis using MCScanX, multiple tandemly duplicated LAC genes were identified across fern genomes (Fig. 4). Most of these duplicated gene pairs were found to have undergone purifying selection, while a few exhibited signatures of positive selection. The results of this study indicate that the patterns observed in the LAC gene family are consistent with those seen in other gene families, such as bHLH and CSE, where tandem duplication predominates in driving gene family expansion [54]. However, given the differing whole-genome duplication histories among the five fern species analyzed, it remains unclear whether tandem duplication is a universal mechanism shaping all gene families in these genomes. Collectively, these findings suggest that gene duplication has played a pivotal role in the expansion and functional diversification of the LAC gene family and represents a major driving force in the evolutionary history of ferns [66].

Gene expression profiles often provide valuable insight into gene function [54]. In A. spinulosa, LAC family members displayed pronounced tissue-dependent expression patterns across different organs (Fig. 5). Notably, over one-third of the identified AspiLAC genes were predominantly expressed in leaves, implying possible roles in leaf morphogenesis and in mediating responses to environmental stimuli [67]. By contrast, AspiLAC44 and AspiLAC54 showed strong transcript accumulation in xylem tissue, consistent with potential involvement in lignin deposition and secondary cell wall assembly. Taken together, the observed expression divergence supports the notion that AspiLAC genes have experienced functional specialization during evolution, with distinct members contributing to diverse aspects of plant growth, development, and environmental adaptation [16].

WGCNA can cluster genes with similar expression patterns and is widely used to infer potential regulatory relationships among genes, as genes within the same module often participate in related biological processes or signaling pathways [68, 69]. In this study (Fig. 6), the lightyellow module showed a strong association with xylem tissues and was significantly enriched in pathways related to lignin biosynthesis. Several xylem-enriched AspiLAC genes within this module exhibited stable co-expression with transcription factors such as AspiWRKY39, AspibHLH68, and AspiMYB20, suggesting that these TFs may participate in regulating AspiLAC expression. Extensive studies have demonstrated that MYB, bHLH, and WRKY transcription factors play central roles in regulating lignin biosynthesis. For instance, in A. thaliana, MYB46 and MYB83 function as “second-layer master regulators” that directly activate secondary cell wall biosynthetic genes, including LAC4 and LAC17, thereby promoting lignin polymerization and secondary wall thickening [70]. However, MYB transcription factors have previously been reported to regulate LAC genes [71, 72]. For example, in Eucalyptus urophylla, EuMYB308 regulates lignin accumulation by directly targeting EuLAC17 [73]. Notably, the promoters of the AspiLAC genes identified in this study contain numerous putative MYB-binding sites, further suggesting that MYB proteins may function as important transcriptional regulators of AspiLAC expression. Together with these reports, the strong co-expression observed between AspiWRKY39, AspibHLH68, AspiMYB20 and multiple xylem-specific AspiLAC genes suggests that similar NAC–MYB or MYB–bHLH regulatory modules known from angiosperms may also exist in ferns. These transcription factors may cooperate to regulate AspiLAC gene expression, thereby contributing to xylem development and lignin deposition in A. spinulosa. Although the current evidence is primarily correlative, our findings provide important clues for deciphering the lignin regulatory network in ferns. Future studies should focus on validating the direct interaction between these transcription factors and AspiLAC promoters using approaches such as ChIP-qPCR, EMSA, or dual-luciferase assays. Additionally, functional characterization via gene overexpression, knockout, or CRISPR-based manipulation will further clarify their roles in lignin biosynthesis and help elucidate the evolutionary conservation and divergence of xylem regulatory mechanisms in ferns.

In addition to transcriptional regulation, the subcellular localization of AspiLAC proteins provides further evidence supporting their functional involvement in lignin biosynthesis. Confocal microscopy analysis revealed that AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29 and AspiLAC42 predominantly localized to the plasma membrane and showed strong co-localization with the plasma membrane marker RCI2A. This localization pattern is consistent with previous studies in angiosperms, in which lignin-related laccases are often associated with the plasma membrane, apoplast, or secondary cell wall, facilitating the oxidation of monolignols at the site of lignin polymerization. In Arabidopsis, laccases such as LAC4 and LAC17 have been shown to localize to lignified secondary cell wall domains and play essential roles in vascular lignification [26]. Similarly, in Populus trichocarpa, PtrLAC17 has been reported to function as a key laccase in lignin polymerization in stem xylem cell walls, thereby affecting lignin deposition [74]. The plasma membrane localization of these xylem-enriched AspiLAC proteins suggests that they may participate directly in monolignol oxidation and lignin polymerization at the cell wall interface. Notably, Table S2 predicted AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29, and AspiLAC42 to localize to the vacuolar membrane, chloroplast, and extracellular space, respectively, whereas transient expression in N. benthamiana showed that all three proteins accumulated at the cell periphery and co-localized with the plasma membrane marker. This discrepancy may reflect the reference nature of bioinformatic prediction, which depends on the algorithm used by the online PSORT suite as well as on the completeness and accuracy of the input sequence annotation. Accordingly, PSORT predictions serve only as preliminary indications of subcellular localization, whereas transient expression assays yield more definitive and experimentally substantiated results. In the functional interpretation, we therefore prioritized the experimentally supported plasma membrane localization of AspiLAC7, AspiLAC29, and AspiLAC42. Taken together with their xylem-specific expression patterns and co-expression with lignin-related transcription factors, our results support potential roles of these genes in secondary cell wall formation and lignin deposition, highlighting both functional conservation and evolutionary continuity of lignin biosynthetic mechanisms between ferns and angiosperms.

The findings of this work deepen our understanding of fern developmental regulation and evolutionary adaptation. Laccases are key oxidative enzymes that catalyze monolignol oxidation and thereby contribute to lignin polymerization and deposition in lignified cell wall domains. In A. thaliana, LAC4 and LAC17 participate in stem lignification, and LAC17 is involved in the deposition of G lignin units in fibers [26]. In addition, laccases have been shown to direct lignification to discrete secondary cell wall domains during protoxylem development and to localize precisely in lignified secondary cell walls during stem development [75, 76]. Therefore, the A. spinulosa LAC genes identified here are more likely to participate in lignin deposition and secondary cell wall formation, rather than in broad “cell wall modification”. Functional diversification of these genes may have been instrumental during the evolutionary shift of ferns from aquatic to terrestrial habitats, supporting increased vascular complexity and mechanical reinforcement [77]. Targeted regulation of individual LAC genes offers a potential strategy for fine-tuning lignin quantity and composition, either to enhance cell wall digestibility or to promote structural strength, thereby improving biomass conversion efficiency [78]. Consequently, LAC genes represent promising candidates for biotechnological applications in bioenergy, papermaking, and fiber-related industries [29]. In addition, dissecting the evolutionary and functional divergence of the LAC gene family provides a conceptual basis for the rational design of plants with optimized lignin architectures and improved adaptive capacity, supporting advances in sustainable agriculture, forestry, and renewable energy.

Conclusion

In this work, we systematically characterized the LAC gene family across five representative fern species, identifying 160 genes that were grouped into five distinct subfamilies. Comparative analyses revealed substantial variation in family size among fern species, with the woody species A. spinulosa exhibiting the largest repertoire. Transcriptome-based analyses further uncovered marked tissue-dependent expression divergence among AspiLAC genes, including preferential accumulation of several members in xylem tissues, consistent with roles in lignin deposition and secondary cell wall assembly. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into lignification-related gene family evolution and vascular development in ferns.
