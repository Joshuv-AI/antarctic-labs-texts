Trends in informal and formal care utilisation and unmet care needs among older adults in Korea: evidence from repeated national surveys
Authors: Eun Ha Namkung, Hyewon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing number of older adults with functional limitations has significant implications for care policy in rapidly ageing societies. This study examined trends in informal care, formal care, and unmet care needs among older Koreans, considering both individual characteristics and broader societal change. Using four waves of the National Survey of Older Koreans from 2014, 2017, 2020, and 2023, we analysed community-residing adults aged 65 and older with functional limitations and estimated survey-weighted multinomial regression models. Adjusted results showed a substantial rise in unmet care needs, from 20.4% in 2014 to 52.4% in 2023. Informal care from family and friends declined sharply, while the increase in formal care use was insufficient to compensate for this loss. Sensitivity analyses restricted to those with severe impairment also showed that unmet care needs still increased over time, but the magnitude of the increase was attenuated. However, among those with severe impairment, predicted probabilities of receiving formal care were relatively stable and even showed a decreasing trend. Older age, cognitive impairment, and a greater number of limitations were consistently associated with formal care utilisation relative to no care. These factors also predicted receipt of informal care relative to no care, although their statistical significance was less evident in recent years. These findings suggest that Korea’s care system has not fully adapted to declining family-based care, accompanied by relatively unchanged formal care receipt rates and rising unmet care needs. Introduction

Population ageing is reshaping social, economic, and health systems worldwide (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs 2024). Although longevity has increased, longer life does not necessarily mean longer healthy life. Many older adults experience limitations in activities of daily living (ADL) or instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), which often require assistance and intensify the demand for care (Amlak et al. 2025; Moon et al. 2024).

These growing care needs highlight the importance of social and long-term care. Social care refers to assistance that enables individuals to live fulfilling lives when they need support with daily activities or social participation, beyond medical services (World Health Organization [WHO] 2024). Care may be provided formally through welfare and healthcare institution or informally through family, friends, and community networks. While formal services provide structured support, informal care remains vital, particularly in societies where family responsibility is culturally emphasised. Yet both systems face constraints, limited accessibility, insufficient resources, and caregiver burden. When neither formal nor informal care is adequate, unmet care needs arise. Such unmet needs are associated with poorer quality of life, deteriorating health, and social isolation among older adults (Kröger 2022), making them a critical social and public health concern.

Although definitions vary, unmet care needs generally refer to situations in which individuals with functional limitations do not receive adequate assistance (Dunatchik et al. 2019; Iliffe et al. 2004; Kröger 2022). From an absolute approach, unmet needs are identified when individuals report functional limitations but do not receive help, regardless of the amount or type of help received (Calderón-Jaramillo and Zueras 2023). Assessing unmet care needs therefore requires considering both formal and informal care simultaneously, because one source of support does not necessarily compensate for the absence or decline of the other (Hahm and Hong 2017; Kröger 2022).

Several theoretical perspectives help explain patterns of care use and unmet needs. Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use (Andersen 1995) emphasises individual characteristics, distinguishing predisposing, enabling, and need factors. Predisposing factors include demographic and social structural characteristics, such as age, gender, and education (Kpolar et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024). Enabling factors refer to resources and conditions that facilitate or constrain access to care, including income, residential area, family resources, and social relationships (Ma et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024; Wei and Zhang 2020). Need factors reflect the extent to which older adults require care, including functional impairment, chronic conditions, and cognitive impairment (Jang and Suh 2023; Kim et al. 2025; Kpolar et al. 2025).

Beyond individual-level determinants, care use is also shaped by broader social and policy contexts. The care regime framework highlights the interplay between the state and family in elder care, including the extent to which countries (1) defamiliarise care through paid services or (2) reinforce family caregiving through cultural expectations, financial incentives, or in-kind support (Leitner 2003; van Damme et al. 2025). Since these broader contexts vary across countries and change over time, this framework helps explain why patterns of formal and informal care utilisation vary by settings and historical periods.

Most existing evidence on care (non-)use comes from Europe and North America. Using the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), the OECD (2023) reported that nearly half of adults aged 65 and older with limitations received neither formal home care nor sufficient informal care. Calderón-Jaramillo and Zueras (2023) similarly found an unmet care prevalence of 53% across 12 European countries, with higher rates in Mediterranean countries where family-centred caregiving remains dominant. In the USA, 42% of adults aged 55 and older needing help received none (Forden and Ghilarducci 2023), while in England the rate reached 58% among adults over 60 with newly acquired limitations (Dunatchik et al. 2019).

Another strand of research explores the partially substitutive relationships between formal and informal care, where increases in one may not fully reduce the use of the other. For example, increased informal care has been associated with modest reductions in formal service use in the USA and Europe (Bolin et al. 2008; van Houtven and Norton 2004), implying that one care system cannot fully replace the other, leaving risks of unmet care.

Despite this literature, evidence from non-Western contexts remains limited. Korea is a particularly important case, suggesting rapid transformations to defamilisation of elder care in both policy approaches and cultural norms (van Damme et al. 2025). Korea has experienced one of the fastest population ageing processes in the world, alongside major changes in family structure, women’s labour force participation, and caregiving norms (Kim et al. 2021; OECD 2018). Policy reforms have also substantially reshaped elder care. The introduction of a National Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) in 2008 replaced local, means‑tested programs with a universal public insurance system for older adults who need assistance with daily living due to physical or cognitive impairment (Kim and Kwon 2021). Although LTCI coverage and service scope were initially limited, the program has expanded steadily. The proportion of adults aged 65 and older using LTCI increased from 7.9% in 2017 to 9.5% in 2020 and 10.9% in 2023 (National Health Insurance Service [NHIS] 2018, 2021, 2024).

In addition, Korea reorganised community-based elder care in 2020 through the Customised Care Service for the Elderly. Before 2020, community-based elderly support was provided through several separate programs, which limited flexibility in responding to diverse needs. The reform aimed to provide more individualised, needs-based support to vulnerable older adults who had difficulty maintaining daily life but were not necessarily eligible for LTCI. Eligibility considers factors such as living alone, income status, functional decline, cognitive impairment, and emotional difficulties (Ministry of Health and Welfare [MOHW] 2023). The target population expanded from approximately 350,000 older adults in 2019 to 450,000 in 2020 (MOHW 2019).

These policy changes occurred alongside rapid shifts in caregiving norms. In 2006, about 63% of adult Koreans reported that caregiving was solely the family’s responsibility; by 2024, this proportion had dropped to about 18% (Ministry of Data and Statistics 2025). Together, policy expansion and declining familialism may have substantially altered care dynamics and unmet needs among older Koreans.

However, empirical evidence on unmet care needs in Korea remains limited. To our knowledge, only two studies have directly examined unmet care needs in the Korean context. Kim et al. (2011) reported unmet need rates of one-third among adults aged 45–74 and 24% among those aged 75 and older, with higher risks among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Cha et al. (2025) examined informal-only care, mixed formal–informal care, and no care in 2010 and 2018, finding that the proportion receiving no help changed from 28 to 25% among men and from 25 to 32% among women. These studies provide valuable evidence, but their estimates were largely descriptive, did not fully adjust for individual characteristics, and predated major policy changes after 2018. Other Korean studies have examined determinants of informal and formal care separately, showing, for example, that women and those living with family were more likely to receive informal care than formal care, while older adults living alone were more likely to use formal services than informal care (Hahm and Hong 2017; Lee 2022). Yet these studies do not fully address unmet needs or link them to recent macro-level changes.

This study addresses these gaps by analysing trends in formal care, informal care, and unmet care needs among older Koreans over the past decades, while adjusting for individual factors associated with care use. Because eligibility criteria and service provision guidelines for formal care services have changed over time, it is difficult to determine whether changes in care receipt reflect program expansion, shifts in individual characteristics related to eligibility, such as impairment severity and living arrangement, or both. Using four waves of the National Survey of Older Koreans (NSOK) from 2014 to 2023, this study examines changes in care patterns within evolving social and policy contexts. In the absence of direct measures of cultural or social–environmental influences, this temporal approach serves as a proxy for broader contextual shifts.

Guided by Andersen’s model, we hypothesised that predisposing, enabling, and need factors would predict care use patterns in each survey year. Given rapid cultural and policy-related changes, we further hypothesised that formal care receipt would increase, while family care only would decrease from 2014 to 2023, even after accounting for individual determinants. By integrating individual-level determinants with macro-level changes in Korea, this study extends the understanding of unmet care needs beyond Western contexts and offers implications for Korea and other rapidly ageing societies.

Methods

Data and sample

Data were drawn from the four most recent waves (2014, 2017, 2020, and 2023) of the National Survey of Older Koreans (NSOK). The NSOK is a nationally representative, repeated cross-sectional household survey conducted every three years under the Welfare of Older Persons Act to monitor the health, socioeconomic conditions, family relationships, care needs, social participation, living environments, and quality of life of community-residing Koreans aged 65 and older (Kang et al. 2023). The survey is commissioned by the Ministry of Health and Welfare and conducted by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs. The NSOK uses a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design. First, census-based statistical districts are selected, and second, households with at least one individual aged 65 or older are sampled. All eligible household members are invited to participate, and proxy respondents, such as spouses or other household member, are permitted when older adults are unable to respond directly. Each wave included over 10,000 community-dwelling older adults. In the 2023 survey, for example, interviewers visited 11,633 households, of which 7,556 households were included in the final valid sample, yielding a household-level response rate of approximately 65.0%. Within these households, interviews were attempted with 10,178 eligible older adults, and 10,078 were included in the final analytic sample, corresponding to an individual-level completion rate of approximately 99.0%.

For this study, we restricted the sample to respondents reporting at least one limitation requiring help from others in Activities of Daily Living (ADL) or Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL). ADL limitations were assessed using the Korean Activity of Daily Living (K-ADL) scale (Won et al. 2002), which includes seven items: dressing, washing, bathing, eating, moving around at home, toileting, and continence. IADL limitations were assessed with the Korean Instrumental Activity of Daily Living (K-IADL) scale (Won et al. 2002), which includes ten items: grooming, housekeeping, preparing meals, doing laundry, managing medications, managing finance, going out for a short walk, shopping, using the telephone, and using transportation. Across waves, the proportion of respondents with at least one ADL or IADL limitation ranged from 12.6% in 2020 to 25.3% in 2017.

All variables had complete data except for two missing cases on household income in 2014, which were excluded. The final analytic sample ranged from 1,153 to 2,902 respondents per wave. Proxy responses were included in the analyses, which accounted for 6.1–10.9% across waves. Because the NSOK dataset is publicly available, this study was deemed exempt from human subjects review.

Measures

Dependent variable Care utilisation was measured using questions identifying whether respondents received help from (1) family caregivers; (2) relatives, friends, or other unpaid caregivers; (3) private paid caregivers; and/or (4) formal services, including LTCI home care, community-based care services for older adults, and other national or local programs. The care utilisation variable was categorized into three mutually exclusive groups indicating (a) receipt of informal care only (i.e. the presence of (1) or (2) only), (b) receipt of formal, paid care services (i.e. receipt of (3) or (4), regardless of whether one also received informal care), and (c) unmet care when respondents reported at least one ADL or IADL limitation requiring help but received neither formal nor informal care. This operationalisation of unmet care follows an absolute approach used in previous European (Calderón-Jaramillo and Zueras 2023) and Korean (Kim et al. 2011; Cha et al. 2025) studies.

Independent variables We selected individual characteristics based on Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use (Andersen 1995). Predisposing factors included age (in years), gender (female, male), and educational attainment (no formal education, elementary school graduate, middle school graduate, high school graduate, college or higher). Enabling factors included area of residence (rural, urban), living arrangement (living alone, living with spouse only, living with children, others), and five household income percentiles. Need factors included the number of chronic diseases (top-coded at 6), cognitive impairment (yes, no, not applicable), and the number of ADL limitations. Cognitive function was assessed using standardised Korean versions of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE). Although some items changed across waves, particularly between 2020 and 2023, the total score remained 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. Cognitive impairment was operationalised using the classification provided in each survey wave (Chung et al. 2014, 2017; Lee et al. 2020; Kang et al. 2023). Respondents who were proxy interviewed and therefore did not complete the cognitive assessment were categorised as not applicable.

Analyses

We first described survey-weighted sample characteristics and examined the prevalence of formal care, informal care only, and unmet care across survey waves. We then estimated survey-weighted multinomial regression models for each wave to identify individual predictors of care receipt. Postestimation predicted probabilities were calculated for receiving formal care, receiving informal care only, and receiving no care.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, because respondents who reported no care were asked why they did not receive help, we redefined the unmet care group to only include respondents who explicitly reported needing help but not receiving it because they did not want to burden others, had no one available, did not know how to access care, or reported other barriers (Park and Tchoe 2018). Because response options changed after 2014, this analysis used the 2017, 2020, and 2023 waves only. However, the main analysis retained all respondents with ADL or IADL limitations requiring help, because no detailed information was available on why some respondents perceived no need for help, and a notable share of them still had limitations in basic functions such as eating or moving around at home (up to 27.4% in 2017).

Second, we restricted the sample to respondents with at least one ADL limitation, representing a more severely impaired group. This criterion is more closely aligned with LTCI eligibility (NHIS 2024) and reflects greater care need. The main analysis nevertheless used the broader ADL/IADL limitation sample to remain consistent with prior studies on unmet care needs (e.g. OECD 2023; Calderón-Jaramillo and Zueras 2023; Cha et al. 2025). All analyses were performed using STATA/MP 18.5.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the study sample by survey year. Across waves, the average age of older adults with ADL/IADL limitations was approximately 79 years. Women accounted for about three-fourths of the sample in 2014 and 2017, but this proportion declined to about 60% in 2020 and 2023. The proportion of rural residents was similar in 2014, 2020, and 2023, but relatively higher in 2017. Living arrangements changed over time: the proportions living alone or with a spouse only increased, while the proportion living with children declined. Educational attainment improved substantially, with the proportion of high school graduates increasing from 6.0% in 2014 to 17.9% in 2023. Household income distribution remained relatively stable.

The crude proportions receiving neither formal nor informal care increased steadily, from 18.3% in 2014 to 52.8% in 2023, with statistically significant increases between survey years (p < 0.05). In contrast, the proportion receiving informal care only declined from 63.6% in 2014 to 27.1% in 2023. Formal care receipt changed less markedly, increasing modestly from 18.1% (95% Confidence Intervals [CI] = 16.1, 20.4) in 2014 to 20.2% (95% CI = 18.0, 22.5) in 2023.

Living arrangement patterns also shifted over time. In 2014 and 2017, older adults living alone were more likely to receive no care, while those living with children were more likely to receive informal care only. In 2020 and 2023, however, those living with a spouse only were most likely to receive no care, and those living alone were more likely to receive formal care in 2023. Socioeconomic patterns also changed. Lower education and income were associated with no care in 2014 and 2017, but, in 2020 and 2023, older adults with lower socioeconomic resources were more likely to receive formal care. Need-related characteristics were consistently associated with care receipt: those receiving formal care were more likely to have cognitive impairment and reported more ADL limitations. The number of chronic conditions also differed across years; the number was highest in formal care groups, although no difference was found between informal care and unmet care groups in 2014 and 2017.

Across survey years, older age consistently predicted higher likelihoods of receiving both formal care and informal care only, relative to no care. Gender differences were limited: in 2014, women were less likely than men to receive informal care only (coefficient = − 0.99), but no significant gender differences were observed in the three later waves. Living arrangement was a significant predictor of care type. Compared with those living with a spouse only, older adults living alone were less likely to receive informal care only in 2014, 2017, and 2020. However, they were more likely to receive formal care in 2014, 2020, and 2023. Compared to those living with a spouse only, older adults living with children were more likely to receive informal care and/or formal care in 2014, 2017, and 2020, but this difference was no longer significant in 2023.

Educational attainment was also associated with care receipt, although its effect weakened over time. In 2014, older adults with lower educational attainments were less likely than high school graduates to receive either informal or formal care versus no care. In 2017 and 2020, significant differences remained only for formal care use among selected lower-education groups. No significant educational differences were found in 2023. Household income was not significantly associated with care type in any way.

Need factors were the most consistent predictors of care receipt. A greater number of chronic conditions predicted formal care use in 2017, 2020, and 2023, and informal care only in 2020. Cognitive impairment, or being unable to complete the cognitive assessment due to communication or functional difficulties, associated with a higher likelihood of receiving both informal and formal care relative to no care. A greater number of ADL limitations also consistently predicted higher likelihoods of receiving formal or/and informal care than none.

However, the increase in no care was attenuated, especially between 2020 and 2023, when the predicted probability remained nearly stable, from 34.7% to 33.4%. Predictors in this more severely impaired sample were generally consistent with the main findings. Older age and living alone predicted formal care use in 2020 and 2023. Discussion

This study examined trends in formal and informal care utilisation and unmet care among older Koreans over the past decade, as well as predisposing, enabling, and need factors associated with care use. It provides new evidence on how the balance between formal and informal care has shifted in Korea and how these changes related to rising unmet care needs. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.

The results can be summarised in two main points. First, consistent with our hypotheses, informal care declined sharply, while unmet care increased substantially. In 2014, nearly two-thirds of older adults with functional limitations relied exclusively on informal care from family and friends, while about one in five reported unmet needs. By 2023, this pattern had reversed: after adjusting for individual characteristics, more than half (52.4%) received no care, and fewer than one-third (27.5%) relied on informal care. The two sensitivity analyses also showed a continuous decline in informal care only, suggesting a robust trend towards defamilisation of care in Korea, regardless of perceived need and impairment severity (van Damme et al. 2025). These findings align with prior studies linking declining informal care to growing unmet needs (Albuquerque 2022; Kröger 2022; Vlachantoni et al. 2011). Korea’s pattern also resembles trends in family-oriented European countries, where rising female labour force participation and weakening family caregiving norms have reduced family-based support and intensified unmet care needs (Calderón-Jaramillo and Zueras 2023).

By contrast, formal care increased only modestly and did not keep pace with the erosion of informal care. This suggests that unmet needs may continue to grow unless formal care systems expand sufficiently. Hahm and Hong (2017) similarly found that formal care complements rather than substitutes for informal care among older Koreans, underscoring the need to strengthen formal services as family-based care declines.

However, the sensitivity analyses revealed a more nuanced pattern. When analyses were restricted to those who explicitly reported needing care or those with more severe impairments, the increase in unmet needs was less pronounced, particularly after 2020. Among those self-reporting care need, the predicated probability of formal care increased between 2017 and 2023. This may reflect the recent expansion of government-funded care, including the 2020 introduction of the Customised Care Service for Older Adults, which used more individualised needs assessments and expanded support for vulnerable older adults.

Nevertheless, the analysis restricted to those with severe impairments suggests that formal care expansion may not fully explain the attenuated increase in unmet care. Among this group, the predicted probability of receiving formal care showed decreasing trends over the past decade. This finding may indicate that older adults with severe impairments remain insufficiently covered by Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) or other formal programs, despite the gradual expansion of LTCI and the development of community-based care for less impaired older adults (MOHW 2023; NHIS 2024). Further research is needed to clarify how policy changes have affected formal care use and unmet needs among those with greater care needs.

Second, several individual characteristics shaped care utilisation. Consistent with Andersen’s theoretical model (1995), need factors—particularly the number of ADL limitations and cognitive impairment—were the most consistent predictors of formal and/or informal care across the main and sensitivity analyses. Among predisposing factors, age consistently predicted care receipt: older adults at more advanced ages were more likely to receive either formal or informal care rather than none, consistent with previous research (Chen et al. 2024; Hahm and Hong 2017; Spiers et al. 2025). Gender was significant mainly for informal care, with older women less likely than men to receive informal care. This may reflect women’s greater likelihood of widowhood and the persistence of traditional gendered caregiving patterns, in which men are more likely to receive care from wives or daughters (Cha et al. 2025; Katz et al. 2000).

Among enabling factors, living arrangement was especially important. Older adults living alone were less likely to receive informal care relative to no care, echoing prior studies (Desai et al. 2001; Dunatchik et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2011; Otero et al. 2003; Qiao et al. 2025). Beginning in 2020, however, living alone no longer predicted informal care; instead, it was associated with a greater likelihood of receiving formal care than living with a spouse, a pattern consistent with Lee (2022) but somewhat different from findings in other countries. This unique pattern may partly reflect the eligibility criteria of the Customised Care Service for the Elderly, launched in 2020, which prioritised older adults living alone (MOHW 2023). Consequently, those living with a spouse only may face disadvantages in accessing formal services while also lacking co-resident adult children who could provide informal support. Future research should examine whether older couples living without children constitute a particularly vulnerable group with unmet care needs.

Other socioeconomic predictors were less consistent. Among those with severe impairment, rural residence and lower income predicted a lower likelihood of receiving formal care in 2017, while lower education predicted lower formal care use among those reporting care needs. These associations were no longer significant after 2020, which may suggest improved equity in care access by residence, income, and education. However, further research is needed to determine whether these patterns continue in more recent policy environments.

These findings have several implications. From the perspective of care regime frameworks (van Damme et al. 2025), both defamilising care burdens and supporting family caregivers are needed. First, as informal care norms weaken, expanding and coordinating formal services will be crucial to prevent unmet needs. Although the number of LTCI and other community-based program beneficiaries has increased, approximately 80% of those with functional limitations in our 2023 sample did not receive formal care, indicating persistent access barriers. Particular attention should be paid to those with severe impairments, who may experience high unmet needs despite greater care requirements. Targeted interventions to improve service navigation and application, along with structural reforms to broaden access, could help reduce disparities.

At the same time, policies must support informal caregivers, who remain critical for preventing unmet care and ensuring quality care. According to the 2022 National Survey of LTCI, 22.5% of eligible older adults did not receive benefits, most often because they were reluctant to accept care from nonfamily caregivers (Lee et al. 2022). Although family caregivers continue to play a central role, they often lack adequate support. Under the current LTCI system, family caregiver support remains limited, with partial compensation available mainly through the Family Caregiver Program, which certifies relatives as paid caregivers. While the number of certified family caregivers has increased, this trend diverges from the original defamilising intent of LTCI, which emphasized professional services (Lee et al. 2022). Future policies should support family caregivers while ensuring that LTCI remains a robust social care system.

This study has several limitations. First, because the data are cross-sectional, we could not examine the within-individual variability over time or distinguish period effects from cohort effects. Future studies should examine how historical and structural changes, separately from generational differences, shape unmet care needs. Second, the survey lacked detailed information on why some respondents reported “no need for assistance”, limiting our ability to distinguish between those without actual care need and those with unrecognised or unacknowledged needs. Third, self-reported data may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Finally, although our operational definition of unmet care is consistent with prior studies, it simplifies the complexity of care needs. As Kröger (2022) notes, unmet needs involve not only access to care but also sufficiency and adequacy of support. Future studies should investigate the quality, intensity, and adequacy of care and their implications for older adults’ well-being.

In conclusion, this study documents a consistent decline in informal care in Korea, alongside limited growth in formal services and rising unmet care needs. It also identifies vulnerable groups, including those living with a spouse only and those with greater impairment. By analysing nationally representative data across a decade, this study provides rare evidence from a non-Western context and shows how demographic, social, and policy changes interact to shape care dynamics. These findings underscore the need for more equitable and sustainable care systems in rapidly ageing societies.
