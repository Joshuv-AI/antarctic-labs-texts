Effect of lower limb mirror visual feedback on cortical activation in healthy subjects: a self-controlled randomized trail
Authors: Wei Cui, Yang Tian, Qian Yu, Lin Huang, Yan Yang, Huifang Liu, Li Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMirror visual feedback (MVF) has been widely applied in the recovery of upper limb motor dysfunction after stroke, but its application in the neurological rehabilitation of the lower limbs is relatively rare, and the neural mechanism of MVF on the lower limbs is not yet clear. This study aims to explore the impact of lower limb mirror visual feedback (LLMVF) on the cerebral cortex and its possible neural mechanisms.Method23 healthy subjects were selected to conduct a self-controlled randomized study using a block design, and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was used to monitor the activation of brain regions in real LLMVF and sham LLMVF. The left lower limb of the subject was set as the active leg to perform active knee flexion and extension movements, and the right lower limb was set as the observation leg (shielded behind the mirror without any movement).ResultsThe mean HbO changes of LLMVF was significantly higher in the bilateral premotor and supplementary motor cortex (PM + SMA) (right: p = 0.010, left: p = 0.049), the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPF) (right: p = 0.030, left: p = 0.003), the left S1 (p = 0.011), and the right FP (p = 0.033) compared to the sham LLMVF.ConclusionLLMVF increases neural activity in the sensory and motor related areas, indicating that LLMVF can promote more activation of brain functional areas, which verifies the top-down positive effect of LLMVF.Keywords: Mirror visual feedback, Lower limb, Function near-infrared spectroscopy, Cortical activation, Top-down control

Introduction

Mirror visual feedback (MVF), also known as mirror therapy, is a treatment method that utilizes visual illusions and visual feedback for rehabilitation training [1, 2]. MVF, as a classic rehabilitation treatment strategy with the advantages of simplicity and low cost, was initially applied to the pain of the affected limb after amputation [3]. With the development of medicine, MVF has been found to play a role in the treatment of post-stroke functional disorders, especially upper limb motor dysfunction [4–7].

In recent years, MVF has been gradually extended to the application of lower limb motor dysfunction. Sutbeyaz et al. [8] first performed MVF dorsiflexion training on ankle joints in subacute post-stroke patients, and found significant improvements in their Brunnstrom staging and Functional Independence Measurement (FIM) motor scores. Kundi et al. [9] found that MVF was effective in restoring lower limb motor function, balance and gait in subacute and chronic post-stroke patients without severe cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination scores ≥ 24) and with functional ambulatory category ≥ 2, and suggested that MVF could be used for the recovery of lower limb motor function. Arya, Ji et al. [10, 11] found that lower limb mirror visual feedback (LLMVF) can better improve lower limb motor function and/or gait in stroke patients who can walk. May, Cui, et al. [12] found that LLMVF could also improve lower limb motor function in stroke patients with more severe motor impairments (Brunnstrom stages I-IV). Guo et al. [13] conducted a study on 5 post-stroke patients and 5 healthy individuals using task-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to examine MVF during unilateral ankle joint movement; They found that the sensorimotor cortex, occipital gyrus, and prefrontal gyrus of the lesioned side were significantly activated in post-stroke patients, while the bilateral motor cortex was strongly activated in the healthy control group. They believed that MVF for ankle joint movement may induce neural activation in the sensory motor cortex of the lesioned side, and cortical reorganization may contribute to stroke motor rehabilitation. Although more and more scholars are investing in the research of MVF and claiming that MVF can lead to neuroplasticity changes, there are still few studies on the neural mechanism of LLMVF. Therefore, it is necessary to continue to explore the neural mechanism of MVF in the rehabilitation of lower limb dysfunction and promote the application of MVF in lower limb rehabilitation treatment.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is an optical imaging technique that studies hemodynamic changes related to brain activity [14]. It uses the light absorption characteristics in the near-infrared spectrum of 700–900 nm to monitor real-time changes in the concentration of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) in the cerebral cortex, indirectly reflecting the neural activity of the brain [15]. Unlike other neuroimaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging, single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), fNIRS directly collects data by wearing a cerebral oximetry monitor on the head, its quality is less affected by the movement of participants in the collection environment [16]. Additionally, it can monitor brain neural activity in real-time under task stimulation in a real environment, and has advantages such as low susceptibility to motion artifacts and high temporal resolution [16–18]. It has also been confirmed that there is a good temporal and spatial correlation between fNIRS and fMRI activation during simultaneous multimodal recording of motor tasks [19, 20]. Therefore, fNIRS is increasingly used in neuroimaging studies [21].

The existing studies on the neural mechanism of LLMVF are still very rare, only a few studies on patients, and even fewer studies on healthy volunteers [13, 22]. To reduce the impact of individual differences caused by stroke, such as different parts of the brain damage, degree of survival motor function, and cognitive function, healthy subjects were selected as the research subjects in this study. We hoped to explore whether LLMVF can activate more brain regions through this study, and further analyze its activated areas and potential neural functional pathways, laying the foundation for future research on LLMVF in post-stroke brain functional remodeling. We have two hypotheses about the activation response of LLMVF to the cerebral cortex. Firstly, based on the previous study on LLMVF in healthy subjects, we supposed that LLMVF might induce activation responses in brain regions related to the mirror neuron system, such as the PM area [13]. Secondly, combined with the results of MVF in healthy individuals of the upper limbs and the results of a small amount of LLMVF in the exploration of the neural mechanism after stroke, we supposed that LLMVF might also induce the activation of more brain areas, such as motor and sensory related brain areas [22, 23]. Therefore, this study used fNIRS to observe the activation of brain functional areas in healthy subjects by LLMVF and sham LLMVF, and selected brain regions that may be related to MVF as regions of interest (ROIs). The changes in bilateral ROIs were observed and analyzed to explore the possible neural functional pathways of LLMVF.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a self-controlled randomized trial, single-blinded (evaluator). The study was conducted from March 2023 to November 2023 in the rehabilitation department of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. Sample size calculation was performed using PASS 2021 software, with the sum of HbO concentrations in the premotor and supplementary motor cortex on the left side of the ROI that passed the pre-test as the main measurement result (mean of paired difference was 0.081, standard deviation of paired difference was 0.165). Then, a sample size of 23 was calculated based on 90% power and 5% two-sided type I error.

Participants

This study involved 23 healthy subjects, including 6 males and 17 females, with an average age of 23.13 ± 4.07 years. All subjects were aged between 18 and 35, right-handed (handedness was self-reported and confirmed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory), had no visual or visual impairments, had normal limb motor function, had no central system diseases, and had no history of physical or psychological illness. Each subject signed an informed consent before the trial and cooperated in completing the study. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. Clinical registration number is ChiCTR2300077838.

Intervention

All recruited subjects were required to complete the real and sham LLMVF under fNIRS monitoring. The subjects entered the assessment room five minutes before the start of the experiment, seated comfortably, familiar themselves with the environment, and relieved their tension. The subjects were asked to avoid large movements of head and neck as much as possible during the experiment, and to avoid noise caused by large amplitude movement. In order to reduce interference with near-infrared signals, the entire experiment was conducted in a quiet room. A mirror of 189 cm× 89 cm was placed vertically in the sagittal plane in the sitting position of the subjects to conduct a self-controlled study of real and sham LLMVF. The order of the two interventions was determined by random drawing of closed envelopes. The left lower limb of the subject was set as the active leg (for active movement), and the right lower limb was set as the observation leg (shielded behind a mirror without movement). Starting at approximately 100 degrees flexion of the left knee, an extension motion (the maximum range of motion with the foot not off the ground) was performed, followed by a flexion motion to approximately 100 degrees. During the LLMVF, the left lower limb was placed on the reflective surface of the mirror, and the right lower limb was blocked on the other side of the mirror. When the left side moved, the subjects were asked to observe the limb in the mirror, see Fig. 1A. During the sham LLMVF [8, 13], the left lower limb was placed on the non-reflective surface of the mirror, and the right lower limb was blocked on the other side of the mirror. When the left side moved, the subjects were asked to observe the non-reflective surface of the mirror, see Fig. 1B. Both real and sham LLMVF were designed using the block paradigm for measuring brain activation, with four repetitions, and each cycle consisting of a 30 s rest phase and a 30 s task phase [24, 25], as shown in Fig. 1C. In each 30 s task phase, knee extension and knee flexion were performed in a rhythm of every 1.5 s, with 10 knee flexion and 10 knee extension movements, respectively. LLMVF and sham LLMVF both have 4 blocks. The flow of this study can be seen in Fig. 2.

Data acquisition of fNIRS

This study used NirSmart-6000 A equipment (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) to continuously measure and record the concentration changes of HbO and HbR in the brain of participants during the experiment. The system consists of a near-infrared light source (LED) and an avalanche photodiode (APD). The wavelength of the light source probe is 730 nm and 850 nm, respectively, and the sampling rate is 11 Hz. There were 48 effective channels composed of 24 light source probes and 16 detectors. See Fig. 3A. The average distance between the source and the detector is 3 cm (range 2.7–3.3 cm). The fNIRS sensor was fixed to the subject’s head. Based on the international 10/20 system, the probe S23 located in the middle of the forehead is aligned with Fpz, and the electrode cap is symmetrically fixed backwards to fit the scalp. See Fig. 3B.

FNIRS data processing

Using NirSpark v1.7.5 software to preprocess fNIRS raw data, existing studies have shown that HbO signals are more sensitive to cortical blood flow changes and have a higher signal-to-noise ratio [26, 27]. Therefore, this study chose HbO mean concentration change as the result measurement for analysis. Using Butterworth bandpass filtering (0.01–0.2 Hz) for processing to eliminate physiological noise unrelated to brain tasks, using sliding average method to remove signal noise to improve signal-to-noise ratio [28]. The concentrations of oxygenated hemoglobin were calculated by the Hemo module [29]. The General linear model (GLM) analysis with typical hemodynamic response curves was used to evaluate the individual level of hemodynamic response, and 4-second half width full width Gaussian smoothing was used for noise compensation. The FDR method was used to correct the p values between channels, P < 0.05 was adopted.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 21.0 software (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) was used for statistical analysis. Based on previous research, this study selected 14 ROIs, namely Chanel 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 covered bilateral primary motor cortex (M1), Chanel 21, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 29, 31 covered bilateral premotor and supplementary motor cortex (PM + SMA), Chanel 22, 25, 35, 42, 30, 33, 39, 47 covered bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPF), Chanel 10, 11, 17, 18 covered bilateral primary somatosensory cortex (S1), Chanel 1, 2, 3, 4 covered bilateral primary visual cortex (V1), Chanel 5, 7, 8 covered bilateral visual association cortex (V2), Chanel 36, 37, 38, 40 covered bilateral frontopolar area (FP). See Fig. 3C. HbO concentrations of relevant channels were expressed in mmol/L*mm and obtained by summing the HbO concentrations of channels in the ROI. The average of channels that overlapped by more than 50% in the ROI was used as the resulting value for the cortex [30, 31]. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was used to analyze HbO data. Time effects, group effects, and time × group interaction effects were used to evaluate the overall differences in HbO between two tasks in terms of time and group, and potential differences in changes between groups. Examination of group effects (fixed effect) was used to analyze the differences between ΔHbO before and after the tasks in the two groups to determine the group differences in task-dependent neural activity between LLMVF and sham LLMVF. Demographic data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. FNIRS data are expressed as mean ± standard errors. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad 8.0 was used to draw bar graphs of HbO results for each ROI.

Results

The real LLMVF was defined as the MVF task, and the sham LLMVF was defined as the MVF-sham task. GLMM on ROIs showed no significant difference in group effect and group × time interaction effect, while the time effect showed significant differences in the right M1 (p = 0.024), the right DLPF (p = 0.019), the left DLPF (p = 0.022), the right S1 (p = 0.002) and the left V2 (p = 0.025) (Table 1). The significant differences in mean HbO changes between the two tasks were found in the right PM + SMA (p = 0.010), the left PM + SMA (p = 0.049), the right DLPF (p = 0.030), the left DLPF (p = 0.003), the left S1 (p = 0.011), and the right FP (p = 0.033), which showed more activation in MVF task. Discussion

As a widely used rehabilitation therapy technique, the mechanism of MVF on the brain is still unclear, especially on the lower limbs. In this study, we performed real and sham LLMVF tasks in healthy subjects, and used fNIRS to observe the immediate hemodynamic cortical activation during different tasks, so as to explore the brain functional mechanism of LLMVF. The results showed that real LLMVF stimulation had more activation responses in bilateral PM + SMA, bilateral DLPF, as well as in the left S1 and right FP.

Firstly, we found that the mirror neuron related bilateral PM + SMA area and right FP area were activated with higher HbO value during the MVF task, and we hypothesized that mirror neuron activation is a possible neural mechanism of MVF in the lower limb. Mirror neuron system (MNS) was initially discovered by Rizzolatti et al. in their functional anatomical study of rhesus monkeys and later confirmed in humans [32]. MNS provides an “observation-execution matching” mechanism and is considered to play an important role in action recognition [33]. Based on this, we propose that motor learning can be induced by activation of the MNS by observing lower limb movements in the recognition mirror during LLMVF. Bonini et al. [34] summarized literature on mirror neurons over the past 30 years and found that the sensorimotor network of mirror neurons includes not only the premotor cortex (PM), primary motor cortex (M1), inferior parietal lobule (IPL), and anterior intraparietal area (AIP), but also the prefrontal cortex (PF) and secondary somatosensory cortex (S2). In addition, Heyes et al. [35] believed that the SMA was also a member of the atypical mirror neuron system. SMA is also a region that is frequently activated during walking [36]. In this study, visual feedback during the process of lower limb mirror movement enhanced the flexion and extension activities of the main walking joint (knee joint). We speculate that this may be one of the reasons why SMA is more significantly activated in the MVF group, which also suggests that LLMVF has been found to promote the recovery of walking function in some studies, possibly due to neural reasons [12, 37]. Our study found activation in bilateral PM + SMA regions and the right prefrontal cortex (FP), but no activation was found in other mirror neuron brain regions. Deconinck et al.'s [23] systematic review found that only part of the MNS had activation reactions such as the PM area after upper limb MVF, which was consistent with our results. We believed that, consistent with the MVF of the upper limbs, MVF of the lower limbs could only activate part of the MNS area.

Secondly, we found that bilateral DLPF also showed significant activation during MVF. As a core node of the prefrontal executive system, DLPF has cortico-cortical connections with the parietal lobe and premotor cortex, and works closely with M1 to form the basic pathways involved in motor tasks of different complexity [38–40]. It plays an important role in the top-down control of motor learning and behavior execution and is associated with many brain regions involved in motor control (such as the premotor area, supplementary motor area, basal ganglia, and primary motor cortex) [41–43]. The MVF designed in this study is mainly to observe the movement of the limb reflected in the mirror and perform motor imagery of the shielded limb, so this study mainly involves the observation and imagination of movement. It has been confirmed that DLPF plays a positive role in motor imagery and motor observation, and it is believed that the activation of bilateral DLPF is related to learning behavior [44]. Hardwick et al. [45] found that the left DLPF was activated by pure motor imagery (without motor execution or motor observation), and the connection between the premotor cortex and the DLPF may be activated during motor imagery [46, 47]. Fink et al. [48] observed differences in brain functional areas after hand mirror movement using positron emission tomography (PET), and found that neural activity in the DLPF significantly increased during MVF with different hand movements. In agreement with the above studies, which focused on the hand or upper limb, the present study also showed increased activation of the DLPF, and our study confirmed that the bilateral DLPF was also involved in the neurostimulation of the unperformed LLMVF. Therefore, it is reasonable to think that the process of LLMVF involves motor imagery, motor observation, and may mediate the top-down motor learning and motor control of the central nervous system through the DLPF. In addition, DLPF (including Brodmann Area 46 and 9) is a part of the frontal parietal lobe attention network, involving many associative regions of higher-order cognitive functions, such as working memory, executive function, motor planning, and attention [49–52]. Visual illusion feedback in MVF increases attention to shielded limbs and triggers neural activity in areas related to attention allocation, such as the DLPF found in this study. This result is consistent with previous research findings, they believe that visual feedback increases neural activity in areas related to attention allocation and cognitive control, such as the DLPF, posterior cingulate cortex, S1 and S2, and anterior cuneiform cortex [23, 53].

Finally, the activation of ipsilateral sensory related brain region S1 has also led us to focus some attention on the impact of MVF on sensation. It has been proven that visual illusion in MVF may regulate the somatosensory cortical network and contribute to the recovery of somatosensory function [7]. Fritzsch et al. [54] observed the mirror task of the left thumb finger to finger task on 15 healthy volunteers and found that mirror vision illusion did not immediately cause changes in the motor area, but significantly activated S1. Wu et al. [7] performed MVF on the upper limbs of post-stroke patients and found that MVF not only improved motor function scores, but also significantly improved temperature perception; They speculated that the multimodal neuronal responses in the motor cortex and posterior parietal lobe induced by visual and motor stimuli in the mirror could respond to sensory stimuli; Temperature sensation, which is the priority of recovery in post-stroke patients (temperature and pain sensation usually precede the recovery of proprioception and light touch), was the first to show significant recovery. Wasaka et al. [55] also believed that S1 may be involved in multimodal integration of sensory information related to visual information. Therefore, we speculate that the unintentional visual feedback of the shielded limb (inactive lower limb) in the mirror during LLMVF leads to an increased demand for attention to the somatosensory information of the shielded limbs, resulting in more activation reactions of ipsilateral S1 in this study. After stroke, the lack of activation in the ipsilateral cortex leads to interhemispheric imbalance, and the increased activation response of S1 on the ipsilateral side found in this study suggests that LLMVF may be beneficial for improving the normalization of interhemispheric balance and promoting the recovery of limb function on the affected side after stroke [56].

Based on the results of this study and other studies, we propose a hypothesis about the brain mechanism loop of LLMVF. The visual illusion information from vision increases the attention to sensory stimuli of the shielded limb, induces more activation of ipsilateral S1, and quickly reaches the prefrontal area (FP) for processing, which eventually leads to enhanced DLPF activation; The implementation of DLPF delegates the responsibility of specific motor tasks to other brain regions, such as basal ganglia, PM, M1, SMA and cingulate motor areas, and promotes different signals to the motor cortex, causing activation of brain motor areas such as PM and SMA to induce motor learning [40, 57]. In summary, LLMVF may affect the perception motion control process through two functional networks. The first is the partially activated MNS, and based on our results, we believe that both LLMVF and upper limb MVF have only a partial regulatory effect on mirror neurons, and both found significant activation responses in the PM area [6]. Secondly, LLMVF enhances sensation and attention to relevant brain regions through visual illusions (significant activation of DLPF, S1 in this study, similar to the results of upper limb MVF), and further promotes the relevant motor cortex [23]. The upper limb MVF mainly involved mirror feedback intervention of the upper limb or hand, our study chose mirror intervention of the lower limb knee joint. We found that the neurological effects of lower limb MVF and upper limb MVF on the brain are basically the same. Although there are some specific differences in brain regions, such as the activation response of the M1 area on the “affected side” found after upper limb MVF [58], this study did not find any. Therefore, we speculate that LLMVF can have a positive impact on the recovery of the sensory and motor systems through a top-down approach, and is a treatment option that post-stroke patients can consider.

Limitations

There were still few limitations in this study. Firstly, the selected subjects are healthy subjects, and although heterogeneity between subjects is avoided to the greatest extent possible, there are differences between the brain of patients and healthy subjects, especially in post-stroke patients. Therefore, in the future, we can further conduct research on LLMVF for post-stroke patients to explore the impact of LLMVF on neurological function remodeling in post-stroke patients. Secondly, the fNIRS device in this study cannot separate the PM and SMA areas, therefore the activation status of the PM and SMA regions cannot be analyzed separately, but rather as a whole. Observations of classical MNS such as the inferior parietal lobule (IPL) and the anterior intraparietal area (AIP) were also not possible. Finally, it has been found that the use of short channels can make the fNIRS measurement to achieve depth sensitivity [59], but unfortunately short channels were not available in this study. In future research, it is necessary to further improve short channel layout and the brain area distribution map of fNIRS collection devices and observe more relevant brain areas for comprehensive analysis.

Conclusion

In this study, LLMVF significantly increased activation of the ipsilateral S1, bilateral DLPF and PM + SMA cortex, as well as more activation in the contralateral FP, indicating that LLMVF can promote more activation of brain functional areas, verifying the positive effect of MVF from the top-down. This study can help us speculate that LLMVF can be applied more in patients with lower limb motor control disorders and/or sensory dysfunction caused by central nervous system injury in the future.
