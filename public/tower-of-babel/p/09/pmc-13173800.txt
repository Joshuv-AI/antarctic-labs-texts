Risk factors for carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii infection in children and A. baumannii-related mortality in the PICU: a retrospective study in Guangxi, China
Authors: Minxue Liu, Zhan Huang, Liying Huang, Chenglan Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) is recognized as a critical-priority pathogen due to the considerable therapeutic difficulties it poses and its association with high mortality. Pediatric patients are particularly vulnerable to the adverse clinical outcomes of these infections.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed the clinical and microbiological characteristics of patients at the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi, China, between January 2019 and December 2025. It further assessed risk factors for both CRAB infection and A. baumannii-related mortality in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors. A predictive model was then developed, and its performance was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsThe study included a total of 436 children, comprising 222 with CRAB infection and 214 with carbapenem-susceptible A. baumannii (CSAB) infection. In the univariate analysis, potential risk factors for CRAB infection (P < 0.05) included younger age, prior hospitalization, PICU admission, specific infections (enteritis, pneumonia, bloodstream infections), the use of invasive procedures or devices (mechanical ventilation, indwelling urethral catheters, central venous catheters, drainage tubes, parenteral nutrition, nasogastric feeding tubes), and prior exposure to glycopeptides, oxazolidinones, carbapenems, and corticosteroids. Potential risk factors for A. baumannii-related mortality in PICU (P < 0.05) included prior hospitalization, tumor, CRAB infection, septic shock, use of indwelling urethral or central venous catheters, parenteral nutrition, prior exposure to β-lactam–β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, tetracyclines, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, or corticosteroids, as well as levels of platelet (PLT) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin (ALB), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression model analysis identified admission to the PICU (Odds Ratio (OR): 5.136, 95% CI: 3.045–8.661, P < 0.001), pneumonia (OR: 2.618, 95% CI: 1.384–4.954, P = 0.003), carbapenems exposure (OR: 3.876, 95% CI: 2.277–6.598, P < 0.001), and prior corticosteroid exposure (OR: 1.860, 95% CI: 1.168–2.961, P = 0.009) as an independent risk factor for CRAB infection. In addition, the independent risk factors for A. baumannii-related mortality in PICU were prior hospitalization (OR: 3.656, 95% CI: 1.808–7.392; P < 0.001), CRAB infection (OR: 3.186, 95% CI: 1.406–7.219; P = 0.006), septic shock (OR: 3.417, 95% CI: 1.440–8.107; P = 0.005), parenteral nutrition (OR: 3.105, 95% CI: 1.248–7.725; P = 0.015), and decreased PLT count (OR: 0.997, 95% CI: 0.995–0.999; P = 0.001).ConclusionTwo logistic regression models were developed to predict CRAB infection in children and A. baumannii-related mortality among PICU patients, respectively. The findings highlight the importance of early identification and management of these risk factors.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Acinetobacter baumannii, CRAB, Mortality, Children, PICU, Risk factors

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) is a significant cause of hospital-acquired infections, including pneumonia, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, meningitis, and wound infections [1, 2]. It ranks as the third most prevalent Gram-negative bacterium in children, following Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli [3, 4]. According to data from The Child Health and Mortality Prevention Surveillance (CHAMPS) Network, A. baumannii was the second most common pathogen causing death among children aged 1–59 months with hospital-acquired pneumonia [5]. Notably, due to its pronounced antimicrobial resistance, A. baumannii poses a major clinical challenge, especially in pediatric populations [6]. In 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) designated carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) as a critical-priority pathogen [7]. Surveillance data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET; https://www.chinets.com) indicate that the CRAB resistance rate in China has risen significantly over the past two decades, increasing from approximately 40% to over 75%.

CRAB is particularly prevalent in intensive care units (ICUs) [8], and infections pose a significant therapeutic challenge due to frequent resistance to most antibiotics [9]. Consequently, mortality attributable to extreme drug resistant A. baumannii in these settings can reach up to 70% [2]. Furthermore, children with CRAB infection also face considerable risks [10]. However, data on the clinical characteristics, antibiotic susceptibility patterns, and risk factors of CRAB in children from Guangxi, China—especially in pediatric ICUs (PICUs)—remain limited. To address this knowledge gap, this study aimed to characterize pediatric A. baumannii infections at a maternal and child health hospital in Guangxi, China and to further identify risk factors for CRAB infection and A. baumannii-related mortality in the PICU.

Methods

Study setting and patients

This retrospective study enrolled pediatric patients diagnosed with A. baumannii infection at the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi, China, from January 2019 to December 2025. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≤ 14 years; (2) meeting the clinical diagnostic criteria for bacterial infection; (3) only the first episode of A. baumannii infection was considered, with recurrent infections excluded. Cases with incomplete or missing medical records were also systematically excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the research scheme

The diagnostic criteria for A. baumannii infection were defined as follows: (1) The presence of infectious symptoms, such as fever, cough, and sputum production; (2) elevated laboratory markers indicative of inflammation, including leukocytosis, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin; and/or (3) radiographic findings consistent with an infectious process; (4) microbiological confirmation, demonstrated either by the isolation of A. baumannii from a sterile site specimen or by the microscopic identification of neutrophils containing phagocytosed gram-negative bacilli in the clinical sample.

To identify risk factors for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) infection in children, patients were stratified into a CRAB group and a carbapenem-sensitive A. baumannii (CSAB) group. Furthermore, to investigate determinants of treatment outcomes among pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) patients with A. baumannii infection, those admitted to the PICU were categorized into a survival group and a mortality group.

Data collection and definitions

Medical data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health record system. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (gender and age), prior hospitalizations, duration of hospital stay, underlying diseases, admitting wards, infection type, invasive procedures and devices use, prior antibiotic and corticosteroid exposure, laboratory findings, and clinical outcomes.

CRAB refers to a clinical A. baumannii strain that demonstrates resistance to imipenem or meropenem, whereas CSAB denotes a clinical A. baumannii strain that remains susceptible to both imipenem and meropenem. A history of hospitalization indicates that an individual has previously been admitted to a healthcare facility for treatment. Prior length of stay refers to the number of days from hospital admission to the day on which the A. baumannii strain was isolated.

Based on medical records, outcomes were classified as follows: patients discharged in a cured or improved condition were recorded as survival, while those who died or were in critical condition at discharge were classified as mortality.

Microbiological methods

All isolates were identified using either the VITEK2 Compact system (BioMérieux, France) or matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Antobio MS600, Zhengzhou, China). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed via broth microdilution with the VITEK 2 Compact or DL-96 A (Dier, Zhuhai, China) systems. Interpretation followed the CLSI M100 (35th edition) guidelines. The following clinically relevant antibiotics were tested: ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, ampicillin-sulbactam, ticarcillin-clavulanic acid, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefoperazone-sulbactam, meropenem, imipenem, amikacin, gentamicin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole, polymyxin B, and minocycline. Quality control strains included Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). Continuous variables following a normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), whereas those with a non-normal distribution are summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as percentages. For group comparisons, normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-test, and non-normally distributed variables with the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables that reached significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors. Additionally, the goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. To evaluate the discriminative performance of the logistic regression model, we constructed the ROC curve based on the model’s predicted probabilities and the outcome.

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 436 pediatric cases of A. baumannii infection were identified in this study. Among them, 222 cases (50.9%) were classified as CRAB infections, and 214 cases (49.1%) as CSAB infections. The cohort comprised 287 males (65.8%) and 149 females (34.2%), with a median age of 9 months. A prior hospitalization history was reported in 181 children (41.5%). Most children were admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) (257, 58.9%).

Among the cohort, the prevalent underlying conditions included anemia (184, 42.2%), enteritis (62, 14.2%), and hypoproteinemia (46, 10.6%). The predominant infection types were pneumonia (361, 82.8%) and bloodstream infections (42, 9.6%), while septic shock was observed in 47 children (10.8%). Regarding invasive support, nasogastric feeding tubes (353, 81.0%) and mechanical ventilation (326, 74.8%) were the most frequently employed procedures.

In terms of prior antibiotic exposure, β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitors (322, 73.9%), cephalosporins (174, 39.9%), and carbapenems (126, 28.9%) were most common, while 254 children (58.3%) had received corticosteroids. The overall mortality rate among children with A. baumannii infection was 16.5%. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

Comparison of drug resistance rates between carbapenem-resistant and carbapenem-susceptible Acinetobacter baumannii isolates

The overall rate of CRAB was 50.9% (222/436). Correspondingly, resistance rates to carbapenems were approximately 50%, specifically 50.7% for meropenem and 48.4% for imipenem. Resistance to most other tested antibiotic classes was also around 50%, consistent with the CRAB phenotype. This included cephalosporins (ceftazidime 53.0%, ceftriaxone 53.4%, cefepime 53.0%), β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (ampicillin-sulbactam 49.5%, ticarcillin-clavulanate 53.7%, piperacillin-tazobactam 49.1%), aminoglycosides (amikacin 47.9%, gentamicin 59.9%), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (51.6%). Notably, carbapenem-resistant isolates demonstrated co-resistance to other β-lactam agents. Slightly lower resistance rates were observed for quinolones: 36.5% to levofloxacin and 40.8% to ciprofloxacin. In contrast, resistance to cefoperazone-sulbactam was 21.3%. The lowest resistance rates were observed for minocycline (14.2%) and polymyxin B (1.4%).

Risk factors for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii infection in children

To identify risk factors associated with CRAB infection in children, we conducted a case-control study. First, univariate analysis was performed to compare children with CRAB infection and those with CSAB infection. The analysis identified several factors significantly associated with pediatric CRAB infection. These included younger age, a history of prior hospitalization, admission to the PICU, underlying conditions (enteritis, pneumonia, bloodstream infections), and the use of invasive procedures or devices including mechanical ventilation, indwelling urethral catheters, central venous catheters (CVC), drainage tubes, parenteral nutrition, and nasogastric feeding tubes. Prior exposure to glycopeptides, oxazolidinones, carbapenems, and corticosteroids was also significantly associated (all P < 0.05; Table 1).

Then, factors with a P value < 0.05 were then included for multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results of this final model are presented in Table 3. The independent risk factors for CRAB infection in children were admission to the PICU (Odds Ratio (OR): 5.136, [95% CI: 3.045–8.661], P < 0.001), pneumonia (OR: 2.618, [CI: 1.384–4.954], P = 0.003), carbapenems exposure (OR: 3.876, [2.277–6.598], P < 0.001), and previous usage of corticosteroid (OR: 1.860, [1.168–2.961], P = 0.009). In contrast, parenteral nutrition was a protective factor (OR, 0.478; 95% CI, 0.248–0.922; p = 0.028). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good model fit (P = 0.689). Furthermore, the model demonstrated good predictive performance for CRAB infection for children, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.789 (95% CI: 0.747–0.831; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Risk factors for A. baumannii-related mortality in the PICU

The mortality and survival rates among children infected with A. baumannii in the PICU were 24.5% (63/257) and 75.5% (194/257), respectively. Univariate analysis indicated that the following factors were significantly associated with mortality in these patients: prior hospitalizations, tumor, CRAB infection, septic shock, indwelling urethral catheters, central venous catheter (CVC) use, parenteral nutrition, prior exposure to β-lactam–β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, tetracyclines, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, prior corticosteroid use, and laboratory findings including PLT count, CRP, ALB, and ALT levels (Table 4).

Discussion

Acinetobacter baumannii represents a significant causative agent of hospital-acquired infections among pediatric populations globally [8, 11–17]. The increasing prevalence of antibiotic-resistant A. baumannii strains has diminished treatment options and led to higher mortality [18]. This threat is particularly pronounced in vulnerable pediatric patients [19]. Some studies on pediatric A. baumannii infections have been reported [19–22]; however, data from Guangxi, China remain limited.

In this retrospective cohort study, we investigated risk factors for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii infection in children and A. baumannii-related mortality in the PICU. First, admission to the PICU, pneumonia, carbapenems exposure, and previous usage of corticosteroid were independent risk factors for CRAB acquisition. Second, independent risk factors for mortality included prior hospitalization, CRAB infection, septic shock, parenteral nutrition, and decreased PLT level.

We conducted a comprehensive literature review spanning from January 2015 to December 2025, focusing on risk factors for CRAB infection and A. baumannii-related mortality in children. A total of 11 studies were included in the final analysis, as summarized in Table 6 [22–32]. Among these, a total of six published studies examined risk factors for CRAB infection in children, while eight studies investigated risk factors associated with A. baumannii-related mortality in children. Within the latter group, three studies focused specifically on antibiotic-resistant strains of A. baumannii, including CRAB, MDRAB, or XDRAB.

In summary, the most consistently reported independent risk factors for pediatric CRAB infection across regions include: prior exposure to carbapenems or other broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., imipenem, aminoglycosides); invasive procedures such as mechanical ventilation, intubation, and catheterization; and host-related factors like prematurity and severe underlying conditions (e.g., severe pneumonia).

Regarding mortality from A. baumannii infection, septic shock and neutropenia are the predominant risk factors. For deaths specifically associated with drug-resistant strains (CRAB/MDRAB/XDRAB), the literature highlights the critical role of inappropriate initial or delayed adequate antibiotic therapy. Additional significant contributors include severe clinical states—prolonged ICU stay, high illness severity scores—and specific laboratory abnormalities such as thrombocytopenia and elevated CRP [30–32].

The profile of risk factors in neonates may differ from that in older children. Data from five of the reviewed neonatal studies [27–30, 32] underscore the unique vulnerability of this population, highlighting factors such as extreme prematurity, low Apgar scores, the use of umbilical catheters, being an inborn infant, need for reintubation, and early-onset infection within the first days of life. These factors are intrinsically linked to neonatal immune immaturity and the intensive, often invasive, supportive care required in the NICU setting.

Our findings on independent risk factors for CRAB infection—namely admission to the PICU, pneumonia, carbapenem exposure, and prior corticosteroid use—partly align with previously reported factors. Zhang et al. [23] also identified pneumonia and carbapenem exposure as significant risks, while prior invasive interventions and gastric intubation were highlighted in their study but not in ours. In contrast, Xu et al. [25] emphasized Oxa-23 expression and prior carbapenem treatment, which echoes our observation on carbapenem exposure. Notably, prior corticosteroid use emerged as a distinctive predictor in our cohort, an aspect that was less emphasized in earlier pediatric-focused studies.

Our identified independent risk factors for mortality associated with A. baumannii infection align with and extend previous findings. The association with septic shock is consistently reported across multiple studies [23, 30], highlighting its critical prognostic role. Similarly, prior healthcare exposure (hospitalization or carbapenem treatment) has been consistently identified across previous studies [23, 25, 28] Our finding of decreased platelet levels resonates with studies identifying thrombocytopenia as a risk factor [30, 32]. Notably, our study uniquely identified parenteral nutrition as a significant risk factor, possibly reflecting the severity of underlying conditions.

Our findings from Guangxi identified distinct sets of independent risk factors for CRAB acquisition (PICU admission, pneumonia, carbapenem exposure, prior corticosteroid use) and for mortality following A. baumannii infection (prior hospitalization, CRAB infection, septic shock, parenteral nutrition, decreased PLT) in children. These factors serve complementary early-warning roles. The former group can help clinicians identify high-risk patients for targeted surveillance or preventive interventions, potentially reducing CRAB incidence. The latter group provides prognostic markers that may alert clinicians to patients at greater risk of poor outcomes, prompting closer monitoring and more aggressive management. Integrating both sets into clinical assessment could enhance both prevention and outcome prediction in pediatric A. baumannii infections.

In addition, these results highlight the critical need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship—specifically by curbing unnecessary carbapenem and corticosteroid use—along with rigorous infection control practices within PICUs. Clinically, early identification of high-risk pediatric patients, particularly those with prior healthcare exposure or significant comorbidities, is essential. Adopting proactive surveillance, ensuring appropriate nutritional support, and initiating timely management of septic shock could collectively contribute to better clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center, retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our conclusions. Future multicenter, prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these predictors and to develop effective, evidence-based preventive protocols for this vulnerable population. Second, molecular characteristics of the clinical A. baumannii strains were not investigated. Therefore, whole-genome sequencing is necessary to analyze virulence factors and to determine whether they constitute risk factors for poor prognosis.

The key strengths of this study lie in its focus on an understudied population and region, as well as its direct implications for clinical practice. By providing a systematic characterization of CRAB infections in the pediatric population of Guangxi, China—a region where pediatric infectious disease epidemiology remains largely underexplored—this study addresses an important geographical and demographic knowledge gap. These findings provide essential evidence to inform infection prevention and control efforts, with the potential to reduce morbidity and mortality associated with CRAB infections in children.

Conclusion

In contrast to CSAB strains, CRAB isolates in our study were associated with significantly higher rates of infection and multidrug resistance, which correlated with poorer clinical outcomes in the pediatric cohort. The identification of key risk factors, such as admission to the PICU, pneumonia, carbapenems exposure, and prior usage of corticosteroid, provides an important clinical basis for the early detection of high-risk pediatric patients. Moreover, among children with A. baumannii infection in the PICU, carbapenem resistance, septic shock, and thrombocytopenia were identified as significant predictors of poor outcomes. These findings may aid in formulating targeted prevention and control strategies to reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with CRAB infection in children.
