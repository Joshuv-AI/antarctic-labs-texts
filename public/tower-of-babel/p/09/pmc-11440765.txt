Assessing the predictive value of pre- and post-operative inflammatory markers in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Khan Akhtar Ali, LingXiao He, Xinyue Deng, Jumei Pan, Hui Huang, Wenkai Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveTotal Knee Arthroplasty (TKA) has proven highly effective in improving quality of life for patients with severe knee conditions. Despite advancements, surgical complications such as periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) pose risks. The potential predictive value of pre- and post-operative inflammatory markers like neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), D-dimer, and albumin levels on surgical outcomes is garnering attention. There is a growing interest in leveraging these markers to enhance patient selection and outcome prediction in the context of TKA.Focusing on the natural course of these markers, and the incidence of PJIs and to refine perioperative care strategies, improve patient outcomes, and identify high-risk patients for targeted intervention.MethodsThe study included 94 patients who underwent total knee arthroplasty (TKA) between 2019 and 2023. Blood tests were conducted before surgery and at 1, 3, 7, and 15 days after surgery to assess various parameters including white blood cell count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein, D-dimers, total protein, albumin, and total cholesterol values and ratios.ResultsFollowing total knee arthroplasty (TKA), key observations in blood markers included a sharp rise in white blood cell (WBC) counts from 5.81 to 10.22 (*10^9/L) on the first day post-surgery, with levels returning close to preoperative values by day-15. Neutrophil counts similarly increased from 3.46 to 8.50 (*10^9/L) on day-1, decreasing to 4.01 by day-15. Hemoglobin levels significantly decreased from 115.70 g/L to 90.62 by day-3 before improving to 100.30 by day-15. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels also saw a significant rise from 6.15 mg/L to a peak of 47.07 on day-3, then reducing to 10.55 by day-15, indicating a response to inflammation.ConclusionFollowing total knee arthroplasty (TKA), a significant initial postoperative increase in white blood cell count, neutrophils, and C-reactive protein levels, indicative of an acute inflammatory response, before returning towards baseline values by day 15. Hemoglobin levels displayed a notable dip post-surgery, gradually improving by the study’s end. These patterns emphasize the dynamic nature of inflammatory and hematological responses after TKA, highlighting their potential role in predicting surgical outcomes and guiding postoperative care.Keywords: Albumin, D-dimer, Inflammatory markers, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Periprosthetic joint infections, Preoperative evaluation, Total knee arthroplasty

Introduction

Total knee replacement (TKA) is highly effective and proven to significantly improve the lives of patients suffering from severe knee osteoarthritis, providing substantial pain relief and enhanced mobility. It’s a common procedure in the U.S., with around 400,000 operations performed yearly, mainly due to osteoarthritis but also for other conditions such as inflammatory arthritis, fractures, or bone deformities [1–3].The prevalence of total joint arthroplasties (TJAs) has seen a significant rise, with forecasts indicating a 673% increase in total knee arthroplasties (TKAs) and a 174% increase in total hip arthroplasties (THAs) by 2030. This surge is coupled with a growing incidence of surgical complications, notably periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs). Addressing this issue necessitates a focus on preemptive measures and meticulous patient assessment to mitigate post-surgical complications. Key strategies include comprehensive preoperative screenings to identify and manage modifiable risk factors such as obesity, anemia, malnutrition, and diabetes, as well as other considerations like Staphylococcus aureus colonization and lifestyle choices. Particularly in older patients, who are at a higher risk of PJIs and exhibit increased mortality rates, early intervention and a multifaceted care approach are critical. By implementing detailed preoperative evaluations and adopting a holistic management strategy for identified risks, the likelihood of SSIs and PJIs can be significantly reduced, enhancing patient outcomes post-TJA [4–9]Recent research indicates a relationship between higher white blood cell counts before surgery and an increase in complications after various types of surgeries. This condition, known as leukocytosis, indicates a general state of inflammation in the body and is a key factor in identifying systemic inflammatory response syndrome [10–14].A strong inverse correlation between CRP and cholesterol concentrations suggests a link between inflammation and cholesterol metabolism. Inflammatory cytokines may increase LDL receptor activity, and heparin used during and after surgery could also contribute to lower serum cholesterol. However, findings might be biased due to standardized replacement therapy during surgery causing blood volume dilution. Additional research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms behind the postoperative cholesterol decline [15–20].The preoperative assessment of hemoglobin levels is a strong indicator of the likelihood of needing a transfusion in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery. Therefore, it is recommended that hemoglobin evaluation is included in the standard pre-surgical tests for these patients [21].The Complete Blood Count (CBC) is a routine test often conducted by surgeons for preoperative evaluation and identifying infectious conditions. It includes the analysis of the neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the platelet/lymphocyte ratio (PLR), derived from the counts of neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes. These ratios are gaining attention as they serve as markers for underlying inflammation. A major benefit of these indicators is their accessibility from standard CBC tests, making them a valuable tool in clinical assessments [22–25] Gölge UH [26] suggests that the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) could be a valuable predictor of chronic Periprosthetic Joint Infection (PJI) when compared with other markers like ESR and CRP. They compared preoperative and postoperative 6th month NLR values to make this determination.D-dimer has been identified as a valuable marker for diagnosing hip and knee prosthetic joint infections (PJI) and has been included as a minor criterion in the consensus definition for PJI in these joint [27, 28].Albumin plays a role in decreasing platelet activity and contributes to preventing blood clots. Reduced albumin levels can indicate increased inflammation, a propensity for clot formation, or illness predisposing individuals to thrombosis. Research indicates that decreased albumin levels can lead to a higher likelihood of lung issues, the need for blood transfusions, subsequent surgeries, and hospital readmissions following total shoulder surgery. Furthermore, it has been determined that albumin levels lower than 35 g/L significantly raise the risk of developing deep vein thrombosis prior to surgery for fractures in the lower extremities [29–33].Insufficient nutrition and protein intake can cause rapid decline in the health and independence of older adults, impacting muscle mass, cardiometabolic health, and functionality. Nutrition is a key aspect of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) programs, designed to minimize stress and promote quicker recovery post-surgery [34–36].Our hypothesis is that variations in pre- and post-operative inflammatory markers, specifically neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), D-dimer, and albumin levels, can predict the likelihood of complications following Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA) beyond the direct outcomes of the surgery itself. Consequently, the main aim of this study is to explore the fluctuations of these markers before and after TKA to determine their potential in forecasting postoperative complications. This endeavor seeks to enhance perioperative care, improve patient selection, and identify high-risk individuals for focused interventions, ultimately aiming to mitigate the risk of complications.

Methods

During this retrospective observational study, 94 patients who had undergone total knee arthroplasty (TKA) between November 2019 and March 2023 were included. The participants had a mean age of 68.6 years, ranging from 39 to 81, with 51 being female and 43 male. Their venous blood was tested for white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein, D-dimers, total protein, albumin, and total cholesterol values and ratios before the surgery and at 1, 3, 7, and 15 days after the surgery. We initially selected 700 patients, but only 94 patients met the eligibility criteria. According to our hospital protocols, active and healthy patients after TKA are discharged within 7 to 10 days after the surgery. Therefore, we only selected patients who were able to return to the outpatient department on the 15th day and were local residents living nearby. Figure 1 study flow chart.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this study, we successfully performed total knee replacement surgeries on participants who followed our guidelines. This included having complete documentation of specific blood markers on days 1, 3, 7, and 15 post-operation. We excluded participants with missing data, those who didn’t have blood samples taken on days 1, 3, 7, and 15, individuals taking medication for heart, neurological, or kidney conditions, those with fractures, as well as patients with systemic lupus erythematosus, chronic cardiovascular or pulmonary diseases, rheumatoid arthritis, or any coagulation abnormalities. Also, participants with cancer or those undergoing anticoagulation treatment were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and figures were performed using the graphpad prism 9.0. Characteristics are presented as numbers, means, standard deviation, and ranges using descriptive analysis. Student’s t-test and Chi-Square test were used to compare whether there is a significant difference between the means or distributions of two groups, One-way ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparisons test were performed in order to asses the differences between preoperative and postoperative tests. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A comprehensive analysis of various blood markers indicates significant changes post-surgery, which are indicative of the acute inflammatory response, modifications in blood components, and metabolic shifts. Specifically, White Blood Cell (WBC) counts significantly increased from a pre-surgery level of 5.81 ± 1.23 × 10^9/L to 10.22 ± 1.67 × 10^9/L on the first day post-surgery, then decreased to closely match the pre-surgery levels by day 15, illustrating a strong acute inflammatory response and its resolution.

Neutrophil counts also surged immediately after TKA but showed a slight elevation above baseline by day 15, pointing towards an ongoing, yet incomplete, resolution of inflammation. Lymphocyte levels decreased post-surgery, highlighting a stress response or a shift in immune function, with only partial recovery observed by day 15.

Platelet counts initially dropped but returned to near preoperative levels by day 7, indicating a transient response.

A significant post-operative reduction in hemoglobin levels suggested considerable blood loss or dilution effect, with a gradual, albeit incomplete, recovery by day 15. CRP levels spiked initially, depicting acute phase inflammation, but showed signs of resolution by day 15.

D-dimer levels rose significantly post-TKA, indicating increased coagulation and fibrinolysis activity, a common response to tissue injury.

Both albumin and total protein levels decreased immediately after surgery, suggesting impacts of acute-phase response, fluid shifts, or nutritional changes, with ongoing recovery by day 15.

Total cholesterol levels also decreased following surgery but showed a slight upward trend by day 15, remaining below baseline, which could indicate metabolic adjustments and stress-related effects. Figure 2. Tables 2, 3.1, 3.2.

Discussion

In this prospective study, we investigated natural course for D-dimer, serum CRP, and ESR after uneventful primary TKA. The most important finding of this study was that D-dimer demonstrated a distribution pattern with incline to its peak level on day 1 and return to a plateau level approximately 3 folds of the baseline levels on postoperative day 3. The incline and decline in the level of D-dimer was much faster than CRP and ESRs in patients undergoing uneventful primary TKA and THA. Comparing with other studies, Ghosh and Chatterji (2019) conducted a review on Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) in total knee replacement surgery, emphasizing the importance of preoperative optimization to reduce infection risks [3]. Antonelli and Chen (2019) highlighted the significance of preoperative optimization in reducing infection risks after total joint arthroplasty, aligning with the emphasis on preoperative risk assessment in the current study [4]. Kurtz et al. (2007) projected a significant rise in primary and revision hip and knee arthroplasty procedures in the United States, indicating the growing importance of efficient preoperative risk assessment and management strategies, as emphasized in the current study [5]. our study’s results showcased a significant association between specific preoperative blood markers, nutritional statuses, and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA). Elevated preoperative levels of white blood cells and C-reactive protein (CRP), along with abnormal blood profiles, including reduced hemoglobin, albumin levels, and altered Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), are strong predictors of postoperative complications(infections and delayed wound healing).Recent studies have shown promise in using serum and synovial biomarkers, along with culture bacteriology and clinical findings, for diagnosing PJI. New assays and markers, like synovial leukocyte esterase, D-dimer, synovial alpha defensin, and synovial C-reactive protein, are being examined for their accuracy in PJI diagnosis. However, a single gold standard test is still elusive, so a stepwise approach is recommended due to the expense and time-consuming nature of many of these tests. Additionally, some tests require specialized equipment and expertise not readily available at all institutions [38, 39].

The changes in D-dimer and CRP levels after surgery, as outlined in our study and Azboy I et al.‘s research, are crucial for monitoring postoperative recovery and identifying potential complications. D-dimer levels rapidly increase after surgery, signaling the body’s response to surgical trauma, while CRP levels indicate the body’s inflammatory response, gradually decreasing after reaching a peak. Monitoring these levels can offer valuable insights into the patient’s recovery, aiding in tailoring postoperative care, and preventing complications, ultimately improving patient outcomes [40].In our study, we found that neutrophil levels increased significantly on the first day after surgery and remained elevated until day 15, indicating a response to surgical stress or infection. Meanwhile, lymphocyte levels decreased post-surgery, suggesting temporary suppression likely due to stress response or immunosuppression. Bao-Zhan Yu et al.‘s research focused on the Neutrophil to Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) in diagnosing infection, highlighting the importance of this ratio over individual counts. These findings provide complementary perspectives on the roles of neutrophils and lymphocytes in postoperative recovery and infection diagnosis [41]. a study by Venkatsaiakhil et al. found that certain marker ratios outperformed traditional markers like ESR and CRP in diagnosing periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) patients. Combining these markers with specific thresholds significantly improved diagnostic accuracy. The highest accuracy was achieved when markers were combined with PLR and PVR, showing near-perfect sensitivity and specificity rates [42].Revision knee arthroplasty often leads to lower hemoglobin levels, increasing perioperative risk, especially with every 1 g/dL drop in Hb. Preoperative anemia is common in knee and hip surgery patients. The “rule of 10” offers a straightforward guideline for surgeons, linking a 10 g/L reduction in Hb to a 40% higher perioperative risk [43].Comparing our study with Kier Blevins BA et al.‘s we found that hemoglobin and albumin levels significantly decreased after surgery. They remained below preoperative levels, indicating a risk for postoperative joint infection (PJI). Our study also showed a notable impact on platelets post-surgery, while Kier Blevins BA et al. emphasized a qualitative relationship between platelets and PJI risk. Additionally, we observed an increase in white blood cells post-surgery, particularly in neutrophils, whereas Kier Blevins BA et al. incorporated white blood cells into a composite marker for PJI risk assessment [44].After surgery, both albumin and total protein levels show significant changes, reflecting the body’s response to surgical stress. Albumin levels, while significantly different from preoperative levels on all postoperative days, exhibit no significant fluctuation between day 1 and day 3, and similarly between day 7 and day 15, indicating a rapid stabilization after the initial post-surgery impact. Total protein levels also differ significantly from preoperative levels immediately after surgery (days 1 and 3) and by day 15, but interestingly, not on day 7. This pattern suggests a temporary stabilization or adjustment phase before levels again diverge from preoperative values by day 15.In essence, both albumin and total protein are significantly affected by surgical stress initially, but their patterns of recovery differ, with albumin showing a quick stabilization and total protein levels demonstrating a more complex recovery trajectory. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring such biochemical indicators for assessing patient recovery after surgery.The plasma contains proteins that can indicate disease states when measured. Normal serum protein level is 6–8 g/dl, with albumin comprising 3.5–5.0 g/dl. Elevated albumin occurs in acute dehydration, while decreased levels result from conditions like liver disease, malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome, and severe burns, leading to fluid shifts and edema formation [45].The loss of muscle mass and function due to reduced activity is caused by a shift in the balance between protein synthesis and degradation. Protein supplementation has been shown to reduce muscle atrophy after surgeries such as ACLR, THA, TKA, and hip fracture treatment, leading to improved functional measures. The stress response to surgery increases protein breakdown, which, combined with immobility, can lead to significant skeletal muscle loss. Adequate nutrition, including carbohydrates for energy and protein for muscle preservation, is essential for addressing the surgical stress response and mitigating muscle loss and reduced functionality [46–48].Importantly, the results of total cholestrol levels in our study showed overall significant changes throughout the postoperative period, smaller differences were observed between day 1 and day 3, as well as between day 7 and day 15. This pattern could indicate that while the surgery has an immediate and lasting impact on total cholesterol levels, the most rapid changes may occur in the initial days following the operation. By day 1, the body responds to the stress of surgery, which could be reflected in alterations in lipid metabolism, including the total cholesterol level. As the body begins to recover, the differences in total cholesterol levels between subsequent days (day 1 vs. day 3 and day 7 vs. day 15) become less pronounced, suggesting a potential stabilization or adaptation in lipid metabolism as the recovery progresses.Several factors such as age, gender, obesity, inflammation, and various metabolic conditions play a role in increasing the risk of osteoarthritis [49–51]. Study conducted by Kellgren and his team highlighted a link between elevated levels of serum cholesterol and the occurrence of osteoarthritis in the hands of women [52]. Furthermore, a comprehensive study involving over a thousand middle-aged women, known as the Chingford study, revealed that high total cholesterol levels in the serum are linked to the development of osteoarthritis in both one and both knees. Nonetheless, this study found no direct correlation between osteoarthritis and the levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) [53].Sepsis affects cholesterol synthesis, transportation, and metabolism, impacting immunity, hormone production, and cell membrane sensitivity. Studies show potential for using cholesterol as a therapeutic agent in sepsis. Cholesterol metabolism increases in sepsis through enzymatic and nonenzymatic oxidation. Inflammation induces cholesterol-25-hydroxylase production and promotes increased cholesterol ester and apolipoprotein metabolism, reducing serum cholesterol levels [54–56].In this prospective study, we found that D-dimer levels peak significantly on the first day after surgery and decrease to about three times the baseline by the third day, unlike CRP and ESR. Literature comparisons reinforced the critical role of preoperative optimization in minimizing post-surgery infection risks and enhancing arthroplasty outcomes. The findings showed a strong link between pre-surgical blood markers, nutritional status, and postoperative complications. The study also highlighted ongoing D-dimer and CRP level monitoring for postoperative complication identification and management, as well as changes in neutrophil and lymphocyte levels post-surgery. These insights underscore the importance of preoperative preparedness and biomarker monitoring in improving patient outcomes post-TKA.

Limitations of the study

The study’s retrospective nature limits establishing causality between preoperative blood markers and postoperative complications. The study population may not be representative of those undergoing TKA, potentially affecting the generalizability of findings. Uncontrolled confounding factors may impact the association between blood markers and postoperative outcomes. Observational study design might result in inadequate adjustment for baseline differences among patients. Additionally, the pre and postoperative use of antibiotics was not mentioned in the study, potentially affecting the results.

Future perspective

Further studies are needed to confirm the value of preoperative and postoperative blood markers in TKA patients and to establish causality. Research should focus on larger, diverse populations for generalizability. Future investigations could explore potential mechanisms linking blood markers with postoperative complications and assess cost-effectiveness. Advanced statistical methods and machine learning techniques could improve predictive accuracy. Additionally, studies should consider the use of antibiotics and examine blood markers and synovial fluids in patients undergoing revision knee arthroplasty to assess infection rates and overall patient health.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study underscores the significant role of pre- and post-operative inflammatory markers in predicting complications and guiding patient management in Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA). The observed variations in markers such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), D-dimer, and albumin, along with traditional indicators like WBC counts and CRP levels, highlight their potential in enhancing patient selection and outcome prediction. Incorporating these markers into preoperative evaluations can facilitate more personalized care, helping identify patients at risk of post-surgical complications like periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs). Our study supports wider use of these markers to enhance surgical outcomes and recovery, requiring further research for validation and broader application.
