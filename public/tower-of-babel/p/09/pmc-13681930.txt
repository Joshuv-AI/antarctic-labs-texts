Research engagement of rare disease patient advocacy groups in Poland: between participation and constrained co-production
Authors: Jan Domaradzki, Dariusz Walkowiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRare diseases (RDs) present complex challenges, including diagnostic uncertainty, limited treatment options, and fragmented research and care systems. These constraints contribute to inequities in access to care, information, and research participation among patients and caregivers across social and institutional contexts. In this setting, rare disease patient advocacy groups (RDPAGs) have emerged as key actors, not only providing support to patients and caregivers but also increasingly engaging in research. However, empirical evidence regarding their research engagement and organisational capacities remains limited, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe. This study examines the engagement of RDPAGs in research and intersectoral collaboration in Poland.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and April 2026 among representatives of 64 Polish RDPAGs using a structured, web-based questionnaire. The questionnaire covered organisational characteristics, research engagement, collaboration, and perceived barriers. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics.ResultsMost RDPAGs reported at least occasional engagement in research, although involvement was heterogeneous and often limited in scope. RDPAGs were primarily active in non-clinical research domains, including social research, patient registries, and dissemination of research findings. Participation in clinical and translational research was less frequent and largely supportive, particularly in recruitment and patient support. A majority of RDPAGs did not initiate research activities, and only a small proportion reported high levels of engagement. Key barriers included a lack of funding, limited human resources, and restricted influence on research processes. Despite these constraints, collaboration with physicians was common, although often informal. The findings indicate that RDPAGs function as intermediary actors and knowledge brokers, partially compensating for structural gaps in healthcare and research systems by facilitating access to information, support, and research participation for patients and caregivers. The findings further suggest a model of constrained co-production, in which RDPAGs participate in research but remain insufficiently integrated into research decision-making and knowledge production.ConclusionsRDPAGs play an important but structurally constrained role in research and knowledge co-production. Strengthening their contribution requires formalised collaboration frameworks, improved access to resources, capacity-building initiatives, and earlier and more meaningful involvement across the research cycle. Background

Rare diseases (RDs) are defined in the European Union as conditions affecting fewer than 1 in 2,000 individuals [1, 2]. Despite their individual rarity, RDs collectively represent a major public health challenge, with more than 10,000 identified conditions [3–5] affecting a substantial proportion of the global population – an estimated 27–36 million people in Europe and over 350 million worldwide [1, 2]. Most RDs are genetic in origin, frequently manifest in early childhood, and are associated with significant morbidity and mortality [6, 7]. They place a considerable burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems. Patients and families commonly face prolonged diagnostic pathways, limited access to effective treatments, fragmented care, and substantial psychosocial and economic burdens [8–13]. These challenges have led to the increasing recognition of RDs as a global health priority and a key area for health system development and innovation [14–18].

A central feature of the RD landscape is the persistent gap between the number of identified conditions and available treatments. Although advances in genomics and biomedical research have significantly improved diagnostic capabilities, only a small proportion of RDs currently have approved therapies [19–22]. This gap is largely driven by structural challenges inherent to RD research and drug development. The small number of patients affected by each condition limits the feasibility of large-scale clinical trials, reduces statistical power, and increases uncertainty in evidence generation. As a result, studies are often smaller, more complex, and less commercially viable, while high development costs and regulatory complexities further constrain investment and slow therapeutic innovation [21–23].

At the European level, RDs have become a distinct policy domain, supported by coordinated strategies including the 2009 Council Recommendation and the development of national plans, registries, and European Reference Networks (ERNs) aimed at improving diagnosis, care coordination, and research integration across Member States [24]. These developments have been accompanied by increasing emphasis on international research collaboration and shared infrastructures, reflecting the need to address the fragmentation and small-scale nature of RD research [25]. Despite increasing harmonisation, substantial disparities in access to care and orphan medicinal products persist across countries due to differences in reimbursement systems, healthcare capacity, and policy implementation [26].

Significant legislative and scientific progress has been made, particularly following the introduction of the Orphan Drug Act in the United States and subsequent European regulations. These policy frameworks introduced incentives such as tax credits, market exclusivity, and research funding, which substantially accelerated orphan drug development and stimulated global regulatory convergence [21, 22, 27]. As a result, orphan drug approvals have increased markedly, from 14 in 2000 to 77 in 2017, with RD therapies accounting for nearly 49% of all FDA novel drug approvals in 2022 [21]. Similarly, in the European Union, more than 200 orphan medicines have been authorised to date, underscoring both the progress achieved and the continuing gap between available treatments and the number of RDs [28–30]. However, despite this progress, therapeutic options remain limited for most RDs [19–22].

In this context, rare disease patient advocacy groups (RDPAGs) have emerged internationally as key actors in addressing systemic gaps in knowledge about RD and in advancing research. Initially established as grassroots initiatives by patients and families aimed at providing mutual support, many RDPAGs have gradually professionalised over time. However, this process has been uneven, with some patient organisations evolving into structured entities engaged in advocacy, education, and research, while others continue to operate primarily as volunteer-led grassroots organisations [31–33]. Recent studies emphasise that patient organisations are not only service providers but also active contributors to knowledge production, shaping research agendas and facilitating collaboration between patients, researchers, clinicians, and industry stakeholders [34–37]. Their roles extend across multiple domains, including patient recruitment, dissemination of research findings, development of Patient-Reported Outcome/Experience measures (PROMs/PREMs), participation in governance structures, and, in some cases, leadership or co-leadership of research projects conducted jointly with clinicians and researchers [33, 36, 38–40]. These forms of engagement have been documented across multiple stages of the research process, including agenda setting, study design, and dissemination, although evidence on their measurable impact remains limited [41]. Importantly, RDPAGs contribute unique experiential knowledge, which complements clinical and scientific expertise and is increasingly recognised as essential for the development of patient-centred and contextually relevant research [32, 34].

More broadly, their growing role reflects a shift towards participatory and co-productive models of research, in which patients are increasingly recognised as partners rather than passive research participants. Recent empirical studies demonstrate that some patient-led organisations are capable of initiating and co-producing research together with clinicians, academic researchers, and industry partners, illustrating both the opportunities and practical challenges of such collaborative models [37, 42, 43]. In the context of RDs, characterised by limited data, fragmented knowledge, and small patient populations, such contributions are particularly critical for advancing research and improving evidence generation.

At the same time, the capacity of RDPAGs to engage effectively in research remains uneven and constrained by structural factors. Evidence points to persistent barriers, including limited financial, organisational, and human resources, unequal access to decision-making processes, and asymmetries in collaboration with researchers and healthcare professionals [36, 44, 45]. Studies further highlight that systemic constraints in RD research, such as fragmented data, limited registries, and insufficient coordination, reinforce the need for intermediary actors capable of bridging gaps between patients, researchers, and policymakers [22, 27]. Moreover, despite increasing recognition of the importance of RDPAGs as stakeholders in governance and regulatory processes, their actual influence on research agendas, priorities, and policy development varies considerably across contexts [46, 47]. This highlights the need for more systematic empirical research examining the roles, capacities, and challenges of RDPAGs as organisational actors within the RD research.

In Poland, the challenges faced by RDPAGs are shaped by a rapidly evolving, yet still developing, RD policy framework. While recent strategies have prioritised improvements in diagnosis, access to modern treatment, care coordination, and integration with European initiatives [48, 49], less attention has been given to the organisational capacities and systemic roles of RDPAGs. Recent qualitative research conducted in Poland showed that RDPAGs act as knowledge brokers across healthcare, education, and policy systems by translating scientific and experiential knowledge, supporting newly diagnosed families, and facilitating collaboration between patients, clinicians, researchers, and policymakers [50]. At the same time, the study confirmed growing activity among RDPAGs but also highlighted persistent structural challenges related to funding, visibility, sustainability, and integration into formal decision-making processes [50, 51]. However, little is known about the prevalence of these activities across RDPAGs or the extent of their engagement in research and intersectoral collaboration. Thus, despite the growing recognition of their importance, there remains a lack of comprehensive quantitative data on the functioning, activities, and research engagement of RDPAGs, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe.

To address this gap, the present study provides a systematic analysis of RDPAGs’ roles in research and intersectoral collaboration in Poland. Specifically, it aims to: 1) characterise their core activities, including educational and research-related functions 2) identify key challenges and barriers affecting RDPAGs’ functioning; and 3) assess their engagement in research processes and collaboration with institutional actors. By examining these roles, challenges, and patterns of collaboration, this study seeks to contribute to a better understanding of the evolving role of RDPAGs within the RD ecosystem and how they may support the development of more inclusive, participatory, and effective research and policy frameworks.

Methods

Study design

While this study forms part of a broader research project examining the role of RDPAGs in Poland led by the first author [50], the quantitative component investigated the organisational functioning of RDPAGs across several domains, including organisational characteristics, educational and advocacy activities, collaboration with institutional stakeholders, research engagement, organisational needs, and barriers to development. The present paper focuses specifically on findings related to research engagement and collaboration with research institutions. A cross-sectional survey was conducted in 2026 following the STROBE guidelines [52] using an anonymous, self-administered, web-based and computer-assisted questionnaire.

The study incorporated elements of Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) [53, 54]. Two RDPAGs were consulted during the development of the questionnaire, which was further pre-tested with the representatives of an additional five RDPAGs differing in organisational size, administrative capacity, and disease focus to ensure clarity and contextual relevance.

Ethical issues

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [55] and received approval from the Bioethics Committee of Poznan University of Medical Sciences (KB-879/25). Before accessing the questionnaire, all eligible participants received information about the study and provided informed electronic consent by confirming their willingness to participate. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were ensured throughout the study.

Participants and setting

RDPAGs were eligible if they operated in the RD field, had a formal structure, and were actively involved in relevant activities. As for respondents, they were expected to be adult representatives of these patient organisations, fluent in Polish, holding leadership or coordinating roles, and able to report on organisational functioning.

A purposive sampling strategy was used to ensure variation across organisational types, complemented by convenience and snowball sampling. The initial sampling frame was based on the publicly available membership list of the National Forum for Rare Disease Therapy ORPHAN, a national umbrella organisation representing 97 RDPAGs in Poland (https://rzadkiechoroby.org/czlonkowie/). Because this registry does not include all Polish RDPAGs, snowball sampling was additionally used to reach eligible organisations beyond the ORPHAN network. Invitations were distributed via email and social media, and participants were encouraged to forward the invitation to representatives of other eligible RDPAGs. The final sample included RDPAGs representing rare, ultra-rare, and hyper-rare conditions and varying in organisational size and scope.

Questionnaire

Questionnaire domains were informed by previous literature describing the roles, activities, collaboration patterns, barriers, and research engagement of patient organisations [33, 34, 39, 47, 56], literature on patient and public involvement in research [53, 54, 57, 58], and findings from a previous qualitative study conducted by the first author [50]. Specifically, previous studies informed items related to organisational characteristics, research engagement, collaboration, governance, and perceived barriers, whereas the qualitative study contributed to the development of questions addressing educational and advocacy activities, intersectoral collaboration, and organisational challenges experienced by Polish RDPAGs. The final questionnaire consisted of structured single- and multiple-choice items covering organisational characteristics, activities, research engagement, collaboration, and barriers.

Although the questionnaire used in the study covered a broad range of issues related to the functioning of RDPAGs, the present analysis focuses on items concerning engagement in scientific research and collaboration with research institutions. These included questions on the extent and forms of research involvement, types of research activities (e.g. clinical, non-clinical, and translational), organisational roles in research processes (e.g. initiation, co-creation, or support of studies), and modes of collaboration with scientists and research institutions (see: Supplementary material). The questionnaire also explored perceived barriers to research engagement, including organisational, financial, and structural constraints, communication challenges, and limited influence over research agendas.

Following pilot testing with the representatives of five RDPAGs, ten items were refined to improve clarity and validity. Pilot data were excluded from the final dataset and not considered in the analysis. The final version was reviewed by representatives of RD patient organisations and an experienced medical sociologist before data collection.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected between January and April 2026 as an online survey. Participants received study information describing the aims, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality protections, and the right to withdraw at any time. Invitations were disseminated via email and through RDPAGs’ websites and social media, which served as a communication channel rather than an independent sampling frame. Three reminders were sent to increase response rates. Only one completed questionnaire from each organisation was retained for analysis.

Data were checked for completeness and consistency and analysed using JASP (Version 0.95.4). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise findings. Associations between selected organisational characteristics and research engagement were examined using the chi-square test. Financial data were collected in PLN and converted to euros (€) for interpretative purposes.

Results

The sample was dominated by relatively young RDPAGs, with 39.1% established within the past five years and 17.2% operating for 6–10 years, while the remaining patient organisations had been active for more than a decade (Table 1). RDPAGs were structured as associations (56.3%) and foundations (43.8%). The majority addressed rare diseases (67.2%), with a substantial proportion also focusing on ultra-rare (37.5%) and hyper-rare conditions (17.2%). Activities were predominantly conducted at the national level (68.8%), although nearly one-third of RDPAGs also operated internationally (29.7%).

While 34.4% of RDPAGs reported more than 50 individuals actively involved, one-quarter operated without a formal membership structure (25%). At the same time, among those with defined teams, organisational capacity was generally limited, with relatively small groups of active individuals predominating.

International collaboration was reported in multiple forms, although 23.4% of RDPAGs indicated no such engagement. Other responses included collaboration with disease-specific international patient organisations (20.3%) and participation in broader international networks (e.g., EURORDIS, Orphanet, ERNs) (76.6%).

Financial capacity appeared limited overall. A substantial proportion of RDPAGs operated with very low budgets, including 9.4% with no budget and 28.1% with annual budgets below €2,320, while only a minority exceeded €46,510 (20.3%).

The data indicate that educational activities for patients, families, healthcare professionals, and the wider public were widely implemented (Table 2), most commonly in the form of educational materials (85.9%), workshops or webinars (78.1%), and individual consultations (70.3%). Media campaigns were also frequently reported (53.1%), whereas activities targeting schools and public administration were less common. The primary target groups were parents and caregivers (90.6%), patients (81.2%), and family members (75%), although a substantial proportion of RDPAGs also addressed the general public and healthcare professionals (60.9 and 59.4%, respectively).

Regarding the frequency of collaboration, physicians were the most frequent collaborators, with 67.2% of RDPAGs reporting regular collaboration and a further 25.0% reporting occasional collaboration. The overall quality of collaboration with physicians was most often assessed as high or very high (59.4% combined). In contrast, collaboration with scientists/researchers was less frequent, with most RDPAGs reporting only occasional collaboration (42.2%). The overall quality of collaboration with scientists/researchers showed considerable variation.

Engagement in scientific research varied across RDPAGs (Table 3), with a majority reporting at least occasional involvement (65.7%), while a smaller proportion reported no engagement (14.1%) or only expressed willingness to participate (20.3%). The most common forms of research support included dissemination of information about studies and research findings (57.8%), support for clinical trial participants (40.6%), and collaboration with research institutions (31.3%), whereas providing financial research support (e.g. grants or funding for research projects) and involvement in research governance were reported less frequently.

Self-assessed levels of involvement were heterogeneous, with the largest groups indicating either no or very low engagement (46.8% combined), followed by moderate involvement (20.3%). High or very high engagement was reported only by a minority (15.6%). The most frequently reported barriers included lack of financial resources (42.2%), limited human resources or competencies (37.5%), and insufficient interest from researchers or clinicians (29.7%). Organisational workload and infrastructural or recruitment constraints were also noted.

Associations between selected organisational characteristics and research engagement of RDPAGs are presented in Table 4. RDPAGs with annual budgets exceeding €11,620 were significantly more likely to report regular collaboration with physicians (85.7% vs. 52.8%, p = 0.01) and scientists or researchers (46.4% vs. 16.7%, p = 0.001) compared with RDPAGs operating with lower budgets. Organisational maturity was also associated with collaboration with physicians, with organisations established for more than 10 years reporting regular collaboration more frequently than younger RDPAGs (85.7% vs. 69.4%, p = 0.04). No statistically significant associations were observed between disease focus (rare vs. ultra-/hyper-rare diseases) and the analysed outcomes, nor between organisational characteristics and the overall extent of organisational engagement in scientific research.

Discussion

This study provides a systematic overview of the roles, capacities, and challenges of intersectoral collaboration of RDPAGs in Poland, with a particular focus on their engagement in research. The findings reveal a complex and uneven pattern of involvement, characterised by substantial activity in education and non-clinical research domains, but more limited and predominantly supportive roles in clinical and translational research. At the same time, organisational engagement is constrained by persistent resource limitations, structural barriers, and asymmetries in collaboration with institutional actors. Taken together, these results point to a gap between the growing expectations placed on RDPAGs and their actual capacity to influence research processes and agendas. Thus, several key themes emerge from this study.

Firstly, the findings highlight the expanding but uneven role of RDPAGs in research in Poland, characterised by strong involvement in non-clinical domains and predominantly supportive rather than leading roles in clinical and translational research. This pattern is reflected in the types of activities most frequently reported, as RDPAGs are most frequently engaged in social research, registries, and dissemination activities, while direct involvement in clinical trial design, initiation, or translational research remains relatively limited. These findings are consistent with previous studies in a variety of countries indicating that patient organisations tend to play more active roles in knowledge dissemination, patient recruitment, and advocacy, rather than in the initiation or leadership of biomedical research [33, 34, 39, 40].

At the same time, the predominance of supportive roles in clinical research reflects structural and institutional constraints that shape opportunities for engagement. Opportunities for participation in advisory structures and collaboration with industry may also depend on the maturity of national RD research ecosystems and the availability of industry-sponsored research initiatives [25, 35, 36]. As highlighted in previous studies, RDPAGs are often positioned as facilitators or intermediaries within research processes, contributing to recruitment, communication, and patient support rather than acting as equal partners in study design or governance [31, 33, 38, 39, 47], reflecting a persistent imbalance in influence and decision-making power. This pattern is also linked to the organisation of clinical research in RDs, which is typically initiated and coordinated by academic institutions or industry sponsors and requires specialised scientific, regulatory, and financial capacities that exceed the resources of most RDPAGs [34, 39]. Moreover, because many RDPAGs represent very small patient populations, particularly in ultra-rare and hyper-rare conditions, opportunities to participate in clinical studies may be further constrained by the limited availability of eligible participants and the methodological challenges inherent to RD research [4, 14].

These findings should not, however, be interpreted as implying that all RDPAGs should engage extensively in scientific research. RD patient organisations differ considerably in their size, organisational maturity, available resources, and strategic priorities, and consequently in their capacity and willingness to engage in research [33, 34, 39, 56]. For many small, volunteer-led RDPAGs, particularly those representing ultra-rare conditions, providing information, peer support, education, and advocacy may appropriately remain their primary mission [33]. Nevertheless, creating opportunities for meaningful research involvement where appropriate is particularly important in the field of RDs, where patient populations are small, experiential knowledge is indispensable, and RDPAGs often constitute the principal link between dispersed patient communities and researchers [47, 57, 58]. Thus, rather than expecting all RDPAGs to engage in research to the same extent, efforts should instead focus on creating institutional conditions that enable those wishing to contribute to participate through equitable partnerships, appropriate organisational support, and capacity-building opportunities [54].

Importantly, the limited involvement of RDPAGs in translational and laboratory research observed in this study reflects broader systemic barriers, including the technical complexity of biomedical research, high entry thresholds, and restricted access to funding and research infrastructures [44, 45]. These constraints are further reinforced by the fragmented nature of RD research systems and the need for coordinated international infrastructures, which remain unevenly accessible to RDPAGs [25]. Additionally, they contribute to a division of labour in which RDPAGs are more active in domains that rely on experiential knowledge and community mobilisation, while remaining underrepresented in areas requiring specialised scientific expertise and institutional resources. Nevertheless, the observed pattern should not be interpreted solely as a limitation. Rather, it reflects a functional differentiation of roles within the RD research ecosystem, where RDPAGs provide unique contributions that complement scientific expertise. Their involvement in non-clinical research, particularly in patient-centred activities such as needs assessment, patient-reported outcomes, and registry development, plays a critical role in shaping more patient-centred and contextually relevant research agendas [32, 47]. However, the findings also suggest that the transition from supportive to more co-creative or leading roles in research remains incomplete, pointing to the need for more inclusive models of collaboration that enable greater participation of RDPAGs across all stages of the research process.

Secondly, the findings point to a persistent mismatch between the scope of the organisational activity of RDPAGs in Poland and the resources available to sustain it, with financial, human, and infrastructural constraints significantly limiting the depth and continuity of engagement. Although many RDPAGs reported extensive involvement in educational activities and participation in research-related processes, this activity is often carried out under conditions of limited capacity. The data indicate that a substantial proportion of RDPAGs operate with very low budgets and small teams, and frequently report barriers related to lack of funding, insufficient staffing, and workload burden. This suggests that organisational engagement is maintained despite, rather than supported by, available resources.

This is in line with previous studies showing that patient organisations, particularly in the RD field, often rely on volunteer labour and informal structures, which constrain their ability to sustain long-term, resource-intensive forms of engagement in research and policy processes [33, 44, 56, 59, 60]. Resource limitations not only affect day-to-day functioning and restrict operational capacity but may also affect the quality and strategic scope of engagement, including the ability to participate in complex research collaborations, particularly in areas requiring stable funding, technical expertise, and institutional infrastructure [31, 35, 45, 47]. The findings also highlight several areas where additional support may be beneficial, including communication training, improved access to expert knowledge, and dedicated funding for educational activities, which may strengthen RDPAGs’ capacity and enhance the sustainability of their educational and communication activities.

At the same time, the findings reveal an important tension: despite these constraints, RDPAGs report relatively high levels of collaboration with physicians, often described as regular and positively evaluated. This suggests that intersectoral collaboration may function as a compensatory mechanism, enabling RDPAGs to extend their reach and influence beyond their internal capacities [34, 36]. This has also been observed in prior studies, in which collaboration with clinicians and researchers partially offsets organisational limitations but often relies on informal networks and individual engagement rather than stable institutional frameworks [41, 59]. However, such collaboration may also mask underlying inequalities, as it is often based on informal networks and relationships and individual commitment rather than institutionalised support structures, which may reinforce dependency and limit organisational autonomy [35, 46]. As a result, the sustainability of these collaborations remains uncertain and potentially vulnerable to changes in resources, personnel, or system-level conditions. Thus, this imbalance reflects a broader structural dynamic in which expectations towards RDPAGs continue to expand, while the resources and institutional support required to sustain their involvement remain limited [44, 45, 61].

Thirdly, the results reveal the presence of both structural and relational barriers in facilitating collaboration with clinicians and researchers, including unclear frameworks for cooperation, limited recognition of RDPAG organisational roles, and asymmetries in influence over research processes. The data indicate that RDPAGs frequently report a lack of clearly defined pathways for collaboration, limited opportunities to influence research priorities, and difficulties in establishing partnerships on equal terms. These challenges are compounded by system-level constraints, such as limited time availability among clinicians, high workload, and organisational pressures within healthcare institutions, which restrict opportunities for sustained engagement. This is consistent with previous studies highlighting that collaboration between patient organisations and institutional actors is often fragmented and insufficiently formalised, relying on ad hoc arrangements rather than structured mechanisms [34, 36, 41, 46]. In such contexts, RDPAGs are frequently positioned as peripheral contributors, valued for access to patients and experiential knowledge, but with limited influence on decision-making processes and research governance [35, 38, 39]. These patterns can be understood through the lens of persistent epistemic and institutional asymmetries, in which biomedical expertise is privileged over experiential knowledge, limiting the extent to which patient organisations can act as equal partners in research processes [62, 63]. The reported asymmetries in collaboration thus reflect broader power imbalances embedded in research systems, where participation of patient organisations is often encouraged but remains structurally constrained.

This tension also aligns with criticism of “tokenistic” or symbolic forms of patient involvement, where engagement is formally present but does not translate into substantive influence over research agendas or decision-making [61, 64, 65]. Studies emphasise that, despite increasing recognition of patient involvement, meaningful participation remains uneven and often limited by institutional norms, professional hierarchies, and the absence of robust mechanisms for integrating patient perspectives into governance structures [57, 66]. In addition, the barriers identified in this study, including lack of time, unclear roles, and limited recognition, highlight the relational dimension of collaboration, where trust, communication, and mutual understanding remain critical but underdeveloped elements of effective partnerships [32, 40, 59]. These constraints suggest that improving collaboration requires not only formal frameworks and resources, but also cultural and organisational changes that support more balanced and inclusive forms of engagement between RDPAGs and institutional actors.

Finally, the findings indicate that the current configuration of roles, resources, and collaboration mechanisms for RDPAGs results in a systemic underutilisation of the RDPAGs as partners in research, reflecting broader systemic inefficiencies and the incomplete integration of experiential knowledge into research governance. The data indicate that a majority of RDPAGs do not initiate research activities, while only a small proportion report high or very high levels of involvement, despite a notable number expressing willingness to engage more actively. At the same time, RDPAGs most frequently report supportive roles, such as dissemination of research findings, recruitment support, or participation in surveys, rather than leadership or co-creation of research. This pattern points to a gap between the readiness and motivation of RDPAGs and the opportunities available for meaningful participation. This supports previous studies showing that, although patient involvement in research is increasingly promoted, its implementation often remains partial and uneven, with limited opportunities for RD patient organisations to shape research agendas or assume more strategic roles [34, 35, 61]. In many cases, patient organisations are engaged at later stages of the research process, particularly in recruitment or dissemination, rather than in priority setting, study design, or governance [40, 41, 57, 58, 67].

The persistence of predominantly supportive roles, combined with reported barriers such as lack of funding, limited organisational capacity, and restricted influence, suggests that underutilisation of RDPAGs is not simply a matter of their organisational readiness but reflects systemic constraints within research ecosystems [27, 44, 45]. Thus, this can be understood as a form of partial or constrained co-production, in which RDPAGs are formally included in research processes but remain limited in their ability to influence core decisions and knowledge production [62, 68]. Rather than relying on the exceptional commitment or negotiating capacity of individual patient organisations to gain recognition, more sustainable models of patient involvement require formal mechanisms that actively invite and support RDPAGs throughout the research process. Meaningful co-production should therefore be enabled through institutionalised partnerships rather than depend on the ability of RDPAGs to secure participation through their own initiative [37, 62, 64, 65, 68].

This pattern is further reinforced by the fragmentation of RD research systems and the uneven development of collaborative infrastructures across Europe, which constrain opportunities for sustained and meaningful involvement of RDPAGs [25]. In this context, there is a need to move beyond symbolic inclusion towards more structured and meaningful integration of RD patient organisations across all stages of the research cycle, including agenda setting, design, implementation, and evaluation [32, 46, 47]. Strengthening such involvement requires not only increased resources and capacity-building, including RDPAGs, but also changes such that researchers and other recognise experiential knowledge as a legitimate and valuable component of scientific research.

Beyond informing research policy, the findings may also have practical value for RDPAGs themselves. By identifying common patterns of research engagement, organisational strengths, and shared barriers, the study provides an evidence base that may support stronger collaboration among Polish RDPAGs, facilitate the development of national networks and joint research initiatives, and help patient organisations benchmark their activities against those of other groups [33, 50]. The findings may also assist RDPAGs in identifying areas for organisational development, prioritising capacity-building efforts, and advocating for greater recognition and institutional support within the Polish rare disease ecosystem. Together with previous qualitative findings [50], the present study contributes to a broader understanding of the evolving role of RDPAGs in Poland and provides an empirical foundation for future research, policy, and organisational development.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged while interpreting the findings. First, although 64 RD patient organisations participated in the survey, the Polish RDPAG landscape is much larger; the results do not capture the full population and limit generalisability. Second, because purposive, convenience, and snowball sampling were used to recruit eligible RDPAGs, combined with open dissemination via publicly available networks, the response rate could not be determined. Third, the voluntary, online survey design introduces potential self-selection bias, likely favouring more active and digitally connected RDPAGs, while smaller or less formalised groups may be underrepresented. Fourth, data were based on a single respondent per RDPAG, typically in a leadership role, which may introduce a managerial perspective bias and does not reflect the diversity of member experiences. Fifth, the use of self-reported data may involve reporting bias, including social desirability and potential overestimation of engagement in research activities. Sixth, the cross-sectional design captures organisational practices at a single point in time, and predefined response categories may limit the depth of insights compared to qualitative approaches.

Despite these limitations, the study offers several strengths. It provides the first systematic quantitative assessment of RDPAG engagement in research in Poland and contributes to a still-limited evidence base, drawing on a relatively large organisational sample. The structured design enables comparability across RDPAGs and identifies broader patterns in research collaboration, organisational roles, and perceived barriers. Importantly, the study captures organisational leaders’ perspectives on the extent and forms of RDPAG engagement in research, collaboration with researchers, and the barriers affecting such involvement. Furthermore, the questionnaire was informed by previous literature, findings from a qualitative study, and Patient and Public Involvement (PPI), and was further refined through pilot testing with representatives of RDPAGs, enhancing its contextual relevance and face validity. Overall, the findings provide novel empirical evidence that may inform future research and policy aimed at strengthening RDPAGs’ involvement in scientific research.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that RDPAGs in Poland have become important actors in the research ecosystem, contributing to patient recruitment, dissemination, needs assessment, registry development, and collaboration with researchers. However, their involvement remains uneven, with activities concentrated in non-clinical domains and predominantly in supportive roles in clinical and translational research. The findings further reveal a persistent mismatch between the growing expectations placed on RDPAGs and their limited organisational resources, alongside structural and relational barriers restricting their influence on research agendas. Together, these findings indicate that the current model of RDPAG engagement reflects a form of constrained co-production, in which RD patient organisations are formally included but remain insufficiently integrated into decision-making and knowledge production. These findings provide new quantitative evidence on the current scope of RDPAGs’ research engagement. They also suggest that stronger integration of RDPAGs into research processes may help strengthen meaningful involvement.

Based on the findings of the present study and the existing literature, the following recommendations may help strengthen meaningful involvement of RDPAGs in research:

Researchers, clinicians, and healthcare institutions should establish formal and standardised collaboration frameworks with RDPAGs, with clearly defined roles and pathways for involvement across all stages of research.Funding agencies and policymakers should provide stable funding mechanisms, training opportunities, and organisational support to strengthen RDPAGs’ capacity for research engagement.Researchers and research institutions should involve RDPAGs earlier in the research process, including priority setting, study design, governance, implementation, and dissemination.National policymakers, research funders, and international research networks should strengthen research infrastructures (e.g. patient registries, collaborative networks, and data platforms) to reduce fragmentation and facilitate long-term collaboration between researchers and RDPAGs.
