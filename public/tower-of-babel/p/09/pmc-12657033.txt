Support systems impacting the mental health of diverse women in biomedical science programs: An ecological approach
Authors: Ida Salusky, Robin Remich, Lidia Monjaras-Gaytan, Cilka Mayumi Hijara, Richard McGee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomedical doctoral trainees experience high rates of mental health distress compared to the general population. Minoritized students are particularly vulnerable to psychological stress during training due to systemic factors including discrimination, bullying, financial constraints, high workloads and constant critical feedback. As such, the mental health of minoritized biomedical trainees is both a social justice and health equity issue. We examined longitudinal narratives of 33 Asian, Black and Latiné women in biomedical doctoral programs to understand how they constructed support systems to manage psychological distress induced by training environments and how institutional agents can effectively support student psychological wellness. By examining the experiences of graduate students over time, we found that students expend time and energy to create support systems using a variety of resources. Using Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Model, the findings show that constructing support can present challenges, particularly when norms and structures of biomedical training conflict with practices to foster good health. We present three themes: 1) students find authentic support through family, friends, and pre-PhD mentors; 2) students seek support from health professionals, though not all services were readily available or perceived as a good fit; and 3) fewer than half of participants experienced support for their mental health from PhD mentors (PIs of their lab).Keywords: Biomedical science, Higher education, Ecological theory, Gender, Minoritized students, Mental health, STEM

Introduction

Minority biomedical doctoral students, particularly those from socially, culturally and economically diverse (SCED) backgrounds (e.g., women, Black, Latiné, Indigenous, LGBTQIA, gender expansive; Toldson, 2023), face persistent challenges in training that compromise their mental health and educational trajectories. Research documents these students are at particular risk for depression and anxiety because of stressors related to discrimination while in high-pressure training environments (de-identified; Bravo et al., 2023; McGee et al., 2021; Miles et al., 2020). Their experiences illustrate how inequities embedded in doctoral education can shape mental health, retention, and career pathways. Poor mental health among biomedical science trainees represents a critical public health and social issue because of the loss of innovation and discovery due to diminished productivity, unfinished degrees, and biomedical graduates leaving the field (Satinsky et al., 2021; Sverdlik et al., 2018). Yet, within U.S. doctoral training programs a mental health crisis exists (Berry et al., 2020; Evans et al., 2018; Satinsky et al., 2021). Doctoral students experience psychological distress at rates significantly higher than the general population, and this is particularly true for women of color (Evans et al., 2018; Satinsky et al., 2021; Wilkins-Yel et al., 2024). This study focuses on Asian, Black, and Latiné women as a group of biomedical PhD students disproportionately affected by systemic inequities in higher education and its impact on mental health. Addressing structural factors leading to poor mental health in PhD training requires an ecological approach that accounts for individual, relational, institutional, and societal contexts.

Theoretical framework

Ecological theory provides a useful framework to understand how nested interactions and systems at multiple levels impact the mental health of SCED students. Specifically, Bronfenbrenner and Morris (2007) offer a model of human development to understand how settings influence individual lives. This model focuses on the integrated role of both person and environment in psychological outcomes. Applying this framework to minoritized women in biomedical PhD training makes visible the interplay of individual experiences (e.g., coping), immediate contexts (e.g., labs, mentoring relationships) institutional practices (e.g., funding, training norms), and broader societal/cultural systems (e.g., academic values). Ecological theory informs our research questions by focusing attention on how support and mental health distress emerge and function across multiple levels of the doctoral training environment.

Levels of analysis

The first level of analysis within Bronfenbrenner’s model is the microsystem, consisting of interactions directly involving an individual. For example, student interactions with their PI constitute a microsystem. Second, the mesosystem consists of interactions between two microsystemic factors. For example, a junior faculty experiencing pressure to meet department tenure requirements may pressure students to work longer hours, thus sacrificing social relationships and personal health. The student is impacted by the interaction of this mesosystem (department and PI). At the third level, the exosystem involves formal and informal contexts which do not directly involve students but impact them. Admissions and hiring practices that result in a predominantly white student and faculty populations is an exosystem factor. A minoritized biomedical student doesn’t have direct involvement in these policies but may experience negative consequences by not seeing themselves represented by peers and faculty on campus. At the fourth level, the macrosystem involves cultural values and norms. A macrosystemic conflict can occur when the ethos within U.S. academia (e.g., normalizing long work hours) runs counter to a student’s familial norms like involvement with family life and biological need for rest. This cultural norm is an artifact of the founding principles of most predominantly white institutions (PWIs) in the U.S.: to educate white, elite men in the values of western hegemony (Wilder, 2013). The fifth level, the chronosystem involves the influence of time and its impact on decisions, opportunities, and events across the life, training, and career course of a biomedical scientist.

Literature review

Existing research identifies elevated rates of mental health distress among graduate students when compared to the general population (Bergvall et al., 2024; Council of Graduate Schools, 2021; Jackman et al., 2022; Schwaller, 2024). Importantly, mental health can impact the ability to perform academically and therefore reduce program retention and completion rates. For example, depression in graduate student populations predicts GPA and the probability of leaving a training program without completing the terminal degree (Bergvall et al., 2024; Satinsky et al., 2021; Sverdlik et al., 2018). Extensive research has identified factors that contribute to poor mental health among doctoral trainees including: high workloads, constant critical feedback, self-doubt around competency, faculty and peer bullying, competitive training environments, lack of work-life balance, limited financial support, and discrimination based on identity (e.g. gender, sexual identity, race, etc.; Bravo et al., 2023; Jackman et al., 2022; Sverdlik et al., 2018).

One issue within research on doctoral student mental health involves the lack of definition operationalization. Some studies use clinical measures and clinical cutoff points to define psychological distress, while others provide no definition or operationalization of psychological distress or well-being (Berry et al., 2020; Lubega et al., 2023; Nagy et al., 2019; Wilkins-Yel et al., 2024). We use the American Psychological Association’s definition of positive mental health:

A state of mind characterized by emotional well-being, good behavioral adjustment, relative freedom from anxiety and disabling symptoms and a capacity to establish constructive relationships and cope with the ordinary demands and stresses of life (American Psychological Association, 2025).

Poor mental health, or psychological distress, is the absence of any or all these characteristics. Many factors influence mental health during doctoral training, including biomedical training norms, mentorship, and discrimination.

Biomedical training norms

Biomedical PhD programs provide research training in the basic sciences, such as microbiology, neuroscience, and immunology. Historically, biomedical PhD programs have prepared students to move to postdoctoral training and then to a tenure-track faculty position. However, over the past two decades most programs and National Institutes of Health (NIH) training grants embrace the reality of students seeking positions outside academia. Training in the PhD resembles an apprenticeship model where students gain skills by doing research in a lab with mentorship from a faculty member, referred to as the Principal Investigator (PI). While students do take courses – usually in the first two years – they spend the majority of their time in a lab, often working beyond 40 hours a week (van Tienoven et al., 2023).

Student experiences vary widely from lab to lab because PIs set standards, hierarchy, and culture for their labs. Therefore, PIs can open or limit access to one-on-one training, project possibilities, and access to a collegial network. Pressure for research productivity, funding agency requirements, and progress toward tenure impact PI time and availability for engaged training and mentoring. Biomedical research often necessitates collaboration. Yet, laboratories can be highly competitive, resulting in trainees vying for scarce resources (Berry et al., 2020; Salusky et al., 2005; Nicholls et al., 2022). A good deal of power rests with PIs whose decisions affect many aspects of the training experience from duration of the training period (5–10 years among our research participants), time for vacation and self-care, to how interpersonal conflict is handled within the lab (de-identified).

Mentorship

Literature on mentoring, encompassing both formal and informal relationships, within higher education consistently highlights countless benefits mentors offer to students (Crisp and Cruz, 2009; Womack et al., 2020). Mentors provide students with emotional support, and multiple additional forms of assistance and guidance (Crisp et al., 2017; Byars-Winston et al., 2018). In doctoral training, mentoring plays an important role in the success of students, especially as they adjust to fundamental differences from undergraduate studies (Ives and Rowley, 2005). Mentors may provide tangible support with academic milestones including thesis topic, qualifying exams, grant writing, and dissertation committee meetings. Access to the PI’s social capital (e.g., professional networks) can support students’ goals. Due to the high stress of doctoral programs, mentors can provide emotional support to students as they navigate rigorous academic demands, isolation, and self-doubt (Welde and Laursen, 2008). These mentoring practices promote good student mental health, while poor mentorship can have profound effects on mentees’ psychological well-being (e.g., decrease in self-efficacy), academic success (e.g., lack of progression), and professional development (Tuma et al., 2021).

Biomedical doctoral students establish a formal mentor, the PI who heads a lab and supervises their thesis research. Typically, the student-PI pairing results from a series of rotations in the first year when students try out labs, and PIs assess student-lab fit. Henceforth, we will refer to this formal mentor as the “PI.” However, the PI does not invariably assume a multi-faceted and sufficient mentoring role throughout a student’s doctoral education, and other faculty may serve as additional mentors.

Discrimination

Racial.

A wealth of research has described discrimination in the context of doctoral training programs, including biomedical PhD training programs. Importantly, a clear connection exists between racialized stress, discrimination and mental health distress for students of color within higher education (de-identified; McGee et al., 2021; Miles et al., 2020). Biomedical science education broadly lacks diversity across gender, ethnicity, economics, and sexual identity (NCES, 2023). For example, women hold less than 40 % of full-time faculty positions in biomedicine, and fewer than 9 % of all doctoral recipients identify as Black/African-American, Latiné, Pacific Islander or Indigenous (NSF, 2019). Consequently, minoritized students experience othering within doctoral programs (Salusky et al., 2005; Gardner, 2013; McGee et al., 2021; Miles et al., 2020). Previous research has found that biomedical faculty, who are disproportionately white and male, lack cultural humility skills to effectively mentor female and Black students (Nicholls et al., 2022). Microaggressions for minoritized biomedical students represent a common and chronic reality; resulting in feelings of invisibility, sense of self-doubt, imposter syndrome, and psychological distress (McGee et al., 2021; Miles et al., 2020). This lack of community, combined with biomedical training norms can engender social isolation and lack of belonging, which is strongly associated with poor mental health (Nicholls et al., 2022). Relatedly, social stigma around mental health can create barriers to help-seeking (Schnyder et al., 2017), and even when services are sought, minoritized students often struggle to find culturally congruent services and multi-culturally skilled providers (Chu et al., 2022). Taken together, mental health in biomedical doctoral training develops and manifests in relation to systemic forces, yet most research focuses on individual characteristics.

Gender.

Women in biomedical PhD programs face unique stressors that create risk for mental health distress (Carter et al., 2013; Hayes et al., 2021; Powless et al., 2024; Remich et al., 2016; Salusky et al., 2005; Wilkins-Yel et al., 2024). Biomedical training culture centers white male heteronormative values that prize work productivity above all, deprioritizes caregiving and self-care, and rewards individual competitiveness (Powless et al., 2024; Salusky et al., 2005; Wilkins-Yel et al., 2024). Previous research highlights how gender discrimination plays out in labs, by PIs, and program faculty. For example, Salusky and colleagues (2025) found training cultures where male PIs told female students that it is a waste of time and effort to provide mentorship and training if female students subsequently marry and have children. Previous research also illustrates the pervasive and quotidian nature of gendered microaggressions within training programs, including jokes about female students intelligence, sexuality and science competency (Carter et al., 2013; Powless et al., 2024; Salusky et al., 2005) Multiple studies document the widespread belief among female PhD students that they underperform and fail in both their academic and professional lives because of the expectation to spend long hours in the lab, which creates a binary choice to either spend little time for family and friends, or to not meet a PI’s and training program expectations (Carter et al., 2013; Powless et al., 2024; Salusky et al., 2005; Wilkins-Yel et al., 2024). Unsurprisingly, women experience a high burden of mental health distress and diagnosed psychopathology during training (Evans et al., 2018; de-identified; Lipson et al., 2019). Taken together, discrimination within biomedical PhD training programs on the basis of gendered race poses a significant and serious threat to the mental health of minoritized women trainees.

Rationale

Most research exploring ecological theory focuses on microsystems (e.g., student relationships with PI, lab mates, peers, etc.) rather than the range of ecologies impacting student well-being within biomedical doctoral training (Salusky et al., 2025). Because of this emphasis on the individual, most recommendations and interventions geared towards graduate mental health focus on individual-level behavior change (i.e., the student) rather than systems level change (Hall, 2024; Jackman et al., 2022). Mental health is shaped by holistic environments, not fragmented components of an ecology. Here we apply multiple levels of analysis to explore the following two questions. How do socially, culturally and economically diverse women in U.S. biomedical PhD programs construct support systems to manage psychological distress induced by the training environment? How do institutional agents effectively support student psychological well-being of socially, culturally and economically diverse women trainees experiencing mental health distress?

Method

Data sources

As described in (Salusky et al., 2025) this research is drawn from two longitudinal studies (Thakore et al., 2014 and Wood et al., 2024). They aim to investigate the training experiences and career decision-making of diverse graduate students in biomedical PhD training in the U.S. Between 2008 and 2011, participants were recruited from undergraduate and post baccalaureate research training programs or upon entering PhD programs. One-on-one interviews were conducted annually until participants dropped out of the study, left their PhD programs without the terminal degree, or obtained a PhD and entered a non-academic career (see supplemental file 1). While the study team attempted to interview participants annually, some participants did not complete all annual interviews (see supplemental file 1).

The study team developed semi-structured interview protocols to gather information about students’ backgrounds prior to the PhD; experiences in graduate school; interactions with mentors and advisors; and reflections on challenges, support, and sense of self as a scientist. Specific questions facilitated reflection of experiences related to mental health and psychological well-being. For example: “were there challenging or stressful times during this past year?”, “in the past year, did you feel your gender, race or ethnicity influenced how you were treated or how others viewed or responded to you?” and “has anything caused you stress in your career and life during the last year?” Interviewers probed for context around participants’ experiences with social identities (e.g., race, gender, sexual identity, social class) in training. Participants could opt out of answering any questions and discontinue participation at any time.

All interviews were professionally transcribed and then checked for accuracy. Interview data were coded using a universal codebook. The universal codebook included broad categories of descriptive codes. Both studies received institutional review board approval from Northwestern University.

Participants

We identified participants in the present study from the two longitudinal studies detailed above. As described in Salusky et al. (2025) the first author used a keyword search strategy to initially identify women participants from these studies who discussed any experiences with health, including mental health distress (see supplemental file 1). The first and second authors used a common working definition of mental health distress that includes self-reported disruptions to aspects of life including relationships, work, school, self-worth and basic daily functioning (e.g., sleep, appetite, etc.). In a couple of cases the authors diverged on whether a participant met inclusion criteria. In both cases the interview data was re-read and discussed until we reached consensus about inclusion. The first and second authors closely read potentially relevant transcript excerpts, resulting in the final set of 33 participants1. Interviews occurred between 2009 and 2022. Significant events with potential to impact participants’ experiences occurred during this time including the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2016 election, several high-profile cases of police brutality, and the rise of political and social movements such as Black Lives Matter and #MeToo. Analysis included an average of seven transcripts per participant (see supplemental file 1).

Twenty-five participants attained their PhD, and eight left graduate school without their PhD. The majority of participants identified as Black, followed by Latiné and Asian American (see Table 1). Eleven participants were first-generation college students (neither parent attained a bachelor’s degree), and four participants had at least one caregiver who had earned a PhD. Ten grew-up in households earning less than $45,000 annually, and three from households earning more than $125,000 annually.

Of the 25 who completed the PhD, four maintained an academic career intention as of 2024. The remaining 21 cited a number of factors in their decision not to pursue an academic career. At least half of this group cited high levels of both general and racialized stress, poor work-life balance, and unhealthy work environments as deterrents to pursuing academic careers.

Six of the 33 experienced mental health challenges prior to graduate school. These participants most often described past episodes of depression and/or clinical anxiety. This suggests there is no selection bias or pre-disposition for participants with clinical mental health issues before entering PhD programs.

Analysis

Analysis for the current study was guided by a constructivist paradigm (Denzin and Lincoln, 2008). We focused on understanding participants’ subjective experiences within systems with the understanding that our own worldviews and experiences informed the lens through which we interpreted participants’ words. We used an inductive strategy to develop codes for the current study (see de-identified for full procedures) which yielded 11 discrete codes (e.g., external coping mechanisms and support; see supplemental file 2). Ida Salusky and Robin Remich then engaged in consensus coding, independently coding the same transcripts and comparing and reconciling coding. Once both authors’ coding synced without discrepancies, they divided the remaining transcripts and coded independently. As described in Salusky et al. (2025) comprehensive case studies of participants provided another coded data source (see supplemental file 1). NVivo software was used for all coding.

After coding, the authors repeatedly read the coded data to develop themes. After the first two authors created themes, Lidia Monjaras-Gaytan joined to engage in multiple rounds of reading the coded data to explore themes. At this juncture, they used Excel Spreadsheets to record excerpts and summaries to identify 1) paths of meaning, 2) conflicts in participants’ perspectives, and 3) reciprocity between concepts (Larkin et al., 2019). Four authors reviewed and refined final analysis. We explored universal or near universal experiences within our sample. We also identified disconfirming cases and divergent experiences. Almost all participants discussed ongoing experiences that resulted in chronic stress, though a few referred to situational and short-lived experiences.

Bronfenbrenner’s ecological theory informed our analytical process— guiding us to look beyond individual coping strategies and to situate experiences within broader ecological contexts (e.g., lab practices, institutional norms, structural inequities), and to identify contributing factors from within biomedical training, lab, and institutional cultures. Second, it highlighted the cross-level dynamics such as how departmental tenure policies and broader academic productivity norms influenced immediate lab environments. Finally, ecological theory pushed us away from binary explanations and solutions of students’ mental health and well-being. For example, if a participant described her PI was personally supportive for her mental health but also encouraged long hours in the lab, we interpreted that pressures at the meso- and macrosystem levels interacted with the microsystem of the lab, ultimately impacting the students’ mental health. In this way, the ecological framework helped us move from describing individual experiences to analyzing how nested systems of biomedical training shaped those experiences.

Credibility, rigor and trustworthiness

The authors used multiple strategies to ensure credibility, rigor and trustworthiness within our data analysis. We engaged in constant comparison, examining data individually and then coming together to interrogate and reconcile different interpretations. We conducted member checking with five participants from whom we substantially quote in the study, inviting feedback on whether our analysis felt true to their experiences. Finally, we triangulated our data across sources (i.e. case studies, interview data and member checking).

Positionality

The authors embody a range of identities and experiences explored in the current study. We are a diverse group disciplinarily, racially, culturally, socially and economically. Four identify as women and one as a man. Two authors are biracial, one identifies as a Mexican immigrant, and two identify as white. All had experience as students in doctoral programs, and two left programs without the terminal degree. Additionally, two authors have served as teaching and research faculty within PhD programs. Some had direct experiences or witnessed peers’ negative experiences during training at the intersections of race, gender, and mental health. Most come to this work with the perspective that higher education systems and doctoral training programs are often inhospitable to minoritized students and lack intentional efforts to foster good mental health, especially for those at the margins. This range of perspectives and experiences allowed for dialogue on differing interpretations of the data and robust discussion of personal biases.

Results

Students find authentic support through family, friends and pre-doctoral mentors

The traditional design of graduate biomedical training uses the apprenticeship model with a student’s PI as their mentor. However, students in our population sought support from many others; including family, friends, peers, and faculty they met prior to the PhD. These sources provided necessary support not received within institutions and highlight the value of connecting with people who share one or more salient social identity (e.g., race, ethnicity, gender). Using Bronfenbrenner’s model highlights the interplay of systems that bolster good mental health, though they sometimes created conflict for trainees.

Family provide a foundational support system

Home and family were sources of support at the micro-level where participants authentically shared frustrations and feelings. Although family might not fully understand graduate experiences, they listened. Reflecting on her entire PhD, Miriam Anna described her sister as her “go-to person” who “calms me down. I can express everything… the listening helps.” Several students described monthly or bi-monthly in-person visits to family including Zeya who drove several hours for bi-monthly visits with her mother. Nikki went home to be “cared for” by her mom when stress was overwhelming; and Abby got encouragement from family when “in the day-to-day I don’t feel super-duper smart.” Some further away from home made regular calls, including Maricela who phoned her mother daily. Belinda noted the value of friends and family because “it was great to talk to [people] who were non-science.” Jaclyn’s discussed how talking with family provided a microsystem of support and a necessary outlet to express herself because she was reticent to show weakness in her training environment that prized confidence over vulnerability.

I’m a PhD student. I’m supposed to do everything right. I held a lot of hurt and anger inside [re: failing her qualifying exam], and I didn’t have an outlet to express that. But I’ve been blessed with really good friends and family. Every time I feel in the pits, I’m able to talk to them.

In some instances, visiting family was not supported by PIs, resulting in a meso-level conflict between the student’s lab and her family. Raquel, whose program was in the Northeast, experienced a “really sad moment” during her first semester, when her first rotation PI questioned her going home to Puerto Rico at Christmastime.

[The PI] at first gives me permission, then she changed her mind. That was the stressful part because I really wanted to go to visit my family… That was the really sad moment of the year, but … she changed her mind. So I [got to] recuperate [from] all the work in the lab.

Tamara, with a strong “cultural connection” to her Black heritage, experienced “bias” from her PI and committee who did not understand her strong family centeredness, nor could they comprehend using vacation time to visit family.

I consider vacation visiting my family, and there are comments consistently about how ‘that’s not vacation. I would never want to spend the holidays with my family’ … it’s just rude and insensitive … When you’re the only person in a room that comes from your background it starts to be really odd that you have a different experience …

Lack of understanding and microaggressions about the need for time and support from family increased students’ stress and social isolation, contributing to poor mental health.

In addition to in-person contact, some found support via family and societal messages. For example, from an early age Kathleen learned to persist in the face of challenges and to always prove her competence: “being Black, as a kid, you get taught how to be resilient [to] just keep going. You learn to base everything on numbers and let your performance speak for myself.” Mekeda, who immigrated from Africa after high school, often spoke with her aunt who narrated that suffering is part of an immigrant’s path to success. When experiencing racial microaggressions within her lab, Yvonne was inspired by her aunt who was a “first” and prominent Black woman in her field. Yvonne also heeded messages from family to just “focus on herself” and to “kill them with kindness.” While women found support and inspiration in these messages, strategies to work harder and endure suffering may unintentionally align with training norms potentially detrimental to mental health. These interactions with family show a mesosystemic relationship between graduate students and their families that supported mental health yet could exacerbate isolation and conflict within training.

Peers with similar identities provide community and validation

Several participants experienced unique support at the micro-level from a person or group who shared their racial/ethnic and/or gender identities, often outside of their program. While this support occasionally involved sharing resources, like skills and opportunities, more often participants described feeling community and connectedness which reduced isolation. Abby explained, “My friend, who is also a Black woman in science, [is] pretty much feeling the same way that I feel… We talk, we commiserate… [we] get it out and move on.” Miriam Anna described how she could identify with another Hispanic woman in science: “I resonate so much with her because she’s the person that talks to me about her fears, about her insecurities, and yet she’s very successful.”

Participants also developed a strong sense of community through affinity groups and spaces. These communities helped participants understand difficult experiences germane to their identity and also validated their feelings. Jaclyn was relieved to find a group her second year: “there are issues that are particular to minorities, specifically, isolation or feeling scrutinized … until you find a group, you think you’re the only one going through this, and you’re not.” Similarly, Monica found a comfortable space to talk about race in the Black graduate student association. Tiffany, who was in a cohort of 28 students, felt stress from gender bias and formed a “girls’ support group” with the two other women in her program. Exosystemic factors within most PWIs result in spaces where Asian, Black and Latiné women do not see themselves represented in faculty and student bodies, particularly in STEM. Consequently, developing relationships with peers who share salient identities can serve as a critical support system while navigating majority white spaces.

Pre-PhD mentors provide support ongoing mentorship

Our data show how women sustained support over time, a chrono-level phenomenon, highlighting the impact of undergraduate faculty and initiatives at the pre-PhD level for underrepresented populations. Trainees sought support from faculty relationships developed prior to the PhD to cope with mentoring and environmental challenges.

Some developed relationships via participating in initiatives right before starting their PhD. Alejandra participated in a national program for beginning PhD students where she was paired with an experienced academic scientist “coach” and developed friendships with students from across the country. This support was especially important: she valued having peers going through similar challenges in their first semester, and she perceived the coach and peers as safe because they were apart from her program. “I’m a very social person … so the support from others is great … [they] understand you, but it’s not on this campus, so I can fully express myself.”

Others turned to faculty from undergraduate and preparatory programs. The strength of these early resources withstood time and their affirmation of belonging was often enhanced by shared identities. At the end of her PhD, Tanisha credited her persistence to her undergraduate research experiences from an Historically Black College (HBCU):

If it were not for those years as an undergraduate researcher I wouldn’t have done well. That’s why I’m an advocate for HBCUs for certain students because the levels of resiliency that’s required to get through that PhD, I didn’t get that at [PhD institution]. I would have quit had it not been for the foundation that I had from [undergraduate program and mentors].

Tamara reflected that attending a women’s college “allowed me to create my voice” which gave her courage to advocate for herself in the PhD. Camille reached out to an undergraduate female science professor who “helped me feel sane” when, at the 10th year of her PhD, she described being “held hostage” by her PI whose demands for additional experiments inhibited progression to graduation.

Nikki participated in an undergraduate program for underrepresented students in STEM that provided crucial support throughout her PhD. A primary reason she chose her PhD university was knowing that older students from the program would be a “family network.” To ease her transition and loneliness at the start of the PhD, she was comforted by this “huge support system… [of people who’ve] been through a lot of the same things together.” Midway in her PhD Nikki was scared to ask her PI for help because of some “awful” interactions. She turned to her undergraduate mentor who explained that a PI’s responsibility is to train students, which empowered Nikki to articulate her needs to her PI. Near the end of her PhD when Nikki’s PI told her she was not ready to apply for postdocs, Nikki reached out to “a lot of really good mentors in my life.” They encouraged her to apply for postdoctoral positions which resulted in her committee granting permission to write and defend. These examples show that going to outside and earlier mentors was essential for these women to navigate PIs whose demands and behaviors inhibited their progress.

Students seek support from health professionals

Participants saw medical and psychological health professionals to address mental health symptoms. These two modes of healthcare are not mutually exclusive, but they approach mental health symptoms differently. While helpful, there were also problems with healthcare systems, and in some cases, access to culturally congruent services.

Medical help-seeking

Almost half of participants (n = 14) sought help from medical professionals for mental health needs during training, including psychiatrists. Importantly, twelve began using medication to manage symptoms, most frequently for anxiety and depression. Medications helped participants cope with micro-level (e.g., PI expectations), macro-level (e.g., training norms that emphasize long work hours), and exo-level (e.g., isolation from lack of representation on campus) pressures. For example, after starting anti-anxiety medication, Chelsea felt less guilt and stress but still worked long hours:

I’ve started taking medication for generalized anxiety disorder and actually that has helped a lot. I don’t feel bad about not spending as much time in lab. I’ve been on a little bit more of a regular schedule where I’m probably coming in 8–10 hours a day and doing a little bit less work on the weekends… there’s a little bit more of a balance.

Medication seemed to have helped Courtney manage messaging she gleaned from macro and mesosystems that “good” and “successful” doctoral students spend more than full-time hours in the lab at the expense of personal balance. Similarly, Jalil described taking anti-anxiety medication to cope with sacrifices affecting her mental health. “I have to be on anti-anxiety medication because I can’t sleep. I don’t have friends because I don’t socialize. I sacrifice a lot of stuff for what I want [the PhD].”

Several participants experienced significant physical illness or disability while in graduate school, which negatively impacted mental health. They used psychotropic medication and/or non-psychotropic interventions (e.g., physical therapy). Non-psychotropic interventions require time, potentially creating conflict with training. Nikki discussed treating pain with regular rehab and rest which caused a micro-level conflict with her PI’s expectations of her presence in the lab.

… my pain has been a problem for so long and my [PI] doesn’t like this. If I’m in lab, and I’m in a lot of pain, I’m just like ‘well, this gel is going to get run tomorrow. I’m going home, and I’m going to lay down now.’ That is a really hard thing for me to do because I need to do things all the time, and I need to feel productive.

Tellingly, Nikki does not equate taking care of her health with “productivity” because her PI reinforced norms that prioritize lab work over physical well-being. While the students quoted above persisted to graduation, it is notable that four others who engaged in medical help-seeking left PhD without the terminal degree. Although treatments for medical conditions can help students manage illness during training, alone they do not promote good mental health nor change the underlying environment that contributes to symptoms and poor mental health.

Therapy & counseling

Two thirds of participants engaged with mental health counseling or therapy. This is of note because statistically minoritized groups like Black, Latiné and Asian American women are less likely to seek therapy relative to white women (Kam et al., 2019). Only one person, Candika, noted reluctance to seek counseling because of the stigma that “therapy means you must be crazy.”

Therapy Provides Support to Cope with Training Environment Stressors.

All participants who sought therapy discussed training and academic workplace micro and macrosystemic factors. Participants often discussed therapy as a space where they learned boundaries and coping skills to manage people and structures contributing to their emotional distress and gained perspectives about training norms from a neutral party. For example, Courtney learned specific strategies from her therapist to respond when her PI made inappropriate comments, including one incident when he offended her religious identity:

Counseling helped in how to deal with difficult relationships, and they gave me some tools on how to not feel as bad when [PI] says things that are offensive and how to get him to be more responsible for what he says … and that really helped.

Even four years after getting her PhD, Courtney was introduced to trauma therapy that “was dramatically helpful for dealing with scientific life.” Similarly, Rachel described counseling as support that helped her understand and navigate implicit training norms by having a resource to “ask a completely objective party any questions about grad school.” Support via therapy was also instructive for some, like Yvonne, to understand “being a minority” in her environment:

I’ve been going to a psychologist to talk about culture shock at [University]. It’s a wonderful school, but I think in terms of being a minority in a predominantly white school this type of environment has been very difficult for me to feel like I belong. Going to a psychologist helps me vent and get rid of depression, or anger, or make [s] me rationalize what’s going on even though some of the stuff that’s going on is irrational. It helps me to calm down, to go back to work or go back to the lab, do what I need to do and just get through it.

Limited Access to Culturally Response Student Mental Health.

For some, seeking therapy or counseling did not result in a positive therapeutic experience. Most participants, as well as graduate students’ writ large, rely on university counseling services. Negative experiences with student mental health services included the following: limited providers on campus, cycling through multiple providers, lack of multiculturally skilled therapists, and few therapists who match salient ethnic and racial identities of participants. Tanisha’s university physician referred her to counseling for ongoing racialized stress in training, and family stressors, but she encountered a provider who lacked racial congruency and cultural humility.

My psychologist was a white woman, and I really didn’t relate to her that much. I didn’t feel comfortable opening up to her, but the one who prescribed the medicine [psychiatrist] was an African-American woman and young and so I felt more comfortable.

Tanisha did not have ready access to a Black therapist at her university health center. Consequently, she decided not to pursue counseling and instead worked with the psychiatrist to manage symptoms through medication. Higher education institutions consistently underfund student mental health and white women dominate the field (Center for Collegiate Mental Health, 2020). This systemic issue impacts the experiences minoritized PhD students have in finding a safe neutral space to talk through psychological distress while in training.

In contrast, some participants reported the value of cultural congruency within the therapeutic relationship. Specifically, participants discussed how clinicians with similar salient identities “get them” and initiate topics around race, ethnicity, and gender within predominantly white male training settings. Janae explained how therapy with a Black woman resulted in different conversations about the role of racialized stress in her mental health compared to her experiences with white male clinicians.

I’ve spent time talking about it with my counselor who’s also an African-American woman and I get a little bit more clarity because she’s been through a PhD program. She’s told me that there’s some things that are universal to the experience of being an African-American woman in graduate school, so there at least I’ve gotten some confirmation that I’m not making it all up … I didn’t realize how important it was for there to be a gender and a race match … It made a world of difference.

Apart from limited access to a consistent clinician who shares salient identities, participants discussed additional barriers, including time for counseling. The nature of biomedical doctoral training, including macrosystemic norms, creates conditions diametrically opposed to taking time to engage with therapy. For example, Maricela discussed experiencing intense stress related to a hostile lab environment, but six years into her PhD program reported “I still haven’t been able to have time to seek out mental health [counseling].” Ashley also connected training conditions to the inability to seek mental health services, “I neglected myself because I was trying to meet the deadlines, meet the goals and keep track… I’m still working on figuring out how to deal with the stress.”

Institutional and PI support for student mental health

Twelve participants shared descriptions of positive interactions and support from PIs during periods of high stress and mental health challenges. This demonstrates that some PIs provide support even as they juggled other demands; sometimes the support was a brief comment or honest discussion. At other times, structures like co-mentoring or institutional recognition of students’ needs facilitated support for trainees.

At a time when Jalil faced setbacks in her research project, she said, “the whole first half of this year was depressing … I just felt stagnant… I doubted myself a lot,” but she felt better and more motivated when she had a transparent discussion with her PI about her self-doubt. Participants also shared that a brief statement from their PI, like “I’m on your side,” were especially meaningful when milestones were not met, including failing a qualifying exam (Jaclyn), or a class (Andrea), or when committee members created unrealistic expectations (Sonja).

In a few instances, PIs went beyond just responding to students, and initiated conversations about mental health. These PIs encouraged students to take time away or to use resources. Iris, who was losing weight and not getting much sleep, said, “my professor suggested that I take time-out [from the] program because … I was showing some pretty nasty signs of depression.” He explained the short-term leave policy, assured her that taking time out would not affect her status in his lab, and subsequently honored that assurance.

Three students discussed the value of a co-mentor. In all cases, the co-mentor was a woman who showed understanding and empathy. Chelsea got “emotional mentorship” in addition to “scientific mentorship” from her co-mentor. She described how it was “really good to get to know [co-PI] better as a person” when they attended a conference. Chelsea explained: “we talk about our families pretty frequently… everything actually, health problems, how we’re feeling that day, what we’ve been eating.” Kathleen got different reactions when talking to her co-PIs about microaggressions. Her female PI was “really understanding… [my male advisor] would interrupt… and say how [the microaggression] couldn’t possibly be what I thought it was.” Tiffany found her co-mentor “more trustworthy” and more supportive than the “sink or swim” approach she experienced with her lab PI. These examples show direct support and acceptance of emotional reactions that honor trainees’ full human experiences, pointing to a need for “soft skills” within biomedical PhD mentoring.

Though doctoral students typically spend most of their time in labs, a couple of students noted multi-layered support from their programs and institutions that explicitly welcomed diversity (e.g., race, educational and minority statuses). Alejandra entered her PhD in an umbrella program that facilitated social interaction before students differentiated into departments and labs. This program design facilitated relationships among peers and faculty that continued throughout her training. The Program Director explicitly supported students, like Alejandra, who were the first in their family to complete high school and college. She said the director “is not a minority, but he’s knowledgeable and he cares.” Alejandra also recognized campus-wide efforts for diversity and inclusion through forums and discussions, saying “it’s big” here and engendered a feeling of collective action toward “equality.” With university resources she helped start a chapter of the Society for the Advancement of Chicanos and Native Americans in Science [SACNAS] whose faculty director facilitated a meeting with members and the university president. This environment of support mediated Alejandra’s feelings of being alone and the burden of being judged by her background:

… it has been very helpful to realize there’s a lot of issues, and it’s not just with you. It’s with other people. And they feel equally bad and burdened about how they’re viewed, and that has been very helpful because it seems like overall even though there are issues at work, but overall there’s a bigger population on campus that recognizes these problems… It’s something that has really helped me because I get to connect with other groups on campus with other people and I realized there’s this movement [that] is larger than the small little things that happen in my little lab.

In another example of multi-layered support, Tanisha found advisors who could, in her words, “oversee my process” of obtaining the PhD at a PWI. Personnel at the university’s diversity office and bridge program were especially valuable as she experienced isolation in her lab. The director of the bridge program was “a big support system” who provided “program information” and resources she was not getting in her lab, and she found friends among other “minority students.”

Also, one of the few Black female professors at her institution “took me under her wing” and “filled voids” where her PI’s mentoring fell short. She guided Tanisha in areas like choosing classes and understanding her PI’s style. At a particularly stressful time she said, “you need to have some fun” and invited Tanisha to a family outing. Later in the PhD, the diversity office brought a research boot camp to campus that hosted faculty women of color to serve as dissertation mentors. Tanisha said, “it was kind of just nice being around those strong women who could just kind of testify about getting through the program.” These examples highlight possibilities for layers of support from program personnel, the diversity office, and other faculty, and show that students benefit by explicit actions toward welcoming and empathy for all students.

Discussion

This study applied Bronfenbrenner’s ecological theory to understand how SCED women in biomedical doctoral programs experienced support to address mental health distress arising from the environmental challenges. Specifically, we asked: How do socially, culturally and economically diverse women in U.S. biomedical PhD programs construct support systems to manage psychological distress induced by the training environment? How do institutional agents effectively support student psychological well-being of socially, culturally and economically diverse women trainees experiencing mental health distress? Our results demonstrate that supports and stressors operate at the microsystem level (e.g., mentor and peer relationships); intersect within the mesosystem (e.g., tensions between lab demands and family commitments); are constrained by the exosystem-level structures (e.g., limited diverse faculty); and reflect productivity at all costs. Longitudinal influences in the chronosystem further shaped women’s trajectories, as relationships and programs established before doctoral studies continued to sustain participants across time. Framing these findings ecologically underscores how student well-being and persistence are shaped by multiple contextual levels and points to the need for interventions beyond the individual.

Applying Bronfenbrenner’s model elucidates the ecological nature of training pressures that impact mental health. This ecological lens illuminates both the compounding effects of pressures across settings and the ways students’ support systems were nested within broader contexts. Consistent with previous research, participants described isolation stemming from issues such as a dearth of colleagues and mentors who shared salient identities, training norms which discouraged openness about difficult experiences, limited time for help-seeking because of training demands, and limited options for formal mental health support within university health systems (Berry et al., 2020; Jackman et al., 2022; Sverdlik et al., 2018). Viewed ecologically, these were not isolated individual barriers but reflect cultural and social norms that shape daily experiences of these students. Our analysis illustrates that minoritized women in STEM doctoral training had low self-reported levels of pre-existing or past episodes of mental health conditions, yet experienced high levels of distress over time in training. This points to the compounding impact of chronic stress and exposure to institutional racism and sexism over time — a chronosystem effect.

When participants described different support systems they constructed to manage psychological distress induced by the training environment, they revealed missing supports in their proximal environments. Regular outreach to family provided “authentic” support, where self-worth, ability and identities were affirmed. Friendship, particularly with other graduate students and those who often shared salient identities, provided a mirror of validation, relief from feeling alone, and comfort knowing others with similar struggles. In interactions with family and friends, participants felt they could “fully express themselves” which was often not an option in competitive training environments. This is consistent with previous research on training norms that force minoritized students to compartmentalize and hide salient parts of their identities to fit into science culture (McGee et al., 2021; Miles et al., 2020; Salusky et al., 2025; Wilkins-Yel et al., 2024). From an ecological perspective, these findings highlight how microsystem supports (e.g., family, friends) buffered against distress generated by mesosystem conflicts (e.g., pressure from the lab) and macrosystem norms (e.g., productivity at all costs). Family and friends for Black, Latiné, mixed-race, and some Asian women were particularly important because they often were the “only one” within a lab, program or department. Some reached back to previous mentors from undergraduate and/or pre-PhD training programs, revealing the durability of early mentoring relationships.

The minoritized women in our study sought out formal support, both psychiatric and psychological at high rates. In contrast to both epidemiological survey data and studies specifically on college student populations that consistently find that Asian, Black and Latiné women seek out mental health services at low rates (Kam et al., 2019), participants in the current study disproportionately engaged in mental health service seeking. This finding suggests that minoritized women in STEM doctoral training programs are highly proactive and willing to engage with health service systems, despite real challenges within those systems like long wait times, a revolving door of student clinicians, and lack of providers who share salient identities of minoritized women or have specific training in cultural humility. The benefit of therapy was often described as finding an “objective party” who could provide an outside perspective for understanding mentoring behaviors, while also teaching coping strategies. Ecologically, these findings show how exosystem-level structures (e.g., campus health systems) both enabled and constrained support, depending on accessibility and cultural responsiveness.

An important aspect of much of the support-seeking was finding safety in interactions that occurred outside of labs and sometimes outside of their school environments. However, participant narratives also highlight how institutional agents effectively support student psychological well-being of SCED women trainees. This often-involved faculty and staff who explicitly emphasized the importance of time for self-care, health and personal needs, which is consistent with an emerging body of systems level research related to doctoral student health and well-being (Kotera et al., 2021; Nagy et al., 2019). Frequently, these mentors were individuals other than their PI, reflecting the critical role of additional on-campus mentors in predominantly male, white, heteronormative spaces. Similarly, some participants benefited from institutional support in the form of diversity initiatives and programming. These accounts emphasized that institutional agents could intervene at both the mesosystem (e.g., departmental) and exosystem (e.g., institutional) levels to buffer stress.

Self-constructed ecologies of support often proved critical for SCED women trainees to persist through and understand norms of PhD training, yet they did not eliminate the psychological toll that STEM training can cost. It took time for women to find and use support which often caused conflict and/or allowed trainees to accept problematic norms for related to time, productivity and sacrifice. In sum, while individual factors such as resiliency are important, they may be insufficient when macrosystem and exosystem structures reinforce harmful norms.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, we offer several recommendations to address the mental health needs of SCED women in biomedical PhD programs. First, it is essential for institutions to implement ongoing psychoeducation for both faculty and students about the mental health risks associated with graduate training and the available support services. This education should not be confined to orientation sessions but integrated throughout the academic year and training life cycle. To enhance support, institutions must invest in their mental health services, ensuring the availability of highly skilled clinicians who are permanent staff members rather than primarily trainees. These clinicians must be trained in cultural humility and reflect the diversity of the student body they serve. Moreover, there is a need for enforced limits on work hours and protected time, particularly when students are dealing with health issues.

Faculty should be discouraged from advising students to use work as a coping mechanism for trauma, hardship, or loss. Instead, institutions can establish umbrella programs that foster connections and support among minoritized students across STEM disciplines, providing them with cohesive cohorts and communities. Institutions should also allocate funding for both internal and external programming to enhance these efforts. Incentive structures from grant funding institutions and universities should prioritize and reward good mentoring practices while disincentivizing poor mentoring.

Finally, institutions and funding agencies need to re-examine the fundamental structure of STEM doctoral training programs. The historical model used today, where a student is expected to attach themselves to a PI and receive the majority of their mentoring and socialization from this one person is unrealistic. The contemporary reality of STEM funding leaves PIs with little time to dedicate to the development of individual students. This model is outdated because it is an artifact of a time when white affluent male PIs trained other white men from affluent backgrounds to enter a workforce that would also look like them. Today there is a strong push to diversify the STEM workforce, but this will require a facilitated model of multiple mentors that can provide varying types of expertise and experiences to meet scientific, social and emotional needs to culturally, socially and economically diverse women trainees. This may in turn require institutional investment to support increased tenure track faculty lines and place significant weight on the quality of mentoring when evaluating faculty for retention and promotion. Critically, the onus should not fall entirely on doctoral trainees to develop a multiple mentor model. This approach disproportionately burdens students who have the least access to cultural capital and scientific networks. Institutional investment in developing and supporting robust internal and external mentoring networks will ultimately yield models that fit the needs of minoritized trainees. Finally, funding agencies must fundamentally shift the benchmarks placed on PIs to foster healthier training environments. PIs are beholden to the demands of both home and funding institutions. The current demands of universities and funding agencies promote unhealthy work and training cultures. Expectations around the speed of productivity and output will shift only when those with the power to fund research demand these changes. By adopting these recommendations, institutions can create a more supportive and inclusive environment that promotes the mental health and well-being of all students.

Limitations

As described in Salusky et al. (2025) the current study design does have some limitations. While all participants were asked about important identities as part of the interview, probing around this topic varied. As a result, some interviewers gained much richer data around the role of salient identities on training experiences relative to others. We used a broad range of search terms to identify participants who experienced mental health struggles while in graduate school, but our search criteria might have missed participants. Likewise, when participants brought up mental health challenges interviewers generally did not ask about specific diagnosis. Lastly, while data were gathered during a period that encompasses many recent key events (2009–2022), we expect that some of these patterns might evolve under the current climate of political attacks on higher education and research funding. For example, stressors in training might be intensified and support networks may become more challenging to build in light of surges in anti-DEI legislation.

Conclusions

Poor mental health among biomedical science trainees’ harms both individual students and the broader biomedical scientific enterprise. Using Bronfenbrenner’s ecological theory, this study demonstrates that distress is not simply a matter of individual vulnerability but the result of pressures and supports operating across nested systems. To navigate threats to mental health, minoritized women in biomedical doctoral training create their own structures of support to weather stressors they encounter in training, a process which requires additional time and labor on top of existing training demands. Systems must change to facilitate the mental well-being of biomedical doctoral trainees, particularly minoritized women. Institutions should target intervention strategies at multiple ecological levels to build environments where diverse students can thrive.
