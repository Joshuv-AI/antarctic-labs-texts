Deep behavioral phenotyping reveals novel features in a mouse model of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis
Authors: Naoaki Sakamoto, Takamasa Numano, Yui Kobayashi, Masahiro Fukuda, Maria Osaki, Keisuke Omori, Taichi Yamamoto, Takahisa Murata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) often suffer from a broad range of extrahepatic symptoms including fatigue and pruritus. However, evaluating behavioral abnormalities in preclinical mouse models remains challenging due to the limitations of conventional short-duration behavioral tests. This study aimed to comprehensively profile 24-hour behaviors of a MASH mouse model using a high-resolution, artificial intelligence (AI)-based approach. C57BL/6J mice were fed a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined high-fat diet from 6 weeks of age. We recorded videos across the dark/light phases at 8, 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age and analyzed behaviors using a novel AI-based behavioral analysis system. Twenty-four-hour behavioral analysis revealed that this model exhibited decreased locomotor activity alongside increased grooming and scratching behaviors as steatohepatitis progressed. These mice also exhibited altered eating-drinking rhythms and rearing patterns. Notably, these behavioral changes may reflect symptoms observed in patients with MASH; for instance, the reduction in locomotor activity is indicative of a fatigue-related phenotype. This comprehensive profiling highlights that AI-based behavioral analysis can identify novel behavioral phenotypes, effectively bridging the gap between mouse models and humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-60820-3.Keywords: MASH, Extrahepatic symptoms, Behavioral analysis, AI, Translational research, Digital biomarkerSubject terms: Diseases, Neuroscience

Introduction

The global prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and steatohepatitis (MASH) has increased by 50% over the past decades1,2. Although most patients remain asymptomatic, some patients complain of fatigue and abdominal discomfort3. Furthermore, beyond hepatic risks such as hepatocellular carcinoma, MASH is associated with various extrahepatic complications. For example, patients with MASLD have an odds ratio of 1.65 for pruritus4. A cohort study revealed the significantly higher hazard ratios of anxiety and depression5. These extrahepatic symptoms significantly reduce the quality of life in patients, yet the mechanisms by which hepatic dysfunction can alter systemic conditions remain elusive. Therefore, preclinical research utilizing model animals is warranted to elucidate pathogenesis for developing management and therapeutic strategies.

In preclinical models, disease symptoms such as anxiety and depression are typically evaluated via subjective behavioral observations using short-duration behavioral tests6. Historically, a lack of technology for 24-hour recordings and automated analysis restricted the detailed profiling of behaviors. This limitation has prevented the capture of behavioral changes across their full temporal dynamics, thereby hindering efforts to bridge the translational gap between humans and model animals. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have remarkably enhanced the resolution of rodent behavioral analysis, allowing for objective, detailed, high-throughput, large-scale, and long-term investigations. We have previously established deep learning-based rodent behavioral analysis methods and unveiled novel phenotypes in mouse disease models7,8. Applying these technologies to MASH models could uncover novel translational phenotypes, thereby advancing our understanding of the disease and reinforcing the clinical relevance of preclinical findings.

In the present study, we aimed to comprehensively profile the 24-hour behavior of a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined high-fat diet (CDAHFD)-fed MASH model using an AI-based behavioral analysis tool.

Methods

Mice

Five-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (n = 12) were purchased from CLEA Japan, Inc. (Fujinomiya, Japan). The mice were housed under 12-h light-dark cycle (lights out: 19:00–7:00) with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. AIA250046). These procedures were carried out in accordance with the Regulations for Animal Experiments of the Central Institute for Experimental Medicine and Life Science (CIEM). Furthermore, this study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

MASH model establishment

Mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 6 per group). Each group was fed a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined, high-fat diet (CDAHFD; A06071302, Research Diets, New Brunswick, USA) or a control diet (Ctrl; A06071314, Research Diets, New Brunswick, USA) from 6 weeks of age, as previously described9. Plasma was collected for biochemical examination at 8, 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age after video recordings by jugular vein blood sampling under isoflurane anesthesia.

Recording conditions

Mice were placed in a white arena (16 × 22 × 34 cm, SHIGUSA, Revamp Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with white paper bedding, under a 12-h light/dark cycle (light out: 19:00–7:00). The videos were recorded during the light or dark phases under indirect light at 150 lx or with infrared light, respectively. Videos were recorded for 24 h from 12:00 (light out: 19:00–7:00) at 8, 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age with a video camera (STC-MCS322U3V, OMRON SENTECH CO., LTD., Kanagawa, Japan) set at a height 30 cm above the bottom of the arena with 60 frames per second (fps). Due to technical issues during video recordings, the sample size at 8 weeks of age was reduced (Ctrl: n = 4; CDAHFD: n = 3). At other weeks of age, all the videos (n = 6 per group) were used for further analysis.

Behavior prediction

The recorded videos were used to analyze mouse behaviors with a commercial neural-network-based behavioral analysis system (SHIGUSA, Revamp Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The software independently classified immobility, drinking, eating, rearing, grooming and scratching frame by frame. When a frame was assigned both immobility and other behaviors, immobility was prioritized and other behaviors were regarded as mispredictions. To prevent overestimation of bouts, the post-processing filters were applied to exclude excessively short intervals and bouts of behaviors as previously described7. First, excessively short intervals (< = 5 frames) were bridged. Then, excessively short bouts (< = 10 frames for drinking, eating, and grooming; <=5 frames for scratching) bouts were removed. For drinking behavior, unlikely long predictions (> = 3600 frames) were removed, as manual review of random videos confirmed the absence of such prolonged drinking. Conflicting frames predicted as several behaviors were prioritized as follows: scratching, eating/drinking, and grooming. Finally, the aforementioned post-processing filters were similarly reapplied.

Definition of bouts and episodes

Bouts were defined as a continuous sequence of frame-level predictions. Episodes were defined as a series of bouts separated by intervals of less than the following threshold (drinking: 30 s, grooming: 30 s, and scratching: 10 s). These thresholds were determined based on frequency histograms of intervals (Supplementary Figs. 3, 8, and 9).

Quantification of the durations of drinking and rearing not accompanied by eating

The durations of drinking, rearing and eating were calculated for each 5-min interval. To isolate behaviors independent of eating, rearing or drinking occurring within intervals in the absence of any eating behavior were defined as “not accompanied by eating”. The durations of these events were then aggregated to represent the total duration of behaviors not accompanied by eating.

Histological examination

Mice were euthanized by exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia at 14 weeks of age. Livers were harvested and fixed with 10% buffered formalin, cut into a 5-mm-thick blocks, embedded in paraffin, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin or Masson’s trichrome. Images were captured using a digital microscope (NanoZoomer S60, Hamamatsu Photonics K.K., Hamamatsu, Japan). The histological scoring (steatosis (0–3), inflammation (0–3), fibrosis (0–4), and ballooning (0–2)) was performed according to the previous study9.

Computer hardware and software

The predictions were conducted with computers equipped with an Intel Core i9 9900 K CPU and NVIDIA RTX2080 GPU, an Intel Core i7 12,700 CPU and NVIDIA RTX3090 GPU, an Intel Core i9 9900 K CPU and NVIDIA GTX1650 GPU, a Ryzen9 3900X and NVIDIA GTX1660 SUPER GPU, and an Intel Core i9 14,900 F CPU and NVIDIA RTX4090 GPU.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Student’s t-test was performed for two-group comparisons. The Mann-Whitney U test was performed to evaluate histological scores. Principal component analysis and permutational multivariate analysis of variance were performed with Python packages: scikit-learn (ver. 1.6.1) and scikit-bio (ver. 0.7.0), respectively. Correlation and Hedges’ g were calculated using Python packages: SciPy (ver. 1.13.1) and pingouin (ver. 0.5.5), respectively. Multiple comparison correction was performed by the Benjamini-Hochberg method with Python package: statsmodels (ver. 0.14.5). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CDAHFD feeding induced MASH progression

C57BL/6J mice had ad libitum access to a control diet (Ctrl) or a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined, high-fat diet (CDAHFD) from 6 weeks of age. Consistent with a previous report9, CDAHFD feeding significantly suppressed body weight gain compared to Ctrl feeding (Fig. 1A). Biochemically, CDAHFD-fed mice displayed higher plasma levels of aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) than Ctrl mice, indicating the development of pronounced liver injury from 8 weeks of age (Fig. 1B, C).

We harvested livers at 14 weeks of age and performed pathological examinations. Absolute and relative liver weight in CDAHFD-fed mice significantly increased compared to that in Ctrl mice (Fig. 1D, E). Histological examination revealed that the CDAHFD feeding significantly induced hepatic steatosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, mild fibrosis, and ballooning (Fig. 1F, G), consistent with the previous study9. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that CDAHFD-fed mice successfully developed the features of MASH.

MASH progression induces marked behavioral alterations

Behavioral recordings were conducted for 24 h (12:00–12:00; dark phase: 19:00–7:00) at 8, 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age. First, locomotor activity, and the duration of drinking, eating, rearing, grooming and scratching during the light and dark phases were quantified at 14 weeks of age. Using these behavioral parameters, we performed a principal component analysis to examine how CDAHFD feeding affected their behaviors. The analysis revealed that there was distinct behavioral separation between Ctrl mice and CDAHFD-fed mice (permutational multivariate analysis of variance: p = 0.003; Fig. 1H). The loading for rearing during the dark phase and locomotor activity and eating across both phases characterized behaviors in Ctrl mice, whereas the other behavioral phenotypes were associated with those in CDAHFD-fed mice (Fig. 1H). These findings prompted us to dissect individual parameters to identify the behavioral signatures associated with MASH progression.

MASH progression leads to hypoactivity, which may reflect a fatigue-related phenotype

The 5-min interval time-course plots revealed the dampened activity especially during the dark phase in CDAHFD-fed mice (Supplementary Fig. 1A–D). While CDAHFD-fed mice maintained comparable activity to the Ctrl mice at 8 weeks of age, they exhibited significant decreases in activity during the dark phase at 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age, and a reduction in activity during the light phase at 14 weeks of age (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). These results indicate that locomotor activity declines during the dark phase from an early stage of MASH development, followed by a generalized reduction that extends into the light phase as the disease progresses. As fatigue is a typical symptom of MASH, this progressive hypoactivity might serve as an index of fatigue in this mouse model.

MASH progression might alter appetite regulation

The temporal plots of eating average duration per 5-min interval at each timepoint revealed a significant progressive reduction in eating across both phases (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 2A, C, and E). Statistical evaluation showed that CDAHFD-fed mice did not display significant changes in either the number or duration of bouts of eating behavior during the light phase but tended to decrease them during the dark phase at 8 weeks of age. In contrast, CDAHFD-fed mice exhibited significant decreases in the number of bouts or duration of eating behavior during the light and dark phases at 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age (Fig. 2C, D). There were no significant changes in the average duration of individual eating bouts at any timepoint (Supplementary Fig. 2G). The intervals between eating bouts significantly increased during the dark phase at each timepoint (Fig. 2E). These results possibly suggest that mice developing MASH lose appetite as the disease progresses, consistent with the results of the reduction in body weight (Fig. 1A).

The MASH model exhibits aberrant drinking patterns

Conversely, the plots of average drinking duration per 5-min interval showed a progressive increase across both the light and dark phases in CDAHFD-fed mice (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 2B, D, and F). The number or duration of bouts increased from 8 to 14 weeks of age (Fig. 2F, G). There were no significant changes in the average duration of individual drinking bouts at any timepoint (Supplementary Fig. 2H). The intervals between drinking bouts significantly increased during the light phase at 10 and 14 weeks of age (Fig. 2H). While the distribution of intervals between eating bouts displayed a single peak (Supplementary Fig. 3), the interval distribution for drinking bouts was bimodal (Supplementary Fig. 4). Therefore, as previously described7, we defined a series of drinking bouts separated by intervals of less than 30 s as a drinking episode. The number of bouts per episode increased during the light phase at 14 weeks of age (Supplementary Fig. 2I, J), indicating the emergence of repetitive drinking behavior within a short timeframe as MASH progressed.

The analysis of transition probabilities between eating and drinking showed that CDAHFD-fed mice developed a significant preference toward drinking over eating (Supplementary Fig. 5A–C). Detailed analysis showed that while drinking was typically accompanied by eating in Ctrl mice, solitary drinking behavior, not accompanied by eating behavior, was observed in CDAHFD-fed mice (Fig. 3A–D). The duration of this solitary drinking increased during both the light and dark phases at 10, 12, and 14 weeks of age (Fig. 3E). In contrast, the duration of drinking accompanied by eating did not show significant differences between groups (Fig. 3F).

MASH progression might alter exploratory-, anxiety-, and sensory-related states

From 10 to 14 weeks of age, CDAHFD-fed mice exhibited a decrease in the number and duration of rearing bouts during the dark phase (Supplementary Fig. 6A–F). The average duration of individual rearing bouts increased during the dark phase (Supplementary Fig. 6G). Representative ethograms and average duration plots revealed that rearing behavior was temporally coupled with eating since mice are required to rear to access food pellets positioned in side-mounted food hoppers (Fig. 4A–D). Consistent with the observed decrease in the duration and frequency of eating bouts, the duration of rearing accompanied by eating decreased in CDAHFD-fed mice (Fig. 4E). Conversely, duration of rearing not accompanied by eating increased in these mice (Fig. 4F). These qualitative shifts of rearing possibly indicate altered exploration activity or anxiety-like state10 in CDAHFD-fed mice.

The plots of average duration of grooming and scratching per 5-min interval showed a progressive increase (Fig. 5A, B, Supplementary Fig. 7A–F). CDAHFD-fed mice exhibited an increase in the number and duration of grooming bouts during the dark phase at 10, 12, and 14 weeks, and an increase in total duration and average duration per bout during the light phase at 12 and 14 weeks (Fig. 5C–E). In contrast, the number and duration of scratching bouts exclusively increased during the light phase at 14 weeks of age (Fig. 5F–H). Since the distributions of intervals between grooming and scratching bouts were bimodal (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9), as previously described7, we defined a series of grooming and scratching bouts separated by intervals of less than 30 and 10 s as grooming and scratching episodes, respectively. Episode analysis showed that CDAHFD-fed mice displayed more repetitive grooming per episode (Supplementary Fig. 7G, H) whereas scratching episodes remained largely unaltered (Supplementary Fig. 7I, J).

These findings suggest that the aberrant behaviors related to exploration, anxiety, and sensory might be observed associated with MASH mouse models.

Behavioral alterations yielded large effect sizes, capturing disease progression distinct from conventional biomarkers

Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between several behavioral variables and traditional biomarkers (AST, ALT, and liver weight; Supplementary Table 1). However, among CDAHFD-fed mice, no significant correlation was observed (Supplementary Table 1). These results demonstrate that CDAHFD-fed mice displayed aberrant behaviors alongside liver dysfunction, but these biological markers did not reflect the extent of the behavioral alterations.

Finally, we evaluated the effect size of behavioral changes by calculating Hedges’ g at 14 weeks of age (Fig. 6). Conventional biological markers AST, ALT, and liver weight showed remarkably large effect sizes (g > 5). Strikingly, the duration of drinking not accompanied by eating, and eating during the dark phase also yielded comparably large values (g > 5). Furthermore, the number and duration of eating bouts during the light phase, duration of grooming and rearing bouts and locomotor activity during the dark phase also showed large effect sizes (g > 2). These results underscore that CDAHFD-fed mice undergo remarkable behavioral shifts in addition to elevations in conventional liver injury biomarkers. Collectively, these behavioral alterations might capture distinct aspects of MASH progression that are not simply reflected by liver injury biomarkers.

Discussion

In the present study, our AI-based 24-hour behavioral analysis revealed that the MASH mouse model underwent marked behavioral alterations. The CDAHFD-fed MASH mouse model displayed aberrant behavioral shifts that may phenomenologically parallel human clinical symptoms (Supplementary Table 2). This comprehensive analysis provides potential translational insights for preclinical research and highlights the importance of temporal behavioral phenotyping.

Fatigue is a common non-specific symptom of MASLD and MASH3. The progressive decrease in locomotor activity, consistent with a prior study11, might serve as a behavioral correlate of clinical fatigue. Although the association between muscle dysfunction and MASH was reported12, the prior study did not observe muscle atrophy in a similarly established CDAHFD-induced MASH model11. The observed hypoactivity might be driven by fatigue mediated by the liver-brain axis via inflammatory cytokines, activated immune cells and other mediators13.

The present study showed impaired eating activity and body weight loss in CDAHFD-fed mice, consistent with the prior studies9,14. Choline deficiency disrupts VLDL secretion and decreases lipid export to peripheral tissues15, contributing to body weight loss. Furthermore, liver damage can modulate the feeding and drinking centers in the central nervous system or the endocrine system through hepatokines and other signaling molecules16. For example, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), whose level was increased in several MASH models17, suppresses food intake18. Collectively, CDAHFD-induced MASH mice are likely to experience body weight loss driven by both metabolic dysfunction and appetite modulation. This appetite modulation shares features with the clinical symptoms of anorexia, sometimes experienced in patients with advanced MASH. Furthermore, we found dynamic alterations of drinking patterns. Epidemiological studies showed that MASLD promotes the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus19, leading to polydipsia. However, given that CDAHFD-fed mice usually do not exhibit insulin resistance14, excessive drinking is unlikely to be a secondary symptom. We hypothesized that fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), whose levels were elevated in the CDAHFD-induced MASH model and human patients20, promoted water drinking partly mediated by neurons in the hypothalamus in mice21. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms.

Patients with MASLD or MASH sometimes suffer from anxiety and pruritus4,5. Regarding psychiatric phenotypes, the increases in duration of grooming might reflect anxiety-like behavior in mice22. In contrast, some studies reported an increased frequency of abdominal licking due to abdominal pain23, a symptom sometimes experienced by patients with MASH. Differentiating between facial and abdominal grooming would enhance the resolution of this behavioral change to distinguish between psychological phenotypes and pain-related phenotypes. Besides, the altered frequency of rearing not accompanied by eating could also reflect shifts of anxiety-like state or exploratory behavior, although interpretation of rearing frequency is controversial10. Additionally, the increase in scratching frequency might be a behavioral correlate of the pruritus observed in human patients. Serum levels of pruritogens including bile acids, whose involvement has been suggested for human MASH-related pruritus24, should be measured in future research. Whereas behavioral alterations were aligned with the MASH progression, behavioral parameters were not significantly correlated with conventional hepatocellular injury biomarkers such as AST and ALT in CDAHFD-fed mice. This suggests that behavioral changes might be driven by indirect, advanced, systemic factors rather than direct, acute liver injury. Collectively, these behavioral profiles suggest that this mouse model could be an invaluable tool not only for elucidating hepatic metabolic pathways, but also for uncovering the mechanisms of extrahepatic manifestations.

Conventionally, behavioral analyses have relied on human observation-based short-duration tests. In MASLD/MASH rodent models, some studies revealed neurobehavioral changes with specialized tests such as the elevated plus maze test25–27. However, these specialized tests generally have low throughput and sometimes suffer from low reproducibility across different researchers and laboratories. The present study employed an AI-based method, which automatically classified mouse behavior for 24 h without human intervention. Of note, some behavioral changes such as locomotor activity were observed during the dark phase in early disease progression but observed across both the light and dark phases in later disease progression. This finding highlights the superiority of AI-based approaches to conventional behavioral tests. Furthermore, scratching behavior was only observed during the light phase at 14 weeks of age. These results highlight the importance of evaluating behavior across an appropriate period; in other words, conventional short-duration methods might miss the crucial phenotypes. AI-based technology facilitates the acquisition of objective, reproducible, long-term and large-scale data.

Despite these findings, the present study has several limitations. First, whether the observed behavioral shifts are exclusively specific to MASH remains elusive. Given that MASH mouse models have different characteristics depending on the induction diet, future investigation is warranted for behavioral profiling of alternative MASH models and metabolic dysfunction models including obesity and diabetes. Furthermore, rescue experiments with pharmacological interventions or reversing the CDAHFD diet to chow diet are required to confirm the specificity and reversibility of these phenotypes. Second, as an observational study, we did not elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms driving these behavioral alterations. Considering that behavioral alteration cannot be solely attributed to steatohepatitis, the relationship between behavioral and biological phenotypes should be examined to uncover the roles of the liver-brain axis in MASH progression.

In conclusion, AI-based approaches successfully identified novel behavioral phenotypes. This non-invasive behavioral profiling strengthens the potential clinical relevance in preclinical studies. AI-based behavioral phenotyping serves as a promising translational framework to bridge the gap between mouse models and human patients.
