Symptom cluster in patients with resected pancreatic cancer during adjuvant chemotherapy: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yun Wang, Ningning Xia, Yuan Song, Neng Shi, Kuei-Ching Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize symptom profiles and identify symptom clusters based on the dimensions of occurrence, severity, and distress in postoperative patients with pancreatic cancer undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited postoperative patients with pancreatic cancer undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy at a tertiary hospital in Jiangsu Province, China, between January and March 2025. Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected one day before the chemotherapy initiation. Nutritional status was assessed using the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002). On Day 7 after the chemotherapy initiation, symptom burden and functional status were assessed using the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS) and the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale, respectively. Symptom clusters were identified through exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and performed separately for each dimension (occurrence, severity, and distress).ResultsA total of 194 patients with a mean of 15.01 ​± ​4.50 symptoms were included in the final analysis. The most prevalent symptom was “Feeling drowsy” (85.8%), while “Feeling nervous” exhibited the highest severity (2.10 ​± ​1.54) and distress (2.10 ​± ​1.56) scores. Five symptom clusters were consistently identified among the three assessment dimensions (occurrence, severity, and distress): gastrointestinal, psychological, neuromuscular, physiological fatigue depletion, and body image disturbance. The inter-dimensional consistency rates for these clusters were 86%, 80%, 75%, 60%, and 67%, respectively.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that postoperative patients with pancreatic cancer undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy experience a significant multidimensional symptom burden. Three symptom clusters (gastrointestinal, psychological, neuromuscular) showed high inter-dimensional consistency (≥ 75%), warranting prioritized intervention. Lower-consistency clusters (physiological energy depletion, body image disturbance) require multidimensional assessment for precise management. Three-dimensional assessment can improve personalized care and optimizes patient outcomes.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most aggressive malignancies of the digestive system,1,2 with a 5-year survival rate of only approximately 11% (the lowest among all cancers).3 PC was responsible for an estimated 467,000 deaths globally in 2022, accounting for 4.8% of all cancer-related mortality.1 Although surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment, only 15% to 20% of patients present with resectable disease at diagnosis.4 Even among these surgical candidates, median overall survival is limited to 8.5–11.1 months postoperatively.5 Although adjuvant chemotherapy has demonstrated significant survival benefits in the postoperative setting,6 regimens such as gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (AG regimen) exhibit substantial toxicity profiles. The convergence of chemotherapy-induced adverse effects, surgical trauma, and psychological distress frequently results in complex, multidimensional symptom clusters that significantly impair patients' quality of life.7

Multiple studies have systematically documented the substantial symptom burden in patients with PC receiving chemotherapy. Kristensen et al.8 prospectively evaluated patients across different chemotherapy regimens, identifying particularly severe gastrointestinal symptoms (pain, nausea, vomiting) that significantly impaired daily functioning. Naoko et al.9 also reported high levels of pain and gastrointestinal symptoms, along with poor quality of life. Through qualitative analysis of semi-structured interviews, Ploukou et al.10 further characterized this multidimensional symptom profile, revealing both physical manifestations (pain, fatigue, cachexia, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea) and underrecognized psychological comorbidities (depression, anxiety, distress). According to patients’ reports, symptom management remained an unresolved need throughout chemotherapy. Existing evidence11 has indicated that patients with PC undergoing chemotherapy experience a mean of 13.39 ​± ​5.83 symptoms— a burden higher than that in other solid malignancies such as breast and lung cancers.10,12 Inadequate symptom management frequently leads to chemotherapy delays or discontinuations, thereby reducing treatment adherence and ultimately impairing overall survival.12,13

In clinical practice, symptoms inherently interrelate, yet current interventions often remain insufficient. This underscores the growing importance of the symptom cluster framework in oncology research and enables more comprehensive assessment and targeted management strategies to improve patient outcomes. Symptom clusters (SCs) are defined as two or more concurrent symptoms that are interrelated, maintain relative stability, and demonstrate statistical independence.14 Crucially, emerging evidence indicates that the synergistic effects of symptom clusters exert more profound impacts on patients' functional status and quality of life than isolated symptoms, with potential implications for overall survival.14 Compared to conventional approaches, SC-based management provides distinct advantages including enhanced clinical efficiency through targeted interventions, improved health care cost-effectiveness, and optimized resource allocation.15 A meta-analysis has confirmed t the effectiveness of this approach in decreasing objective symptom severity and ameliorating patients' subjective experiences.16

Therefore, scientific identification of symptom clusters is of critical importance. Based on the Theory of Unpleasant Symptoms (TOUS) and recommendations from the National Institute of Nursing Research, symptom experiences are now recognized as multidimensional phenomena that should be assessed in terms of occurrence and regarding their severity and associated distress.17,18 TOUS19 proposed that patients' symptom perception is multidimensional, with each dimension offering both independent and synergistic value in guiding symptom management. The occurrence dimension quantifies the epidemiological characteristics of symptoms to identify the most prevalent ones; the severity dimension categorizes symptom intensity to determine the clinical significance of specific symptoms and inform treatment decisions; while the distress dimension evaluates the degree to which symptoms disrupt patients' physical and psychological integrity, revealing new targets for symptom management within the biopsychosocial medical model.

In fact, existing research on symptom clusters has predominantly examined solid tumors including breast and lung cancers. However, there are few studies on systematic characterization of multidimensional symptom profiles in patients with PC — particularly those receiving postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. Conventional unidimensional assessment paradigms inadequately reflect the complexity of symptom burdens, thereby limiting the advancement of precision symptom management interventions. In this study, TOUS conceptual foundation was used to characterize multidimensional symptom clusters (occurrence, severity, distress) in postoperative patients with PC undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy. Guided by TOUS, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed to identify symptom clusters based on their interactions; and comprehensive multidimensional symptom clusters assessment was carried out. The primary objectives of this study were to: (1) systematically characterize early symptom onset patterns during adjuvant chemotherapy; and (2) identify clinically relevant symptom clusters based on multidimensional characteristics. These TOUS-derived insights will inform the development of temporally precise, cluster-specific intervention strategies, with particular focus on the acute treatment phase when symptom burden peaks.

Methods

Study design

This single-center study was conducted at the Pancreatic Center of Nanjing Medical University Affiliated BenQ Hospital. A pilot investigation in July 2024 involved 20 postoperative patients with PC receiving chemotherapy at this center. Preliminary findings revealed significantly lower symptom occurrence among patients undergoing their first chemotherapy cycle (n ​= ​5) compared to those who had completed two or more cycles (n ​= ​15). This pilot study had been approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University Affiliated BenQ Hospital (Approval No. 2024-KL005), with all participants providing written informed consent for secondary data use. According to these findings, the main study protocol was optimized to include only patients who had completed at least one full chemotherapy cycle, thereby enhancing clinical representativeness. Patient recruitment for the main study began on January 20, 2025, following receipt of full ethical approval (Approval No. 2025-KL002). Notably, the pilot data (n ​= ​20) are used exclusively for study design refinement and were not included in the main study analysis (n ​= ​194). Both studies strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Inclusion criteria: adult patients (≥ 18 years) with postoperative PC diagnosed pathologically, who were receiving adjuvant AG chemotherapy and had completed at least one full chemotherapy cycle. Participants were required to be able to communicate verbally and provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria: patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy; were undergoing concurrent radiotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy; had missing key biochemical data (e.g., liver/kidney function tests, complete blood count [CBC]); had a history of other malignancies; and suffered from severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, organ dysfunction, or psychiatric/cognitive disorders.

EFA was employed to identify symptom clusters, strictly adhering to the sample size requirements for factor analysis (i.e., 5–10 participants per symptom item, with a minimum of 100 cases). Based on the 32 symptom items included in the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS), 160 patients were found to be the calculated minimum sample size requirement. Ultimately, 194 patients were enrolled, achieving a ratio of 6.06 cases per item. Validation results confirmed Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) values of 0.770–0.789 (across dimensions), a significant Bartlett's test of sphericity (P ​< ​0.001)— all factor loadings ≥ 0.40, fully meeting the requirements for factor analysis.

Measurement

The general information questionnaire, developed by the research team, included demographic data (e.g., age, sex, education level) and clinical characteristics (e.g., body mass index [BMI], tumor location, American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] stage, presence of metastasis, and time since diagnosis). Relevant clinical data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records.

The MSAS, developed by Portenoy et al., in 1994,20 is a multidimensional self-report tool used to assess symptoms experienced by patients over the past week. It consists of 32 items: the first 24 evaluate symptom occurrence, frequency, severity, and distress, while the remaining 8 assess only occurrence, severity, and distress. Frequency and severity are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, and distress is measured on a 5-point Likert scale. The MSAS is widely used in cancer symptom research and has demonstrated good reliability, validity, and cross-cultural applicability, making it suitable for evaluating the symptom burden in patients with PC. This study employed the Chinese version of the MSAS (MSAS-Ch), which was rigorously validated by Cheng et al.21 in 2009. The Chinese adaptation demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, with a content validity index of 0.94 and Cronbach's α coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 0.87 across different subscales— indicating good reliability and validity. The scale has been widely adopted in Chinese oncology research and clinical practice.

Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002), recommended by the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN),22 was adopted to evaluate nutritional risk in hospitalized patients. The scale has shown substantial inter-rater reliability (κ ​= ​0.78) and good diagnostic accuracy, with a sensitivity of 0.98 and specificity of 0.77.23 Here, we employed the Chinese version of the NRS-2002, which was introduced to China by Chen Wei et al., in 200524 and has been widely applied for nutritional risk screening in clinical settings among Chinese cancer patients. It consists of three components: disease severity, nutritional status, and age adjustment for patients aged ≥ 70 years. A score of ≥ 3 indicates nutritional risk.

Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale was utilized to evaluate functional status in cancer patients based on their ability to perform daily activities and self-care. Scores range from 0 (death) to 100 (normal function), with higher scores indicating better functional status.

Procedure

Before the study initiation, approval was obtained from the hospital administration and the leadership of relevant departments. All research personnel received standardized training to ensure proficiency in questionnaire content and interview techniques.

Baseline Data Collection: Upon enrollment, trained research staff reviewed patients' electronic medical records and conducted face-to-face interviews with patients (or their family members, if applicable) to complete the General Information Questionnaire. Nutritional status was assessed using the NRS-2002, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Symptom and Functional Status Assessment: On Day 7 of the chemotherapy cycle, patients' symptom experiences over the past week were evaluated using the MSAS, and their functional status was assessed using the KPS scale. To ensure accuracy, researchers read each item aloud in a neutral tone, and responses were recorded based on the patient's subjective report. All completed questionnaires were collected immediately and checked for completeness; missing or inconsistent data were promptly clarified and corrected promptly.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0. EFA was conducted to identify the shared pathophysiological mechanisms underlying symptom clusters by extracting latent common factors, while precisely quantifying the strength of inter-symptom associations through factor loading matrices. Therefore, EFA was conducted based on three symptom dimensions (occurrence, severity, and distress) to identify multidimensional symptom clusters in patients with PC. Only symptom items with an occurrence rate ≥ 20% 25 were included to ensure analytical rigor, epidemiological representativeness of symptom clusters, and reliability of underlying biological mechanisms.

Before the factor extraction, data suitability was assessed using the KMO measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity. A KMO value ​> ​0.5 and a Bartlett's test P-value < 0.05 indicated adequate sampling for factor analysis. Criteria for factor retention included: (1) eigenvalues ≥ 1.0; (2) factor loadings ≥ 0.4, with cross-loaded items assigned to the factor with the highest loading; and (3) each factor containing at least two items. After factor extraction, the internal consistency of each symptom cluster was evaluated using Cronbach's α. Finally, symptom clusters were named in accordance with the item composition of each factor, supported by literature review and clinical expertise.

Consistency analysis of the number and types of symptom clusters

The criteria proposed by Kirkova and Walsh was referred to assess the consistency of symptom clusters identified across different dimensions (occurrence, severity, and distress).26 If a symptom cluster shared at least 75% of its symptom components and included the most representative symptoms (i.e., items with the highest factor loadings), it was considered consistent across the three dimensions. The consistency percentage was calculated using the following formula:

Consistency (%) = (Number of shared symptoms across the three dimensions / Total possible symptoms in the cluster) ​× ​100%.

Results

The analysis included 194 patients with complete data and no missing values.

Most patients (74.7%) had an education level of junior high school or below, and the primary caregivers were predominantly spouses (58.1%). A total of 84.0% of patients reported no smoking history, and 74.2% had no alcohol consumption history. Chronic comorbidities were present in 60.3% of the patients, and 57.2% were diagnosed with malnutrition. Discussion

Main findings

In this work, multidimensional symptom experiences among postoperative patients with PC undergoing AG chemotherapy were explored comprehensively for the first time. Among the 194 patients evaluated, an average of 15 symptoms per individual was reported. “Feeling drowsy” emerged as the most prevalent symptom, whereas “Feeling nervous” was rated as the most severe and distressing. The findings are consistent with the TOUS, which postulates that symptoms are multidimensional and interconnected through shared biological and psychosocial pathways.

Five symptom clusters were identified, including gastrointestinal, psychological, neuromuscular, physiological energy depletion, and body image disturbance. These results reinforce the intrinsic interrelatedness among symptoms. Through TOUS-guided multidimensional analysis, we further demonstrated that the first three clusters exhibited particularly high consistency across all assessment dimensions (occurrence, severity, and distress), highlighting their stability and clinical significance as priority targets for symptom management. In contrast, physiological energy depletion and body image disturbance clusters showed lower consistency. This can be attributed to individual or cultural factors, emphasizing the need for multidimensional assessment to avoid underestimating burden. Symptom clusters demonstrating high dimensional consistency can be used as effective candidates for broad-based screening and early intervention protocol. Nevertheless, clinical decision-making should remain patient-centered, guided by comprehensive evaluations across all the symptom dimensions to ensure accurate symptom cluster identification and the development of personalized management strategies.

These findings align with prior studies. Hu et al.27 analyzed postoperative gastric cancer patients undergoing first-cycle chemotherapy. Han et al.28 focused on gastrointestinal cancer patients, and Harris et al.29 concentrated on patients with various solid tumors; these studies demonstrated similar symptom cluster types and structures under multidimensional assessment. These studies supported their stability and the feasibility of using a single dimension for initial identification. However, RA et al.30 reported that only the gastrointestinal cluster remained consistent across all dimensions, while others varied in gynecological cancer patients. This discrepancy highlights the need for further research into symptom clustering mechanisms and the refinement of clinical assessment strategies. However, our assessment timepoints differ significantly from previous studies. Unlike Han et al.,28 who evaluated symptoms before the next chemotherapy cycle (2–3 week intervals), our assessment on day 7 post-chemotherapy offers advantages in capturing acute-phase symptoms more accurately, minimizing recall bias, and enabling early clinical intervention. By comparison, Han et al.'s approach can better assess cumulative/delayed effects and symptom progression trajectories. This comparison underscores the complementary value of different assessment timings and thus suggests that future research should integrate both timepoints to establish a more comprehensive symptom monitoring system.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Gastrointestinal symptom cluster, consistently identified across all three dimensions—included Loss of appetite, Vomiting, Dry mouth, Nausea, Feeling bloated, and Diarrhea. Although chemotherapy-induced GI symptoms are well-documented (primarily due to cytotoxic mucosal damage31), our findings differ from prior studies where nausea and diarrhea predominated.32 This study found that loss of appetite was particularly prominent in patients with PC, likely reflecting a unique pathophysiological mechanism: pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI). PEI, commonly observed after pancreatic surgery, impairs digestive enzyme secretion, leading to gut lipid accumulation, CCK-1 receptor activation, and ultimately hypothalamic appetite suppression via vagal pathways.33,34 Approximately one-third of postoperative patients develop PEI within a year,35 significantly impacting nutritional status and quality of life. Given the high prevalence of appetite loss during AG chemotherapy, clinical management should include routine monitoring of biomarkers such as fecal elastase-1, serum prealbumin, and retinol-binding protein to facilitate early identification of PEI and malnutrition risk. For patients exhibiting signs of PEI, timely initiation of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) is recommended to improve nutrient absorption and mitigate appetite suppression. Additionally, albumin-bound paclitaxel may chelate zinc ions, impairing sweet taste receptor sensitivity and reducing CA6 enzyme activity,36 while gemcitabine disrupts mucosal regeneration—both contributing to taste alterations and anorexia.37 Cancer-related anorexia worsens nutritional intake, emotional well-being, and social functioning. Therefore, in addition to nutritional support, targeted management of dry mouth, nausea, and vomiting is critical. Zinc-containing mouthwashes may help restore taste and oral comfort, improve dietary intake, treatment adherence, and quality of life.

Psychological symptom cluster, consistently identified across all three symptom dimensions, mainly included Feeling sad, Feeling nervous, Worrying, Feeling irritable, and often co-occurred with Difficulty sleeping. Recent systematic reviews by Harris et al.38 and Yang et al.39 emphasize that psychological symptom clusters are among the most prevalent and distressing in cancer populations, underscoring their significance as key targets for comprehensive symptom management. Despite consistently identified, psychological symptom clusters show notable variability across studies because of differences in cancer types, disease stages, and assessment tools. For example, Ma et al.40 reported sadness and distress as the dominant psychological symptoms in lung cancer patients, whereas studies in breast cancer populations41,42 often describe a “fatigue–sleep disturbance–depression” cluster. Harris et al.38 highlighted “worry, anxiety, sadness, and irritability” as the core psychological symptoms across chemotherapy settings—a pattern closely aligned with our findings, suggesting a degree of cross-cancer universality. In PC, psychological distress is often intensified by poor prognosis, high recurrence rates, and low chemotherapy tolerance. These stressors may activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, disrupt neuroendocrine-immune function, and impair sleep and circadian rhythms. The prevalence of depression in this population has been reported to range from 33% to 76.82%,43,44 which are markedly higher than in many other cancers. Considering their impact on quality of life and treatment adherence, psychological symptoms warrant proactive and individualized clinical management.

Neuromuscular symptom cluster primarily included symptoms such as Numbness/tingling in hands and feet, Swelling of arms or legs, and pain, —indicative of peripheral neurotoxicity associated with the AG regimen. In this study, 53.6% of patients reported “glove-and-stocking” paresthesia, a hallmark of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), particularly paclitaxel-induced neuropathy (PIPN). PIPN45 may become dose-limiting at cumulative doses > 300 mg/m2, causing burning pain, sensory hypersensitivity, and motor dysfunction.46 Known risk factors include older age, diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, obesity, certain medications, and lifestyle factors such as smoking and inactivity. Early identification and intervention in high-risk patients are essential for minimizing functional impairment and preserving quality of life.46 Additionally, some patients experienced limb swelling, potentially due to postoperative lymphatic dysfunction or increased vascular permeability, which warrants differentiation from complications such as lymphatic fistula or deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Notably, 60.3% of patients reported pruritus, though with low distress. Due to its perceived lower importance, this symptom may be underestimated, highlighting the necessity of proactive assessment to avoid missing opportunities for supportive care.

Physiological energy depletion cluster—comprising symptoms such as Feeling drowsy, Lack of energy, and Difficulty concentrating—reflects widespread metabolic dysfunction during chemotherapy. “Feeling drowsy” was the most commonly reported symptom (85.8%) and served as a key indicator in postoperative patients with PC receiving AG chemotherapy. 72.7% of patients reported lack of energy, slightly higher than reported by Yang et al.,11 but clearly lower than in studies using more sensitive tools like the FACT-F,47 highlighting potential underestimation due to assessment differences and cultural perceptions. Cancer-related fatigue (CRF), a persistent exhaustion unrelieved by rest, significantly impairs physical, emotional, and social functioning,48 yet often remains under-recognized. Difficulty concentrating, a core component of this cluster, is increasingly linked to chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment (CRCI),49 characterized by deficits in attention, memory, and executive function. CRCI is multifactorial, with oxidative stress and neuroinflammation as key mechanisms,11 and is influenced by factors such as anemia, malnutrition, sleep disorders, aging, and comorbidities. In this study, 65% of patients reported cognitive symptoms including inattention and slowed thinking, indicating varying degrees of cognitive decline. These symptoms exacerbate CRF and negatively impact treatment adherence and quality of life. Given their interplay, an integrated intervention model is essential. A “closed-loop” care approach—eat well, move well, stay calm, supported by meds, monitored by markers—is recommended. This includes nutritional and exercise interventions, psychosocial support, targeted medications, and biomarker monitoring to promote cognitive recovery and improve overall well-being.

Body image disturbance symptom cluster encompasses “Hair loss” and the subjective feeling of “I don't look like myself”, reflecting the multidimensional impact of chemotherapy-induced changes in appearance on patients' self-esteem, body image, and social interactions. In this study, hair loss was reported by 57.2% of patients, representing the core symptom. Although not life-threatening, such changes often lead to identity disturbance and reduced self-esteem.50 Previous studies have identified appearance changes as an independent risk factor for psychological distress. A Japanese survey51 found that 58.1% of cancer patients experienced appearance-related changes, with hair loss being the most prevalent. These changes particularly affect women and working-age individuals, contributing to anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal—factors that may subsequently reduce treatment adherence. Therefore, clinical care should systematically address the psychosocial effects of appearance changes. Pre-treatment counseling, access to wigs or headscarves, and psychological support can help patients cope more effectively, improve quality of life, and enhance treatment engagement.

Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insights into symptom clusters in patients with PC receiving adjuvant chemotherapy, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the 20% prevalence threshold for symptom inclusion is methodologically sound, it may exclude rare but clinically significant symptoms that could impact patient outcomes. Second, the single timepoint assessment at day 7 post-chemotherapy, though optimal for capturing acute toxicity, limits our understanding of symptom trajectories and delayed treatment effects that may manifest later in the treatment cycle. Third, the study's reliance on a single-center design and retrospective symptom reporting introduces potential selection and recall biases. These limitations highlight the need for future multicenter studies incorporating longitudinal assessments to fully characterize the symptom burden spectrum throughout chemotherapy process.

Conclusions

This study reveals that patients with PC undergoing adjuvant AG chemotherapy experience a significant symptom burden, with the most prevalent being Feeling drowsy, Feeling sad, Feeling nervous, and Lack of energy. Through multidimensional assessment, five symptom clusters were identified, including gastrointestinal, psychological, neuromuscular, physiological energy depletion, and body image disturbance, of which the first three demonstrated particularly high cross-dimensional consistency (≥ 75%). These findings provide robust evidence for developing precision nursing interventions, suggesting clinicians should prioritize management of high-consistency clusters. Future studies should explore factors contributing to lower consistency in certain clusters and investigate the biological, psychological, and social mechanisms underlying symptom cluster formation to support precision nursing.
