The small bacterial membrane protein YohP induces nucleoid condensation in E. coli and inhibits oligomerization of antimicrobial peptides
Authors: Ana Natriashvili, Nahid Mohammadsadeghi, Eva Smudde, Bork Berghoff, Maximilian H Ulbrich, Hans-Georg Koch, Axel Brakhage
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProkaryotic organisms execute multiple stress response mechanisms in order to cope with rapidly changing environments. Some mechanisms respond to specific cues, such as the OxyR-dependent response to hydrogen peroxide or the SOS-response that is induced upon DNA-damage. These specific responses complement general mechanisms that respond to multiple and diverse stressors. One example is nucleoid condensation, which is a rapid and effective mechanism for genome protection and observed in response to various stresses, including entry into stationary phase. Recently, the upregulation of small membrane proteins (SMPs) in response to stress was observed, but details on how this emerging class of proteins modulate the stress response is largely unknown. Here, we demonstrate that the production of two SMPs, YohP and YncL, cause nucleoid condensation in Escherichia coli. Nucleoid condensation is the result of YohP-/YncL-induced sublethal membrane depolarization, which induces the phage-shock response and leads to a reduction of global protein synthesis. YohP production also prevents the oligomerization of the antimicrobial peptide magainin-2 in the E. coli membrane and reduces the metabolic activity of E. coli cells. Thus, the synthesis of YohP and likely of other SMPs potentially protects bacterial cells against some unfavorable conditions by shifting them into a metabolically silent state.Keywords: nucleoid condensation, metabolic silencing, stress response, membrane potential, YncL, small membrane proteins

Introduction

Small open reading frames (ORFs) are present in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes and are usually defined as ORFs of <100 codons in eukaryotes or <50 codons in prokaryotes (Steinberg and Koch 2021, Burton et al. 2024). A recent systematic analysis in Enterobacteriaceae predicted the presence of about 67 000 small ORFs in intergenic genome regions, which could potentially encode for almost 10% of the bacterial proteomes (Fesenko et al. 2025). However, despite the vast abundance of small ORFs in bacterial genomes, a functional characterization of the encoded small proteins has been achieved for only a few of them (de Alvarenga et al. 2020, Kraus et al. 2020, Gutt et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2023, Üresin et al. 2023, Rauch et al. 2025). RNA-seq and transcriptome data sets indicate that the expression of small proteins is often regulated by growth conditions, e.g. by oxygen limitation (Avican et al. 2021, Fesenko et al. 2025). This is rationalized by the involvement of small membrane proteins (SMPs) in the assembly of respiratory complexes (Zickermann et al. 2010). In Escherichia coli, the SMP CydX is involved in the assembly of the cytochrome bd ubiquinol oxidase (VanOrsdel et al. 2013, Hoeser et al. 2014), a terminal oxidase with high oxygen affinity that supports growth under microaerobic conditions (Grauel et al. 2021). Another example is CcoS of Rhodobacter capsulatus (Kulajta et al. 2006). CcoS is required for cofactor insertion into the high-affinity cytochrome cbb3-type cytochrome c oxidase (Ekici et al. 2012, Rauch et al. 2025), which terminates the electron transfer chain of many pathogenic bacteria.

Nutrient limitation, transition into stationary phase, and stress conditions also lead to an upregulation of many SMPs in E. coli, e.g. of YoaJ, YkgO, YncL, or YohP (Hemm et al. 2008, 2010). YoaJ is upregulated in diamide-treated cells (Hemm et al. 2010) and has been used as a tag to enhance the production of disulfide-bridge containing proteins (Ma et al. 2020), suggesting a possible role in maintaining the redox balance. YkgO is upregulated on minimal media and when cell envelope stress is induced by treatment with SDS(sodium dodecyl sulfate) and EDTA (ethylendiaminetetraacetic acid) (Hemm et al. 2010). YkgO is a paralog of the ribosomal subunit protein L36 (Ueta et al. 2020) and substitutes L36 in stationary phase ribosomes (Lilleorg et al. 2019), suggesting a role in translation during stress conditions. The expression of yncL and yohP is upregulated when cells are grown in minimal media. In addition, yncL levels increase upon heat shock, whereas yohP levels rise under oxygen-limiting conditions (Hemm et al. 2010). YohP has also been used as a model protein for studying the membrane insertion pathway of SMPs in E. coli (Steinberg et al. 2020, Sarmah et al. 2023). YohP is, like most other bacterial membrane proteins, targeted to the membrane by the signal recognition particle (SRP) (Steinberg et al. 2018). However, SRP binding to YohP occurs posttranslationally, which is untypical for SRP and potentially explained by the inability of SRP to cotranslationally bind to proteins of <~45 amino acids (Denks et al. 2017). Interestingly, YohP that is synthesized from already membrane-bound yohP mRNAs can be inserted independently of the SRP pathway (Sarmah et al. 2023). This is likely important during stationary phase, when the SRP pathway is blocked by the accumulating alarmones (p)ppGpp (Czech et al. 2022, Zegarra et al. 2023). The presence of an alternative insertion mechanism for YohP during stationary phase or stress conditions indicates that YohP might play an important role in stress adaptation. Cell envelope and oxidative stress are also suggested to induce yohP expression (Hemm et al. 2010), but as for most SMPs, it is currently unknown which specific function(s) these proteins execute under unfavorable conditions.

In the current study, we have analysed the consequences of YohP production in E. coli and show that YohP production is associated with nucleoid condensation, which is a cellular strategy to protect DNA from environmental stresses and to facilitate DNA repair (Walker et al. 2024). In addition, our data show that in YohP-containing membranes, the oligomerization of the antimicrobial peptide magainin-2 is blocked.

Material and methods

Strains and plasmids

The E. coli K-12 strain MG1655 (Richmond et al. 1999) was used as wild type strain and the MG1655 variant containing the chromosomally SPA-tagged yohP copy (GSO333) was a gift from Dr Gisela Storz, National Institute of Health, USA. The Δdps, ΔhupA, ΔhupB, and Δfis strains were obtained from the KEIO collection (Baba et al. 2006) via Thermo Fisher Scientific (Darmstadt, Germany) and the ΔyohP strain has been described before (Natriashvili et al. 2025). Escherichia coli cells were routinely grown on LB(Lysogeny Broth)-medium at 37°C in either LB liquid media or on solid media (LB medium + 1.5% agar–agar) unless otherwise stated. The plasmids pRS1-YohPHis and pRS1-MtlAHis have been described previously (Jauss et al. 2019, Steinberg et al. 2020) and encode C-terminally His-tagged YohP or MtlA in the vector pRS1. pRS1 (ampR) contains an pBR322 origin and encodes the T7 RNA polymerase under the control of the constitutive EM7 promoter (Jauss et al. 2019). pRS1-YohP(Flag3) was constructed via PCR (polymerase chain reaction) and Gibson Assembly and contained a triple-Flag-tag instead of the His-tag at the C-terminus. pRS1-YncL6His was constructed via PCR using MG1655 chromosomal DNA and the Gibson assembly strategy according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Protocol E5510 from New England Biolabs). N- and C-terminal lysine replacements in YohP with either alanine or glutamate were produced using Q5® Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit from New England Biolabs according to manufacturer’s instructions. pBad-YqjD encoding the N-terminally His-tagged YqjD was described before (Njenga et al. 2024) and subcloned into the pRS1 vector to generate pRS1-YqjD(His). The plasmid pBad+42-tisB encodes nucleotides +42 to +354 of tisB and has been described before (Unoson and Wagner 2008). For simultaneous expression of yohP and tisB in E. coli MG1655, cells were first transformed with plasmid pBad+42-tisB. Colonies were selected in the presence of 100 µg ampicillin and subjected to second transformation with plasmid pRS1(Km)-YohPHis, in which the ampicillin cartridge was replaced by a kanamycin cartridge. Escherichia coli cells containing pBad+42-tisB and pRS1(Km)-YohP plasmids were then selected on LB plates supplemented with 100 µg of ampicillin and 50 µg of kanamycin.

The magainin-2 nucleotide sequence with NcoI and NdeI cleavage sites for cloning into the pET19b plasmid was obtained from Genescript Biotech (Rijswiijk, Netherlands), which also delivered the magainin-2 and pleurocidin antimicrobial peptides, each with a C-terminal amidation. All PCR reactions were carried out using Q5® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (M0491) or Phusion® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB Biolabs) according to manufacturer’s instructions. All nucleotide primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Delta visionTM ultra microscopy

For the evaluation of nucleoid condensation, 8 × 108 cells were collected from an E. coli culture grown to exponential phase on LB medium unless stated otherwise. Cells were collected by centrifugation and washed three time with sterile 1X PBS (phosphate-buffered saline), followed by a first staining with DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindol, 0.5 mg/ml from 5 mg/ml stock in DMSO (dimethly sulfoxide), for 5 min at 37°C) and then with Nile Red [10 µg/ml, from 1 mg/ml stock in DMSO, for 5 min at room temperature (RT)]. After staining, cells were washed three times with 1X PBS, and 5 µl cell culture was transferred onto a clean Glass Bottom Dish [35 mm dish with 20 mm bottom well # 1,5 Glass (0.16–0.19 mm)] and covered with 800 µl of 0.8% agarose in ddH2O. Imaging was performed with a Delta VisionTM Ultra microscope, equipped with a sCMOS pco.edge camera at a magnification of 100X (Olympus UPlanSAPO), and immersion oil with a refractive index of 1.5140. Exposure time was 0.2 s at a 10% laser power for Blue and 0.2 s at 32% laser power for Red. DIC images were obtained at a 5% laser power and 0.075 s exposure. Recording was performed using a 3-µm Z-Scan with sectioning of 0.1 µm. Deconvolution post imaging processing was done after scanning with the program Acquire ultra (softWoRx). Deconvolved images were analysed with the ImageJ/Fiji plug-in software (NIH, Bethesda, USA). For imaging in the presence of magainin-2, E. coli cells were grown to exponential phase and washed with 1X PBS buffer buffer. After resuspension in 1X PBS, magainin-2 in 1X PBS as added to an aliquot containing 8 × 108 cells at a final concentration of 250 µg/ml (Kozlowska et al. 2014). Samples were then incubated with gentle agitation (300 rpm, RT) for 30 min before microscopy. Controls were just incubated with sterile 1X PBS. For monitoring nucleoid condensation over time, E. coli MG1655 was grown on LB and 8 × 108 cells were collected for staining with DAPI at different time points and optical densities (ODs).

Transmission electron microscopy

Wild type and YohP-producing E. coli cells were initially fixed by treatment with 4% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 20 min. After fixation, cells were embedded in 2% low melting agarose (Invitrogen number 16520050) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer and postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in ddH2O for 60 min on ice and then washed six times in ddH2O. The embedded cells were incubated in 1% aqueous uranyl acetate solution for 2 h in dark and washed two times in ddH2O. Dehydration was performed by 15 min incubation steps in 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 2 × 100% ethanol and 2 × 100% acetone. After embedding in Durcupan resin, ultrathin sections were performed using a UC7 Ultramicrotome (Leica) and collected on formvar‐coated copper grids. Poststaining was done for 1 min with 3% lead citrate in ddH2O and subsequent washing and drying. Grids were imaged using a Zeiss Leo 912 transmission electron microscope (Oberkochen, Germany).

Cell viability assay

Escherichia coli K-12 MG1655 cells harboring the indicated plasmids were cultivated in LB medium, induced at an OD600 of 0.5, and incubated at 37°C for 2 h postinduction. From each culture, three aliquots containing 8 × 10⁸ cells were taken and treated with either 250 µg/ml magainin-2 (prepared from a 2 mg/ml stock in 1X PBS), 125 µg/ml pleurocidin (from a 1 mg/ml stock in 1X PBS), or sterile 1X PBS as a control. Samples were incubated at 37°C for an additional 30 min, followed by washing with sterile 1X PBS at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. After complete removal of the supernatant, cell pellets were resuspended in 20 µl of Dilution Buffer (QUANTOM™ Viable Cell Staining Kit). The subsequent steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Viable cells were quantified using the QUANTOMTx Microbial Cell Counter (BioCat, Heidelberg, Germany).

Membrane potential measurement in whole cells

The E. coli K12 MG1655 strain with the indicated plasmids were grown on LB and protein production was induced at OD600 = 0.5 and further incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Aliquots of 8 × 108 cells were collected from each sample and washed once with sterile PBS. When indicated 1 mM of CCCP (100 mM stock in DMSO) was added. All samples were adjusted to a total volume of 100 µl of and further incubated for 30 min at 37°C. The samples were washed with PBS and diluted with 500 µl of PBS supplemented with 2 µg of the dye DiBAC4(3) (Stock solution: 10 mg/ml in DMSO, working solution: 200 µg/ml in sterile deionized H2O, freshly prepared). Samples were incubated at RT for 20 min in the dark, centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 3 min at RT, washed two times with sterile PBS, and incubated with 2% paraformaldehyde in a total volume of 100 µl PBS for 10 min at RT. Samples were then washed with PBS and finally resuspended in 200 µl PBS. 100 µl from this solution was transferred on a black 96-well plate (Greiner, Frickenhausen Germany) and fluorescence was measured in a Tecan Spark plate reader after excitation at 485 nm and an emission of 510 nm.

In vivo metabolic labeling

Escherichia coli MG1655 containing either plasmid pRS1 or pRS1-YohP(His) were grown and induced for 2 h on M63 minimal medium (Neidhardt et al. 1974) supplemented with 20 amino acids. Cells were harvested and washed three times with M63 medium lacking methionine and cysteine, resuspended in same medium and induced for additional 30 min. An aliquot of 2 × 108 cells was then added to 1 ml M63 medium and supplemented with 1 μCi 35S-methionine and cysteine labeling mix (Perkin Elmer, Rodgau, Germany). 100 µl samples were taken from each culture at different time points after the addition of radioactive labeling mix and directly precipitated with 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), separated with SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and visualized using autoradiography.

Spheroplast preparation

Escherichia coli MG1655 strains carrying the indicated plasmids were cultured in LB medium and induced at an OD600 of 0.5 using either 1 mM IPTG or 0.2% l-arabinose for 2 h. From each culture, aliquots containing 1 × 109 cells were collected, washed with spheroplast buffer 1 (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), and incubated in spheroplast buffer 2 (20 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 20% w/v ultrapure sucrose, 1 mg/ml lysozyme, 0.5 mM PMSF, pH 8) at 25°C for 30 min. The resulting spheroplasts were washed twice with washing buffer (20 mM Tris, 20% w/v ultrapure sucrose, 0.5 mM PMSF, pH 8) and then resuspended in 50 µl of the same buffer. A 5-µl aliquot from each preparation was examined using an Olympus BX51 microscope with a 100X objective to confirm successful spheroplast formation.

In vitro synthesis of radioactively labeled magainin-2 and incubation with INVs and spheroplasts

Magainin-2, cloned under the control of the T7-promoter in plasmid pET19b was in vitro synthesized and radioactively labeled using a coupled transcription/translation system (Koch et al. 1999), The 35S-labeled magainin-2 was subsequently incubated with E. coli inner membrane vesicles (INVs, 0.1–0.2 µg protein) or spheroplasts (from 1 × 109 cells) for 25 min at 37°C. Samples were then treated with proteinase K (PK, 0,5 mg/ml) for 30 min at 25°C to determine membrane insertion. Samples were then TCA-precipitated, separated by SDS-PAGE and analysed by autoradiography.

For carbonate extraction, 35S-labeled magainin-2 was incubated with INVs (inner membrane vesicles) or spheroplasts as above and treated with 0.1 M Na2CO3 (pH 11.3) for 40 min at 4°C. Samples were then centrifuged for 1 h at 45 000 rpm in a Beckmann TLA 55 rotor. The supernatants were neutralized with glacial acetic acid and all samples were precipitated with 5% TCA for at least 30 min, separated on SDS-PAGE and analysed by autoradiography.

To determine the role of YohP in magainin-2 oligomerization in spheroplasts, spheroplasts were prepared from E. coli MG1655, harboring the indicated plasmids and incubated with radiolabeled magainin-2 as described above. Ultracentrifugation was performed for 1 h at 45 000 rpm using a Beckman TLA 55 rotor. The supernatant (representing the unbound fraction) and the pellet (membrane-associated fraction) were then subjected to TCA precipitation and denaturation, followed by separation on 16% Tris–tricine SDS-PAGE and visualization by autoradiography. For investigating time-dependent magainin-2 oligomerization, in vitro synthesized and radioactively labeled magainin-2 was incubated with spheroplasts and aliquots of 100 µl were taken at 1, 5, 15, and 30 min following the start of incubation and immediately processed as described above.

SDS-PAGE and western blot analyses

Proteins were separated via denaturing SDS-PAGE. Two different gel systems were used in this study: 15% Tris–glycine PAGE for separation of proteins between 10 and100 kDa and 16.5% Tris–tricine PAGE for the separation of small proteins between 0.5 and 10 kDa (Steinberg et al. 2020). Samples were denatured at 37°C or 56°C for 10 min in Laemmli loading buffer (278 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 44.4% glycerol, 4.4% SDS, 0.02% bromophenol blue) containing fresh DTT (dithiothreitol) at a final concentration of 25 mM. For the immune detection of proteins in whole cells, routinely the protein content of 1 × 108 cells was precipitated with 5% TCA (final concentration) and incubated for 30 min on ice. The sample was subsequently centrifuged at 30 000 × g and 4°C for 15 min, the pellet was resuspended in 20 µl of SDS loading buffer and incubated for 10 min at 56°C before loading on SDS-PAGE. The SDS-PAGE-separated proteins were then transferred via tank blot onto 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare, Freiburg, Germany) at 750 mA for 2 h. For the detection of YohP, the gel was transferred by semidry western blotting onto 0.22 µm PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) PsQ membranes (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk powder in T-TBS buffer for at least 1 h before the addition of the primary antibodies.

Mainz. Antibodies against YidC (used at a 1:2000 dilution) were raised against the purified protein and have been reported before (Koch et al. 1999, 2002). Western blot samples were analysed by using ImageQuant (GE Healthcare) or the ImageJ/Fiji plug-in software (NIH). All experiments were performed at least twice as independent biological replicates and representative gels/blots/images are shown. When data were quantified, at least three independent biological replicates with several technical replicates were performed and the signal intensity observed for wild type cells or cell extracts was set to 100%.

Autoradiography

Gels were fixed in a fixing solution (100 ml 160 µM acetic acid, 400 ml 86 µM ethanol, and 500 ml water), followed by three washes with demineralized water (15 min each). The gels were then dried under vacuum for 1.5 h at 65°C. Dried gels were exposed to a phosphor screen overnight and imaged using a Typhoon FLA 7000 phosphor imager (GE Healthcare).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Western blot and autoradiography samples were analysed by using the ImageQuant (GE Healthcare) or the ImageJ/Fiji plug-in software (NIH). All experiments were performed at least twice as independent biological replicates and representative gels/blots/images are shown. When data were quantified, at least three independent biological replicates with several technical replicates were performed. Mean values and standard deviations were determined by using either Excel (Microsoft Corp.) or GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism Corp. San Diego). For quantification of cells with condensed nucleoids, manual counting was performed on three biological replicates per sample, each with at least four technical replicates. Manual counting was executed independently by two different researchers. Data were normalized to 100 cells per sample. A two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test was used to determine statistically significant differences between samples. Significance levels of 0.05 and 0.01 were applied.

To analyse the level of nucleoid condensation and cell length in YohP-producing cells, a convolutional network for biomedical image segmentation based on the U-NET architecture and subsequent postprocessing was used (Negassi et al. 2020) (Fig. S1). Training images were generated by manually annotating the membrane in images from WT cells, YohP-containing cells and cells producing the YohP variant YohP(K24E), to cover all occurring phenotypes. Also, the inside of the cells was annotated automatically by completely filling the area inside the membrane outline. Training was performed with 2000 iterations and a learning rate of 10–4. The training was validated by visual inspection of the resulting segmentations. If the training showed incomplete separation of adjacent cells, it was extended by a small number of iterations (200) until a satisfactory performance was achieved. The resulting segmentations were postprocessed using custom scripts in Wolfram Mathematica to remove segmentations with incomplete border and cell shapes that exceeded the average width and length by factors of 1.5 and 2, respectively. The obtained masks were used to crop the inside of the cell from the DAPI channel of the original images, hereby yielding the nucleoid images of individual cells. Form these, the maximum intensity was determined; the fraction of pixels above 50% of this maximum is the measure of condensation, with a small fraction relating to strong condensation. To obtain the length of a cell, the image was rotated such that the half-axes of an ellipse fitting best the cell outline were oriented parallel to the image edges, and the distance between the leftmost and rightmost pixels was measured.

Results

YohP induces nucleoid condensation

We had recently observed that the production of YohP caused nucleoid condensation in E. coli strain BL21 (Steinberg et al. 2020). This was validated in the current study for E. coli MG1655 by fluorescence and electron microscopy. For fluorescence microscopy, the membrane boundary was stained with Nile Red and the nucleoid with DAPI. Exponentially growing wild type E. coli cells carrying the empty plasmid pRS1 showed a low compaction of the nucleoid, which was visible as diffuse structure covering large areas in the middle of the cell (Verma et al. 2019) (Fig. 1a). In contrast, cells expressing yohP from the T7-dependent plasmid pRS1 (Jauss et al. 2019, Steinberg et al. 2020), showed a condensed nucleoid that was confined to a small portion of the cell (Fig. 1a). In addition, some lipid-containing inclusion bodies were visible as red dots in YohP-producing cells (Valax and Georgiou 1993). Nucleoid condensation was also visible by transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 1a), which revealed a highly compacted nucleoid in most of the yohP expressing cells, but not in the control cells carrying the empty plasmid (Fig. 1a). Nucleoid condensation is frequently observed when cells encounter stress conditions (Verma et al. 2019, Walker et al. 2024), but can also result from protein overproduction (Yilmaz and Schnetz 2022). We therefore monitored whether the overproduction of the membrane proteins YncL or MtlA also caused nucleoid condensation. YncL is like YohP, a SMP that is induced during stationary phase or stress conditions (Hemm et al. 2008, 2010); in contrast the 60 kDa mannitol permease MtlA belongs to the phosphotransferase family of sugar transporters and is frequently used as a model membrane protein in E. coli (Koch et al. 1999, Koch and Muller 2000, Boy and Koch 2009). YncL-producing cells showed like YohP-producing cells a large portion of cells with condensed nucleoids, while in MtlA producing cells, the nucleoid was evenly distributed throughout the cell (Fig. 1b). Manual quantification of the number of cells with condensed nucleoids showed that ~50%–60% of all YohP- and YncL-producing cells had a condensed nucleoid, compared to only ~20% of MtlA-producing cells or control cells (Fig. 1c). The observation that not all YohP- or YncL-producing cells showed a condensed nucleoid is likely the result of a phenotypic heterogeneity that is often observed in plasmid-containing bacteria (Hernandez-Beltran et al. 2024). Immune detection demonstrated that the YohP levels were higher than the YncL levels, but comparable to the MtlA levels (Fig. 1d). Therefore, we considered it unlikely that differences in the protein concentrations were the reason for the observed differences in nucleoid condensation, although the three proteins could not be separated on a single SDS-gel due to their large size difference. The levels of the inner membrane protein YfgM served as loading control. Thus, nucleoid condensation is apparently linked to the presence of the SMPs, YohP and YncL. The data also confirmed the strong propensity of YohP to form SDS-resistant dimers (Fig. 1d), which is in agreement with published data (Steinberg et al. 2020, Natriashvili et al. 2025).

Nucleoid condensation increases with YohP production until mid-stationary phase.

Nucleoid condensation is frequently observed when E. coli cells enter stationary phase (Kleckner et al. 2014, Walker et al. 2024) and under those conditions the YohP-levels also increase (Hemm et al. 2008). For analysing the growth-phase-dependent YohP production, we employed the E. coli MG1655 derivative that contained a chromosomally SPA-tagged yohP copy under its native promoter (Hemm et al. 2008). The 8 kDa sequential peptide affinity (SPA)-tag consists of a triple Flag-tag and the calmodulin-binding peptide (CBP). YohPSPA was almost undetectable during early exponential phase, but its level increased when cells reached late exponential/early stationary phase. After that the YohP-levels stayed approximately constant up to OD600 = 4.0 (Fig. 2a). In contrast to the plasmid-encoded C-terminally His-tagged YohP (Fig. 1d), the chromosomally encoded C-terminally SPA-tagged YohP did not show dimer formation. This could be due to the large size of the tag, but this was not further analysed.

In order to correlate the levels of the endogenous YohP with those of the plasmid-encoded YohP, we generated a YohP variant with a triple Flag-tag at the C-terminus [pRS1-YohP(Flag3)]. The expression levels of YohP(Flag3) were then adjusted to the expression levels of the endogenous YohP during early stationary phase (Fig. 2b). Like the SPA-tagged chromosomally encoded YohP, the plasmid-encoded YohP(Flag3) variant showed no dimer formation, indicating that large modification at the C-terminus of YohP prevent stable dimer formation. Since the physiological importance of dimerization is currently unknown, we continued to use the His-tagged YohP variants in pRS1 under expression conditions that matched those of the YohP(Flag3) variant and the endogenous YohP levels in stationary phase (Fig. 2c).

The increase of YohP during transition into stationary phase coincided with nucleoid condensation in E. coli wild type cells (MG1655), but at late stationary phase (OD600 4.0), the number of cells with a condensed nucleoid was reduced (Fig. 2d and e). Nucleoid condensation involves multiple DNA-binding proteins and one is the protein Dps (DNA-binding protein from starved cells), which is the most abundant protein associated with the nucleoid in stationary E. coli cells (Janissen et al. 2018, Orban and Finkel 2022). The Dps-DNA structures show diverse morphologies with both highly packed and loosely packed condensates in late stationary phase (Walker et al. 2024). In addition, rapid nucleoid condensation in bacteria is sometimes followed by a decompaction phase (Vauclare et al. 2024), which might explain the reduced nucleoid condensation in late stationary phase. Two other highly abundant nucleoid binding proteins are the histone-like heterodimeric protein HU (Histone-like protein, unstable), encoded by hupAB, and Fis. We therefore analysed nucleoid condensation in strains deleted for dps, hupA, hupB, or fis. We selected the hup single mutants over a hupAB double mutant, because the double mutant shows impaired growth (Wada et al. 1988). These deletion mutants carried either the empty plasmid pRS1 or expressed the membrane proteins yohP, yncL, or yqjD. The latter was selected as a control, because it is like YncL and YohP induced during stationary phase (Njenga et al. 2023, 2024). YqjD encodes a 12-kDa C-tail anchored membrane protein that is involved in ribosome hibernation (Njenga et al. 2024). The production of YohP or YncL, but not of YqjD, caused nucleoid condensation in wild type E. coli cells (Fig. 3a and b). In addition, the production of YohP, YncL, and YqjD slightly increased E. coli cell length compared to the corresponding strains carrying the empty plasmid (Fig. 3a), a phenomenon often observed when membrane proteins are expressed from plasmids (Gubellini et al. 2011). YohP- and YncL-induced nucleoid condensation was also observed in the Δdps strain, suggesting that Dps is not required for the YohP-/YncL-induced nucleoid condensation. In contrast, the deletion of hupA or hupB, reduced the fraction of cells with a condensed nucleoid. This was not related to changes in the YohP-levels, as validated by western blotting (Fig. S2). However, in both deletion mutants the percentage of cells with a condensed nucleoid was already reduced in the presence of the empty pRS1 plasmid (Fig. 3a), indicating that HU is required for nucleoid condensation even in the absence of YohP or YncL production. To assess whether the absence of HU influenced YohP-/YncL-induced nucleoid condensation, the data were normalized by dividing the percentage of cells with condensed nucleoids in the deletion strains by the percentage observed in wild type cells. This revealed a small, but significant reduction of nucleoid condensation when yohP or yncL were expressed in the ΔhupA and ΔhupB strains (Fig. 3b). The deletion of fis on the other hand had no significant influence on nucleoid condensation caused by YohP or YncL production, which is likely explained by the observation that Fis is mainly associated with the nucleoid in exponentially growing cells and almost undetectable in stationary phase (Ali Azam et al. 1999, Azam and Ishihama 1999). Thus, a reduction of theYohP-/YncL-induced nucleoid condensation was only observed in the absence of HU. However, considering that even in the plasmid control (pRS1), the ΔhupA and ΔhupB cells showed a lower nucleoid condensation than the corresponding wild type strain, the contribution of HU to YohP-/YncL-induced nucleoid condensation needs to be further explored.

The C-terminal lysine residue of YohP influences its function by regulating YohP dimerization and nucleoid condensation independently

SMPs, such as YohP consist basically of only a single hydrophobic transmembrane domain with only a few residues facing the periplasm or cytoplasm (Fig. 4a). In addition, several SMPs are rather resistant toward mutations (Rauch et al. 2025) and are suggested to have mainly a scaffolding or chaperoning function (Zickermann et al. 2010, Steinberg and Koch 2021). YohP forms SDS-resistant dimers (Fig. 1d) (Steinberg et al. 2020, Sarmah et al. 2023), and Alphafold3-predictions suggest a parallel orientation of the two monomers in the membrane (Fig. 4b). The transmembrane domain of YohP is flanked by a lysine residue on each side of the membrane (Fig. 4a and b) and these residues were individually replaced by either alanine or glutamate. The expression levels of YohP and its variants were comparable, but the YohP(K24E) variant failed to form stable dimers (Fig. 4c). Structural modeling of the YohP(K24E) variant by Alphafold3 suggested that the altered orientation of the bulky side chain of Phe23 potentially prevent a stable dimerization of YohP(K24E) (Fig. S3A and B). In a previous study, the importance of an unusual glycine motif within the transmembrane domain (Fig. 4a) for YohP dimerization was demonstrated (Steinberg et al. 2020) and the data here show that adding a negative charge to the C-terminus also prevents YohP dimerization. This potentially also explains why adding the SPA- or Flag-tags to YohP prevent dimerization (Fig. 2), because these tags contain a consecutive stretch of negatively charged aspartic acid residues.

We then monitored the effect of these mutations on nucleoid condensation. For quantifying nucleoid condensation in a large number of cells, a convolutional network for image segmentation based on the U-NET architecture (Negassi et al. 2020) was implemented (see the section “Materials and methods”). The algorithm was trained on E. coli cells stained with Nile Red and DAPI to recognize the cell boundaries and to label the nucleoid, respectively. For quantification, the cell length and the amount of nucleoid condensation of at least 200 individual cells were determined (Fig. 4d). As a measure of the condensation, the fraction of the area inside the cell that had a DAPI intensity of >50% maximum intensity was taken. For cells with a highly condensed nucleoid, the bright area covers only a small fraction of the total cell area, resulting in low nucleoid occupation values of 0%–30%. In contrast, for an uncondensed nucleoid, the bright area covers a much larger fraction of the cell in the range of 50%–80%. Accordingly, a low value indicates a strong condensation. For visualization, the 2D histograms were transformed into probability density functions, and the contour lines containing 5%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 95% of cells were drawn, with the innermost contour approximating the highest density of cells (Fig. 4d).

When we used this approach for MG1655 cells containing either pRS1 or pRS1-YohP(His), a strong increase in nucleoid condensation and a moderate increase in cell length were visible for YohP-expressing cells compared to the control, confirming that the automated approach yields the same results as the manual quantification of the YohP-induced nucleoid condensation (Fig 4d).

Replacing the lysine residue close to the N-terminus (K2) of YohP with either alanine (K2A) or glutamate (K2E) only moderately reduced the number of cells with a condensed nucleoid and resulted in an overall heterogenous population (Fig. 4e and Fig. S4). Replacing K24 close to the C-terminus of YohP with neutral alanine also showed a mixed population, but the number of cells with a condensed nucleoid was slightly reduced in comparison to cells expressing wild type YohP. Strikingly, the YohP(K24E) variant that failed to form stable dimers showed the strongest phenotype. The amount of nucleoid condensation was only slightly reduced in comparison to wild type YohP, but the production of YohP(K24E) increased cell length (Fig. 4e and Fig. S4). Thus, although our data demonstrate the importance of the C-terminal lysine residue, there seems to be no strict correlation between YohP dimerization and its ability to induce nucleoid condensation. The possible effect of YohP-dimerization on cell length and cell division was beyond the scope of this study and not further analysed.

YohP-induced dissipation of the membrane potential causes nucleoid condensation

We had recently shown that the expression of YohP reduces the membrane potential and induces the stringent response (Natriashvili et al. 2025). The stringent response is a multifaceted stress-response pathway that leads to a reduction of many metabolic processes, such a nucleotide biosynthesis or ribosome assembly (Steinchen et al. 2020, Njenga et al. 2023). A typical response to a reduced membrane potential and membrane damage is the upregulation of the phage-shock response (Wang et al. 2010, Ravi et al. 2024, Junglas et al. 2025) and YohP-producing cells showed elevated PspA and PspC levels (Natriashvili et al. 2025). This was also observed for YncL-producing cells, but not for MtlA-producing cells (Fig. 5a). Increased levels of PspC and PspA were also observed in cells treated with the protonophore CCCP (cyanide-m-chlorophenyl hydrazine) (Fig. 5a).

The membrane potential in these strains was determined via the potential-sensitive dye DiBAC4(3) [Bis-(1,3-dibutylbarbituric acid) trimethine oxonol]. DiBAC4(3) enters depolarized cells and binds to intracellular proteins and to membranes. Increased depolarization results in increased influx of the anionic dye and increases fluorescence. Thus, DiBAC4(3) fluorescence at 530 nm upon excitation at 485 nm is a suitable read-out for membrane depolarization. In YohP- and YncL-expressing cells and in CCCP-treated cells, the fluorescence was significantly higher than in the pRS1-containing strain, while no increase in fluorescence was observed in MtlA-expressing cells (Fig. 5b). This demonstrates that both YohP and YncL cause a dissipation of the membrane potential. In cells expressing the YohP(K24A) or YohP(K24E) variants, DiBAC4(3) fluorescence was also increased in comparison the pRS1-containing cells, however, the dissipation of the membrane potential was weaker than observed for wild type YohP (Fig. 5b).

CCCP-treatment of wild type cells or treating cells with the antimicrobial peptide magainin-2 also caused nucleoid condensation (Fig. 5c) (Kozlowska et al. 2014). Like YohP or YncL, Magainin-2 targets the bacterial membrane (Ludtke et al. 1996, Arnusch et al. 2007, Bechinger 2011) and dissipates the membrane potential (Rashid et al. 2020). Thus, the YohP-/YncL-induced loss of the membrane potential is likely the reason for the condensed nucleoid. This is supported by the reduced nucleoid condensation in YohP(K24A)- and YohP(K24E)-producing cells, which also show a lower dissipation of the membrane potential compared to wild type YohP.

Cells expressing yohP or yncL are likely hypersensitive to magainin-2 treatment, because of additive effects on the membrane potential. This was tested by incubating cells with sublethal concentrations of magainin-2 (250 µg/ml) and pleurocidin (125 µg/ml) (Kozlowska et al. 2014), another pore-forming antimicrobial peptide (McMillan and Coombs 2021). Magainin-2 or pleurocidin treatment of MG1655 cells containing the empty plasmid (MG1655 pRS1) had only a small effect on the viable cell count (Fig. 6a). In contrast, YohP- or YncL-producing cells showed reduced cell viability and were highly sensitive toward magainin-2 and pleurocidin treatment (Fig. 6a), while MG1655 cells expressing YqjD or cells lacking YohP (ΔyohP) showed a sensitivity that was similar to the control (MG1655 pRS1) (Fig. S5A). The additive effect of YohP/YncL and antimicrobial peptides on the membrane potential was also validated by DiBAC4(3) measurements, which revealed that both CCCP and magainin-2 further reduced the membrane potential in YohP- and YncL-expressing cells (Fig. 6b). The reduced membrane potential of magainin-2 treated YohP producing cells should have a drastic effect on their metabolic activity and this was analysed by a metabolic labeling experiment with 35S-labeled cysteine and methionine. This assay monitors the global protein synthesis rate over time and shows that YohP and magainin-2 have an additive negative effect on protein synthesis (Fig. S5B).

Thus, the reduced cell viability when YohP-/YncL-containing cells were treated with magainin-2 (Fig. 6a) is likely caused by a collapse of the membrane potential. However, it is important to emphasize that the assay used for monitoring the viable cell count is based on cleavage of the dye calcein-acetoxymethyl by intracellular esterases in viable cells. The assay cannot detect viable but metabolically silent cells, and therefore we cannot exclude that the combined activity of YohP and magainin-2 reduce the metabolic activity to a level that is undetectable by the assay.

Both YohP production and magainin-2 treatment caused nucleoid condensation, and we therefore tested whether this effect was also additive. Escherichia coli MG1655 containing pRS1 did not show condensed nucleoids, unless treated with magainin-2 (Fig. 6c). Surprisingly, magainin-2 treatment of YohP-producing cells resulted in a more dispersed nucleoid in comparison to the untreated cells (Fig. 6c and d). In addition, we observed some polar localized DAPI-stained spots, which likely represent DNA-containing inclusion bodies (Valax and Georgiou 1993). A more dispersed nucleoid was also observed when YncL-producing cells were treated with magainin-2 (Fig. 6c and d). Thus, in contrast to our expectation, the simultaneous presence of YohP and magainin-2 did not enhance nucleoid condensation. Whether this was also observed for another membrane-targeting peptide was analysed by determining nucleoid condensation in the presence of the membrane toxin TisB. TisB is the toxic peptide of a type I toxin–antitoxin system and induces like magainin-2 membrane depolarization (Leinberger et al. 2024, Shore et al. 2024) (Fig. S6) and nucleoid condensation (Fig. 6e). However, unlike magainin-2, nucleoid condensation was also observed when both TisB and YohP were coexpressed in the same cell (Fig. 6e and F; Fig. S6). In general, when both YohP and TisB were coexpressed, their protein levels were slightly lower than in cells expressing them separately, which is probably expected considering that both reduce the membrane potential (Fig. S6). For the immune detection of TisB, a Flag-tagged version in the same expression vector was used, while nucleoid condensation and the membrane potential measurements were performed with an untagged version, for excluding any effect of the tag on the dissipation of the membrane potential or nucleoid condensation. Although TisB-induced membrane depolarization was weaker compared to the magainin-2 induced depolarization (Fig. S6), the extent of nucleoid condensation was comparable. This could indicate that nucleoid condensation is induced when the dissipation of the membrane potential exceeds a certain threshold-level. In conclusion, the dissipation of the membrane potential by YohP, YncL, magainin-2, TisB, or CCCP causes nucleoid condensation in E. coli. However, the simultaneous presence of YohP and magainin-2 prevents nucleoid condensation by a so far unknown mechanism.

YohP interferes with magainin-2 oligomerization

Magainin-2 likely dissipates the membrane potential by forming toroidal pores in the membrane (Ludtke et al. 1996, Rashid et al. 2020), but it also interacts with integral membrane proteins and membrane-associated proteins (Di Somma et al. 2022). Membrane insertion of proteins can be monitored in vitro by probing their proteinase resistance or their extractability by sodium carbonate (Weiche et al. 2008). Magainin-2 was in vitro synthesized in a coupled transcription/translation system in the presence of INVs, which were isolated from different E. coli strains. Subsequently, the samples were treated with proteinase K for monitoring membrane insertion of magainin-2. However, magainin-2 showed only weak proteinase K resistance (Fig. S7A), indicating that it is accessible to the protease and not protected by the lipid environment. This is also supported by the observation that magainin-2 was recovered from the supernatant after carbonate extraction and ultracentrifugation (Fig. S7B). However, in general, the recovery of magainin-2 after carbonate extraction was low, indicating that most of the material either formed insoluble aggregates after extraction or could not be recovered from the centrifuge tubes. Nevertheless, the weak proteinase K and carbonate resistances indicate that magainin-2 is only peripherally associated with the membrane, which is in line with its amphipathic character and its ability to form toroidal pores. When INVs were isolated from cells containing pRS1-YohP(His) and incubated with magainin-2, a slight increase in the proteinase K and carbonate resistance was observed (Fig. S7A), suggesting a more tighter membrane interaction. However, the effect is likely too small to explain the lack of nucleoid condensation in the presence of both YohP and magainin-2.

Magainin-2 generated pores have a diameter of 2–3 nm and their formation requires the presence of multiple magainin-2 molecules (Imura et al. 2008). Whether YohP influences magainin-2 oligomerization was analysed in a time–course experiment by incubating E. coli spheroplasts with radioactively labelled magainin-2. By using spheroplasts, potential effects of magainin-2 on the outer membrane can be disregarded. Already after 1 min incubation, almost all magainin-2 was found in the pellet fraction (P) after centrifugation, indicating that it was bound to the spheroplast membrane (Fig. 7a). However, magainin-2 oligomers were not yet clearly visible after 1 min. In contrast, higher-molecular-mass bands emerged after 5 min and intensified over 15 min and 30 min of incubation (Fig. 7a). Magainin-2 has a mass of 2.4 kDa and the visible bands up to ~15 kDa likely reflect the preferred dimeric to pentameric states of magainin-2 (Su et al. 2020). For other pore-forming antimicrobial peptides, such as melittin, the pore was suggested to contain at least four monomers (Leveritt et al. 2015), which is also in line with our observation. Strikingly, magainin-2 showed only a weak propensity for oligomerization when incubated with spheroplasts from YohP-producing cells (Fig. 7a). Thus, magainin-2 further reduces the membrane potential in YohP-containing membranes and lowers the viability—or at least the metabolic activity—of YohP-producing cells. However, YohP appears to inhibit the formation of stable higher-order magainin-2 assemblies.

The replacement of lysine residues in YohP did not strongly influence nucleoid condensation (Fig. 4), but the YohP(K24E) mutation prevented YohP dimerization. Whether these lysine replacements also influenced magainin-2 oligomerization was tested by incubating radioactively labeled magainin-2 with spheroplasts isolated from E. coli strains expressing the YohP variants. However, as observed for wild type YohP, all four YohP variants prevented magainin-2 oligomerization (Fig. 7b), demonstrating that YohP can interfere with magainin-2 oligomerization independently of its own oligomeric state.

The magainin-2 oligomers were observed on SDS-PAGE and their absence in the presence of YohP could simply reflect an increased SDS-sensitivity. Therefore, a Blue-Native PAGE (BN-PAGE) was employed, which allows for the visualization of protein complexes under almost native conditions (Wittig et al. 2006). Although BN-PAGE is usually used for the analysis of larger protein complexes and has only limited resolution in the lower molecular mass area (Heuberger et al. 2002, Kulajta et al. 2006), two magainin-2 complexes below the 66 kDa marker band were detected in spheroplasts of E. coli cells carrying the empty plasmid (Fig. 7c). Although magainin-2 oligomers preferentially contain two to five monomers, oligomers of up to 20–25 monomers have also been reported (Su et al. 2020). Importantly, both bands were significantly weaker in spheroplasts of YohP-producing cells, but not in in spheroplasts of YqjD-producing cells. These data support the hypothesis that YohP interferes with magainin-2 oligomerization in the membrane.

In conclusion, the dissipation of the membrane potential by either YohP or YncL causes nucleoid condensation and potentially involves the histone-like protein HU. Our data furthermore show that YohP/YncL production and the addition of the antimicrobial peptide magainin-2 have an additive effect on the membrane potential, but surprisingly an antagonistic effect on nucleoid condensation.

Discussion

Despite their abundance in all domains of life, the function(s) of many SMPs remain enigmatic (Steinberg and Koch 2021). This applies in particular to SMPs that are upregulated during stress conditions or when bacterial cells enter the stationary phase (Hemm et al. 2010, 2020). Examples include YohP and YncL, which are each present at ~1000 copies per E. coli cell grown in MOPS-medium (Li et al. 2014). We had recently shown that membrane insertion of the stationary-phase induced SMP YohP in E. coli reduced the membrane potential and induced the stringent response (Natriashvili et al. 2025). In addition, an earlier observation suggested that YohP production was associated with nucleoid condensation, although details about a possible mechanism are still unknown (Steinberg et al. 2020). The current study was initiated to determine whether similar effects were also observed for other stress-induced SMPs and whether nucleoid condensation can be linked to the dissipated membrane potential in SMP-producing cells.

Nucleoid condensation has been observed upon the induction of some type I toxin–antitoxin systems, such as dinQ/agrB or ldrD/rdlD in E. coli (Shore et al. 2024). These systems consist of an antitoxin RNA that regulates the production of an inner membrane toxin. We show here that nucleoid condensation is also induced by the type I toxin TisB, which dissipates the membrane potential (Berghoff et al. 2017, Leinberger et al. 2024). The proton motive force (pmf), an electrochemical gradient across the inner membrane, is essential for ATP (adenosine triphosphate) synthesis via the ATP synthase (Rabbers and Bruggeman 2022). The depletion of the pmf and, as a consequence, likely of ATP is associated with impaired growth but also with increased resistance against toxic compounds and antibiotics (Wan et al. 2023). This is explained by the fact that the transport of these compounds across the bacterial membrane depends on transport systems that require ATP hydrolysis or the proton gradient as driving force (Huang et al. 2017, Zhang et al. 2019). The reduced metabolic activity and the reduced protein synthesis rate further adds to the overall resistance and can lead to a dormant state, also known as persistence, which favors cellular survival under stress conditions (Harms et al. 2016, Lee et al. 2023).

Nucleoid condensation further adds to stress resistance, because protein-mediated changes in the local DNA architecture can block access of RNA polymerase and prevent gene expression (Lim et al. 2012). In addition, nucleoid condensation by nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) physically protects DNA by a coating mechanism (Holowka and Zakrzewska-Czerwińska 2020). This appears to be one of the most rapid and effective stress response mechanisms, in particular when conditions suddenly change (Holowka and Zakrzewska-Czerwińska 2020). Thus, nucleoid condensation triggered by YohP or YncL may represent a protective cellular response. Of the tested NAPs, only the absence of the heterodimeric DNA-binding protein HU, encoded by hupAB, slightly decreased the YohP- or YncL-induced nucleoid condensation. HU is one of the most abundant NAPs and present at ~30 000 copies in E. coli (Ali Azam et al. 1999, Azam and Ishihama 1999, Li et al. 2014). Like many NAPs, HU has a high affinity structure-specific binding mode that is involved in site-specific recombination and DNA repair, but also a low affinity DNA binding mode that is structure-independent and involved in DNA condensation and coating (Verma et al. 2019). The absence of HU generally causes a reduction of nucleoid condensation (Macvanin et al. 2012), which is also observed here independently of YohP or YncL production. We did not observe a specific upregulation of HU or other NAPs in a recent proteome analysis of YohP producing cells (Natriashvili et al. 2025), which is likely explained by the fact that primarily Dps and Fis show a strong growth-phase-dependent variation in abundance (Ali Azam et al. 1999). However, neither the absence of Dps nor Fis seem to have a strong influence on YohP-induced nucleoid condensation. Despite differences in their binding motifs, E. coli NAPs occupy largely identical areas of the bacterial chromosome (Kahramanoglou et al. 2011, Prieto et al. 2012) and have at least partially overlapping functions. This makes it difficult to assign the YohP-/YncL-induced nucleoid condensation to a single NAP. Nevertheless, the previously observed induction of the stringent response upon YohP production (Natriashvili et al. 2025), the YohP-induced dissipation of the membrane potential and the nucleoid condensation are likely part of the same strategy that E. coli utilizes to protect itself against nonfavorable conditions. It remains a possibility that any SMP that dissipates the pH gradient will cause such a series of effects, including cytoplasmic condensation, nucleoid condensation, and protein aggregation (Cayron et al. 2024, Leinberger et al. 2024).

One interesting observation of our study is that although YohP-producing cells are hypersensitive toward membrane-targeting antimicrobial peptides, magainin-2 treatment of YohP-producing cells antagonized nucleoid condensation. Hypersensitivity is likely explained by the additive effect of YohP and magainin-2 on the dissipation of the membrane potential. The strong dissipation of the membrane potential in the presence of both YohP and magainin-2 potentially also explains the lack of nucleoid condensation. In stationary phase, E. coli contains ~100 000 copies of Dps, 30 000 copies of HU, and about 30 000 copies of H-NS (Ali Azam et al. 1999, Azam and Ishihama 1999, Li et al. 2014). However, our data show that magainin-2 enhances the inhibitory effect of YohP on protein synthesis, which may impair E. coli’s ability to maintain the high concentration of NAPs required for nucleoid condensation under those conditions. In addition, the dissipation of the membrane potential will likely reduce the cellular ATP levels and thus reduce the activity of ATP-dependent enzymes involved in nucleoid condensation, such as the SMC proteins MukBEF (Badrinarayanan et al. 2012) and type II DNA topoisomerases (Nolivos et al. 2016). Therefore, we speculate that the lack of nucleoid condensation is a consequence of the rapid and extensive loss of the membrane potential when both YohP and magainin-2 are present in the membrane. In contrast to magainin-2 treatment, nucleoid condensation was still observed when YohP and TisB were coexpressed in the same cell. This difference could reflect the ability of externally added magainin to target also components of the outer membrane (Di Somma et al. 2022), and would also support previous in vitro studies that suggest that magainin-2 has a more disruptive effect on membrane integrity than TisB (Grage et al. 2016).

The partial loss of the membrane potential can also explain the reduced oligomeric state of magainin-2 in the presence of YohP. In vitro studies have demonstrated that pore formation by maganin-2 is influenced by the membrane potential and that disruption of the membrane potential by CCCP reduces pore formation (Nakamura et al. 1999). Pore formation is likely transient and stable pores do not seem to exist (Gregory et al. 2009). The number of monomers required for pore formation is still unclear (Gregory et al. 2009), and although oligomeric structures of 2 up to 25 monomers have been reported (Su et al. 2020), lipid-lined pores can form with only a few monomers (Huang et al. 2004). This indicates that the reduced oligomeric state of magainin-2 in the presence of YohP does not necessarily reduce its ability to dissipate the membrane potential.

In conclusion, the available data demonstrate that the production of YohP and YncL have a significant negative effect on the vitality of E. coli. We have previously shown that YohP induces the stringent response and causes membrane depolarization and we show here that it augments the effect of antimicrobial peptides and causes nucleoid condensation. It is important to emphasize that the levels of YohP (and likely YncL) used here do not significantly exceed the native levels when cells enter stationary phase. The major difference to the native situation is that YohP and YncL are expressed in our study already during the early exponential phase. The production of YohP, YncL, and potentially other SMPs when cells enter stationary phase could reflect a bet-hedging strategy that allows E. coli to survive under suboptimal conditions at the expense of a generally lower fitness.
