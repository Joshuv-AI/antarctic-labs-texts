Progression of dengue during an outbreak (2024) in Dhaka, Bangladesh: a hospital based longitudinal study

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBangladesh has experienced recurrent dengue outbreaks since 2000, driven by climatic changes and socio-demographic vulnerabilities. Limited evidence on circulating serotypes and immune diversity has hindered preparedness for severe dengue (SD). Identifying clinical and laboratory predictors of progression to severe dengue is key to strengthening outbreak response and clinical management.MethodsA longitudinal cohort study was conducted in four hospitals in Dhaka between January and November 2024. Patients with confirmed dengue with warning signs were enrolled. Clinical data and blood samples were collected at enrollment, clinical deterioration/discharge, and convalescence (day 21 ± 7). Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with SD compared with dengue with warning signs (DWS). Associations were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values < 0.05 considered y significant.FindingsAmong 3135 dengue patients, 354 with warning signs were enrolled. 62 (17.5%) progressed to SD, including 2 deaths. Most of the patients (60%) were aged between 21 and 40 years, and 76% were male. Lethargy was more frequent in SD patients (29%) than DWS patients (11%). DEN-2 was the predominant serotype (90%). Multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for potential confounders, identified arthralgia (95% CI: 1.04–3.98), anorexia (95% CI: 1.40–11.7), lethargy (95% CI: 1.97–10.7), diabetes mellitus (95% CI: 1.01–6.17), pleural effusion (95% CI: 4.97–21.1) and prolonged APTT (95% CI: 1.02–1.08) as independent predictors for SD.InterpretationThe findings show that a few simple and easily measurable indicators can support early risk assessment and guide timely hospital admission and clinical management.FundingUnited States Agency for International Development (USAID) under the Crisis Modifier Grant (GR-01909) in response to the dengue outbreak in Bangladesh.Keywords: Dengue, Severe dengue, Predictors, Serotype, Outbreak, Bangladesh

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Our comprehensive search on PubMed, Medline, Embase, Scopus encompassed publications from resource-limited contexts in English from March 16, 2016, to March 16, 2026. To identify relevant research regarding progression to severe dengue, we used the keywords (“dengue” OR “dengue fever” OR “DF”) AND (“severe dengue” OR “dengue with warning signs” OR “dengue hemorrhagic fever” OR “dengue shock syndrome” OR “shock” OR “bleeding” OR “organ failure” OR “pleural effusion” OR “ascites”) AND (“clinical features” OR “risk factors” OR “predictors” OR “serotype” OR “DENV-1” OR “DENV-2” OR “DENV-3” OR “DENV-4” OR “IgG” OR “IgM” OR “cytokine” OR “immune response”) AND (“outcome” OR “survival” OR “death” OR “ICU admission” OR “hospital stay” OR “readmission” OR “rehospitalization”). Previous research links clinical warning signs, hematological and biochemical differences, secondary infections, and circulating viral serotypes to severe dengue. The results vary between country contexts, specifically regarding the relative impact of unpredictable clinical course, serial laboratory changes, serotype variation, and immune response patterns. Evidence from Bangladesh includes mostly cross-sectional analysis, surveillance records, and outbreak summaries. Few longitudinal data exist on the factors that cause hospitalized dengue patients to advance from warning signs to severe dengue.

Added value of this study

This research presents longitudinal evidence regarding the progression from dengue with warning signs to severe dengue in hospitalized patients during a major outbreak in Bangladesh in 2023. In contrast to earlier research predominantly reliant on cross-sectional or retrospective data, this study involved a longitudinal approach, tracking patients over time to assess the progression of their clinical conditions and laboratory findings.

The primary strength of the study lies in its comprehensive evaluation of clinical features, serial laboratory parameters, dengue serotypes, and immune markers, including IgG and IgM. This provides a deeper knowledge of the progression of severe dengue compared to studies that depend solely on symptoms or standard laboratory results.

The investigation holds significant importance in Bangladesh, where the 2023 outbreak was characterized by a substantial case burden, elevated mortality rates, and a notable shift in circulating serotypes, including the re-emergence of DENV-2. The results present context-specific evidence that could enhance clinical monitoring and outbreak response in regions where dengue is endemic.

Implications of all the available evidence

The evidence presented, along with the results of this study, indicates that the progress to severe dengue is influenced by an interaction of clinical characteristics, laboratory alterations, immune responses, and the presence of circulating serotypes. The rapid progression of severe disease underscores the necessity for vigilant observation of patients exhibiting warning signs, especially while in the initial critical phase.

In outbreak scenarios, identifying high-risk clinical and laboratory patterns can enhance triage processes, inpatient monitoring, timely escalation of care, and referral decisions. Growing understanding of serotype circulation and immune response at the public health level may contribute to the improvement of surveillance, preparedness, and clinical management strategies.

In Bangladesh and other regions where dengue is endemic, these findings advocate for a more risk-oriented strategy in hospital care and emphasize the importance of ongoing patient evaluation beyond the initial diagnosis.

Introduction

Since its global emergence, dengue has become a major public health concern, with an estimated 150 million patients reported annually.1 In response to its widespread transmission, the World Health Organization (WHO) has declared dengue as a public health emergency in more than 100 countries.2 In 2023, South and Southeast Asia were identified as dengue “hotspot”, accounting for over 50 percent of global patients.3 Tropical regions in South Asia are particularly vulnerable as climate change and heavy monsoon rainfall creates favorable conditions for mosquito breeding, lefiading to seasonal increases in hospital admissions from this rapidly spreading vector-borne disease.4,5

Bangladesh has experienced recurring dengue outbreaks, with the first epidemic identified in 2000, followed by increasing patients of dengue infections in subsequent years. Major outbreaks of dengue fever have been identified in 2019 and 2023, with the latter identified as the largest outbreak, with 321,000 patients of dengue fever, including 1705 deaths, which translates to a 0.5% case fatality rate.6,7 Previous studies have identified differences in the prevalence of dengue virus serotypes in various years of outbreaks. Previous dengue outbreaks were commonly associated with DENV-3, whereas recent surveillance studies have identified an increase in the prevalence of DENV-2, in addition to DENV-3.5,8 These shifts in circulating serotypes, together with demographic and environmental factors, may influence patterns of disease severity in Bangladesh.

To capture the diverse clinical manifestations of dengue fever, WHO revised its classification in 2019. The updated system includes dengue with warning signs (DWS) and severe dengue (SD), categorized according to clinical features with or without laboratory findings.9,10 The dengue virus (DENV) is transmitted through the bite of an infected female Aedes aegypti or Aedes albopictus mosquito and causes viremia during both asymptomatic and symptomatic phases of infection.

Although numerous studies have evaluated clinical and laboratory predictors for severe dengue, most studies have assessed risk factors for dengue in all dengue patients, rather than specifically evaluating dengue in patients who already have warning signs.7,11 Despite hospitalization of patients presenting with warning signs, approximately 10% of dengue patients still progress to SD, regardless of treatment.11 Previous studies conducted in South and Southeast Asia often had limitations, as many studies were conducted in retrospective settings and many had limited prospective data to assess dynamic clinical progression in dengue fever.3 Various factors have been investigated for their potential role in influencing disease severity, including viral serotypes, host genetic susceptibility, comorbidities, and the number of prior dengue infections.1 There is limited evidence for clinical and laboratory parameters that predict progression from dengue with warning signs to severe dengue in hospitalized patients in Bangladesh, especially in an outbreak setting. Dysregulated host immune responses, particularly cytokine and chemokine imbalances, are thought to play a central role in the pathogenesis of severe dengue.12 We have selected following biomarkers based on their importance in dengue research as found in earlier studies. Serum IL-10, IFN-γ are elevated in patients in SD and can be used as potential biomarkers.12 In addition, CCL20 (Macrophage inflammatory protein-3) and CSF-2 (Granulocyte-Macrophage Colony –Stimulating Factor) are also key mediators involved in the severe inflammatory response during dengue virus (DENV) infection.13 This study addresses these gaps by using a prospective multi-hospital cohort design to evaluate clinical, laboratory, immunological and viral factors associated with progression to severe dengue during an outbreak setting.

This study aimed to investigate the factors associated with progression to SD in Bangladesh. It compared hospitalized dengue patients with warning signs to those with SD, based on clinical features, laboratory parameters, and circulating serotypes. Additionally, the study sought to identify factors independently associated with SD through comparative analysis of the two groups. A comprehensive understanding of these determinants is essential, as it may aid in the early identification of SD, reduction of fatality, and improvement of clinical outcomes among high-risk populations during the outbreaks.

Methods

We conducted a hospital-based longitudinal cohort study in four sites in Dhaka, Bangladesh, between January and November 2024. The study sites included DNCC Dedicated COVID-19 Hospital, Mugda Medical College and Hospital (MuMCH), Dhaka Medical College Hospital (DMCH) and Dhaka Hospital of the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b). The first three are dengue dedicated government hospitals, while the latter is a non-profit diarrheal disease hospital where patients receive treatment free of cost. We enrolled both adults (≥18 years) and children (≥1 year) from all four hospitals who met the eligibility criteria. The inclusion criteria were: documented fever for 2–6 days and a reactive NS1and/or Dengue IgM test, along with one or more warning signs, such as severe abdominal pain or tenderness; vomiting >3 times/day; diarrhea >3 times/day; clinical fluid accumulation; mucosal bleed, lethargy or restlessness; liver enlargement >2 cm; increased hematocrit >10% accompanied by rapid decrease in platelet to 50,000/cmm or less. We excluded patients who were critically ill requiring immediate life-saving resuscitation, (e.g., cardiopulmonary resuscitation or assisted ventilation), inotropes for blood pressure stabilization, those who had already developed SD at the time of admission, or those who refused to provide written informed consent.

Screening of patients was conducted daily at the triage of the study sites from 8 a.m. to 2:30 p.m. Patients who arrived beyond this period were evaluated for eligibility in the ward on the following day.

Eligible participants were identified during admission to the inpatient ward. A dedicated study team comprising a study physician, nurse and health worker was responsible for the screening process at each hospital. Upon meeting the enrollment criteria, the caregiver or attendant was approached for informed written consent. Once consent was obtained, the patient was enrolled in the study. A detailed medical history was collected from each enrolled patient, and findings were documented in the case report form (CRF). Relevant data, including socio-demographic characteristics, household information, co-morbidities, and past medical history, were also collected and recorded. Study variables were obtained from hospital medical records and laboratory databases and from verbatim responses from the patients or caregivers. Demographic and clinical data (e.g., age, biological sex, symptoms such as lethargy, anorexia, and arthralgia) were extracted from physician-documented patients records at admission.

Study physicians were responsible for documenting vital signs, including any new signs of severity or warning signs, every 4 h in the CRFs. They followed patients until discharge or final outcome to confirm the classification of dengue severity and warning signs were assessed following the operational definitions of national guideline.14 A tourniquet test was performed at the time of enrollment. Fever was defined as an axillary temperature of 38 °C or higher.

Blood samples were collected from enrolled patients at all study sites (DMCH, MuMCH, and DNCC) and transported to the Clinical and Immunology Laboratory of icddr,b to ensure consistency in reference values. Samples collected from icddr,b patients were directly sent to the same laboratory for testing. Laboratory reports were delivered on the same day to the site physicians attending the patients in the wards. Blood samples were collected at three time points: at enrollment (T1), at discharge or upon clinical deterioration (T2), and on day 21 (±3 days) during the convalescent phase (T3). The volume of blood collected was up to 8 ml for children and up to 15 ml for adults. Serum samples were stored at −80 °C within 6 h of collection for subsequent analysis of serotypes, immunology and cytokines.

Molecular serotyping was conducted at the Emerging Infections & Parasitology Laboratory of the Infectious Diseases Division of icddr,b. RNA was isolated from 140 μL of serum using QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions. Extracted RNA samples were stored at −80 °C until batch wise real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed for dengue serotype identification. Detection of dengue serotype was carried out using the CDC-recommended qualitative RT-PCR assay targeting DENV-1 to DENV-4, as previously described.15 Briefly, multiplex RT-PCR was performed using 5 μL of RNA along with specific oligonucleotide primers and dual-labeled 5′-end fluorescent TaqMan probes. Thermal cycling was conducted on a CFX-96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, California, USA) under the following conditions: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min, enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 45 amplification cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Fluorescence signals were recorded at 60 °C. Thresholds were manually set for each run, and amplification curves with cycle threshold (Ct) values below 37 were considered positive.15,16

Molecular serotyping of current dengue infections was performed using RT-PCR, while the presence of IgM and IgG antibodies was assessed by ELISA. Prior dengue exposure was determined by IgG ELISA. Serotyping of previous dengue infections was not included in this study.

IgM and IgG capture ELISA was performed for the qualitative detection of IgM and IgG antibodies (DENV Detect™ by InBios, which is US FDA-approved) to DENV recombinant antigens (serotypes 1 to 4) in serum. The assay was intended for use in patients with clinical symptoms consistent with dengue fever or dengue hemorrhagic fever, including secondary infections. The complete kit included a 96-well strippable plate with all necessary reagents, controls and an instruction manual for interpreting as positive, negative, or equivocal.

2Serum or plasma specimens were stored at −80 °C for measurement of cytokine levels (IL-10, CCL-20, IFN-γ, and CSF-2) in dengue patients. Cytokine assays were performed and quantified according to the manufacturers’ instructions using kits from R&D systems, Abcam, and Sigma.

Chest x-ray and ultrasonography of chest as well as abdomen was done as part of confirmation of clinical assessment of fluid accumulation, including pleural effusion and ascites.

At enrolment (day 1), patients were informed about the follow-up plan at discharge and at 21 days after enrolment. The study team requested that patients return to the respective hospital for a physical follow-up and collection of blood samples during the convalescent phase for IgM, IgG, and cytokines testing.

Rapid increase in hematocrit count: The laboratory warning signs used for study inclusion included a rapid increase in hematocrit of more than 10% from baseline.14

Rapid decrease in platelet count: he laboratory warning signs used for study inclusion included a rapid decrease in platelet count to ≤50,000 cells/mm3.14

The primary outcome was development of SD, defined as the presence of any of the following: severe plasma leakage (shock or fluid accumulation with respiratory distress); severe bleeding; severe organ involvement (liver, brain, heart or other organs); or moderate to severe metabolic and electrolyte derangements.

The secondary outcomes included the identification of circulating dengue virus serotypes (DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, DENV- 4) and the characterization of primary and secondary immune responses using IgM and IgG profiles. The study also evaluated potential laboratory indicators, such as hematocrit, white blood cell and platelet counts, coagulation profile, C-reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, serum albumin, liver enzymes, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) to assess the differences between non-severe and SD patients. Radiological findings (chest x-ray and ultrasonography of chest and abdomen) were also evaluated for patients who progressed to SD.

Several steps were taken to minimize potential sources of bias. To reduce selection bias, all consecutive patients meeting the inclusion criteria during hospital admission hours were enrolled throughout the study period. Standardized case definitions based on World Health Organization (WHO) dengue guidelines were used to minimize misclassification.9 Information bias was reduced by extracting data from routinely recorded hospital records and laboratory databases, which follow standardized measurement protocols. To address confounding, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed, adjusting for clinically relevant variables selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined using a two-step approach to ensure adequate precision and statistical power for detecting factors associated with progression to SD. Considering an anticipated 5% progression rate, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, and an expected 10% attrition rate, the calculated sample size was 354. Data was managed in an electronic platform REDCap. Data management and statistical analyses were performed using STATA (version 16.0) and SPSS for Windows (version 26.0; SPSS Inc, Chicago, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Categorical variables were presented using frequency (percentage), while continuous variables expressed as mean with standard deviation (sd), or median with interquartile range (IQR) depending on their distribution. Statistical plots were also generated to visualize data distribution and identify potential outliers. Categorical variables were compared using chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate, while continuous variables were analyzed using Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as applicable.

To assess the association between binary outcomes (SD versus DWS) and predictor variables such as age, biological sex, clinical status at admission, and laboratory findings, multivariable logistic regression was performed. Variables with a p-value <0.05 in univariable analysis, along with clinically relevant variables, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of progression to SD. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Missing data was minimal; 351 of 354 patients were included in the final regression model, indicating that only three patients (<1%) had missing values. Given this low level of missingness, a complete-case analysis was performed without imputation. Given the limited number of outcome events, the number of variables included in the final model was restricted to avoid overfitting, following the events-per-variable rule.

Model performance was assessed through both discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and additional calibration metrics, including mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE).

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (comprised of Research Review Committee and Ethical Review Committee) of the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) (approval no. PR- 23077, version 4.0, dated 20-12-2023). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06697041). Approvals were also obtained from all participating hospitals to conduct the observational study without interfering with patient management during hospitalization.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation or writing of the manuscript.

Results

A total of 3135 patients were screened from January and November 2024, of whom 354 (11.3%) laboratory-confirmed dengue patients were enrolled. Among them, 292 (82.5%) were classified as DWS, and 62 (17.5%) progressed to SD (Supplementary Figure S1). The median time to progression from enrolment to SD was 3 days (IQR: 2–4) during hospitalization.

The mean and median ages were slightly higher in the SD group compared to the DWS group. Most patients in both groups were between 16- and 40-years age, indicating a predominance of working-age individuals among the hospitalized patients (Supplementary Table S1). Within this age group, the distribution of occupations included laborers (13%), business workers (16%), students (7%), service holders (32%), housewives (26%), and others/unemployed (3%) (Table 1).

A significant difference was observed in sex distribution, with overall male predominance. In the SD group, 66% were male and 34% female, while in the DWS group, 78% were male and 22% female (Table 1).

Before hospital admission, fever was reported by all patients. Other common symptoms included headache, joint pain, fatigue, nausea, loss of appetite, diarrhea, and bleeding in the SD and DWS groups. However, after admission, several clinical features were significantly more frequent among patients who progressed to SD (Supplementary Figure S2).

Two serious complications related to fluid accumulation showed significant differences between the groups. Pleural effusion occurred in 76% of the SD group versus 22% of the DWS group, while ascites was found in 52% of the SD group compared to 16% of the DWS group. Bleeding-related symptoms were similar across both groups (Supplementary Table S1).

Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of diabetes was higher among SD patients (18%) than DWS patients (9%), which was statistically significant (p = 0.039). Asthma was also more frequent in the SD group, Supplementary Table S2).

Laboratory parameters were evaluated at admission, at discharge or clinical deterioration, and on day 21 follow-up. Significant differences were found between SD and DWS patients. Other laboratory parameters (Fig. 1) showed no significant differences between groups.

Among hospitalized patients with confirmed dengue infection by RT-PCR (n = 289), DENV-2 was the most prevalent serotype, detected in 90.3% of patients (Fig. 3). This overwhelming predominance suggests DENV-2 was the primary circulating serotype during the study period. Among the remaining infections, DENV-3 accounted for 6.6%, while DENV-1 and DENV-4 each represented slightly over 1%. Additionally, two patients of co-infection with DENV-2 and DENV-3 were identified.

IgG and IgM antibody responses were measured in 916 and 1025 serum samples, respectively, collected at three different time points (T1, T2, and T3). ELISA results were categorized as positive, negative and equivocal based on kit instructions. Positive IgG responses increased steadily across the three time points: 90.9% at T1, 93.5% at T2, and 96.6% at T3. For IgM antibodies, the corresponding positive response rates were 63.6% at T1, 81.0% at T2, and 62.3% at T3 (Supplementary Table S7).

When stratified by the dengue patients by disease severity, differences in IgM responses across time points remained significant only among non-severe patients, with positive rates of 65%, 82%, and 62% at T1, T2, and T3, respectively. IgG positivity was 90%, 93%, and 97% at T1, T2, and T3, respectively, while among severe patients, the corresponding rates were 93%, 97%, and 96%. No statistically significant differences across time points were observed for IgG in either severity group (Supplementary Figure S4).

The multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several factors independently associated with progression to SD. After adjustment for potential confounders, significant associations were found with diabetes mellitus, joint pain or arthralgia, anorexia, lethargy, pleural effusion, and prolonged APTT at admission had higher odds of developing SD (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study evaluated clinical, demographic, and laboratory factors associated with progression from DWS to SD among hospitalized patients. We observed a 17% occurrence of SD, notably higher than the 5% prevalence reported nationally among all detected patients.7 This discrepancy can be explained by several factors. National data include all laboratory-confirmed dengue patients reported through the dengue dashboard, regardless of hospitalization status, whereas our study population consisted solely of hospitalized patients, who are inherently at higher risk for severe disease. Additionally, this investigation was conducted in a dengue-dedicated hospital located in urban setting, both of which likely capture a higher proportion of severe patients compared to national estimates that encompass both urban and rural data.5,6,17 The national dengue dashboard also reports a higher burden of SD in Dhaka division compared with other regions, further substantiating these findings.18

Consistent with earlier reports, most hospitalized patients in our cohort were young adults aged 16–40 years identifying a demographic that may warrant targeted prevention and intervention strategies.19 Although female sex was not retained as a significant predictor in the adjusted analysis, its association in the unadjusted model suggests potential underlying gender-related and socio-behavioral differences in exposure or care-seeking behavior. This pattern aligns with prior literature indicating that men may have greater exposure risk and distinct healthcare-seeking behaviors compared to women.7,20 Conversely, our findings underscore the importance of exploring socio-behavioral factors influencing disease severity in women, particularly among housewives, who comprised a higher proportion of SD patients despite no independent association in regression analysis.

During the study period, DENV-2 was the predominant circulating serotype. Clinical features, such as cough, anorexia, joint pain, and lethargy were significantly associated with progression from DWS to SD. Among laboratory and comorbidity-related variables, prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and the presence of diabetes mellitus emerged as significant predictors of severe disease after admission.21,22 Notably, the median time from onset of SD to clinical stabilization was approximately two days, highlighting the critical importance of early recognition and close inpatient monitoring to reduce morbidity.

Clinical symptoms prior to admission demonstrated considerable overlap between severity groups, except for fever, which was universal.11 At admission, patients with SD presented more frequently with anorexia, headache, diarrhea, cough, restlessness, and lethargy. The independent association of cough and SD, may indicate early pulmonary involvement or plasma leakage a hypothesis supported by the observed relationship between pleural effusion, drowsiness and severe disease.17 These clinical indicators could assist in the prompt identification of patients at elevated risk of progression to SD.

Diabetes was associated with SD in an unadjusted model but lost statistical significance after adjustment. This observation aligns with evidence suggesting that metabolic dysregulation impairs immune function and vascular integrity, potentially exacerbating disease progression. While asthma exhibited an increased odds ratio for SD, the association did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample size. Nonetheless, the clustering of multiple warning signs among patients with asthma warrants further investigation.11

Laboratory findings revealed characteristic abnormalities associated with SD, including thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated liver enzymes (SGPT), and increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, reflecting multi-organ involvement and systemic inflammation. Prolonged APTT, identified as a significant predictor, likely reflects coagulopathy intrinsic to the pathophysiology of SD.23,24

The predominance of DENV-2 during the study period reflects a major serotype shift from 2023, when DENV-2 replaced DENV-3 as the dominant circulating strain.16 This resurgence mirrors prior epidemic cycles in which DENV-2 circulation was linked to greater clinical severity, reinforcing its critical role in disease outcomes.17,20 In this cohort, cytokine profiling revealed distinct immune responses between SD and DWS patients, suggesting differential immune regulation across disease severities. Elevated levels of the chemokine CCL20 and cytokine CSF2 in DWS patients indicate enhanced recruitment and activation of innate immune cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, which are pivotal for early viral clearance.19,20 This augmented innate response may facilitate more effective antibody-mediated immunity, thereby preventing progression to severe disease. In contrast, consistent with previous studies, elevated levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, were observed in severe dengue patients.21 IL-10 is thought to counterbalance excessive inflammation during the acute phase of infection and has been identified as a predictive biomarker of dengue hemorrhagic fever.25,26 The absence of significant variation in IgG serostatus may be attributed to the slower kinetics and long-term persistence of IgG antibodies.27 Additionally, prior dengue exposure (secondary infection) may result in pre-existing IgG at baseline (T1), thereby fewer observable changes across time points.28

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in tertiary hospitals in Dhaka, which may limit the generalizability of findings to rural or peripheral areas with different epidemiological characteristics. Second, the low prevalence of certain comorbidities restricted assessment of their role in SD progression. Third, only hospitalized patients were enrolled, potentially introducing selection bias towards more severe patients. Fourth, tests for detecting co-infection, such as COVID or influenza was not performed during the study period. Finally, although all participants were NS1-positive at enrollment, some later-presenting severe patients tested serotype-negative by RT-PCR, likely due to viral RNA decline beyond the acute phase.

Despite these limitations, the study possesses notable strengths. Its prospective design and adequate sample size enhance the reliability of findings. Inclusion of serotyping provided valuable epidemiological insights into circulating dengue strains during the study period. Furthermore, systematic clinical monitoring alongside assessment of inflammatory biomarkers enabled a comprehensive understanding of host–pathogen interactions and the immunopathogenesis of disease severity.

In conclusion, this study highlights key demographic, clinical, and laboratory predictors of severe dengue. Early warning signs include arthralgia, diabetes mellitus, anorexia, lethargy, pleural effusion and prolonged APTT. I Recognizing these indicators can help hospitals provide timely care. Integrating hospital-based management with community-level preventive has the potential to reduce dengue-related illness and deaths.
