Therapeutic effects of silibinin in preclinical animal models of gastrointestinal cancers: a systematic review and meta‑analysis
Authors: Davood Mohammadi, Amir Hossein Aghayan, Diba Mohammadi, Zahra Jamalpoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastrointestinal (GI) cancers represent a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Silibinin, the primary active constituent of milk thistle, possesses anti-proliferative, pro-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory properties. This study systematically evaluates the therapeutic effects of silibinin in preclinical animal models of GI cancers.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Studies were selected based on PICOS criteria, and risk of bias was assessed using the SYRCLE tool. Outcomes were pooled using a random-effects model and reported as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to address heterogeneity.ResultsFourteen studies met inclusion criteria. Silibinin significantly reduced tumor volume (SMD = -2.72) and tumor weight (SMD = -1.86), demonstrating promising antitumor efficacy. Notably, body weight was not significantly altered (SMD = 0.53, 95% CI: −0.41 to 1.48) and diet consumption showed a significant increase (SMD = 1.09), together indicating that silibinin exerts its anticancer effects without compromising nutritional status suggesting preliminary tolerability in animal models. At the molecular level, PCNA expression was significantly suppressed (SMD = -4.31), reflecting a consistent reduction in cellular proliferation, while apoptosis was significantly elevated (SMD = 6.00), confirming pro-apoptotic activity across GI cancer models.ConclusionSilibinin significantly suppresses tumor growth and promotes apoptosis in preclinical GI cancer models while demonstrating encouraging preliminary tolerability. These findings support its potential as an adjunctive anticancer agent; however, further clinical studies are warranted to validate these results and enable more definitive conclusions regarding its therapeutic efficacy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16526-w.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers refer to malignancies of the digestive tract, ranging from the oral cavity and esophagus, through the stomach, small intestine, and colorectum, to the liver, bile ducts, pancreas, and appendix [1, 2]. Collectively, they account for over a quarter of global cancer diagnoses and more than one-third of cancer-related deaths [3, 4]. Among these, colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide [5, 6], while gastric cancer is the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer death [7–9]. Liver cancer also poses a substantial public health challenge, with incidence predicted to increase by more than 50% by 2040 [10].

Despite important advances in multimodal care, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy, GI cancers remain associated with poor outcomes, particularly in advanced stages [11, 12].

Current treatments suffer from shortcomings, such as substantial toxicity, high cost, and limited efficacy in advanced disease. For example, metastatic gastric cancer still has a median survival of less than one year, and cytotoxic chemotherapy provides only modest survival benefit at the expense of considerable toxicity [13, 14]. Biomarker-guided therapies targeting HER2, MSI, and PD-L1, such as trastuzumab, ramucirumab, and immune-checkpoint inhibitors, benefit only a small subset of patients and often compromised by primary or acquired resistance [15, 16]. Similarly, colorectal cancer management remains dependent mainly on surgery and conventional chemotherapy, which are associated with high toxicity and poor outcomes in late-stage disease [13–16]. Taken together, these limitations underscore the need for complementary or alternative therapeutic with broader applicability, higher efficacy, and lower toxicity. Natural compounds are of particular interest in this context.

Milk thistle (Silybum marianum), a member of the Asteraceae family, has long been used in traditional medicine [17, 18]. This plant contains a bioactive complex called silymarin, in which the flavonolignan silibinin is the main active component, constituting approximately 70–80% of its composition and largely responsible for its medicinal effects [18, 19]. Geographically, Silybum marianum is native to southern Europe, southern Russia, Asia Minor, and northern Africa. Furthermore, it has been widely naturalized in North and South America, as well as in South Australia, typically thriving in warm and dry soils. Today, the commercial supply of the plant predominantly originates from cultivated sources in countries such as China, Argentina, Romania, and several Mediterranean nations [19]. Silibinin possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, and anticancer activities, including induction of apoptosis, inhibition of angiogenesis, and suppression of tumor cell proliferation and migration across multiple cancer types, including GI malignancies [17, 20, 21]. Importantly, silibinin’s underlying mechanisms—such as the suppression of chronic mucosal inflammation (e.g., via NF-κB and STAT3 pathways) and the modulation of aberrant lipid metabolism and oxidative stress— specifically match the distinct inflammatory and metabolic microenvironments that drive gastrointestinal carcinogenesis [22–24]. Preclinical studies suggest that silibinin could serve as a promising candidate for cancer prevention or adjunctive therapy [20, 21].

In animal models, tumor volume is a standard, objective endpoint for evaluating anticancer efficacy [25]. It provides a dynamic, three-dimensional measure of tumor growth, often correlating with clinical outcomes, and is commonly calculated using the formula V = 0.5 × (length² × width) [25]. Compared with tumor weight, volume measurements enable more sensitive and longitudinal assessments, facilitating cross-study comparisons [26, 27].

Despite increasing preclinical evidence indicating that silibinin has anticancer effects in various tumor types, the available data remain fragmented and dependent on experimental conditions. Individual preclinical trials investigating silibinin in GI models frequently rely on small sample sizes, which has occasionally led to conflicting data regarding its efficacy. Furthermore, there is a distinct lack of pooled dose-response analyses to consolidate these disparate findings. At this juncture, a systematic review and meta-analysis of this specific preclinical space is necessary to overcome the limitations of individual small-scale studies, resolve conflicting reports, and provide a unified consensus. In particular, silibinin’s effect on tumor volume in animal models of gastrointestinal cancers has not been systematically quantified, and the conditions under which silibinin is most effective remain unclear. A comprehensive, consistent, and evidence-based understanding of the antitumor activity of this compound is essential before initiating clinical trials. Therefore, the present study, in the form of a systematic review and meta-analysis, was conducted with the aim of critically evaluating the therapeutic effects of silibinin monotherapy on tumor volume in preclinical animal models of gastrointestinal cancers to provide integrated evidence and clarify its effectiveness. Given the increasing attention to natural and non-toxic compounds as adjuvant or alternative treatments for conventional chemotherapies, conducting this systematic review of preclinical findings can guide future research in the field of translational and clinical studies towards safer and more effective anticancer strategies.

Methods

Reporting guideline

Adherence to the PRISMA 2020 reporting framework guided the design, conduct, and documentation of this review, ensuring that all methodological procedures and synthesized findings were presented with maximal transparency and rigor [28].

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility of primary studies was determined based on several fundamental benchmarks [29]. Regarding the study population, we included original investigations using mammalian animal models of gastrointestinal cancer, irrespective of age or sex. Studies involving human subjects, non-mammalian species, or strictly in vitro environments were omitted. The intervention of interest was the administration of silibinin. Studies evaluating silibinin as monotherapy across any dosage or route were eligible. Studies involving combination therapies or co-administered pharmacological agents were generally excluded unless the silibinin-alone arm was reported separately and could be extracted independently for analysis, with an appropriate control group available. Control groups were required to consist of untreated animals with gastrointestinal malignancies. The primary endpoint for inclusion was tumor volume, reflecting oncogenic progression. Secondary endpoints encompassed tumor weight, body weight (as a safety metric), diet consumption (metabolic tolerance), and molecular markers including PCNA (cell proliferation) and the TUNEL assay (apoptosis). Studies failing to report the primary outcome were excluded. We restricted our scope to original, peer-reviewed preclinical controlled trials, excluding non-original formats such as reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts to ensure methodological integrity.

Search and selection

To identify relevant literature, a comprehensive and multi-database inquiry was executed across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search architecture was specifically tailored for each repository, integrating Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) with free-text keywords to maximize the sensitivity of the retrieval process (the full search strings are documented in Supplementary Data S1). The screening process was conducted in two stages: initially, two investigators (DA.M and D.M) independently vetted titles and abstracts using the Rayyan platform [30]. Subsequently, the same duo performed an autonomous full-text appraisal of the shortlisted manuscripts to ensure alignment with the predefined eligibility criteria. Any consensus gaps or interpretative discordances were adjudicated through consultation with a senior author (A.H.A).

Data collection

All eligible studies were systematically reviewed, and relevant information was extracted independently by two investigators (DA.M and D.M)using a predefined data extraction framework. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (A.H.A). The collected data included bibliographic details (first author and year of publication), cancer subtype, characteristics of the animal model, sex and/or age of the animals, route and dosage of administration, method of tumor induction, and the timing of outcome evaluation. In addition, quantitative data required for meta-analysis were obtained for both primary and secondary outcomes. Specifically, the number of animals allocated to control and intervention groups, mean outcome values, and corresponding standard deviations (SDs) were recorded. When numerical data were not explicitly reported in the text or tables, values were estimated from graphical representations using WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.6). This approach ensured comprehensive data retrieval while maintaining analytical accuracy.

Risk of bias assessment

To evaluate the internal validity of the selected studies, two independent investigators (DA.M and D.M) rigorously appraised the risk of bias utilizing the Systematic Review Center for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) framework [31]. Each trial was systematically scrutinized and stratified into categories of high, low, or unclear risk of bias across predefined domains. Any inter-reviewer discordance during the assessment phase was reconciled through deliberative discussion (Z.J). In cases where a bilateral agreement could not be reached, the corresponding author served as an arbiter to facilitate a final consensus.

Data analysis

Data synthesis was performed by calculating the Hedges’ g standardized mean difference (SMD) to harmonize disparate measurement scales across the included preclinical trials, while accounting for potential small-sample bias [32]. The precision of these estimates was determined using 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The clinical relevance of the observed effects was benchmarked against established criteria, where SMDs of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were indicative of small, moderate, and large therapeutic impacts, respectively. Recognizing the inherent biological and methodological diversity across the included animal studies, a random-effects model was prespecified for all analyses to account for potential variation in the true effect sizes across studies. This choice was based on conceptual and methodological differences among studies rather than solely on statistical heterogeneity indicators such as I², as model selection should not be determined exclusively by the magnitude of observed heterogeneity [33, 34]. Inter-study inconsistency was rigorously quantified using the Cochran’s Q test and the 𝐼2 statistic, with 𝐼2 values exceeding 50% signifying substantial heterogeneity. To further explore the drivers of this variance, particularly regarding the dose-response relationship, a random-effects meta-regression was conducted utilizing the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator. Additionally, categorical sources of heterogeneity were investigated through subgroup analyses. The stability of the pooled findings was verified via leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to ensure no single study disproportionately influenced the overall outcome. Potential reporting bias was examined through a combined approach involving funnel plot visualization, Begg’s and Egger’s statistical tests, and the trim‑and‑fill adjustment method [35, 36]. All computational workflows were executed in STATA version 14.2, with a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 defining statistical significance.

Results

Study selection process

The study identification and selection process are summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). A comprehensive search of the predefined electronic databases yielded 3,639 records, including 1,157 from Web of Science, 836 from Scopus, 918 from PubMed, and 539 from Embase. After removing 2,199 duplicate entries, the remaining records underwent title and abstract screening by two independent reviewers. During this stage, 1,387 articles were excluded because they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria.

Subsequently, the full texts of 53 potentially relevant articles were retrieved and assessed in detail. After a thorough evaluation, 14 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final quantitative synthesis. The remaining full‑text articles were excluded for several reasons, including insufficient or non‑extractable data, outcomes not aligned with the predefined endpoints, co‑administration of silibinin with other compounds, or the use of experimental models that did not satisfy the eligibility criteria.

Study characteristics

Among the reviewed articles, fourteen met all the predefined criteria and were included in this meta-analysis (Table 1). Of these, five studies investigated the effects of silibinin in colorectal cancer models, five examined hepatocellular carcinoma, two focused on pancreatic cancer, one evaluated cholangiocarcinoma, and one assessed esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. The articles were published between 2009 and 2023. Six of the studies were conducted in China, five in the United States, two in India, and one each in France and South Korea. The duration of the studies evaluating the effects of silibinin on cancer models ranged from 14 days to 11 weeks.

Animal models

All of the included studies were conducted using mouse models. Eight studies employed athymic (nu/nu) nude mice, five used BALB/c athymic (nu/nu) nude mice, and one study utilized C57BL/6J mice. Regarding the sex of the animals, eight studies were performed on male mice and two on female mice, while in four studies the sex of the mouse models was not reported.

Route of administration

In eleven of the studies, mice bearing cancer models were treated orally with silibinin. One study utilized intravenous (IV) injection, and another employed peritumoral injection for silibinin administration. Furthermore, one study did not specify the method of silibinin administration. Because only two non oral studies were available and their experimental contexts were not sufficiently comparable, no subgroup analysis by route of administration was performed.

The dosage of silibinin varied among the included studies. In those studies employing oral administration, doses ranged from 80 mg/kg to 500 mg/kg of the mice’s body weight, and in one study silibinin was administered at a concentration of 1% w/w (weight/weight). In the study using peritumoral injection, doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg were applied, whereas in the study using intravenous administration, a dose of 200 mg/kg was used.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the fourteen included studies was assessed using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool (Fig. 2). Adequate sequence generation was reported in 12 of the 14 studies (85.7%), while the remaining two studies (14.3%) provided insufficient details and were therefore rated as unclear. Baseline characteristics were comparably well reported, with 12 studies (85.7%) classified as low risk and two (14.3%) as unclear.

Fig. 2Methodological quality appraisal of the included studies conducted using the SYRCLE risk-of-bias tool

Methodological quality appraisal of the included studies conducted using the SYRCLE risk-of-bias tool

Allocation concealment was not explicitly described in any of the included studies; consequently, all studies (14/14, 100%) were judged as having an unclear risk for this domain. Randomization of housing conditions was adequately reported in 10 studies (71.4%), whereas four studies (28.6%) lacked sufficient methodological detail. Similarly, randomization of outcome assessment was considered low risk in 12 studies (85.7%) and unclear in two (14.3%).

Blinding procedures were rarely documented. Neither blinding of housing nor blinding of outcome assessment was reported in the included studies, resulting in all studies (14/14, 100%) being rated as unclear for these domains. Regarding attrition bias, incomplete outcome data were assessed as low risk in 12 studies (85.7%) and unclear in two (14.3%). In contrast, selective outcome reporting demonstrated strong methodological transparency, with all studies (14/14, 100%) classified as low risk.

Based on the overall SYRCLE judgment, seven studies (50.0%) were considered to have a low overall risk of bias, three studies (21.4%) were categorized as unclear, and four studies (28.6%) were judged as having a high risk of bias. Overall, while several domains, particularly sequence generation, baseline comparability, and outcome reporting—showed generally favorable methodological quality, the lack of reported allocation concealment and blinding procedures represents a consistent limitation across the included preclinical studies.

Primary outcome

Tumor volume is one of the most widely used indicators in preclinical oncology research because it enables longitudinal monitoring of tumor growth dynamics throughout the experimental period. Tumor volume allows for repeated measurements in living animals and therefore provides a continuous assessment of treatment efficacy and tumor progression over time. Consequently, this parameter offers valuable insight into the temporal antitumor activity of therapeutic agents. In the present meta-analysis, tumor volume was designated as the primary outcome to evaluate the inhibitory effect of silibinin on tumor growth across preclinical models of gastrointestinal cancers.

Fourteen independent studies were included in the quantitative synthesis for this outcome, comprising 157 animals in the silibinin-treated groups and 154 animals in the control groups. The pooled analysis demonstrated that silibinin administration significantly reduced tumor volume compared with untreated controls (SMD = − 2.72, 95% CI: −3.42 to − 1.83) (Fig. 3A). However, moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 68.6%), indicating variability in the magnitude of treatment effects among different experimental conditions.

Subgroup analyses according to cancer type revealed that the tumor-suppressive effect of silibinin was consistently observed across multiple gastrointestinal malignancies (Fig. 3B). In colorectal cancer models, five studies demonstrated a significant reduction in tumor volume (SMD = − 2.63, 95% CI: −3.68 to − 2.05; I² = 49.1%). Similarly, in hepatic cancer models, the pooled estimate from five studies showed a pronounced inhibitory effect on tumor growth (SMD = − 2.99, 95% CI: −4.02 to − 1.97; I² = 65.6%). In pancreatic cancer models, two studies also indicated a beneficial effect favoring silibinin (SMD = − 1.27, 95% CI: −2.26 to − 0.28), although heterogeneity remained moderate (I² = 60.0%). In addition, single-study evidence was available for esophageal cancer and cholangiocellular carcinoma models. The study on esophageal cancer reported a substantial reduction in tumor volume following silibinin treatment (SMD = − 5.82, 95% CI: −8.01 to − 3.62). Likewise, the cholangiocellular carcinoma model demonstrated a notable inhibitory effect (SMD = − 3.51, 95% CI: −5.49 to − 1.53). Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of studies available for these cancer types.

A further subgroup analysis was conducted based on the overall methodological quality of the included studies (Fig. 3C). Experiments judged as having a low risk of bias demonstrated a pronounced reduction in tumor volume (SMD = − 2.42, 95% CI: −3.07 to − 1.76) with moderate between‑study heterogeneity (I² = 41.7%). In contrast, studies classified as high or unclear risk of bias showed a somewhat larger pooled effect size (SMD = − 2.99, 95% CI: −4.00 to − 1.98), although this estimate was accompanied by substantially greater heterogeneity (I² = 78.6%). Despite the slightly stronger effect observed among studies with less certain methodological rigor, the consistency of the direction of effect across both strata suggests that the overall tumor‑suppressive activity of silibinin is not predominantly driven by lower‑quality evidence, and that potential overestimation in studies with higher risk of bias did not materially distort the pooled findings.

To investigate potential dose-response relationships and sources of heterogeneity, a meta-regression analysis was conducted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, with silibinin dose (mg/kg) as the covariate (Fig. 3D). The results indicated no statistically significant association between the administered dose of silibinin and the observed reduction in tumor volume (Coefficient = -0.000302, 95% CI: -0.0047295 to 0.0041255, P = 0.887). This suggests that the variability in treatment effects on tumor volume cannot be attributed to differences in silibinin dosage among the included studies. Furthermore, the meta-regression model explained negligible residual variation, with a high residual I² of 58.46% and a negative adjusted R-squared, implying that dose did not account for a substantial proportion of the observed heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach indicated that the pooled effect estimate remained stable after sequential exclusion of each study (Fig. 3E). No influential outlier studies were identified, suggesting that the overall results were robust and not disproportionately driven by any individual experiment.

Secondary outcome

Tumor weight

Tumor weight represents a commonly employed endpoint in preclinical oncology studies, particularly in animal experiments, as it provides a direct quantitative measure of tumor burden at the termination of the experiment. Unlike longitudinal tumor volume measurements, tumor weight reflects the final mass of excised tumors and is therefore considered a reliable indicator of the cumulative antitumor effect of an intervention. In the present meta-analysis, tumor weight was analyzed as a secondary outcome to complement tumor volume measurements and to provide an endpoint-based assessment of the therapeutic efficacy of silibinin in preclinical models of gastrointestinal cancers.

A total of 11 independent studies contributed to the quantitative synthesis of this endpoint, encompassing 115 tumor-bearing animals in the treatment groups and 112 animals in the control groups. The pooled analysis demonstrated that administration of silibinin significantly reduced tumor weight compared with untreated controls, indicating a consistent inhibitory effect on tumor burden across gastrointestinal cancer models (SMD = -1.86, 95% CI: -2.35 to -1.37) (Fig. 4A). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 50.4%), suggesting a degree of variability in the magnitude of treatment effects across experimental settings.

Pooled analysis of the effect of silibinin on tumor weight in gastrointestinal cancer models. Subgroup analyses according to cancer type revealed that the tumor-reducing effect of silibinin was observed across several gastrointestinal malignancies, although effect estimates differed in magnitude and precision (Fig. 4B). In colorectal cancer models, based on five studies, silibinin markedly decreased tumor weight (SMD = -2.15, 95% CI: -2.81 to -1.49; I² = 39.8%). In hepatic cancer models, the pooled estimate from three studies likewise indicated a significant reduction in tumor weight (SMD = -1.42, 95% CI: -2.07 to -0.76; I² = 0.0%). For pancreatic cancer models, two studies suggested a beneficial effect favoring silibinin (SMD = -1.70, 95% CI: -3.24 to -0.17), although heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 79.9%). Additionally, the single available study on cholangiocarcinoma indicated a notable decrease in tumor weight (SMD = -2.56, 95% CI: -4.76 to -0.36). Nevertheless, the limited number of studies in some subgroups, particularly pancreatic cancer and cholangiocarcinoma, necessitates cautious interpretation of these subgroup-specific estimates.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, a meta-regression analysis was performed. The results suggested a slight positive trend between increasing silibinin dose and the magnitude of tumor weight reduction, implying that higher doses may be associated with stronger antitumor effects (Fig. 4C). However, this association did not reach statistical significance (β = 0.0021, P = 0.192), and the corresponding confidence interval crossed the null value. Visual inspection of the regression plot also suggested only a weak linear relationship. Considering the residual heterogeneity (I² ≈ 36%) and the relatively limited number of available studies, particularly at higher dose levels, the current evidence remains insufficient to establish a definitive dose–response relationship.

Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out procedure demonstrated that the pooled effect size remained stable after sequential removal of each individual study, indicating that the observed reduction in tumor weight was not driven by any single experiment (Fig. 4D).

Body weight

Body weight is a key supportive outcome in preclinical cancer studies, as it provides an indirect indicator of general health status, treatment tolerability, and potential toxicities during therapeutic interventions. Stabilization or modest increases in body weight following drug administration are often interpreted as signals of general tolerability and physiological resilience in tumor‑bearing animals.

The pooled results from the included studies showed no statistically significant difference in body weight between silibinin‑treated animals and controls (SMD = 0.53, 95% CI: − 0.41 to 1.48) (Fig. 5A), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 83.6%). Although the overall estimate was not significant, the direction of the pooled effect suggested a tendency toward weight maintenance or slight improvement in treated animals.

Subgroup analyses by cancer type reflected variable patterns (Fig. 5B). In colorectal cancer models (2 studies), silibinin was associated with a small, non‑significant increase in body weight (SMD = 0.76, 95% CI: − 0.06 to 1.58; I² = 45.6%). In pancreatic cancer models (2 studies), findings showed no clear difference between groups (SMD = − 0.17, 95% CI: − 1.83 to 1.50), alongside considerable heterogeneity (I² = 87.9%). In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (1 study), a pronounced increase in body weight was observed in the treatment group (SMD = 4.12, 95% CI: 2.20 to 6.03). Conversely, the single hepatocellular carcinoma study indicated a slight, non‑significant reduction in body weight following silibinin treatment (SMD = − 0.75, 95% CI: − 1.77 to 0.28). Given the limited number of studies within several cancer-specific subgroups, these estimates should be viewed as exploratory.

Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach showed that no individual study exerted an undue influence on the overall pooled estimate, indicating stability of the findings despite the heterogeneity observed (Fig. 5C).

Diet consumption

Diet consumption is an important supportive indicator in preclinical cancer experiments, as reduced food intake in tumor‑bearing animals often reflects systemic illness, treatment‑related toxicity, or impaired metabolic status. Conversely, preservation or improvement of dietary intake following therapeutic intervention may signal preliminary tolerability, adequate physiological function, and potentially a favorable response to treatment.

The pooled analysis demonstrated that silibinin administration was associated with a statistically significant increase in diet consumption compared with controls (SMD = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.04 to 2.14) (Fig. 5D), although heterogeneity across studies was substantial (I² = 81.2%). This overall trend suggests a potential beneficial effect of silibinin on feeding behavior in tumor‑bearing animals.

Subgroup analyses by cancer type revealed variable effects (Fig. 5E). In colorectal cancer models (2 studies), the pooled estimate indicated a non‑significant tendency toward higher diet consumption in the silibinin group (SMD = 0.96, 95% CI: − 0.74 to 2.67; I² = 85.2%). In pancreatic cancer models (2 studies), findings similarly showed a positive but non‑significant difference favoring silibinin (SMD = 1.27, 95% CI: − 0.40 to 2.94). As with the previous outcome, the limited number of studies within each subgroup warrants cautious interpretation of these findings.

Sensitivity analysis using the leave‑one‑out method indicated that no individual study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate, suggesting that the overall findings were stable despite the notable heterogeneity (Fig. 5F).

PCNA expression

Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA) is a well‑established marker of cellular proliferation and is widely used in preclinical oncology studies to quantify tumor growth dynamics at the molecular level. PCNA functions as a processivity factor for DNA polymerase δ during DNA replication and is highly expressed in cells undergoing active division. In tumor tissues, elevated PCNA levels reflect accelerated proliferation and aggressive biological behavior. Therefore, a reduction in PCNA expression following therapeutic intervention is considered an indicator of antiproliferative efficacy and suppression of tumor growth at the cellular level.

In the pooled analysis of included studies, silibinin treatment resulted in a significant decrease in PCNA expression compared with controls (SMD = − 4.31, 95% CI: − 6.23 to − 2.38) (Fig. 6A), although heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 88.8%). These findings suggest a substantial antiproliferative effect of silibinin across gastrointestinal cancer models.

Subgroup analysis identified four studies conducted in colorectal cancer models (Fig. 6B), which demonstrated a stronger effect size (SMD = − 5.14, 95% CI: − 6.99 to − 3.28; I² = 74.4%), indicating a marked reduction in proliferative activity within colorectal tumors. In contrast, the single study conducted in a pancreatic cancer model showed a smaller and statistically non‑significant effect (SMD = − 1.83, 95% CI: − 5.50 to 1.85). However, because the pancreatic subgroup was represented by only one study, the reliability of this estimate is limited and the finding should be interpreted with caution.

Sensitivity analysis revealed that the study by Dhanya Nambiar, which utilized the PANC‑1 cell‑derived pancreatic tumor model, acted as an influential outlier (Fig. 6C). Several methodological factors likely contributed to this disproportionate impact, including differences in tumor induction method, drug dosing schedule, variability in measurement techniques for PCNA quantification, and the unique biological behavior of PANC‑1 tumors (notably their slower proliferation rate and heterogeneous response to antiproliferative agents). When this study was removed, heterogeneity was markedly reduced and the pooled estimate became more stable, indicating that this particular dataset introduced considerable variability into the overall analysis.

TUNEL assay

The TUNEL (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling) assay is a widely applied histological method for detecting DNA fragmentation, a defining feature of cells undergoing late-stage apoptosis. In preclinical oncology research, the TUNEL signal reflects the extent of treatment‑induced apoptotic cell death within the tumor microenvironment. Because many antitumor agents exert their therapeutic effects by activating intrinsic or extrinsic apoptotic pathways, an increased TUNEL‑positive index is considered a valuable molecular indicator of antitumor activity and suppression of tumor cell survival. Thus, quantifying DNA fragmentation with TUNEL provides mechanistic insight into the cytotoxic potential of experimental therapeutics such as silibinin.

In the pooled analysis, silibinin treatment led to a substantial and statistically significant increase in apoptotic cell death (SMD = 6.00, 95% CI: 3.41 to 8.58) (Fig. 6D), although the degree of heterogeneity across studies was high (I² = 92.5%). This indicates a consistent pro‑apoptotic effect across models, but with considerable variability in magnitude.

Subgroup analysis revealed that the four studies performed in colorectal cancer models demonstrated a pronounced enhancement in apoptosis following silibinin administration (SMD = 8.70, 95% CI: 5.35 to 12.05; I² = 81.6%), suggesting a strong and reproducible apoptotic response in this cancer type (Fig. 6E). In contrast, the single study conducted in a pancreatic cancer model reported only a small and non‑significant increase in TUNEL positivity (SMD = 0.37, 95% CI: − 0.28 to 1.01). Given that some subgroups were represented by only one study, these estimates should be interpreted conservatively, as limited evidence restricts the precision and generalizability of the observed effects.

Sensitivity analysis identified the experiments conducted by Dhanya Nambiar, using both BxPC‑3 and PANC‑1 pancreatic cell‑derived tumor models, as influential outliers (Fig. 6F). Several methodological and biological considerations may explain their disproportionate effect on the overall model. These include the inherently variable apoptosis susceptibility of BxPC‑3 and PANC‑1 cells, differences in baseline apoptotic rates, potential deviations in TUNEL staining protocols, timing of tissue collection relative to peak apoptotic activity, and the unique pharmacodynamic behavior of silibinin in pancreatic models compared with colorectal tumors. Removal of these datasets resulted in a more stable pooled estimate, indicating that these studies contributed substantially to between‑study heterogeneity.

For tumor volume, visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed marked asymmetry, and both Begg’s and Egger’s statistical tests were significant (P < 0.05), indicating the presence of small‑study effects and potential publication bias. Nevertheless, application of the trim‑and‑fill method did not impute any additional studies and the pooled effect estimate remained unchanged (Fig. 7A). This pattern suggests that while statistical and graphical approaches highlight asymmetry, it may largely be attributable to methodological heterogeneity among studies rather than unaccounted publication bias.

For tumor weight, the funnel plot appeared broadly symmetrical; however, Begg’s and Egger’s tests were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Despite this, trim‑and‑fill analysis did not identify any missing studies and the pooled estimate remained unchanged, suggesting that the observed asymmetry may partly reflect interstudy heterogeneity rather than true publication bias (Fig. 7B).

For body weight, the funnel plot was approximately symmetrical and both Begg’s and Egger’s tests were non‑significant (P = 0.53 and P = 0.21). Trim‑and‑fill imputed one study, but the adjusted estimate remained non‑significant (SMD = 0.163, 95% CI: −0.868 to 1.19), indicating minimal influence of publication bias (Fig. 7C).

For diet consumption, the funnel plot showed noticeable asymmetry, with Begg’s test approaching significance (P = 0.06) and Egger’s test indicating significant asymmetry (P = 0.018). Trim‑and‑fill imputed two studies, and although the adjusted estimate remained directionally favorable to silibinin, statistical significance was lost (SMD = 0.378, 95% CI: −0.746 to 1.493), suggesting a potential influence of small‑study effects (Fig. 7D).

For PCNA expression, funnel plot asymmetry and significant Begg’s and Egger’s tests (P < 0.05) were observed; however, trim‑and‑fill did not identify missing studies and the pooled antiproliferative effect remained unchanged, implying that heterogeneity or methodological differences may explain the asymmetry (Fig. 7E).

For the TUNEL assay, funnel plot asymmetry was evident and both statistical tests were significant (P < 0.05). Trim‑and‑fill imputed four studies, after which the pooled estimate remained directionally favorable but lost statistical significance (SMD = 1.745, 95% CI: −0.873 to 4.363), suggesting that the magnitude of the pro‑apoptotic effect may be partly overestimated (Fig. 7F).

Overall, publication bias analyses showed a heterogeneous pattern across outcomes. While tumor volume and PCNA expression appeared robust to trim‑and‑fill adjustment and body weight showed minimal evidence of bias, diet consumption and particularly the TUNEL assay were more susceptible to potential small‑study effects. Therefore, although the overall evidence supports the antitumor, antiproliferative, and pro‑apoptotic effects of silibinin in preclinical gastrointestinal cancer models, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and substantial heterogeneity in some analyses.

Discussion

GI cancers, encompassing esophageal, gastric, liver, colorectal, pancreatic, gallbladder and biliary tract malignancies—account for over a quarter of all global cancer cases and remain a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [51, 52]. The global burden is substantial, with a lifetime risk of one in 12 for developing, and one in 16 for dying from, GI cancers [53]. Furthermore, this global health burden is continuously escalating; recent projections based on the GLOBOCAN 2022 database indicate that GI cancer incidence and mortality will nearly double by 2050, reaching 9.06 million new cases (85% increase) and 6.42 million deaths (93% increase). This escalation will be particularly devastating in lower-HDI (Human Development Index) countries, with pancreatic cancer leading the rise in incidence (+ 95%) and colorectal cancer driving the increase in mortality (+ 103%) [54]. The continued increase in incidence and mortality highlights the urgent need for targeted and tailored cancer planning [51, 53]. Furthermore, treating GI cancers is highly challenging due to their complex cellular diversity and altered metabolism within the tumor environment [52]. Together, these epidemiological and biological challenges highlight the necessity of exploring novel, effective therapeutic interventions.

Silibinin (C₂₅H₂₂O₁₀) is the primary bioactive polyphenolic flavonoid isolated from milk thistle (Silybum marianum) [55]. While classically established as an effective and remarkably safe, non-toxic hepatoprotective supplement [56, 57], its promising anticancer properties have increasingly garnered substantial scientific interest. Silibinin demonstrates a broad-spectrum antineoplastic capacity by profoundly inhibiting tumor cellular proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastatic dissemination, while concurrently accelerating apoptotic pathways [58, 59]. Consequently, its therapeutic efficacy has been extensively investigated across a diverse array of malignancies, including skin [60], lung [61], ovarian [62], glioblastoma [63], bladder [64], and prostate cancers [65]. Furthermore, compelling evidence highlights silibinin’s potential as a highly effective therapeutic adjunct, capable of synergistically enhancing the efficacy of conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens [22, 66].

Mechanistically, silibinin exerts its multifaceted anti-tumor efficacy in GI malignancies by modulating a diverse array of interconnected cellular pathways. Across various models, silibinin consistently induces cell cycle arrest—primarily at the G1 phase—by downregulating cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) while upregulating CDK inhibitors such as p21 and p27, alongside triggering caspase-dependent and mitochondrial apoptotic cascades [37–39, 49]. A central mechanism of action involves the profound suppression of key pro-survival and proliferative signaling axes, notably the PI3K/Akt and ERK pathways in colorectal, hepatic, and cholangiocarcinoma cells [37, 38, 49], as well as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in colorectal carcinoma [40, 41]. Furthermore, silibinin effectively targets the STAT3/c-MYC signaling node, which not only induces metabolic reprogramming and reduces glycolysis to prevent cachexia in pancreatic cancer [46], but also diminishes cancer stemness and anti-apoptotic protein expression in hepatocellular carcinoma [48]. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, silibinin alters the tumor microenvironment by exerting potent anti-angiogenic effects via the downregulation of VEGF, HIF-1α, and iNOS [37, 41]. Additionally, it acts as a critical modulator of cellular stress and metabolism by activating AMPK to suppress esophageal squamous cell carcinoma invasion [45] and inhibiting TGF-β /Smad signaling to prevent epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and radiation-induced intestinal fibrosis [47]. The present study aimed to investigate the efficacy of silibinin in the treatment of gastrointestinal malignancies by analyzing data from relevant animal models. Through this study, vital parameters such as tumor volume, changes in animal body weight, DNA fragmentation (Tunnel assay), and PCNA levels were systematically analyzed. The results obtained from the analysis of the selected articles showed the effect of silibinin in reducing tumor growth and killing cancer cells. In addition, silibinin demonstrated preliminary tolerability in the animal models, as evidenced by the lack of significant weight loss or other severe clinical side effects during the study. These findings, which are the result of a pooling and analysis of similar articles on gastrointestinal cancers, are consistent with the findings of studies conducted on the effect of silibinin in other articles.

The findings of our review are consistent with prior preclinical research that has established the anticancer efficacy of silibinin. For instance, Guo et al.(2021) demonstrated that silibinin significantly inhibited tumor growth in a lung adenocarcinoma xenograft model, achieving an approximate 10% reduction in tumor size compared to the control group following 27 days of administration at 100 mg/kg [67]. Similarly, a study by Lee et al. (2013) in a skin cancer model reported an even more substantial effect, with silibinin treatment over 4 weeks reducing tumor size by approximately 30% [68]. These individual reports of tumor suppression are in strong agreement with the pooled result of our analysis, which yielded a standardized mean difference (SMD) of − 2.72, confirming a significant reduction in tumor size in silibinin-treated samples. Among the investigated gastrointestinal malignancies, the most pronounced reduction in tumor volume was observed in esophageal cancer (SMD = − 5.82) within the single-study category, and in hepatic cancer (SMD = − 2.99) among the models supported by multiple studies.

In addition to tumor volume, tumor weight serves as a vital complementary endpoint. Measured directly upon excision, it provides a highly objective assessment of the final tumor burden and the cumulative therapeutic efficacy of an intervention.

Consistent with the volume reductions, our pooled analysis (11 studies, 227 animals) demonstrated that silibinin significantly decreased final tumor weight compared to untreated controls (SMD = − 1.86). This dual efficacy in reducing both tumor volume and final mass provides strong evidence for silibinin’s capacity to suppress overall tumor burden in preclinical models GI cancers, though further studies remain necessary to conclusively validate these anti-proliferative effects.

Previous studies investigating the antitumor activity of silibinin have consistently reported substantial inhibition of xenograft tumor growth while being generally well-tolerated, as reflected by the absence of treatment-related weight loss and the preservation of normal physiological function in experimental animals [69–71]. Our findings support silibinin’s preliminary tolerability. While significantly reducing tumor burden, silibinin did not adversely affect body weight (SMD = 0.53) and significantly preserved dietary intake compared to controls (SMD = 1.09). Since weight loss and reduced food consumption are frequently associated with disease progression or treatment toxicity, the maintenance of these parameters suggests that tumor suppression was achieved without compromising general health or normal feeding behavior.

Taken together, the simultaneous reduction in tumor burden alongside the preservation of these physiological parameters suggests that silibinin can inhibit gastrointestinal tumor growth without inducing overt systemic toxicity. While these characteristics highlight its potential clinical relevance, further well-designed preclinical and clinical studies are needed to confirm these safety advantages and to evaluate their applicability in vulnerable populations, including elderly individuals, pediatric patients, and those with underlying medical conditions.

PCNA functions as a non-histone nuclear co-factor for DNA polymerase delta, and its expression levels are directly linked to the rates of DNA synthesis and cellular proliferation. Investigating a skin cancer xenograft mouse model, Gu et al.(2007) reported that administering silibinin (1%w/w, 5 days/week for 25 weeks) resulted in a 10% reduction in the proliferation index, as determined by the quantification of PCNA staining [72]. Similarly, Singh et al. (2003) demonstrated in a prostate cancer xenograft model that a 28-day silibinin treatment at a 0.1% (w/w) concentration led to an approximate 27%decrease in the PCNA proliferation index [73].

These observations are highly consistent with the findings of the present review regarding the anti-proliferative efficacy of silibinin. The marked reduction in proliferative activity specifically within colorectal tumors (SMD = − 5.14) suggests that silibinin may possess a heightened therapeutic capacity for this specific cancer subtype. Ultimately, these corroborating results provide compelling evidence of silibinin’s capacity to inhibit cancer cell proliferation, establishing a strong foundation for future, comprehensive investigations into its mechanisms of action in gastrointestinal malignancies and other cancers.

The TUNEL assay serves as a valuable molecular indicator of apoptosis and antitumor activity. Previous research has clearly established the pro-apoptotic properties of silibinin in non-gastrointestinal malignancies. For instance, Gu et al.(2006) showed that in skin cancer models, dietary administration of 1% (w/w) silibinin for 25 weeks (or topical application of 9 mg) elicited a 60% to 80% increase in TUNEL-positive cells compared to untreated tumor tissue [74]. Similarly, silibinin treatment induced an approximately 60% increase in apoptotic cells within prostate cancer xenografts [75]. Aligning with these historical findings, our pooled analysis confirmed a consistent pro-apoptotic effect of silibinin across most gastrointestinal models (SMD = 6.00). However, subgroup and sensitivity analyses highlighted distinct tissue-dependent variations; colorectal cancers exhibited pronounced apoptosis induction (SMD = 8.70).

Contextualizing our findings within the broader scientific literature supports the consistency of our results and establishes a strong foundation for future research. Although silibinin has demonstrated encouraging preliminary tolerability and promising therapeutic properties in various preclinical models, extrapolating these animal data to human applications requires caution. Therefore, while it may hold potential for future clinical investigations targeting GI cancers, further rigorous translational studies are necessary. Furthermore, subsequent preclinical and early-phase studies should prioritize evaluating silibinin in combination with standard chemotherapeutic regimens. A critical objective for future research is to determine whether adjunctive silibinin can synergistically enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of conventional GI cancer treatments or mitigate their associated adverse side effects.

Limitation & recommendation

The present study has several limitations. First, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed across several pooled outcomes, including tumor volume, body weight, diet consumption, PCNA expression, and apoptotic cell death. Although subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed, residual heterogeneity remained for several endpoints, suggesting that the observed variability could not be fully explained by the available study-level characteristics. This heterogeneity likely reflects methodological and experimental diversity among the included studies, including differences in animal species or strains, tumor induction or implantation methods, baseline tumor burden, silibinin dosage regimens, routes of administration, treatment duration, and outcome assessment methods. In addition, subgroup analyses of tumor volume according to cancer type showed variation in both effect size and heterogeneity across colorectal, hepatic, and pancreatic cancer models, indicating that model-related factors may influence the magnitude of the observed antitumor effects. Therefore, although the overall findings support a beneficial effect of silibinin in preclinical gastrointestinal cancer models, the pooled estimates, particularly for outcomes with high heterogeneity, should be interpreted with caution.

Second, the pooled analyses combined several biologically and experimentally distinct gastrointestinal malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, and esophageal cancer. This approach was necessary because of the limited number of available preclinical studies for each individual cancer type; however, it remains an important limitation of the present meta-analysis. These malignancies differ substantially in tissue of origin, pathophysiology, tumor microenvironment, molecular drivers, progression patterns, and likely responsiveness to silibinin. In addition, the corresponding animal models, such as chemically induced, xenograft, or orthotopic models, may have distinct biological and methodological profiles that can influence treatment response. As a result, the pooled estimates may mask cancer-specific and model-specific differences in treatment effect and reduce the biological precision and translational applicability of the summary findings. Accordingly, the overall results should be interpreted as a broad preclinical signal of antitumor activity rather than as evidence of uniform efficacy across all gastrointestinal malignancies. Future preclinical studies and meta-analyses with larger tumor-specific and model-specific datasets are needed to generate more precise conclusions.

Third, an important translational limitation relates to the dosing regimens used in the included animal studies. Several experiments administered relatively high doses of silibinin (e.g., 100–200 mg/kg/day), which, although associated with marked tumor inhibition in preclinical models, may not directly correspond to clinically feasible or pharmacologically realistic human dosing strategies. Without systematic allometric scaling to estimate human equivalent doses (HED), the translational relevance of these effective animal doses remains uncertain. Furthermore, differences in bioavailability, metabolism, formulation, and route of administration between animal models and humans may further complicate dose extrapolation. Therefore, while the observed antitumor effects are promising at the preclinical level, caution is warranted when interpreting their potential applicability to human clinical settings. Future preclinical studies and systematic reviews should incorporate standardized human equivalent dose calculations, together with pharmacokinetic, safety, and tolerability data, to better contextualize the clinical relevance of effective animal dosing regimens.

Fourth, Additionally, a major limitation of silibinin is its poor water solubility and low bioavailability, which poses a significant translational challenge in extrapolating these promising animal findings to human clinical applications.In this meta-analysis, we focused exclusively on studies using standard formulations of pure silibinin to maintain uniformity and isolate its baseline efficacy, avoiding confounding variables introduced by advanced delivery systems like nanoparticles. Moreover, to overcome the aforementioned pharmacokinetic barriers, future systematic reviews could prioritize the evaluation of advanced drug delivery systems to assess the bioavailability and clinical translationability of silibinin with the aid of these advanced systems.

Fifth, some of the included studies showed suboptimal methodological quality and a high risk of bias, which may have affected the reliability of the pooled findings. In addition, the indirect extraction of data from graphical representations may have introduced measurement error. Publication bias analyses further suggested that some outcomes may be influenced by small-study effects, particularly apoptosis-related outcomes such as the TUNEL assay. Notably, trim-and-fill adjustment attenuated the pooled pro-apoptotic effect and rendered it statistically non-significant, indicating that the magnitude of this effect may be partly overestimated. A similar pattern was also observed for diet consumption. In addition, the possibility that preclinical studies reporting null or unfavorable results remained unpublished cannot be excluded, which may have led to an underrepresentation of negative evidence in the available literature. Therefore, although the direction of the overall findings remains supportive of a beneficial effect of silibinin, the magnitude of some pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution. To improve the rigor of future preclinical research, greater adherence to standardized methodological frameworks, such as the SYRCLE risk of bias tool, is recommended, along with the use of larger sample sizes and more consistent experimental designs. Future systematic reviews may also benefit from incorporating grey literature, such as theses, dissertations, and conference proceedings, and from contacting authors to obtain unpublished data. Finally, preregistration of preclinical studies in platforms such as PreclinicalTrials.eu or the Animal Study Registry, together with sharing raw data and detailed protocols in open-access repositories, would enhance transparency and reduce the risk of selective reporting.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the therapeutic and preventive efficacy of silibinin in GI malignancies, which represent a major global health burden. Our findings demonstrate that silibinin significantly inhibits tumor cell proliferation and reduces tumor volume in animal models of GI cancer. Furthermore, preliminary data suggest that silibinin is generally tolerated without causing significant weight loss in murine models. However, comprehensive toxicological assessments are required to definitively establish its safety profile. Before considering future clinical applications, preclinical research must utilize advanced animal models that accurately recapitulate human GI cancers. Such detailed studies will be crucial to investigate whether silibinin can serve as an adjunctive therapeutic agent alongside standard treatments, potentially enhancing therapeutic efficacy or mitigating the side effects of common interventions such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy.
