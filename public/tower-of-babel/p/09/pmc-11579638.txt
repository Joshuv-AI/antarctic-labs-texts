Child temperament and trajectories of student–teacher relationships quality
Authors: Elizabeth Harvey, Michèle Déry, Jean‐Pascal Lemelin, Vincent Bégin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the associations between child temperament and trajectories of the three dimensions of the student–teacher relationship (Closeness, Conflict, and Dependency) during elementary school. Latent class growth analyses conducted among 744 French‐Canadian students recruited between 2008 and 2010 (46.8% girls; M age = 8.39; 90.9% White; 49.7% with externalizing behavior problems) revealed four Closeness trajectories and three Conflict trajectories, but no significant variability between children in mean levels of change in Dependency. Surgency‐Extraversion and Effortful control were associated with specific trajectories of Closeness and Conflict. Effortful control was also associated with cross‐sectional assessments of Dependency. These results suggest that temperament is a useful construct in understanding developmental patterns of the student–teacher relationship across the elementary school years.

student–teacher relationship quality

The quality of the relationships between students and their teachers may play a significant role in the student's school path and subsequent social adjustment. More specifically among school‐age students, high‐quality student–teacher relationships have consistently been linked to positive academic and behavioral outcomes (Hughes & Cao, 2018), such as better school engagement and higher achievement (Roorda et al., 2017) or lower depressive symptoms (Rucinski et al., 2018). Conversely, poor‐quality student–teacher relationships have been associated with negative consequences on both the academic (Roorda et al., 2021; Spilt et al., 2012) and behavioral levels (Collins et al., 2017; Husby et al., 2023). Furthermore, the accumulation of low‐quality student–teacher relationships over several school years has been associated with greater academic and behavioral difficulties (Spilt et al., 2012). Considering the influence student–teacher relationship quality (STRQ) may have on academic success, it is important to understand developmental patterns of STRQ across the school years and their predictors.

The STRQ involves the contribution of several influencing factors in the development of student–teacher relationships patterns, including the characteristics of each person involved in the relationship, and is widely recognized as a bidirectional process. Several studies support the importance of students' characteristics in the development of STRQ. For example, longitudinal studies have revealed considerable variability in the quality of teachers' relationships with different students, but distinctly more stability in the quality of the relationships between a student and several teachers (Rudasill, 2011). These observations suggest that some individual characteristics of students can be significant, long‐term predictors of different patterns of STRQ over time. Thus far, studies have shown that students' characteristics, such as sex and temperamental, behavioral, and cognitive characteristics, are associated with STRQ (McGrath & Van Bergen, 2015). However, there is only a handful of studies on the predictors of STRQ trajectories over the school years (Bosman et al., 2018; Collins et al., 2017; Jerome et al., 2009; O'Connor et al., 2012; Shi & Ettekal, 2021; Spilt et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2018). Considering that teachers change every year in elementary school, identifying predictors of STRQ trajectories over years based on the students' characteristics may help in identifying students who are the most vulnerable to the development and accumulation of poor STRQ.

This study focused on the role of child temperament in the development of student–teacher relationship patterns across the elementary school years. Using a person‐centered approach of the STRQ, the main objective was to examine, beyond the students' behavioral or cognitive characteristics, the role of individual differences in temperament in the evolution of STRQ over a period of 4 years in a sample of children of whom approximately 50% were at‐risk of experiencing low‐quality student teacher relationship due to high level of externalizing behavioral problems.

STRQ framework

One of the most influential models of STRQ in the field, is the conceptual model of Pianta's (1999) which conceptualizes STRQ in three dimensions. The Closeness dimension refers to the degree to which the student–teacher relationship is characterized by openness, warmth, and security. The Conflict dimension concerns the level of negative, conflictual, unpredictable, or unpleasant student–teacher interactions. Finally, the Dependency dimension reflects the extent to which the student needs constant reassurance from the teacher (Pianta, 2001). This latter dimension, however, may be more developmentally salient for younger children in comparison to older children and adolescents (Baker et al., 2008). According to Pianta's model, a student–teacher relationship pattern characterized by high level of Closeness and low levels of Conflict and Dependency (high‐quality relationship) would be optimal for school and social adjustment. Conversely, relational patterns characterized by high levels of Conflict or Dependency and low level of Closeness (low‐quality relationship) would hinder students' school and social adjustment.

To identify students who may be more at risk of accumulating low‐quality relationships, studies have typically taken two approaches: to examine the mean trajectories of STRQ dimensions to identify general trends (variable‐centered studies) or to capture the heterogeneity in these trajectories (person‐centered studies) with the aim of identifying subgroups of students who follow distinct trajectories of STRQ. For instance, studies that have examined the average trajectory of Closeness have reported that the level of Closeness is not only generally high, but also gradually decreases over time (Hughes & Cao, 2018; Jerome et al., 2009), which may represent the “normative” trajectory of the Closeness dimension through the school years. Studies that have focused on heterogeneity, however, have typically identified two to four trajectories of Closeness. These studies (Bosman et al., 2018; Collins et al., 2017; Ettekal & Shi, 2020; O'Connor et al., 2012; Spilt et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2018) generally identified high‐stable or slightly decreasing Closeness trajectories—thus normative or low risk groups—and moderate or low‐decreasing Closeness trajectories that could be at‐risk for adjustment outcomes. Those at‐risk trajectories are usually overrepresented in samples academically (Ettekal & Shi, 2020; Spilt et al., 2012) or economically (Collins et al., 2017) at‐risk. These results highlight the importance of looking beyond general trends in studying the evolution of STRQ to captures meaningfully different trajectories using a person‐centered approach. In addition, some characteristics of children, including being a boy (Spilt et al., 2012) and having a low level of verbal skills (Bosman et al., 2018) increase the probability of following these at‐risk Closeness trajectories.

Concerning the Conflict dimension, variable‐centered studies generally show either a low‐increasing (Jerome et al., 2009) or a low‐decreasing average trajectory (Hughes & Cao, 2018) of Conflict during the elementary school years. On the other hand, person‐centered studies have revealed 3–6 typical trajectories, which illustrates the diversity of the developmental patterns of this STRQ dimension (Bosman et al., 2018; Collins et al., 2017; O'Connor et al., 2012; Spilt et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2018) and may at least partly explain the contradictory results reported regarding its mean trajectory. These latter studies identified subgroups of students who fall into a high‐stable or a slightly decreasing trajectory of Conflict—thus being at higher risk for school and social adjustment outcomes. Here again, boys tend to be overrepresented in these at‐risk trajectories (Collins et al., 2017; Spilt et al., 2012).

The body of evidence regarding the Dependency dimension is still very limited. According to Rudasill (2021), one of the reasons this dimension has often not been included in previous studies are the internal consistency issues of its corresponding scale in the Student–Teacher Relationship Scale (Pianta, 1999), the most often used instrument to assess the STRQ. To the best of our knowledge, there has been only one study (Bosman et al., 2018) conducted on trajectories of Dependency in a vast population‐based sample. It revealed that most students, regardless of sex, followed a low and slightly decreasing Dependency trajectory between kindergarten and sixth grade, whereas a minority were on a slightly increasing trajectory.

Temperament framework

Rothbart's psychobiological and multidimensional model of temperament (Rothbart, 2011; Rothbart & Bates, 2006; Rothbart & Derryberry, 1981) is a reference model to understand temperament and its influence on human development. The model posits that child temperament is influenced by a combination of biological, genetic, and environmental factors throughout childhood and defines temperament as the relatively stable individual differences in reactivity and self‐regulation that affect a child's emotional or behavioral response patterns in various environments (Rothbart & Bates, 2006). It also proposes that temperament often influence children's adjustment through its interaction with elements of the environment, such as the quality of parenting or other social relationships (Rothbart, 2011). According to this model, temperament as assessed in childhood is organized in three factors, each comprising several dimensions. The first two factors refer to reactivity (i.e., the level of excitability of the behavioral and physiological systems). Those factors are Negative Affectivity (with the dimensions Fear, Sadness, Anger‐Frustration, Discomfort, and Soothability [reversed]) and Surgency‐Extraversion (Activity Level, High‐Intensity Pleasure, Impulsivity, and Shyness [reversed]). The third factor, Effortful Control, comprises dimensions that refer to regulation (Low‐Intensity Pleasure, Inhibitory Control, Attentional Focusing, and Perceptual Sensitivity). Effortful Control concerns the processes that modulate (facilitate or inhibit) the level of reactivity (Rothbart, 2011; Rothbart & Derryberry, 1981). Rothbart (2011) maintains that the capacity to regulate develops over time under the influence of maturation and of the environment that it increasingly becomes the reflection of a conscious cognitive effort, and that, consequently, its effect on reactivity increases with age.

Given their temperamental characteristics, children differ in the way they perceive and react to a same experience, as well as in their capacity to manage it, which in turn influences how the environment responds (McCormick et al., 2015). According to the goodness–of‐fit concept, first proposed by Chess and Thomas (1999) and later recuperated and expanded in Rothbart and colleagues' model (Rothbart & Bates, 2006), a child's adjustment and development are optimized when the environment aligns with or accommodates the child temperament. Inversely, a “mismatch” between the environment and temperament may hinder the child's adjustment. For instance, given the highly structured environment of the school setting, one could expect students with high levels of Negative Activity (e.g., high levels of Fear or Anger‐frustration) and Surgency‐Extraversion (e.g., high Activity Level, Impulsivity) or low levels of Effortful Control (e.g., low Attentional Focusing) to be more likely to fail to meet teachers' expectations; hence, to be at risk of accumulating poor‐quality relationships with teachers over the school years. In other words, this specific social and educational context may present more challenges to some children than others based on their temperamental differences (Rothbart, 2011).

Temperament factors in the prediction of STRQ

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no study in which a person‐centered approach was used to examine the evolution of the STRQ dimensions across the school years according to students' temperamental characteristics. In the few longitudinal studies conducted on these associations, specific temperament dimensions were examined in relation to subsequent levels of Closeness and Conflict, and the Dependency dimension was largely overlooked. Further, these longitudinal studies were often conducted during the same year, which does not allow to capture patterns of STRQ across school years. Some studies did assess the STRQ over several years, however. For instance, Rudasill and colleagues examined longitudinal associations between a latent variable composed of four dimensions representing “difficult temperament” (e.g., Anger‐frustration, Inhibitory control [reversed]) and two other latent variables representing Closeness and Conflict (but not Dependency), assessed from kindergarten to grade 3 (Rudasill et al., 2013) and from grade 4 to grade 6 (Rudasill et al., 2010). In both studies, the authors observed that children with a more “difficult temperament” had more conflictual relationships with their teachers. No significant association was found with the Closeness dimension. These two studies, however, did not allow to identify which specific dimension(s) of temperament was responsible for these long‐term student–teacher conflicts.

Other studies provide valuable insight pertaining to the associations between specific temperament dimensions and STRQ. Concerning the dimensions from the Negative Affectivity factor, Diaz et al. (2017) observed in a sample of kindergarten students a positive association between a variable operationalized by the Anger‐frustration, Sadness, and Fear dimensions measured early in the school year and the level of student–teacher Conflict measured at year‐end. They found no significant association with the level of student–teacher Closeness. However, in a sample of prekindergarten children, Rudasill et al. (2016) did not report any link between the levels of Anger‐Frustration and the level of Conflict measured 4 months later, the only STRQ dimension examined in this very short longitudinal study. Nonetheless, Buhs et al. (2018) did not observe any associations either between the levels of Anger‐Frustration and Sadness measured at preschool age and the level of Conflict or of Closeness of the STRQ measured in Grades 3 and 5. Therefore, these few studies do not suggest longitudinal links between the dimensions of the Negative Affectivity factor and the Closeness dimension; however, their results are mitigated regarding the Conflict dimension. Nonetheless, there are still too few of these studies, and their measurements of the Negative Affectivity factor remain too fragmented to lead to definitive conclusions on the associations between this factor and the STRQ dimensions. Moreover, none of these studies included the dimension of Dependency. Other studies are needed to provide a more complete view of the links between the Negative Affectivity factor and the evolution of STRQ.

Concerning dimensions from the Surgency‐Extraversion factor, Shyness seems to be the most studied dimension, although little testing has been done on its longitudinal associations with the STRQ dimensions. Results from short‐time longitudinal studies have indicated that shyer preschool‐age students are less likely to develop relationships of Closeness (Rudasill, 2011; Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009) and of Conflict (Rudasill, 2011; Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009). Here again, the Rudasill et al. (2016) study conducted with prekindergarten students did not show any association between the level of Shyness and the level of Conflict with the teacher 4 months later. Concerning Dependency, the results of a rare longitudinal study on this STRQ dimension showed that kindergarten students who scored high on a measure of behavioral inhibition (used as a proxy for Shyness) were perceived by teachers as being more dependent, that is, more clingy and more needy of their attention and help (Verschueren & Spilt, 2021). Longitudinal studies where other dimensions of the Surgency‐Extraversion factor were tested are rare, and only the Closeness and Conflict dimensions were targeted. Their results showed nonsignificant associations (Buhs et al., 2018; Rudasill et al., 2016).

Finally, concerning dimensions from the Effortful Control factor, previous longitudinal studies focused mainly on the Inhibitory Control and Attentional Focusing dimensions. However, whether the studies were short or long term, they similarly showed that a high level of these characteristics in students was related to a high level of Closeness and, conversely, that a lower level of Effortful Control was related to a high level of Conflict (Berger et al., 2017; Buhs et al., 2018; Diaz et al., 2017; Hernández et al., 2017; Rudasill, 2011; Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009; Rudasill et al., 2016). The association between Dependency and Effortful Control remains unexplored.

Moderating effect of sex

A meta‐analysis on sex‐related differences in child temperament showed that girls scored higher than boys on the dimensions relating to the Effortful Control factor, while boys scored higher than girls on dimensions relating to the Surgency‐Extraversion factor (Else Quest et al., 2006). Such differences related to regulation and to reactivity suggest that girls' temperament characteristics are more likely to be suited to the school context, which would make girls less at risk than boys of developing low‐quality student–teacher relationships over time. This hypothesis is echoed in the studies on STRQ that have long shown differences related to the student's sex in relationships with teachers (McGrath & Van Bergen, 2015). More specifically, girls have closer relationships with teachers, whereas boys' relationships are more conflictual (e.g., Jerome et al., 2009; Spilt et al., 2012). In this regard, Spilt et al. (2012) noted in their study that only boys followed a high‐stable Conflict trajectory between the first and sixth grades.

Given its association with STRQ, student's sex is often controlled for or used as a predictor in studies. However, in a few rare studies, all of which were cross‐sectional, the authors examined the potential moderating role of sex on the association between temperament and STRQ (e.g., Justice et al., 2008; Koles et al., 2009). These studies suggested that being a boy or a girl may accentuate certain negative effects of a temperamental characteristic on STRQ or may act as a protective factor on the temperament‐STQR association. To gain a more refined understanding of the mechanisms underlying the associations between temperament and the evolution of STRQ, the moderating effect of sex on these associations must be explored.

This study

Previous longitudinal studies support the view that child temperament may be a long‐term predictor of STRQ. This literature, however, remains fragmented and is limited by (i) the small number of temperamental dimensions (and factors) that are often considered, (ii) the little knowledge about the contribution of child temperament on the evolution of the STRQ over several school years, thus with different teachers, and (iii) the almost exclusive focus on the Closeness and Conflict dimension of the STRQ and, consequently, the lack of knowledge on the associations between child temperament and Dependency. To fill these gaps, this study aimed to determine, based on Rothbart's temperament model, the contribution of all factors and dimensions of child temperament to the evolution of all three dimensions of STRQ across elementary school. This study also aimed to advance knowledge on the possible moderating effect of child sex in these associations.

Using longitudinal STRQ data collected over a 4‐year period, the study opted for a person‐centered approach to identify students following specific trajectories of STRQ across school years, and hence, across teachers. The sample was composed of boys and girls of whom half presented externalizing behavior problems at study entry. The use of such a sample increased the probability of identifying trajectories reflecting low STRQ and of examining the associations between temperament and STRQ beyond those that have been well documented between behavioral problems and STRQ (e.g., Husby et al., 2023). Considering the importance of STRQ for the academic and social adjustment of students with behavioral problems (e.g., Collins et al., 2017), it is imperative to better understand the factors associated with the development of the STRQ (Ettekal & Shi, 2020) in such a population.

More specifically, the study pursued three objectives. Following the presence or absence of a priori hypotheses, the first was both confirmatory and exploratory and aimed to identify which factors (confirmatory) and dimensions (exploratory) of child temperament were associated with trajectories of the Closeness and Conflict dimensions of STRQ. Based on the existing literature and on theoretical considerations (Chess & Thomas, 1999; Pianta, 1999), we expected that high levels of temperament factors reflecting reactivity, that is, Negative Affectivity and Surgency‐Extraversion, would be associated with a greater probability of following trajectories of lower STRQ (high level of Conflict; low level of Closeness). Given the results of empirical studies, however, we expected that Negative Affectivity would not be associated with Closeness trajectories. Regarding the Effortful Control factor, which reflects regulation, we expected that higher levels of this factor would be associated with a greater probability of following high‐quality student–teacher relationships trajectories (high level of Closeness; low level of Conflict).

The second objective was exploratory and aimed to identify factors and dimensions of child temperament that are associated with the Dependency dimension of STRQ. Although Dependency is most often left out of studies, either because it is less developmentally salient for older children or because of internal consistency issues of its measurement, it remains important to examine the potential associations of temperament with this dimension. Given the limited state of knowledge on these associations, however, no specific hypothesis was provided for this objective.

Finally, the third objective also was exploratory and aimed to examine the moderating effect of child sex on the associations between temperament and STRQ trajectories. No hypothesis was made regarding this moderating effect given the current lack of knowledge on this matter.

METHOD

Participants

The participants in this study were those from a longitudinal study pertaining to the evolution of behavior problems among girls and boys since childhood. The sample consisted of 744 French‐Canadian students (348 girls and 396 boys), between 6.25 years and 9.99 years of age (M age = 8.39; SD = .93 at study entry). Nearly half of these children (n = 370, i.e., 149 girls and 221 boys) were receiving assistance services at school for externalizing behavior problems (more specifically conduct problems and opposition problems). The students were recruited in three successive waves, between 2008 and 2010, in 155 elementary schools from four regions of the province of Quebec, Canada. Students with externalizing behavior problems were selected from lists of students receiving services for these problems (participation rate: 75%). Because more boys than girls were receiving these services, girls on these lists were oversampled. Students not receiving such services at school were recruited randomly in such a way that they did not differ significantly by age and sex from the student receiving services.

Of all children who were recruited, 90.9% were White, 3.8% were African Canadian, and 5.3% were from other ethnicities. At the time of recruitment, 50% of these children were from intact families, 20% lived in a blended family, and 30% lived in a single‐parent family. The median annual income of the families under study was between CAN$50,000 and CAN$59,999 which is below the median annual income of Canadian families (CAN$69,860) according to the data from the 2010 census (Statistics Canada, 2016).

Procedure

At the beginning of the study (T1), structured interviews were conducted at home with one of the parents (the mother in most cases) during which the parent was invited to complete questionnaires about the family's and child's characteristics, including temperament. Children also completed a cognitive assessment. With the parent's consent, the child's main teacher was contacted by phone to complete a questionnaire regarding STRQ. Of note, teachers rarely completed the STRQ scale for more than one child in their class, given that children in the sample were divided among a very large number of schools (n = 155) and attended classes of different grades. This last procedure with teachers was conducted annually for 4 years, that is, from T1 (study entry) to T5 (five time points of measurement) to cover the period of elementary school. In the province of Quebec, elementary school covers grades 1–6, and children transition between elementary and middle school around the age of 12–13 years, meaning most of the participants were still in elementary school at the end of the study (93% at T4; 62% at T5). In middle school, the same procedure was applied, meaning that the STRQ questionnaire was completed by a teacher who interacted with the student on a daily basis, typically the student's language or mathematics teacher.

During the longitudinal follow‐up, 101 participants (13.6% of total sample) left the study (n = 21 at T2, n = 12 at T3, n = 13 at T4, and n = 55 at T5). None of the covariates or temperament factors predicted attrition in a multivariate model using the exact same set of predictors as the main statistical models of the study (see below). Of note, all 744 participants were included in the analyses (see the Statistical Analyses section). Measures

Temperament

At T1, the Children's Behavior Questionnaire‐Short Form (CBQ‐SF, Putnam & Rothbart, 2006; French version by Lemelin et al., 2020) was completed by the parents. This instrument comprises 94 items, each answered on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = extremely untrue of your child and 7 = extremely true of your child) and measures 15 temperament dimensions, of which 13 are grouped under the three broad factors. The French version of the CBQ‐SF replicates the three‐factor structure established with the original version of the instrument (Lemelin et al., 2020). The Negative Affectivity factor (α = .87 in this study) comprises dimensions of Fear (six items, e.g., “Is afraid of the dark”; α = .78), Anger‐Frustration (six items, e.g., “Has temper tantrums when he or she doesn't get what he or she wants”; α = .84), Sadness (seven items, e.g., “Seems to feel depressed when unable to accomplish some task”; α = .54), Discomfort (six items, e.g., “Is quite upset by a little cut or bruise”; α = .77), and Soothability (six items, e.g., “Changes from being upset to feeling much better within a few minutes”; α = .76). The Surgency‐Extraversion factor (α = .83) comprises dimensions of Activity Level (seven items, e.g., “Is full of energy, even in the evening”; α = .78), High‐Intensity Pleasure (six items, e.g., “Likes to play so wildly and recklessly that he or she might get hurt”; α = .75), Impulsivity (six items, e.g., “Often rushes into new situations”; α = .60), and Shyness (six items, e.g., “Is sometimes shy even around people he or she has known a long time”; α = .83). The Effortful Control factor (α = .87) comprises dimensions of Attentional Focusing (six items, e.g., “When building or putting something together, becomes very involved in what he or she is doing, and works for long periods”; α = .74), Inhibitory Control (six items, e.g., Can easily stop an activity when he or she is told “no”; α = .74), Low‐Intensity Pleasure (eight items, e.g., Likes being sung to; α = .67), and Perceptual Sensitivity dimensions (six items, e.g., “Seems to listen to even quiet sounds”; α = .67). The higher the scores, the more the child presents a high level of the temperament trait measured. It should be noted that the Soothability and Shyness dimensions scores are reversed before the computation of their respective factor scores.

Student–teacher relationship quality

From T1 to T5, a French version of the Student–Teacher Relationship Scale (Pianta, 2001) was used to evaluate teachers' perceptions of the quality of their relationship with a student. The instrument contains a total of 28 items evaluated on a 5‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = definitely does not apply and 5 = definitely applies) and measures three STRQ dimensions. The Closeness dimension (11 items) pertains to the degree of openness, communication, and positive affect between the student and the teacher (e.g., I share an affectionate, warm relationship with this child; This child values his or her relationship with me). A high score on the Closeness scale indicates that the student–teacher relationship is characterized by affective warmth. The Conflict dimension (12 items) measures the level of negative and unpleasant student–teacher interactions (e.g., This child easily becomes angry with me; This child sees me as a source of punishment and criticism). A high score on the Conflict scale indicates that the teacher perceives the student as being frequently angry and unpredictable. Finally, the Dependency dimension comprises five items referring to the student's need to be in constant interaction and to be reassured by the teacher (e.g., This child asks for help even if he or she does not need it; This child reacts strongly to separation from me). A high score on this scale indicates that the teacher perceives the student as being overly dependent and is concerned about the student's overreliance. Pianta (2001) reported internal consistency coefficients of .86 for the Closeness dimension and of .92 for the Conflict dimension, but of .64 for the Dependency dimension, which may be related to the lower number of items on this scale. The STRS was first developed for children aged from 4 to 9 years (Pianta, 2001). In a more recent study, the 3‐factor structure of the STRS was confirmed from 3 through 12 years of age, and the three scales showed adequate reliability from preschool to the end of elementary school (Koomen et al., 2012). In our study, the internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach's α) calculated from T1 to T5 varied from .85 to .87 for Closeness, from .90 to .91 for Conflict, and from .66 to .71 for Dependency. Supplementary analyses were also conducted to verify the factor structure and measurement invariance of each STRQ scale, with results showing satisfying fit indices for all factor analyses and supporting their measurement invariance, both across sexes and longitudinally, except for scalar invariance of the Dependency scale, which was not supported at specific assessment time points nor longitudinally. A detailed description of these results is provided in Tables S1–S3.

Control variables

The control variables were measured at study entry with the parent (child's sex, date of birth, externalizing behavior problems, and family income) and with the child (cognitive‐verbal abilities). We evaluated behavior problems using the externalizing behavior scale of the Child Behavior Checklist of the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment 6–18 (Achenbach & Rescorla, 2001). The scale contains 32 items (e.g., “breaks rules,” “gets in fights”) rated on a 3‐point Likert‐type scale (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat or sometimes true, and 2 = very true or often true; α = .88). Annual family income was assessed with a 20‐point ordinal scale ranging from 1 ($0 to $999) to 20 (more than $160,000). The scale was weighted to ensure an equal distance between each of its units, and the scores were therefore used as a continuous measure. We assessed cognitive‐verbal abilities with the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (Dunn et al., 1993). The test is a measure of receptive vocabulary, and the total score is often used as an index of language development and verbal intelligence (Dunn et al., 1993). The total score was used in the analyses.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted with Mplus software version 8.1 (Muthén & Muthén, 2018). Missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood estimation since the data were missing completely at random (Little test: χ 2 = 479.275, df = 465, p = .314). Latent class growth analysis (LCGA) was performed to identify STRQ dimensions trajectories. LCGA allows to classify individuals into subgroups based on their individual developmental trajectory of a given construct over time, here the STRQ dimensions (Jung & Wickrama, 2008). LCGA was performed using the default maximum likelihood robust estimator, which takes into account non‐normal distribution of study variables in the estimation of standard errors (Muthén & Muthén, 2018). Preliminary unconditional growth models were conducted to determine whether a linear or a quadratic trajectory shape best fit the data. As the Bayesian information criteria (BIC) increased when a quadratic latent variable was included (indicating a deterioration in model fit) and since this quadratic latent variable was nonsignificant in all models, linear models were retained as more accurate depictions of the evolution of the STRQ dimensions across time. In the case of the Dependency dimension, however, these preliminary models showed no significant variance at the slope level, suggesting that levels of Dependency evolved similarly among all students in our sample. Consequently, and also considering this scale's scalar non‐invariance across sexes at specific assessment time points and longitudinally (see Supporting Information), developmental trajectories were not modeled for this dimension of the student–teacher relationship, and a slightly different analytical approach was adopted for the following steps (see below).

For Closeness and Conflict, models with one to six trajectories were estimated and compared based on conventional fit indices used to determine the number of classes in such models: the BIC (a lower value is preferred), the Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (its nonsignificance indicates that a model with one fewer class is preferred), entropy (higher is preferred, and values of at least .70 indicate clear classification of participants across trajectory groups), and posterior probabilities of class membership (higher is preferred; Nylund et al., 2007; Wang & Wang, 2012). Model parsimony and theoretical signification were also considered. All models were conducted using square root‐transformed sum scores to ensure trajectory models were not affected by disparities in normality across assessment time points.

Once optimal models for Closeness and Conflict dimensions trajectories were chosen, multinomial logistic regressions of temperamental predictors on trajectories were conducted using a 3‐step approach with the AUXILIARY option (Muthén & Muthén, 2018), with control variables included in all models. Temperamental predictors were standardized prior to these analyses. In the presence of significant associations between a temperament factor and the STRQ trajectories, we performed additional regression analyses, this time using all dimensions of this factor as predictors to assess their relative contributions. These analyses also included all control variables. It should be noted that, at the dimension‐level only of analysis, the original Soothability and Shyness scores (i.e., non‐reversed, where a high score indicates a high level of the trait) were used. The trajectory that could be associated with a high‐quality relationship was used as a reference trajectory.

In the case of the Dependency dimension, linear regression analyses predicting individual values at the intercept (i.e., first assessment), and using temperamental factors (or dimension in the presence of significant associations between one or more factors and the Dependency dimension) and the exact same set of covariates (externalizing behavior problems, age, sex, cognitive‐verbal abilities, and family income) were performed.

Finally, to examine the moderating effect of child sex on the associations between the temperament factors and the STRQ trajectories (or initial levels in the case of Dependency specifically), we conducted the main statistical models again, each time including an interaction term between child sex and a temperament factor as an additional covariate.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics are reported in Table 1. The table shows, notably, that the means of the STRQ dimensions (Closeness, Conflict, and Dependency) decreased progressively from T1 to T5. The correlations for a same STRQ dimension over time (from T1 to T5) were all significant and positive. The temperamental factors Negative Affectivity and Surgency‐Extraversion were positively associated with Conflict and Dependency, with stronger associations for Extraversion‐Surgency. The associations between these two factors and Closeness were for the most part nonsignificant. Finally, Effortful Control was negatively associated with Conflict and Dependency and positively associated with Closeness for most of the measurement times. All correlations between each temperament dimensions, as well as between temperament dimensions and the three dimensions of the STRQ from T1 to T5, are provided in Tables S4 and S5.

Temperament and trajectories of closeness

For the Closeness dimension, the four‐trajectory model presented the best fit to the data (Table 2). In this model, the majority of students (60.9%) were on a very high, although slightly decreasing, trajectory (Intercept = 6.68, p < .001; Slope = −.08, p < .001; estimated means on a 1–5 scale: T1 = 4.05, T5 = 3.68) and 20% of the students were on a high‐decreasing trajectory (Intercept = 6.36, p < .001; Slope = −.23, p < .001; estimated means on a 1–5 scale: T1 = 3.68, T5 = 2.69). Temperament and dependency

Results from linear regression analyses predicting Dependency scores from the three temperament factors indicated that only the Effortful control factor was associated with Dependency over and above child's externalizing behavior, age, sex, cognitive‐verbal abilities, and family income. Results indicated that higher Effortful control was associated with lower levels of Dependency (b = −.119, p < .05). The dimension‐level analyses indicated that both Inhibitory control and Attention focusing were negatively associated with Dependency (b = −.065, p < .05; b = −.059, p < .05, respectively).

Moderating effect of child sex

We found no significant moderating effect of child sex on the associations between temperament factors and Closeness trajectories. Child sex was, however, directly associated with these trajectories: Being a girl reduced the probability of being on a low‐increasing (b = −1.26, p < .001, OR = 0.29), a low‐decreasing (b = −2. 50, p < .01, OR = 0.08), or a high‐decreasing trajectory of Closeness (b = −1.05, p < .01, OR = 0.35).

We also found no moderating effect of child sex on the associations between temperament factors and Conflict trajectories. Here again, child sex was significantly associated with trajectories of this factor: Being a girl reduced the probability of being on a high‐decreasing trajectory of Conflict (b = −.68, p < .05, OR = 0.51).

No moderating effect of child sex was found in the associations between temperament and Dependency, nor any direct association with this factor.

DISCUSSION

The main objective of our study was to examine the role of individual differences in temperament in the evolution of STRQ across the elementary school years. In using a person‐centered approach of STRQ as well as a fine‐grained approach of temperament, which considers all three factors of Rothbart's model and their dimensions (Rothbart, 2011; Rothbart & Bates, 2006; Rothbart & Derryberry, 1981), this study filled important gaps in the literature by showing which factors and which dimensions of child temperament were most closely associated with a lower STRQ across elementary school years. First, the study showed that high levels of Extraversion‐Surgency factor (but not of Negative Affectivity factor) and its Shyness, Impulsivity, and High Intensity Pleasure dimensions increased the likelihood of belonging to lower quality trajectories in the domains of Closeness and Conflict. On the other hand, high levels of Effortful Control factor and its Low intensity pleasure and Inhibitory control dimensions were most closely associated with high quality level of student–teacher relationships across the elementary school years. Second, the study also advanced knowledge on the cross‐sectional associations between temperament and the Dependency domain of the STRQ, in showing that the Effortful Control factor, through its Inhibitory control dimension, was closely associated with a low level of Dependency. Third, this study revealed that cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations of temperament with STRQ did not differ among boys and girls.

Temperament and trajectories of closeness and conflict

Factors and dimensions of temperament related to reactivity

Based on the goodness‐of‐fit model (Chess & Thomas, 1999; McClowry et al., 2008), our hypothesis that students who scored high for the Negative Affectivity and Surgency‐Extraversion factors were more at risk of being on trajectories reflecting lower quality relationship was confirmed only for the Surgency‐Extraversion factor. Although both factors represent the student's level of reactivity, Surgency‐Extraversion refers more closely to behavioral aspects while Negative Affectivity mostly captures emotional aspects. Thus, our results suggest that the behavioral component of reactivity plays a greater role with regard to the child's fit with the context and demands of the school setting.

The absence of significant association between Negative Affectivity and the Conflict dimension was also observed in two longitudinal studies (Sadness: Buhs et al., 2018; Anger‐frustration: Buhs et al., 2018; Rudasill et al., 2016), although significant associations had also been reported between dimensions of this factor (Sadness, Anger‐frustration, Fear) and the subsequent level of Conflict with the teacher (Diaz et al., 2017). Overall, those results support the idea that the student's level of Negative Affectivity does not contribute, beyond the other temperament factors, to the patterns of evolution of STRQ across the school years. Conversely, the Surgency‐Extraversion factor was a predictor of the trajectories of Conflict and Closeness. These results thus suggest that some dimensions specific to this factor have a more direct effect on relationships with teachers. The “fine‐grained” approach of our study helps to better understand these associations between the Surgency‐Extraversion factor and the evolution of STRQ. Concerning the Conflict dimension, our results indicated that it was mainly high level of High‐Intensity Pleasure and Impulsivity that were associated with patterns of student–teacher relationships that were rather high in Conflict. These results support the idea that thrill‐seeking and more impulsive students are less likely to adapt to the demands of elementary school settings and, consequently, that these temperamental characteristics are more conducive to the development of relationships characterized by Conflict with their teachers during the school years. In this regard, our results corroborate the kindergarten teachers' accounts reported by Rimm‐Kaufman et al. (2002), who identified impulsivity among the more worrisome characteristics of children. Shyness was also a dimension from the Surgency‐Extraversion factor that was associated with the patterns of development of Conflict in student–teacher relationships. Our results indicated that shyer students are less at risk of following relational patterns characterized by high level of Conflict with their teachers.

Concerning the Closeness dimension, the results showed that the student's level of Shyness was the dimension of the Surgency‐Extraversion factor most closely associated with the Closeness trajectories. Shyer students are more at risk of being on trajectories that are moderate or low in Closeness with their teachers during the school years. These results corroborate those of other studies (Arbeau et al., 2010; Coplan & Rudasill, 2016; Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009) and support the importance of identifying preventive intervention avenues for these students, for whom the social nature of the school setting and the constant changes that occur there do not seem to favor a good fit (Kalutskaya et al., 2015).

Inasmuch as our results show that shy students are less likely to develop relationships high in Conflict or high in Closeness with teachers, our results could reflect that shy students often go unnoticed in the classroom (Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009) and that they have few interactions with their teacher. In this regard, Coplan and Rudasill (2016) explained that shy children are cautious and reluctant in school settings and that, even if they would like social contact, they often refrain from interactions because of social fear and anxiety. These behaviors, which seem contrary to those expected in school settings, could also hinder the establishment of relationships with teachers. Therefore, a low‐increasing Conflict trajectory could reflect more of an absence of relationships for shy children than a high‐quality relational trajectory, another hypothesis that remains to be verified.

Factor and dimensions of temperament related to regulation

As several other studies have suggested (Acar et al., 2018; Buhs et al., 2018; Hernández et al., 2017; Rudasill, 2011; Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009), our results showed that students with high level of Effortful Control were less at risk of being on trajectories of low‐quality relationships with their teachers, for both the Closeness and the Conflict dimensions of STRQ. These results are consistent with the goodness‐of‐fit model (Chess & Thomas, 1999; McClowry et al., 2008): Children who are more regulated fit better in the school setting and are better placed to develop more positive relationships.

Our study highlighted the fact that the dimensions of the Effortful Control factor most closely associated with the STRQ trajectories were the Low‐Intensity Pleasure (Closeness trajectories) and Inhibitory Control (Conflict trajectories) traits. These results for Inhibitory Control confirm the relationship previously reported in several other studies (e.g., Buhs et al., 2018, Rudasill, 2011; Rudasill et al., 2016) showing that students with a good capacity to inhibit inappropriate reactions and the ability to follow instructions easily are less at risk of having conflictual relationships with their teachers during their school years. Our results also show that a predisposition for Low‐Intensity Pleasure was the only temperamental dimension of the Effortful Control factor to increase the probability of having close relationships with the teachers during their schooling. To the best of our knowledge, the authors rarely retained the Low‐Intensity Pleasure dimension to operationalize the Effortful Control factor (Berger et al., 2017; Buhs et al., 2018; Hernández et al., 2017; Rudasill, 2011; Rudasill & Rimm‐Kaufman, 2009; Rudasill et al., 2016; Valiente et al., 2012), and this may highlight the relevance of considering all the dimensions of a factor to better understand its association with STRQ. Students who have temperamental traits characterized by Low‐Intensity Pleasure enjoy activities that are calm or whose level of stimulation is not too high in terms of quantity, complexity, or novelty (Gartstein et al., 2012). These are students who, for example, will like being told a story, listening to conversations and soft music, or looking at picture books. It is thus not surprising that this trait is likely to favor and maintain student–teacher relationships that are high in Closeness throughout the school years because they seem particularly well adapted to the demands and realities of the school context.

Temperament and dependency

To the best of our knowledge, this study remains the first to examine the associations between the three factors of temperament and the Dependency dimension of the STRQ, although cross‐sectionally. Only Verschueren and Spilt (2021) have examined behavioral inhibition, a construct similar to Shyness (on the Extraversion‐Surgency factor), in relation with Dependency, and observed that more inhibited children were perceived by kindergarten teachers as being more dependent. In our study, none of the factors related to the child's reactivity (Extraversion‐Surgency and Negative Affectivity) were associated with the Dependency dimension. However, students with higher levels of Effortful Control, more specifically with higher abilities to inhibit inappropriate behaviors or to maintain focus on tasks, were those for whom teacher reported lower levels of Dependency. Once again, such results may illustrate that students whose temperamental characteristics are more in line with the demands of the school environment have a better‐quality relationship with teachers. Furthermore, the fact that in our study, Inhibitory control was related to both the Dependency (cross‐sectionally) and Conflict dimensions (longitudinally) shows the importance of this temperamental characteristic in the school environment and its relevance to prevent the development of low‐quality relationships between student and teachers.

Moderating effect of sex

Our study indicated that the cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations between temperament and STRQ were similar for boys and girls. Other studies had shown a moderating effect of sex on the temperament‐STRQ associations, but based on a cross‐sectional design (Justice et al., 2008; Koles et al., 2009). Nonetheless, our results clearly showed that child's sex was a predictive factor of STRQ trajectories of Closeness and Conflict: Girls had a lower probability than boys of being on lower quality student–teacher relationships trajectories. These results, which confirm the longitudinal associations previously observed between the students' sex and the STRQ trajectories (Bosman et al., 2018; Spilt et al., 2012), suggest that the role of sex on the evolution of student–teacher relationships is more direct than indirect during the school years. However, as mentioned, the moderating effect of sex have still barely been explored in this field and need to be further examined before definite conclusions can be drawn in this regard.

Strengths, limitations, and suggestions for future research

This study has several strengths, notably the use of a sample comprising students with externalizing behavior problems to further highlight the diversity of the temperamental characteristics among students and STRQ trajectories; the use of a 4‐year longitudinal design with a person‐centered approach to identify these trajectories; the measurement of various temperament factors and of all their related dimensions; and the consideration of several control variables, including externalizing behavior problems, cognitive‐verbal abilities, and annual family income, in addition to the children's age and sex.

Nonetheless, the study also has some limitations that must be considered in the interpretation of the results. Child temperament was only assessed at T1. Therefore, this measure did not assess potential changes in temperament over time and the possible impacts of these changes on the STRQ. Moreover, given that other studies (e.g., Rudasill et al., 2014) have shown that parent and teacher ratings of child temperament can yield different associations with school outcomes, using only parent rating might represents another limitation. Also, our design did not cover the prekindergarten and kindergarten periods, during which students develop their first relational patterns with their teachers. Identifying these patterns from the outset would provide a better view of the role of temperament in the development of STRQ. Although some studies have shown the role of teachers' characteristics on STRQ, it was not possible in this study to control for these characteristics in the STRQ trajectories. Also, the STRQ trajectories that were established in this study may be more limited in terms of generalization because they may overrepresent the STRQ trajectories of students with externalizing behavior problems. However, for the Conflict and Closeness dimensions, we identified “typical” trajectories that were similar to those reported in the literature for socioeconomically (e.g., Collins et al., 2017) or academically (e.g., Spilt et al., 2012) at‐risk samples. Finally, the study design was correlational. Although we controlled for the effects of a number of relevant confounds, other variables that were not measured in this study protocol (for instance, teachers characteristics) could also contribute to the explanation of the STRQ trajectory profiles that were observed. Caution is therefore needed when interpreting these results as they are not indicative of a causal relationship between child temperament and STRQ trajectories.

In future research, more attention should be given to the child age or the developmental stage when designing the study. This would help provide a better understanding of the mechanisms in the development of STRQ. Particularly, an age‐based approach could be advantageous for studying Dependency trajectories. Moreover, our results should be replicated in other longitudinal studies. In accordance with the goodness‐of‐fit model, future longitudinal research could examine the coevolution of child temperament and STRQ, as well as the cumulative and interactive contributions of child temperament and teachers' own traits in explaining the STRQ. Finally, although the results obtained suggest that the associations observed were the same for boys and girls, the fact remains that there is a very small number of longitudinal studies where the moderating role of sex in these associations between temperament and STRQ was examined. These results should also be replicated or extended in further studies.

Implications for practice

Equity and inclusion are at the heart of the inclusive education paradigm. Inequality is often associated with characteristics of the child and our results suggest that child temperament may be a source of inequality in the education system. Students whose temperament is characterized by Surgency‐Extraversion, notably through high level of impulsivity, High‐Intensity Pleasure, or shyness may be at greater risk of cumulating lower STRQ through elementary school and should be the target of preventive strategies. Child temperament is unlikely to change within a short period, but the school environment can be arranged to suit specific temperamental profiles. For instance, the evidence‐based program INSIGHTS into temperament (McClowry et al., 2005) helps teacher interpret students' traits and behaviors like shyness or High‐Intensity Pleasure not as a lack of motivation or opposition behaviors, but as the expression of a temperamental trait that may placing them at risk for school and social adjustment problems. The program aims to help teachers adapt their practices to child temperament and, thus, to promote the goodness‐of‐fit between child characteristics and school context. Since our results also indicated that students with high level of Effortful Control, especially Low‐Intensity Pleasure and Inhibitory control, tend to develop high quality of relationships with their teachers, the development of self‐regulation abilities is also a promising intervention avenue. Once again, INSIGHTS into temperament provide scaffolding to reinforce these abilities among children (Sealy et al., 2021).
