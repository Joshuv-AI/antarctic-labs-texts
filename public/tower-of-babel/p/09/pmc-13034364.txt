Adipokines and mortality in burns and traumatic brain injury
Authors: Yangmyung Ma, Dionisius Reinard Ardiyanto, Amanda Veiga Sardeli, Jon Hazeldine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdipokines are known to regulate inflammation and immunity, but their association with mortality in burns and trauma remains unclear.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis comparing circulating levels of adipokines between survivors and non-survivors of burn and trauma was conducted across six databases. Standardized mean differences were calculated using fixed or random effects models.ResultsNine studies were included (three burns, six traumatic brain injury (TBI)). Adipokine levels were significantly lower in survivors compared with non-survivors on day 1 postburn (resistin −1.26, leptin −1.01, respectively) and at admission after TBI (leptin −1.51, visfatin −1.44, adiponectin −1.51, and resistin −1.29, all p<0.05). Other adipokines showed no significant associations.ConclusionAdipokines, particularly resistin and leptin, are linked to mortality in acute burns and trauma, but inconsistent data and small studies prevent firm conclusions. Further research is needed to confirm their prognostic value and underlying mechanisms. Level of evidenceLevel II, Systematic Review and Meta-AnalysisKeywords: Brain Injuries, Traumatic; mortality

Background

Burns and major traumatic injuries remain a significant global health burden, contributing to high mortality, morbidity, and healthcare costs.1 Beyond the immediate tissue damage, these injuries trigger complex systemic inflammatory and metabolic cascades that critically influence survival.

Adipokines, including leptin, adiponectin, and resistin, are bioactive proteins secreted by adipose tissue and immune cells that regulate inflammation, immunity, and wound repair.2 Experimental studies suggest potential prognostic roles for adipokines, with reduced leptin levels in the circulation associated with infection risk, raised adiponectin levels linked to suppression of hyperinflammation with prolonged release leading to chronic inflammation, and elevated resistin concentrations associated with impaired neutrophil activity and sepsis susceptibility.3 4 However, their relationship with mortality is unclear.4,6 Methodological heterogeneity with respect to patient cohorts, adipokines measured, sampling time points, and outcome assessments may explain the inconsistency in study findings. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis set out to evaluate the associations between adipokine levels and mortality in acute burns and trauma patients, taking these potential confounding factors into account.

Methods

This study was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251144820). Studies were eligible if they included acute burn or trauma patients, compared survivors and non-survivors, and reported peripheral blood adipokine levels. Prospective and retrospective cohort studies, as well as randomized controlled trials, were included. Reviews, case reports, abstracts, and planned surgical trauma were excluded.

The search was conducted on PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar on August 25, 2025, combining synonyms for adipokines, mortality, and the patient population (burns or trauma). Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and extracted data on study design, population, adipokines measured, timing of sampling, and survival outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion. The risk of bias within studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.

Means and SDs of adipokine levels at each study time point as well as the sample size of each survivor and non-survivor group were extracted for meta-analysis. Adipokines’ standardized mean differences and 95% CIs were calculated between survivors and non-survivors, separated by group of patients (burns and trauma) and time point, using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (V.3.0; Biostat, Englewood, New Jersey, USA). Raw mean differences (RMDs) were calculated for subgroups of studies presenting data in the same unit of measure. Fixed or random effects models were applied depending on heterogeneity between studies assessed by Q test (p<0.05 was considered statistically significant).

Results

Of the 3029 articles screened, nine studies (patients; mean age 35 years; 70% male) were included (2007–2021). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow chart of included studies is presented in figure 1. Three studies involved burn patients (mean total body surface area: 38%) and six investigated traumatic brain injury (TBI) with a mean Glasgow Coma Scale Score of 7. Although ‘trauma’ as a search term was applied broadly in the search strategy, only studies involving TBI met the inclusion criteria. Most were prospective cohort studies (n=7). Figure 2 shows the meta-analyses of adipokines between survivors and non-survivors of acute burn injury. On postinjury day 1, levels of resistin and leptin were significantly lower in burn survivors than in non-survivors (−1.26 (−2.17; −0.34), p<0.007, K=1; −1.01 (−1.92; −0.11), p<0.03, K=1, respectively). Other adipokines, such as adiponectin, angiotensinogen, chemerin, vaspin, visfatin, and C-X-C motif chemokine-12/stromal cell-derived factor-1 (CXCL-12/SDF-1) were tested, but differences between groups did not reach statistical significance. Ghrelin levels measured at day 3 postburn were no different between survivors and non-survivors. For day 7 postburn, all of the aforementioned adipokines were tested, but no significant differences between survivors and non-survivors were found.

Sensitivity analysis of RMD for each scale of each outcome was performed to infer the clinical significance of these results. For day 1 postburn, levels of resistin (0.23 (−0.39; −0.07) log10 ng/mL, p<0.005, K=1) and leptin were lower for survivors (0.1 (−0.19; −0.01) log10 ng/mL, p<0.02, K=1). For TBI at admission, RMD analysis showed lower leptin (9.2 (−11.8; −6.6) ng/mL, p<0.005, K=1), adiponectin (7.65 (−9.44; −5.86) μg/mL, p<0.005, K=1), and visfatin levels (43.7 (−57.2; −30.2) ng/mL, p<0.005, K=1) in survivors.

Discussion

This review demonstrates that adipokine alterations are associated with mortality in burns and trauma patients, with resistin and leptin levels lower in survivors on day 1 postburn, and in TBI survivors on hospital admission. These results suggest that there may be an upregulation of the inflammatory and metabolic response that influences survival.

Compared with healthy subjects, a previous study reported higher levels of resistin and lower levels of leptin in plasma samples acquired from burn patients 7 days postadmission to intensive care units.7 The study also reported a moderate positive correlation between resistin levels and multiple organ dysfunction scores.7 Although this study did not show associations with mortality, the results of resistin suggest the ongoing maladaptive inflammatory state of the patient in the acute phase of the burn. Furthermore, high levels of resistin have been implicated in increased sepsis risk through impaired neutrophil function, which may explain why elevated levels have been detected in patients with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome and subsequent mortality postburn.4 In other physiological contexts, such as obesity, leptin is normally elevated, whereas adiponectin behaves conversely. Given that burns induce a catabolic state, one might expect leptin to fall and adiponectin to be raised; however, both adipokines were suppressed simultaneously. Badoiu et al8 tested resistin and leptin levels at three time points (days 2, 10, and 21) and reported peak resistin levels at day 10, which reduced at day 21, and peak leptin levels at day 2, which reduced in days 10 and 21 in burns patients compared with healthy controls.8 Though this study did not compare these levels to mortality outcomes and did not find statistically significant correlations to other prognostic outcomes, such as the Revised Baux Score, other parameters, for example, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), showed significant correlations, with a negative correlation found with resistin and a positive correlation found with leptin. This discrepancy highlights the complex and different roles of adipokines in acute burns and the need to investigate specific changes in adipokine levels and their associated effects at various time points.

With regard to TBI, one study in a murine model reported significantly elevated resistin messenger RNA (mRNA) levels compared with a control group at 12 hours post-TBI.9 On an intracerebral injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), resistin expression increased in the TBI model compared with the control group. This suggested that resistin may participate in the acute responses after TBI, likely through the induction of inflammation.9 The same study found a significant increase in expression of leptin in the TBI model compared with control, and a reduction of leptin expression compared with control with LPS simulation, though no significant difference was identified between leptin expression with and without LPS stimulation. In light of the neuroprotective properties of leptin, through prevention of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA)-induced cytotoxicity, this may reflect the rapid inflammatory changes occurring during this time point (12 hours post-TBI).9 10

Several confounding factors could have influenced our results. Although we attempted to isolate some of these factors, such as type of adipokine and injury type, other factors remain unexplored, such as the heterogeneity in patient populations (eg, pediatric vs adult), pre-existing comorbidities, time points for adipokine measurement, and injury severity. These limitations contribute to the inconsistent findings, and future studies should further isolate these factors to improve our understanding of adipokines in the context of associations with acute injury. Large multicenter studies with standardized sampling methodologies and outcomes are needed to validate whether adipokines can complement existing prognostic tools in burns and trauma.

Conclusion

Adipokines show associations with mortality in burns and trauma, with resistin and leptin most consistently implicated in the acute phase. However, inconsistent findings, methodological heterogeneity, and small sample size limit a conclusive consensus. Future studies are required to clarify whether adipokines may serve as reliable indicators in clinical practice, and the possible mechanisms behind their influence on acute burns and trauma prognosis.
