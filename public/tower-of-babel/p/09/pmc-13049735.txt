The relationships between patient activation, patient–physician relationship, and patient engagement and medication adherence: model research on the mediating role
Authors: Ferhat Bolukçu, Şebnem Yücel

AbstractBackgroundMedication adherence remains a critical problem in the treatment of type 2 diabetes (T2D); however, research on this issue is still limited. For this reason, further scientific, academic, and societal efforts are needed to optimize treatment, slow disease progression, and improve adherence. The aim of this research was to examine the associations between the activation levels (PAM-13) of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) their relationships with doctors (PDRQ-9), and medication adherence (MARS-5), and to investigate the mediating role of patients’ health engagement (PHE-S) in this associations. The Patient Activation Measurement, the Patient-Doctor Relationship Scale, the Medication Adherence Report Scale, and the Patient Health Engagement Scale were used to collect data. Background

Medication adherence is a key determinant of treatment adequacy, achievement of clinical goals, and improved health outcomes [1, 2], reflecting the extent to which patients follow prescribed treatments based on motivation and perceived benefits [3–5]. Despite its clinical importance, medication adherence remains suboptimal worldwide, particularly among individuals with chronic conditions such as type 2 diabetes (T2D) [6]. Low adherence is a major contributor to treatment failure and increases the risk of diabetes-related complications [7]. Consistent with this, previous studies have reported poor adherence in 57% of patients with T2D and in up to 66.5% of adults receiving specialist diabetes care [7, 8].

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition that requires sustained medical follow-up and effective patient self-management to prevent acute complications and long-term sequelae [9, 10]. As the global prevalence of T2D continues to increase, improving medication adherence has become a central objective in diabetes management. While earlier research has largely focused on clinical and demographic predictors of adherence, growing evidence suggests that patient-centered factors play a critical role in shaping adherence behaviors.

Among these factors, patient activation has received increasing attention. Patient activation refers to an individual’s knowledge, skills, ability, and willingness to manage their own health and healthcare [11]. Higher levels of patient activation have been associated with greater engagement in self-management behaviors [12], improved adherence to treatment regimens [13, 14], better glycemic control and maintenance of diabetes care [15], fewer diabetes-related hospitalizations and emergency department visits [16], and overall improved clinical outcomes in individuals with T2D [17].

In addition to patients’ internal capacities, relational aspects of care, particularly the patient–doctor relationship, have been shown to significantly influence medication adherence. Defined as a mutually consensual interaction in which the patient seeks medical care and the doctor assumes responsibility for treatment [18], the patient–doctor relationship plays a pivotal role in chronic disease management [19–21]. In individuals with T2D, high-quality patient–doctor relationships have been linked to enhanced self-management behaviors, greater perceived personal control and self-efficacy, improved psychological well-being, stronger commitment to diabetes care, and fewer diabetes-related symptoms and complaints [9, 10]. Conversely, ineffective patient–doctor relationships are associated with poorer self-management and lower adherence to prescribed treatment regimens [20].

Patient engagement represents another important patient-centered construct related to medication adherence and reflects patients’ emotional, cognitive, and behavioral involvement in managing their health [22–27]. Patient engagement develops through psychological adjustment to illness and patients’ perceptions of their role within the healthcare system [23]. In the context of T2D, patient engagement is closely related to patient activation and is considered an indicator of patients’ readiness to participate in self-management behaviors [25].

Despite advances in diabetes care, medication adherence among individuals with T2D remains a persistent challenge [6]. Treatment adherence encompasses the accuracy, consistency, and willingness with which patients follow prescribed therapeutic regimens, including taking medications as directed, adhering to dietary recommendations, monitoring treatment, attending medical appointments, and implementing lifestyle modifications [6]. Poor medication adherence in T2D poses a significant risk for disease progression and complicates the management of coexisting chronic conditions. Although its clinical importance is well recognized, research examining medication adherence in T2D, particularly from a patient-centered perspective, remains limited. Therefore, further scientific investigation is warranted to identify modifiable factors such as patient engagement that may enhance medication adherence, improve glycemic control, and ultimately reduce disease progression.

In line with this need, the current study was conducted to examine the relationships between patient activation, quality of the patient-doctor relationship, and medication adherence among individuals with T2D and to investigate the mediating role of patient involvement in these relationships. Furthermore, the potential mediating role of the patient-doctor relationship within this framework was also investigated. Although patient activation, quality of the doctor-patient relationship, medication adherence, and patient engagement have been examined separately in previous studies, these interrelated concepts have rarely been combined into a single comprehensive model. The lack of such integrative approaches has limited the understanding of the mechanisms by which activation and relational factors influence medication adherence and patient engagement. This study aims to fill an important gap in the literature and provide new evidence for patient-centered care research by addressing these variables simultaneously within an integrated model.

In this study, based on the patient activation theory literature framework, the theoretical basis of the conceptual model aimed at explaining the fundamental determinants of medication adherence was strengthened by relating it to the PDRQ and PHE variables. Although studies directly focusing on this topic are limited in the literature (14, 20, 25, 32), this study aims to determine whether there is a positive and significant relationship between patient activation and both patient-physician interaction and health-related behaviors. This relationship, a fundamental component of patient-centered care, represents a mechanism through which higher levels of patient activation can translate into improved medication adherence. PHE was included as a second instrumental variable to measure patients’ emotional and experiential relationships with healthcare services, reflecting an additional pathway through which activation could influence adherence outcomes. These instruments were modeled in parallel to represent both relational and experiential mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Study population

The population of this study consisted of patients diagnosed with T2D who presented to the Internal Medicine Outpatient Clinic of Ankara 29 Mayıs State Hospital for diabetes control between November 20, 2023, and February 20, 2024. This cross-sectional, quantitative study included 431 Turkish-speaking adult T2D patients who volunteered to participate. Patients who were receiving treatment at Ankara 29 Mayıs State Hospital, were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes at least 6 months prior, were aged 18 years and over, were conscious, and agreed to participate voluntarily were included in the study. Individuals with communication difficulties, those under the age of 18, and patients who developed serious complications during the survey and were admitted to emergency rooms were excluded from the study.

Data collection

A convenience sampling method was employed to collect data through face‒to‒face interviews with participants. Despite its potential limitations ( non-probability sampling), convenience sampling was used in this study as it is frequently preferred in clinical research to find participants due to its low cost, practicality, and ease of reaching the target population [33]. The selection of consecutive patients was based on their initial polyclinic visits. Participants were informed that the survey form had to be completed in full for inclusion in the study. As a result, no missing data were observed for the study variables, and all cases were included in the analyses.

In structural equation modeling, the final sample size of 431 participants for non-complex models (4 parameters and 32 items) exceeds the minimum thresholds recommended in the literature (10–20 participants per parameter, 32 × 10 = 320) [34].

Instruments

Four instruments were used in the study:

Patient Activation Measure (PAM-13)

Patient Activation Measure (PAM-13)

The 13-item Patient Activation Measure (PAM), developed by Hibbard et al. (2005) to assess the activation level of individuals with chronic illnesses, is a unidimensional, Guttman-type scale with high validity and reliability [28]. The raw score is obtainable from a scale ranging between 0 and 52. When the responses to the 13 items are entered sequentially into the scoring table, the individual’s activation score and corresponding activation level are calculated. The Turkish adaptation and psychometric validation of the scale were conducted by Koşar [29]. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient of the original scale was reported as 0.91 [28], whereas in Koşar’s (2015) Turkish adaptation study, it was found to be 0.81 [29].

2.Patient‒doctor relationship questionnaire (PDRQ-9)

Patient‒doctor relationship questionnaire (PDRQ-9)

This scale was developed by Van der Feltz-Cornelis et al., Turkish validity and reliability analyses were performed by Mergen et al. [30, 31]. The scale consists of 9 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scale is one-dimensional and does not contain reverse-worded questions. A score of 9 points minimum and 45 maximum can be achieved on the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient of the original scale developed by Van der Feltz-Cornelis et al. (2004) was reported as 0.94, while in the Turkish adaptation study conducted by Mergen et al. (2012),0.91.

3.The Medication adherence reporting scale (MARS-5)

The Medication adherence reporting scale (MARS-5)

The Medication Adherence Report Scale–5 (MARS-5) was developed by Horne and Hankins (2001) to assess medication adherence [35]. The scale consists of five items rated on a 5-point Likertranging from 1 (always) to 5 (never), with total scores ranging from 5 to 25, where higher scores indicate greater adherence. The Turkish adaptation and psychometric validation of the scale were conducted by Temeloğlu Şen et al. [36]. In the original study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of0.85 [35], whereas in the Turkish adaptation study, it was found to be 0.78 [36].

4.Patient health engagement scale (PHE-S)

Patient health engagement scale (PHE-S)

This measurement tool assesses an individual’s level of emotional awareness and psychological adjustment regarding his/her own health status [24]. It also allows the design of preventive interventions to optimize care processes by predicting the risk of patient disengagement from health management [24, 25]. It is a one-dimensional and categorical scale consisting of five items. The adaptation of the scale to the Turkish scale and the psychometric validity study were carried out by Ateş [27]. The PHE-S is a measurement tool consisting of five questions in a sequential, categorical structure (for the purposes of confirmatory factor analysis and path analysis, the scale was modeled as a continuous latent variable, in line with common SEM practice and previous applications of the PHE framework). A minimum of 5 points and a maximum of 20 points can be obtained from the scale. Since the responses to each statement exhibit sequential categorical data characteristics, the median value is preferred for calculating the score obtained from the scale. The ordered structure of the response options allows participants to position themselves on an experimental continuum. Each item has seven ordered response options (A, B, C, D, E, F, G). For example, the patient is asked to select the most appropriate emotional state for themselves from among the options specified in the first, third, fifth, and seventh options for each item. The patient receives 1 point when they select the “A and B” option, 2 points when they select the “C and D” option, 3 points when they select the “E and F” option, and 4 points when they select the “G” option [24, 25]. Although the PHE-S items are ordinal, the construct was modeled as a continuous latent variable in CFA and path analysis, as the scale demonstrated adequate psychometric properties and the use of continuous estimation methods is considered appropriate for ordinal measures with five response categories. Cronbach alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.82 in the original study [24], this value was 0.95 in the Turkish adaptation study [27].

Data analysis

Descriptive, normality, reliability and validity analyses were performed for all scales. In this study, the potential effects of participants’ demographic characteristics on the measured variables were not included in the analysis. The Pearson correlation coefficient method was used to determine the linear relationship between the scales. In the final step, path analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between variables and test hypotheses. Because the assumption of normality was not met, multivariate normality was assessed during the analysis. Since the data did not satisfy the assumption of multivariate normality (Mardia cr > 7), the Asymptotically Distributed-Free (ADF) method was used for parameter estimation. ADF is an estimation method used when the assumption of multivariate normality is not met. This method makes no assumptions about the distribution of the data and is therefore called “distribution-independent. For these analyses, 95% confidence intervals were used. The Jamovi, SPSS 25.0 and Amos 25.0 package programs were used in the data analysis.

In this case, a one-unit increase in patient activation was associated with a 0.148-unit increase in medication adherence. Similarly a one-unit increase in the PDRQ-9 score was associated with a total increase of 0.136 units in medication adherence.

Discussion

This study evaluates the potential relationships between patient activation, the patient–physician relationship, patient engagement, and medication adherence. In our study, the mean patient activation score was 41.9, corresponding to 80.5 points on a 100-point standardized scale. The lowest observed activation score was 16 (30.76/100), whereas the highest score reached the maximum standardized value of 100, with 18 participants achieving a full PAM score. Overall, these findings indicate that patient activation levels in this sample were relatively high. High patient activation reflects greater levels of participation, knowledge, confidence, and self-management capacity, which are essential for improving health outcomes [43]. Accordingly, individuals with higher activation levels are more likely to meet their health needs [44], engage in regular physical activity, and adhere to recommended dietary practices, such as low-fat diets [43]. Moreover, among individuals with type 2 diabetes, higher PAM scores have been associated with more favorable clinical indicators, including improvements in blood pressure, cholesterol, and triglyceride levels [17].

Evidence from previous studies further underscores the importance of patient activation in diabetes self-management. For instance, Rask et al. (2009) found that patients with higher activation scores were more likely to perform regular foot care, undergo eye examinations, maintain exercise habits, and experience fewer difficulties in diabetes self-management [15]. Similarly, Su et al. (2019) reported that higher patient activation was associated with better glycemic control [45]. Conversely, low patient activation has been linked to limited knowledge of diabetes [46]. and its clinical indicators, increased healthcare utilization, poorer HbA1c control, and lower rates of diabetic foot and retinopathy examinations [46–48]. Individuals with low activation are also more likely to be hospitalized or to visit emergency departments for diabetes-related complications [16].

In contrast to the high activation levels observed in the present study, Selomon et al. (2019) reported predominantly low activation among individuals with type 2 diabetes, accompanied by limited lifestyle-related knowledge, low physical activity, and inadequate meal-planning skills [49]. These differences may be explained by variations in sociodemographic characteristics, access to healthcare services, or exposure to structured diabetes education and follow-up programs. Taken together, these findings suggest that the relatively high activation levels observed in the present sample may reflect effective patient education, ongoing follow-up, or greater access to healthcare resources. This highlights the potential value of interventions aimed at enhancing patient activation as a strategy to improve medication adherence and self-management behaviors among individuals with type 2 diabetes.

In our study, the participants’ average PDRQ-9 score (out of 45 total points) was 38.68. This score corresponds to 4.29 on a 5-point Likert scale. Therefore, in this study, the overall average satisfaction score regarding the relationship between Type 2 diabetes patients and their doctors was found to be quite high. The highest PDRQ-9 score obtained in the study was 45 (full score), and the lowest PDRQ-9 score was 19. Fernández Castillo et al. (2021) tested the validity of the PDRQ-9 to measure the patient-doctor relationship in 307 patients with type 2 diabetes visiting a public hospital and concluded that this measurement tool is a useful, valid, and reliable tool in patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes [21]. In this Mexican study, the average PDRQ-9 score was 4.35, with the item that patients were most satisfied with being “I can talk to my doctor” (4.54) in first place, followed by “I am satisfied with my doctor’s treatment” (4.45) in second place. Furthermore, the items with which patients were least satisfied were “I trust my doctor” (4.22) and “I can easily reach my doctor” (4.24), respectively [21]. In this context, the average score of the scale responses of the patients in the relevant study (4.35) is quite close to the average score of our study (4.29).

In our study, the participants’ average MARS-5 score was 23.7, with the highest MARS-5 score being 25 (full score) and the lowest MARS-5 score being 5. The overall average of all statements related to the medication adherence scale was found to be above 4. Therefore, in this study, the overall average satisfaction score regarding medication adherence among Type 2 diabetes patients was found to be quite high. Regarding medication adherence levels in the study, the item with the highest adherence level among patients was “I take less than what the doctor prescribed.” In contrast, the item with the lowest adherence level in the study was “I forget to take my medication.” On the other hand, according to the survey results of a study conducted on medication adherence and contributing factors among participants with Type 2 diabetes followed at the Adama Hospital Medical School in Eastern Ethiopia, 70 of 245 participants always forget to take their medication, and 31 participants never forget to take their medication [50]. Additionally, 90 participants always changed their medication dose, while 30 never changed it. In the same study, 31 participants stated that they always took less than the doctor prescribed, while 78 participants stated that they never took less than the doctor prescribed. In this context, the results of the study conducted by Yosef et al. (2023) on patients with Type 2 diabetes show a significantly lower level of medication adherence compared to the results of our current study. In another study examining the relationship between treatment adherence and clinical outcomes and quality of life in patients with type 2 diabetes, it was found that 57% of patients had a low medication adherence score [7]. In a study examining the relationship between diabetes-related distress and medication adherence in adult patients with type 2 diabetes followed by a diabetes specialist, 66.5% of patients were found to have poor medication adherence. In the same study, the mean MARS-5 score was found to be 21.2 ± 3.6, and in contrast, one-third of patients had the best medication adherence [8]. In this context, it can be said that the mean medication adherence level score in studies conducted on the Type 2 diabetic patient population in the literature is significantly lower than that in our study. This difference may be due to many reasons, such as the homogeneous and heterogeneous nature of the research population, sociodemographic characteristics, and the level of awareness regarding health status.

The PHE-S scale [24], which assesses the patient’s psychological readiness to take an active role in healthcare, is constructed with 5 statements, 7 response options, and 4 levels. The first level, options A and B, indicates that the patient is at an unconscious level in terms of health engagement. The second level, options C and D, indicates that the patient is at the arousal level in terms of health engagement. The third level, options E and F, indicates that the patient is at the adaptation level in terms of health engagement. Finally, the fourth level, known as option G, indicates that the patient’s health-related quality of life is at the level of well-being [24, 25, 51]. In our study, the overall mean score (median score) of the participants on the PHE-S was found to be 3.15 (the participants’ average PHE-S score was 15.7; the highest PHE-S score was 20 (full score), while the lowest PHE-S score was 7). Therefore, it can be said that the Type 2 diabetes patients included in the study are at the level of health-engagement, in other words, they are adapting to their disease. Similar to our current study, in a study conducted by Graffigna et al. (2017), the average (median) PHE-S score was found to be 3 (the adaptation level) [25]. Similarly, in a study evaluating the association of the patient health engagement model (PHE-S) on patient activation and self-efficacy in heart patients, the average (median) value of the PHE-S scale was found to be 3 [52].

Since the normality assumption was not met in our study, a multiple normality distribution test was performed during the analysis process, and the ADF method was used as the estimation method when the Mardia coefficient (cr) was greater than 5 and, in any case, greater than 7. In the literature, there are studies showing that the ADF can be adapted to very large sample requirements but produces stable results in medium-sized samples and medium-complexity models (where the number of parameters is low and model complexity is low) [53–55]. Our study consisted of 32 statements and 4 parameters (variables) and, as a result of its very low model complexity [53–55], produced highly meaningful and consistent results for a sample population of 431. This study demonstrates that the ADF method produces consistent results in a relatively medium-sized sample (N = 431) and under limited model complexity, providing a contextual contribution to the limited literature on the use of ADF in small and medium-sized samples as presented.

In this study, PAM-13 was positively and significantly associated with both PDRQ-9 and MARS-5, indicating that higher levels of patient activation were related to better patient–doctor relationship quality and higher medication adherence. The small but statistically significant direct effect of patient activation on medication adherence is consistent with patient-centered theoretical models that conceptualize activation as a distant determinant of health behaviors [25]. While patient activation reflects individuals’ levels of health-related knowledge, skills, and motivation, medication adherence represents a more immediate behavior based on daily practices and influenced by numerous contextual, clinical, and environmental factors. In addition, a significant positive association was observed between PDRQ-9 and MARS-5, suggesting that patients who reported more favorable relationships with their physicians also tended to report higher adherence to medication.

When PDRQ-9 was included in the model, the association between PAM-13 and MARS-5 was attenuated but remained statistically significant, indicating that the patient–doctor relationship may be an important factor associated with this relationship. The standardized direct, indirect, and total coefficients suggest that PAM-13 is related to MARS-5 both independently and through its association with PDRQ-9. Furthermore, in the second model, both PAM-13 and PDRQ-9 showed significant positive associations with PHE-S, indicating that higher patient activation and stronger patient–doctor relationships were related to higher levels of patient health engagement.

Given the critical importance of medication adherence for the early and effective management of type 2 diabetes (T2D), the primary objective (H1) of this study was to examine and validate the relationship between patient activation and medication adherence. Consistent with previous research [13–15, 56, 57], the findings demonstrated that higher levels of patient activation had a significant and positive effect on medication adherence in individuals with T2D (β = 0.20, p = .008). These results suggest that patient activation is a significant predictor of medication adherence behaviors in chronic disease management. On the other hand, the relatively small direct effect of patient activation on medication adherence suggests that medication adherence is influenced not only by activation but also by numerous contextual and clinical factors [50]. Indeed, patient activation, is influenced by numerous factors such as employment status, age and health status [58], education level [46], income level [59], socio-cultural characteristics, psychological factors, and other individual differences [60].

In support of these findings, a meta-analysis study showed that interventions aimed at patient activation were associated with improvements in clinical outcomes such as glycosylated hemoglobin, systolic blood pressure, body weight, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels in adults with diabetes [61]. Similarly, Skolasky et al. (2011) found that patient activation score showed a positive and significant relationship with physical activity, regular exercise, and medication adherence [62].

Consistent with previous studies [9, 10], the second hypothesis (H2), which proposed a positive association between patient activation and the doctor–patient relationship, was supported in this study (β = 0.19, P = .002). This is because ineffective communication or poor relationships with doctors can hinder patients’ ability to effectively manage their diabetes [20, 63]. In this context, doctors’ attitudes, communication styles, and interactions with patients, along with patients’ active participation in their own care, play a very important role in the treatment and care process [64]. This is because the doctor-patient relationship is widely accepted as a central component of medical care, especially in the management of chronic diseases such as diabetes [65]. The findings also show that patients’ rapport with their doctors and the establishment of a high-quality doctor-patient relationship are significantly associated with patients feeling happier after medical consultations and being less likely to consider changing doctors within a year [66]. Furthermore, positive interactions between patients and healthcare providers were found to significantly increase patients’ ability to take responsibility for their own care, boost their confidence in maintaining or improving their health, and strengthen their self-management skills [67]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the importance of developing patient-centered communication and supportive doctor-patient relationships to improve health outcomes in individuals with type 2 diabetes.

The literature indicates that the doctor-patient relationship is an important determinant of treatment adherence [19, 21], is considered one of the fundamental components of medical care for individuals with diabetes [65], and plays a critically important role in improving diabetes-related clinical outcomes [66]. An effective and high-quality doctor-patient relationship can improve patients’ health outcomes and quality of life by contributing to the reduction of complications associated with Type 2 diabetes; in parallel, it can help reduce healthcare utilization and costs [66, 68]. Studies in the literature also report that the doctor-patient relationship is an important determinant of treatment adherence [19, 21], is considered one of the fundamental components of medical care for individuals with diabetes [65], and plays a critical role in improving diabetes-related clinical outcomes [66]. Indeed, an effective and high-quality doctor-patient relationship can improve patients’ health outcomes and quality of life by contributing to the reduction of complications associated with Type 2 diabetes; in parallel, it can help reduce the use of healthcare services and costs [65, 68].

In light of the confirmation of the first three hypotheses, the fourth hypothesis of the study was also supported. In this context, statistically significant indirect (β = 0.025, 0.019) and total (β = 0.169, 0.001) associations were observed between patient activation and MARS-5 through PDRQ-9. When the patient–doctor relationship variable was added to the model, the significant association between patient activation and medication adherence was maintained; however, the magnitude of this association decreased from 0.19 to 0.14. Despite the positive associations observed between patient activation, the quality of the patient–doctor relationship, and medication adherence, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the cross-sectional design of the study does not permit causal inferences [63]. Moreover, within the context of the current healthcare system, performance-based practices may limit the time physicians can devote to individual patients, potentially affecting the quality of the patient–doctor relationship. This situation may reflect limited awareness of the importance of allocating sufficient time to support medication adherence, as well as existing gaps in knowledge in this area [69].

In this respect, the findings of the present study contribute to the literature by emphasizing the role of the quality of the doctor–patient relationship in clinical outcomes and patient-centered diabetes care. Furthermore, higher levels of patient activation and a supportive clinical approach that strengthens the doctor–patient relationship appear to be associated with improved medication adherence among individuals with diabetes [14, 56].

In the present study, patient activation among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) showed a positive, statistically significant, and moderate association with patient health engagement (PHE) (β = 0.172, 0.01), supporting the fifth hypothesis of the study. This finding is consistent with previous research conducted in samples of individuals with T2D, which reported a positive and moderate association between PHE and patient activation (β = 0.30 p<.001), suggesting that higher levels of engagement are related to greater confidence in adopting a proactive and empowered role in the care process [26].

The present study identified a positive and moderately significant association between the quality of the patient–doctor relationship and patient health engagement. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating a similar moderate relationship between PDRQ-9 and PHE [25], reinforcing the role of relational quality as an important facilitator of patient engagement. From a patient-centered perspective, a high-quality patient–doctor relationship fosters trust, open communication, and shared decision-making, which together create a supportive context for patients’ emotional and psychological engagement with their health [51, 52]. However, the moderate magnitude of this association suggests that while the patient–doctor relationship plays a meaningful enabling role, health engagement is also strongly shaped by patients’ internal psychological processes, such as illness acceptance, motivation, and self-regulatory capacity. Thus, engagement appears to emerge from the interaction between relational support and individual activation rather than being driven solely by healthcare interactions.

In contrast, the direct association between PHE-S and medication adherence (MARS-5) in the present study was weak (β = 0.30) and not statistically significant (p = .60). As a result, the seventh and eighth hypotheses were not supported. From a statistical perspective, mediation requires a significant association between the proposed mediating variable (PHE) and the dependent variable (MARS-5); the absence of such an association indicates that a mediating relationship cannot be established in this model [70]. The lack of correlation may be related to ceiling effects, the highly skewed nature of MARS-5, or cultural differences in response to PHE agents. Furthermore, within the context of this study (Type 2 diabetes patients in Türkiye), behavioral adjustment may be more strongly driven by cognitive activation and relational trust than by emotional involvement.

One possible explanation for this finding may be the conceptual proximity between patient activation, the patient–doctor relationship, and patient engagement, which may reduce the distinct contribution of PHE as a mediating variable and attenuate its observable association with medication adherence [71]. This result contrasts with previous studies reporting a mediating role of PHE in the relationship between healthcare professionals’ support for autonomy and patient activation [25]. Nevertheless, prior research has consistently emphasized the importance of the PHE model in relation to quality of care, patient activation, and treatment adherence [25, 26], suggesting that its role may vary depending on population characteristics, study design, or outcome measures.

On the basis of the results of the path analysis of our study, a unit increase in the activation level of T2D patients increased the medication adherence level by 0.148 units. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies. For example, one study reported that a 10-point increase in the PAM score corresponds to a 13% increase in medication adherence [67], whereas another study reported an 18% increase for every 5-point increase [72]. Another study revealed that each one-point increase in the PAM score increased the likelihood of adherence to medication by 1.34 times [73]. In short, when the analysis results were examined in general terms in the studies where the PAM-13 measurement tool was used, each one-point increase in the PAM score was found to reduce the risk of hospitalization by 2% and increase the likelihood of medication adherence by 2% [74, 75].

In our study, the standardized direct effect of the PDRQ-9 variable on the dependent variable (the MARS-5) is 0.132 units, and the standardized total effect (with an indirect effect of 0.004 via the mediating variable (PHE-S)) is 0.136 unitsIn comparison, Deniz et al. (2021) reported a stronger relationship between the patient–doctor relationship and medication adherence, with a direct association of 0.416 and an indirect association of 0.192 through shared decision-making, resulting in a total association of 0.608 [76]. Their findings suggest that higher perceived quality of the patient–doctor relationship was strongly associated with better medication adherence, particularly when shared decision-making was taken into account. Differences in the magnitude of these associations may be attributable to variations in study design, sample characteristics, or the inclusion of different mediating mechanisms. While the indirect association observed in the present study was relatively small, the overall pattern remains consistent with the broader literature, which generally indicates a significant positive association between patient activation, the quality of the patient–doctor relationship, and medication adherence.

In the case of patients with chronic conditions such as type 2 diabetes, the findings of this study indicate that the quality of the doctor-patient relationship is closely related to health outcomes [21]. In line with this perspective, patients who reported stronger relationships with their doctors also reported greater medication adherence, and the relationship between patient activation and adherence was stronger when the doctor-patient relationship was taken into account.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that there are significant relationships between patient activation, quality of the patient-doctor relationship, patient health participation, and medication adherence among patients with type 2 diabetes. Higher levels of patient activation were associated with better medication adherence, both directly and through stronger patient-physician relationships. Furthermore, patient activation and the quality of the patient-physician relationship were positively associated with patient health participation, highlighting the interconnected nature of these patient-centered concepts. The results also suggest that relational factors in clinical encounters, particularly the perceived quality of the patient-physician relationship, may play an important role in shaping adherence-related behaviors and patient engagement.

PDRQ-9 showed a total association of 0.122 units with PHE-S and 0.136 units with MARS-5 (0.132 direct association and 0.004 indirect association through PHE-S). PAM-13 showed a total association of 0.148 units with MARS-5 (0.139 direct association and 0.009 indirect association through PHE-S). In this case, a one-unit increase in patient activation was associated with a 0.148-unit increase in medication adherence. Similarly a one-unit increase in the PDRQ-9 score was associated with a total increase of 0.136 units in medication adherence.

In conclusion, medication adherence remains a significant ongoing issue among patients with type 2 diabetes. Inadequate adherence to type 2 diabetes-specific pharmacological treatment is a serious concern, as it can negatively impact the management and prognosis of comorbidities. Despite its clinical importance, research focusing on medication adherence in T2D populations is limited. Therefore, more comprehensive scientific, academic, and societal efforts are needed to improve medication adherence, optimize disease control, and limit the progression of T2D.

Limitations of the study and suggestions for future research

This research has several limitations. First, the fact that the sample was selected from a single hospital and using convenience sampling and limits the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, it is thought that future studies should use multi-center designs and probability sampling methods in order to increase the generalizability of the findings.

Second, the cross-sectional design prevents drawing causal inferences about the relationships among variables.

In addition, possible ceiling effects on the MARS-5 scale may have contributed to the observed non-normal distribution of medication adherence scores. High clustering of responses at the upper end of the scale may have reduced score variability, thereby limiting the sensitivity of the analyzes and the ability to detect potential mediation effects through Patient Health Engagement (PHE-S).

All study variables were assessed using self-reported questionnaires administered within a single survey; this may have led to social desirability bias and common method variance. Future studies with multicenter, longitudinal designs and objective adherence measures are recommended to validate and extend these findings. Additionally, it should be considered a significant limitation that variables such as duration of diabetes, type and number of medications used, complexity of the treatment regimen, presence of diabetes-related microvascular and macrovascular complications, and HbA1c level, although important determinants of medication adherence, were not included in the study. Therefore, future studies should include these clinical variables in their research model in order to evaluate medication adherence in a more comprehensive and holistic manner.

During the treatment and care of T2D patients, it is important for patients to be active in managing their health. Informed, confident, and competent patients more often exhibit behaviors that improve their health. In this context, activation needs to be identified for individuals with T2D to be able to properly manage their disease.

Based on our study, which shows that patient activation and the patient-physician relationship positively predict medication adherence, it is necessary for physicians to involve patients in reducing their anxiety, provide an environment conducive to patients actively taking charge of their own healthcare, place the patient’s goals at the center of care, and maximize the patient’s quality of life and comfort.
