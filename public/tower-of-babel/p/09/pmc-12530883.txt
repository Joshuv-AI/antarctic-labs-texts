Apoptotic bodies derived from hypoxic bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells reverse LPS-induced apoptosis delay in neutrophils
Authors: Yifan Zhao, Xiaoyan Li, Yitong Liu, Jiling Qiu, Siyan Liu, Juan Du, Zhenhua Luo, Junji Xu, Yi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune system disorders underlie numerous chronic inflammatory diseases. Functional status and normal apoptosis of neutrophils are of great significance in the regulation and outcome of inflammation. Excessive activation and delayed apoptosis of neutrophils can lead to additional tissue damage, which may be an important cause of delayed disease recovery. Stem cells have immunomodulatory effects, but transplanted stem cells are prone to apoptosis in the hypoxic-inflammatory microenvironment. However, the capacity of stem cell-derived apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs) to regulate neutrophil functions, along with the specific components and mechanisms involved, is still unclear. Our study revealed that ApoBDs stemming from hypoxia-induced apoptosis in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells reversed Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced delayed neutrophil apoptosis, without notable effects on neutrophil apoptosis under physiological conditions. This functional shift was attributed to the phagocytosis of miR-125b-5p within ApoBDs by neutrophils, which inhibited the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. In a mouse skin wound healing model, ApoBDs and the miR-125b-5p they contain also promoted neutrophils apoptosis around the wound and early wound closure. In summary, we demonstrated that hypoxia-induced apoptosis of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells produced ApoBDs by transferring miR-125b-5p to neutrophils to reduce the expression of PI3 Kinase p110α and inhibit the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby reversing the delay of neutrophil apoptosis induced by LPS, which may be a novel option for the treatment of diseases associated with abnormalities in neutrophil apoptosis.Keywords: apoptosis, apoptosis delay, apoptotic bodies, miR-125b-5p, neutrophils, PI3K-AKT, wound closure

Apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs), a significant byproduct of stem cell apoptosis posttransplantation, exhibit immunomodulatory potential. Hypoxia-induced ApoBDs released from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells reversed the delay of LPS-induced neutrophil apoptosis by reducing the expression of PI3 Kinase p110α and inhibiting the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway via miR-125b-5p. Apoptotic bodies and the miR-125b-5p they contain could promote neutrophil apoptosis and wound closure. This is important for advancing stem cell-based therapies and treating diseases induced by delayed neutrophil apoptosis.

Introduction

Skin is an essential barrier against external stimuli. Accelerating skin wound healing or closure significantly prevents infection and reduces scars. Studies have shown excessive neutrophil activation coupled with delayed apoptosis might account for most chronic and nonhealing wounds.1–3 In acute wounds, neutrophils may impede wound re-epithelialization or increase scar tissue formation.4,5 In addition, neutrophils often play a “double-edged sword” in inflammation. Their normal functionality aids in eliminating pathogenic microorganisms, resolving inflammation, and maintaining host health. However, the overactivation of neutrophils and delayed apoptosis may cause additional tissue damage and prolong disease recovery.6,7 Therefore, revitalizing dysfunctional neutrophils is pivotal for facilitating inflammation resolution and tissue repair.

Mesenchymal stem cells positively promote wound healing.8 Many preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of stem cells in treating various diseases. Nevertheless, several challenges remain in the practical application of stem cells, such as source, quality control, and tumorigenicity.9,10 Developing extracellular vesicles may be a promising avenue to circumvent these problems. The paracrine secretion of stem cells is identified as a key mechanism for improving the microenvironment and the function of other cells.11,12 However, the hypoxic-inflammatory microenvironment of the body presents a significant challenge for the long-term survival of transplanted stem cells, with the majority undergoing apoptosis.13,14 Apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs) have also been shown to possess therapeutic potential in treating various diseases.15,16

Accordingly, this paper investigated the effect of hypoxia-­induced ApoBDs derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) on neutrophil apoptosis and wound closure. ApoBDs reversed the delayed apoptosis of neutrophils induced by LPS and accelerated wound closure. As for the mechanism, we found miR-125b-5p played a vital role in the ApoBDs. Collectively, the study provided a novel strategy to regulate the abnormalities of neutrophil apoptosis, which could be a potential solution for the reduction and regression of chronic inflammation. Our study also laid a new foundation for applying and translating stem cells.

Materials and methods

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Capital Medical University and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (KQYY-202208-001). All animals were purchased from the SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Eight-week-old and 11-week-old male wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice were used to isolate cells, and 8-week-old male WT C57BL/6 mice were used to establish skin wound healing models. The mice were kept under specific pathogen-free conditions (24 °C, 12-h light/dark cycles, and 50% humidity) with free access to food and water. Observe the wound healing every other day.

Cell culture

Eight-week-old male WT C57BL/6 mice were used to harvest BMSCs. The hindlimbs were removed, and then bone marrow cells were separated by flushing marrow cavities and seeded in a medium of alpha-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 2 mM l-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 g/mL streptomycin (all from Invitrogen). Cells were digested and passaged using 0.25% trypsin (Invitrogen) at 80% confluence. When the cells were cultured to the third passage, they were used for subsequent experiments.

Eleven-week-old male WT C57BL/6 mice were used to harvest neutrophils. Neutrophils were extracted from mouse bone marrow and cultured in a medium of RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 g/mL streptomycin. According to the protocol of the Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Milteny Biotec), after lysing the erythrocytes in the mouse bone marrow, the non-neutrophils in the bone marrow were magnetically labeled with the Neutrophil Biotin Antibody Cocktail and Anti-Biotin Microbeads included in the kit. Finally, the LS column (Milteny Biotec) was used to adsorb the non-neutrophils to obtain high-purity neutrophils. Cells were cultured in vitro for 24 h before being used for subsequent experiments.

All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Flow cytometric analysis of cell phenotype

Neutrophils and BMSCs were resuspended in Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma). Fc receptors were then blocked by preincubating the cells with 0.25 μg anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (156603, Biolegend) per 106 cells in a volume of 100 μL for 10 min on ice. After washing twice with PBS containing 0.5% BSA at 300×g for 10 min, neutrophils were incubated with PE anti-mouse/human CD11b antibody (101208, monoclonal, Biolegend) and APC anti-mouse Ly6G antibody (127614, monoclonal, Biolegend) for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C. BMSCs were incubated with APC/Cy7 anti-mouse/human CD11b antibody (101225, monoclonal, Biolegend), ABflo 647 Rabbit anti-Mouse CD90.2 mAb (A25561, Abclonal), PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse Ly-6A/E (Sca-1) antibody (108124, monoclonal, Biolegend), Anti-Mo CD45 antibody (2892570, eBioscience) and APC anti-mouse CD73 antibody (127210, monoclonal, Biolegend) for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C. Finally, the cells were washed twice in PBS, and the flow cytometer detected positively stained cells.

Isolation and characterization of ApoBDs

First, BMSCs were placed in a sealed AnaeroPack culture tank for 48 h to induce apoptosis. The cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin without EDTA (Invitrogen) and stained with a FITC Annexin V apoptosis detection kit. The percentage of apoptosis in BMSCs was analyzed by flow cytometry. The medium was collected and centrifuged at 300×g at 4 °C for 10 min to remove cells and debris. Then, the supernatant was sequentially passed through the filter membrane of 5 and 1 μm (Millipore). Vesicles that passed through 5 μm but not 1 μm were collected and centrifuged at 2000×g at 4 °C for 20 min. The resulting precipitates were identified as ApoBDs.

The obtained ApoBDs were resuspended with 100 μL 1× binding buffer, and 5 μL Annexin V-FITC dye (BD Biosciences) was added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, ApoBDs were washed with 1 mL PBS, centrifuged at 2000×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitates were resuspended in 100 μL PBS, incubated with 1 μL Hoechst dye (Beyotime) in the dark at room temperature for 10 min, and then washed twice with PBS. Under fluorescence microscopy, the obtained ApoBDs were labeled with Hoechst and Annexin V dyes.

The morphological characteristics of ApoBDs were observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi), and the size distribution of ApoBDs was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis by Zetasizer Nano ZSE (Malvern). Approximately 50 μL of the sample droplet was placed and adsorbed onto Formvar-carbon electron microscopy grids, followed by a 10-min incubation at room temperature. Subsequently, the grids were washed twice with ultrapure water for 2 min and then immersed in phosphotungstic acid for 5 min. After washing twice and removing excess fluid using filter paper, the grids were dried in an oven at 30 °C for 30 min. Imaging was performed by using TEM.

Neutrophils apoptosis assay

Neutrophils were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. Neutrophils were treated with different concentrations of LPS (0, 0.1, 1, 10 ng/mL) (Novoprotein), ApoBDs (2 μg/mL) containing different contents, miRNA mimics (50 nM), and miRNA inhibitors (100 nM) for 18 h. According to the instructions of the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences), the collected cells were resuspended with 100 μL 1× binding buffer. Then, 5 μL PI and Annexin V-FITC dye were added to every 100 μL cell suspension. After incubation in the dark for 15 min, 400 μL of 1× binding buffer was added to each tube. Flow cytometry was performed within 1 h.

Western blots

Neutrophils were treated with LPS (0.1 ng/mL) and ApoBDs (2 μg/mL) containing different contents for 18 h. Neutrophils were collected, and the neutrophil proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (NCM) with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (APPLYGEN), protease inhibitor (Beyotime), and calcineurin inhibitor (Beyotime). After quantification by Coomassie Brilliant Blue assay (Bio-Rad), 20 μg of protein was used for each western blot according to standard protocol. Then, the membranes were blocked with 5% BSA or 5% Blotting-Grade Blocker (Bio-Rad) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Next, the membranes were incubated at room temperature with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (APPLYGEN) for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were detected by the imaging system (Bio-Rad) and quantified by ImageJ software. Primary antibodies included GAPDH (1:1000) (Cell Signaling Technology, 2118S, monoclonal), Cleaved-caspase3 (1:1000) (Cell Signaling Technology, 9664S, monoclonal), AKT (1:1000) (Cell Signaling Technology, 9272S, monoclonal), Phospho-AKT(Ser473) (1:1000) (Cell Signaling Technology, 4060S, monoclonal), and PI3 Kinase p110α (1:1000) (Cell Signaling Technology, 4249S, monoclonal).

Immunofluorescence co-localization

According to the protocols of kits, neutrophils were labeled with the PKH67 cell membrane staining kit (Bestbio) and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 15 min. After washing twice with PBS, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended. ApoBDs were labeled with the PKH26 cell membrane staining kit (Bestbio) and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing twice with PBS, the supernatant was discarded, and ApoBDs were resuspended. Then, neutrophils pretreated with LPS were co-cultured with ApoBDs for 18 h and observed under the fluorescence microscope.

miRNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

The total miRNA of ApoBDs, BMSCs, and neutrophils was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara) and SevenFast Total RNA Extraction Kit (Sevenbio), respectively. After that, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a Bulge-Loop miRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (C10211-2, Ribo). Then, the qPCR procedure was performed with the CFX96TM system (Bio-Rad) using Bulge-Loop miRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit and different specific Bulge-Loop miRNA qRT-PCR Primer Sets (Ribo). U6 was used as the internal control.

Manipulation of ApoBDs containing miRNA mimic

The ApoBDs were subjected to hypotonic treatment by resuspension in a hypotonic lysis buffer consisting of 10 mM tris (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by gentle sonication for 5 s (VCX 130 PB, Sonics). After centrifugation at 100×g for 10 min to remove the debris, the ApoBD ghosts were concentrated by centrifugation at 10 000×g for 10 min and then washed in buffer at least 3 times until the intracellular components were removed.17 A recombinant (cApoBDs) with high expression of one miRNA was constructed by mixing 1 μL miRNA mimic with 2 μL ApoBD ghost. Then, the suspension was sonicated for 2 min intermittently and gently in a bath sonicator (SY25-12, Shengyuan Supersonic). After washing with PBS, the cApoBDs were concentrated after centrifugation at 5000×g for 10 min.15

Transfection of miRNA mimic and inhibitor

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, miRNA-125b-5p mimics and inhibitors and miR-23a-3p mimics and inhibitors were transfected into neutrophils using a riboFECT CP transfection kit (Ribo), respectively, to silence and overexpress the corresponding miRNAs for 18 h. The transfection complex was composed of 20 μM miRNA mimic, 1× riboFECTCP Buffer, and riboFECTCP Reagent in riboFECT CP transfection kit. 1.25 μL of 20 μM miRNA mimic was diluted with 30 μL of 1× riboFECTCP Buffer, and 3 μL riboFECTCP Reagent was added after gentle shaking, gently mixed, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, transfection complexes were co-cultured with neutrophils and LPS for 18 h.

Skin wound healing model

According to the 3R principle, the mice were randomly divided into ApoBDs, ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p), and control groups, with 8 in each group. In addition to this, 15 mice were used to model type 1 diabetes (T1DM). Streptozotocin (60 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally to the mice after fasting for 14 h per day for 5 consecutive days. The mice showed significant weight loss with typical diabetic symptoms such as excessive drinking, eating, and urination, in addition to a randomized blood glucose level higher than 16.7 mmol/L. Diabetic mice were randomly divided into T1DM, T1DM+ApoBDs, and T1DM+ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) groups, with 5 in each group. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbitone sodium (40 mg/kg). After shaving and cleaning, a full-thickness wound (8 mm in diameter) was created on the dorsal skin. In the experimental group, 4 μg (in 75 μL PBS) of ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) were injected around the wound on the first and second days after modeling. An equal volume of PBS was injected into the control group. The wound was exposed to normal conditions after the surgery. The wound healing rate was calculated using the formula: Dn wound healing rate = (D0 wound area − Dn wound area)/D0 wound area × 100%. The mice were sacrificed on days 4 and 9, and the skin tissues were harvested for further analysis.

Histological staining

On day 9 after modeling, skin tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and underwent dehydration with graded ethanol. Then, the samples were embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-μm-thick sections. H&E and Masson staining were carried out using commercial staining kits (Solarbio).

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin sections of skin tissue on day 4 after modeling were used to examine the effect of ApoBDs on neutrophil apoptosis in the tissue. After deparaffinization, the paraffin sections were antigen-repaired with 20 μg/mL proteinase K without DNase for 30 min at 37 °C. Sections were treated with endogenous peroxidase blocking agent (ZSGB-BIO) for 20 min at room temperature. Then, the sections were blocked with Goat serum (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 30 min and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Next, the sections were incubated with alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime) at 37 °C for 30 min. The color was developed with Fast Red chromogen (ZSGB-BIO). Then, TUNEL staining (Beyotime, China) was performed according to the protocol of the manufacturer. Sections were incubated in prepared biotin labeling solution in the dark for 60 min at 37 °C. After washing once with PBS, sections were incubated with the labeled reaction termination solution for 10 min at room temperature. Streptavidin-HRP working solution was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min after washing 3 times with PBS. After washing with PBS, the color was developed with DAB chromogen. Lastly, the nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. The rate of neutrophil apoptosis was determined by double staining. The primary antibody involved in this study included Ly6G (1:1000, ab238132, Abcam).

Statistical analysis

All the data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-tailed Student’s t-tests were performed to compare 2 groups, and comparisons of multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey correction using the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Hypoxia-induced ApoBDs from BMSCs reversed LPS-induced delay in neutrophil apoptosis

Neutrophils and BMSCs were isolated from mouse bone marrow and characterized (Figure 1A, Figure S1). Following a 48-h hypoxic culture, BMSCs exhibited characteristic morphological changes under the light microscope: cell shrinkage, cytoskeleton disorder, intercellular contact disappeared, cell membrane foaming, and dead cells significantly increased (Figure 1B). Flow cytometry analysis using Annexin V and PI staining validated the induction of apoptosis in BMSCs, with 99.8% of the cells undergoing various degrees of apoptosis (Figure 1C). ApoBDs were isolated from supernatants and characterized (Figure 1D-F). Transmission scanning electron microscope showed that the size of the ApoBDs was approximately 1 µm, consistent with the DLS data.

Previous studies have demonstrated that LPS at different concentrations exerts varying effects on neutrophil apoptosis.18–22 We induced neutrophils in vitro with different concentrations of LPS and found that 0.1 ng/mL LPS significantly inhibited neutrophil apoptosis (Figure 2A). In subsequent experiments, we prestimulated neutrophils with 0.1 ng/mL LPS for 2 h to induce delayed apoptosis, followed by the addition of ApoBDs or other components to co-culture neutrophils for 18 h. To determine whether ApoBDs produced by ­hypoxia-induced apoptosis of BMSCs affected neutrophil apoptosis, we added 2 μg/mL ApoBDs to normal and inflamed neutrophils, respectively. The apoptosis of neutrophils was detected by Annexin V-FITC apoptosis kit and further validated at the protein level. The results showed that the ApoBDs could partially reverse the LPS-induced delay in neutrophil apoptosis, with no statistically significant effect on neutrophil apoptosis under physiological conditions (Figure 2B and C).

Neutrophils acquire miRNA in ApoBDs by phagocytosis

The mechanism by which ApoBDs partially reversed the LPS-induced delay in neutrophil apoptosis was further investigated. ApoBDs contained a variety of miRNA from apoptotic cells.23 Neutrophils were phagocytic immune cells that underwent apoptosis after engulfing microorganisms. Therefore, we speculated that the effect of ApoBDs on neutrophils might be related to the miRNAs encapsulated in ApoBDs. These miRNAs might act on LPS-mediated signaling pathways to achieve the regulation of neutrophil apoptosis.

To prove our hypothesis, we labeled neutrophils with PKH67 dye and ApoBDs with PKH27 dye. After co-culture for 18 h, fluorescence colocalization analysis demonstrated the phagocytosis of ApoBDs by neutrophils (Figure 3A). Based on previous miRNA sequencing analysis, relevant literature, and predictions from the Target Scan database, 7 miRNA candidates (let-7b-5p; miR-125b-5p; miR-23a-3p; miR-16-5p; miR-130-3p; miR-29b-3p; miR-671-5p) were selected for further validation (Figure 3B).15 We extracted total RNA in ApoBDs and detected the expression of 7 miRNAs in ApoBDs by PCR. The results showed that all 7 miRNAs were expressed in ApoBDs, among which let-7b-5p had the highest expression, followed by miR-125b-5p and miR-23a-3p. Other expressions were low (Figure 3B). We then examined the expression of 7 miRNAs in BMSCs after hypoxia induction. The expression of let-7b-5p, miR-125b-5p, and miR-23a-3p were all reduced, which might be because most of them were loaded into ApoBDs (Figure 3C). Furthermore, we investigated the expression of 7 miRNAs in LPS-pretreated neutrophils after co-culturing with ApoBDs. Notably, only miR-125b-5p and miR-23a-3p were upregulated after adding ApoBDs (Figure 3D). Taken together, we suggested that miR-125b-5p or miR-23a-3p in ApoBDs may play a key role in reversing the delayed apoptosis of neutrophils induced by LPS.

miR-125b-5p in ApoBDs inhibited PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

To determine the crucial miRNA in ApoBDs responsible for reversing LPS-induced delayed neutrophil apoptosis, we introduced the mimics and inhibitors of miR-125b-5p and miR-23a-3p into neutrophils to directly manipulate their expression levels (Figure S2). Compared with the control group, neutrophils overexpressing miR-125b-5p exhibited increased apoptosis, whereas those with miR-125b-5p silenced showed inhibited apoptosis. Conversely, neither the mimic nor the inhibitor of miR-23a-3p had a notable impact on neutrophil apoptosis (Figure 4A and B). Based on this, we administered inhibitors of miR-125b-5p along with ApoBDs, and we found that the effect of ApoBDs in promoting neutrophil apoptosis was then suppressed (Figure S3).

We attempted to verify this by overexpressing and silencing miR-125b-5p and miR-23a-3p, respectively, in ApoBDs. However, since miR-125b-5p and miR-23a-3p might be involved in the apoptosis process, directly introducing miRNA mimics and inhibitors into BMSCs might affect the production of ApoBDs. According to the method mentioned in the study by Li et al.,15,18 we constructed recombinant ApoBDs (cApoBDs) with high expression of miR-125b-5p or miR-23a-3p, respectively, for verification. We refer to the 2 recombinant ApoBDs as ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) and ApoBDs (miR-23a-3p), respectively. PCR analysis confirmed the expression of miRNA in the recombinant ApoBDs (Figure 5A). We then added the recombinant ApoBDs to LPS-pretreated neutrophils. Compared to controls, apoptosis was increased in neutrophils treated with ApoBDs highly expressing miR-125b-5p, whereas apoptosis in neutrophils treated with ApoBDs highly expressing miR-23a-3p did not change significantly (Figure 4C).

Since the delayed effect of LPS on neutrophil apoptosis was partly achieved by activating the PI3K-AKT pathway, we further analyzed this pathway at the protein level. We found that ApoBDs and recombinant ApoBDs overexpressing miR-125b-5p could downregulate the expression of PI3-kinase p110α and p-AKT (Figure 5B and C).24 Consequently, miR-125b-5p in ApoBDs inhibited the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, promoted the activation of caspase3, and promoted the apoptosis of neutrophils (Figure 5D).

BMSCs-derived ApoBDs and the miR-125b-5p they contain promoted skin wound closure and neutrophil apoptosis

To clarify whether ApoBDs and miR-125b-5p they contain have therapeutic effects on disease and promote neutrophil apoptosis in inflammation in vivo, we observed the effects of ApoBDs on wound healing and neutrophil apoptosis around the wound. No aseptic treatment was performed during the modeling process and subsequent treatment. Both ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) accelerated early wound closure compared to the control and diabetic groups (Figure 6A, Figure S4A). Histological staining with HE and Masson trichrome staining also suggested that wound closure in the ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) groups was superior to the control (Figure 6B, Figure S4B). To further investigate the effect of ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) on neutrophil apoptosis around the wound, we double-stained wound tissue on the fourth day post-modeling with TUNEL and Ly6G. We found that ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) could also promote neutrophil apoptosis in vivo, which might be the reason why they promoted early closure of skin wounds (Figure 7). Discussion

Delayed apoptosis and dysfunction of neutrophils are prevalent in a range of inflammatory diseases.6,25–27 Neutrophils can effectively control local infection, secrete cytokines, and orchestrate other cells to complete anti-infectious and pro-inflammatory effects. Subsequently, activated neutrophils undergo apoptosis under the influence of intracellular and extracellular death signals. They are swallowed by macrophages, promoting the transformation of macrophages into an anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby facilitating the resolution of inflammation.28,29 In chronic inflammation, neutrophils do not undergo regular or timely apoptosis, which leads to an accumulation of activated neutrophils in the body, resulting in additional tissue damage, or even an expansion of the inflammation to the point of causing tissue damage in other distal organs.30,31 Therefore, regulating neutrophil apoptosis in chronic inflammation is significant.

In our study, LPS was used to induce delayed apoptosis in neutrophils, which is consistent with the chronic inflammatory environment modeled in vitro in many studies. However, the concentration of LPS employed in this study was lower than that used in other studies, potentially due to different sources of neutrophils and LPS.19,32,33

ApoBDs, extracellular vesicles produced during cell apoptosis, encapsulate a variety of DNA, RNA, proteins, and organelles from apoptotic cells. Various cells can recognize and engulf them to realize intercellular signal transmission and functional regulation.16,34 Compared with stem cell therapy, the scale production and quality control of ApoBDs are more cost-effective and less complex. In addition, the ethical, species, tumor formation, and other issues can be effectively avoided.34,35 ApoBDs also hold promising potential as carriers of drugs for targeted therapy, underscoring the significance of studying them. However, ApoBDs derived from different cell sources and apoptotic induction methods may exhibit distinct functionalities, potentially due to variations in their surface proteins or encapsulated contents.16,34,36–38 Studies have shown that stem cell-derived ApoBDs can regulate the functions of various immune cells.34,37

To explore the effect of stem cell-derived ApoBDs on neutrophils under hypoxic conditions in vitro, neutrophils with or without LPS preconditioning were co-cultured with hypoxia-induced ApoBDs. ApoBDs reversed LPS-induced delayed apoptosis of neutrophils but did not affect normal apoptosis.

Considering the heterogeneity of ApoBDs components, a thorough investigation into the specific molecular mechanisms underlying their function is crucial for advancing stem cell therapy and ApoBDs applications. By labeling neutrophils and ApoBDs with distinct surface dyes, we observed through immunofluorescence co-localization that neutrophils can phagocytose ApoBDs. This suggests that the contents of ApoBDs released into neutrophils after phagocytosis may play a pivotal role. Apoptotic bodies carry a variety of contents from apoptotic cells, with miRNAs being a well-studied component.15,17,23 MicroRNA, a class of non-coding single-stranded RNA molecules encoded by endogenous genes, can regulate gene expression after transcription. It plays a regulatory role by binding to the target mRNA to disrupt the stability of the mRNA or to inhibit the translation of the target mRNA.39

To test our suspicions, we screened 7 miRNAs that might play a pro-apoptotic role. Based on their expression in ApoBDs, BMSCs, and neutrophils, we finally identified miR-125b-5p and miR-23a-3p for further validation. These miRNAs were chosen due to their high expression in ApoBDs and significant upregulation in neutrophils following ApoBDs addition. Through direct overexpression and silencing of these 2 miRNAs in neutrophils and the construction of cApoBDs with high expression of these miRNAs, we confirmed that miR-125b-5p reverses LPS-induced neutrophil apoptosis delay. Since ApoBDs specifically reversed the delayed apoptosis induced by LPS but had no effect on normal neutrophil apoptosis, we speculated that miR-125b-5p in ApoBDs might be involved in the signaling pathway of LPS inhibiting neutrophil apoptosis. Studies have shown that LPS could inhibit neutrophil apoptosis through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.22 The Target Scan database showed that miR-125b-5p could target PI3 Kinase p110α, the catalytic subunit of PI3K. We further verified the regulatory effect of miR-125b-5p on the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and found that miR-125b-5p could inhibit the expression of PI3 Kinase p110α and inhibit the activation of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

The results of this study demonstrated that ApoBDs derived from hypoxia-induced apoptosis of stem cells could inhibit the expression of PI3 Kinase p110α and the activation of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by delivering miR-125b-5p to neutrophils, thereby reversing the delay of neutrophil apoptosis induced by LPS.

Large and chronic wounds increase the risk of infection and scar formation, and promoting wound healing has been a pressing clinical problem. While neutrophils play a less pivotal role in wound healing in low-infection-risk scenarios, the accumulation of neutrophils in wounds delayed epidermal re-epithelialization and wound closure.2,4,5 The rate of wound closure also correlates, to some degree, with neutrophil apoptosis. The activation of neutrophils by continuous bacteria and low-grade inflammation during the wound healing process aligns more closely with the conditions of in vitro experiments.

To minimize the influence of ApoBDs on the infiltration function of other immune cells and neutrophils, the local injection of ApoBDs was conducted after the extensive infiltration of neutrophils into the wound (ie, on the first day after modeling). The injection ceased before the neutrophils entered the apoptotic phase and the extensive infiltration of macrophages (ie, on the third day after modeling). The results showed that ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) promoted early wound closure and the apoptosis of neutrophils in the wound area. This experimental result was consistent with the results of Liu et al., who reported that the local application of ApoBDs wrapped in a gel had a good effect on wound healing.37 ApoBDs and ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) also promoted the healing of chronic wounds, such as diabetic wounds. The results of TUNEL staining of skin tissues suggested that the ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) did not obtain a superior effect to ApoBDs in promoting neutrophil apoptosis. We speculate that it may be due to the presence of other contents in ApoBDs that also have the ability to promote apoptosis, which needs to be further explored. In addition to this, the uptake and utilization of ApoBDs (miR-125b-5p) by other cells, such as keratinocytes, may also be responsible. As mentioned in the study of Liu et al., ApoBDs inevitably affected other cells during treatment.37 However, our study specifically focused on the apoptosis of neutrophils, acknowledging this as a limitation and a potential area for future research. In future studies, we aim to delve deeper into the role of miR-125b-5p in ApoBDs for disease treatment within an in vivo environment. Beyond ApoBDs, diverse types of extracellular vesicles hold promise for wound healing therapies, albeit with varying degrees of effectiveness.40 Comparing the effectiveness of different vesicles for wound treatment is also worth exploring further.

Apoptosis is a multifaceted process governed by various signaling pathways, both intracellular and exogenous. Regardless of the pathway activated, caspase-3 activation is essential for executing apoptosis.41 Therefore, the expression of cleaved caspase-3 was selected as a criterion for determining apoptosis in our study. However, this may be insufficient for a comprehensive investigation of the intricate apoptotic signaling pathways involved in ApoBDs. Moreover, the functions of ApoBDs and the pathways involved may vary under different environmental conditions. The effects of ApoBDs on neutrophils and the underlying molecular mechanisms are likely multifaceted and will continue to be a focus of our future research.

Conclusion

In this study, we delved into the effect of ApoBDs produced by BMSCs under hypoxia on neutrophil apoptosis. Our findings revealed that ApoBDs could reverse the delayed apoptosis of neutrophils induced by LPS, but had no statistically significant effect on the apoptosis of normal neutrophils. Further studies showed that the mechanism of ApoBDs on neutrophil apoptosis was involved in delivering miR-125b-5p to neutrophils, thereby inhibiting the expression of PI3 Kinase p110α and inhibiting the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This discovery holds substantial significance in regulating neutrophils with abnormal function in chronic inflammation. Furthermore, this study is valuable for clarifying the mechanisms of immune regulation by transplanted stem cells in vivo, which may be a potential therapeutic target for chronic inflammation.
