Gaps in lipid screening and statin use among uninsured patients in a charitable community clinic
Authors: Tanvi A Ingle, Christie Tran, Michael Pannell, Marcus Hurt, Josh Peedikayil, Laura Umana, Nora Gimpel, Amit Khera, Zahid Ahmad
Published: 2026

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and timely lipid screening with appropriate initiation of statin therapy is central to primary and secondary prevention [1]. Socioeconomically disadvantaged communities face structural barriers—including limited access to laboratory testing, medication affordability, and inconsistent healthcare—that contribute to higher rates of ASCVD [2]. Yet, little is known about how these gaps manifest within charitable clinics that serve uninsured patients. To address this evidence gap, we evaluated disparities in lipid screening rates, statin use, and statin-eligibility among uninsured individuals at a large free clinic.

Methods

Study design and data acquisition

We conducted a retrospective study using de-identified Athena Health electronic medical record (EMR) data from a large free clinic in Texas. All patients with ≥1 clinical encounter between 5/23/2016 through 12/31/2024 were included. Data extracted included demographics, diagnoses, labs, medications, vitals, and smoking status. The UT Southwestern IRB approved this study (STU-2022–0494).

Lipid screening and statin use

Lipid screening was defined as ≥1 completed lipid panel. Children 9–11 years, adolescents 17–21 years, and all adults (≥ 18 years) were eligible for screening [3]. Statin use was defined as EMR-documented active prescriptions and was categorized by intensity [4].

Statin eligibility classification

Patients who completed lipid testing were classified sequentially into the ACC/AHA guidelines benefit groups, contemporaneous with the study period [4]:

For primary prevention, ASCVD risk scores were calculated using the PCE and PREVENT models [[5], [6], [7]]. For the PCE, age, sex, smoking, lipid panels, blood pressure, and race were required. For the PREVENT base model, age, sex, lipid values, smoking, diabetes, hypertension (treated or untreated), blood pressure, and eGFR were required. When available, we used laboratory eGFR; otherwise, we calculated eGFR from the latest serum creatinine. For the PREVENT full model, the base parameters were required along with three optional fields: urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, hemoglobin A1c, and social deprivation index. The model applied default coefficients for missing optional variables. Scores were not calculated if required variables were omitted.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using frequencies, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR). We fit a logistic regression model to identify predictors of statin prescription; covariates included age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, highest total cholesterol, highest LDL-C, and lowest HDL-C. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were included; all lipid-related parameters were reported per 10 mg/dL and age was reported per 10 years. Model performance was assessed with the likelihood ratio χ² test, Brier score, and Harrell’s C-index.

Results

Study population

During the study period, 11,167 patients received care at the charity clinic. Of these, 8277 were adults (median age 43, IQR [31–53]), predominantly female (62.8%), and Hispanic/Latino ethnicity (87.4%). The pediatric cohort (<18 years) included 2890 patients (median age 11, IQR [8–14]), also predominantly Hispanic/Latino (96%). Of 34,300 encounters, 15,634 (45.5%) were for chronic diseases, 10,601 (30.9%) were for acute care, and the remaining involved miscellaneous services (nutrition, vaccines). Additionally, 60.2% of all patients had ≥ 1 encounter, with a median of 4 [IQR 2–7] appointments among returning patients.

Lipid screening

Among adults, 2379 (28.7%) completed lipid screening, with a median LDL-C of 106 mg/dL [IQR 85–130]. Of these, 30 (1%) had ASCVD, 589 (24.8%) diabetes, 965 (41%) hyperlipidemia, and 890 (37.4%) hypertension (Fig. 1 Panel A). Only 1 of 686 children aged 9–11 years and 49 of the 1042 adolescents aged 17–21 years completed lipid testing, with a median LDL-C of 81 mg/dL [IQR 72–99]. The median encounters until lipid testing was 1 [IQR 1–2], and the median time-to-first lipid test was 1 day [IQR 0–117]. Furthermore, 1998 (83%) of patients who completed lipid testing did so within 12 months of establishing care.

Statin use by eligibility group

Of the 2379 adults who completed lipid screening, 860 (36.1%) met criteria for a statin benefit group and 490 (57%) of these eligible patients received statins. Statin use was most common among ASCVD (70%) or LDL ≥ 190 (78%) groups; however, while these 101 patients were eligible for high-intensity statins, only 40 (39.6%) were on high-intensity statin therapy. For primary prevention, 41% of patients classified by PCE received a statin and use was marginally higher among the PREVENT groups (Fig. 1 Panel B). Eligibility for primary prevention varied by the ASCVD-risk model applied. The PCE at a 7.5% risk threshold identified 287 individuals, comparable to the 304 identified using the PREVENT base at a 3.5% threshold (Fig. 1 Panel C).

Predictors of statin prescription

We fit a logistic regression model to identify factors associated with statin prescriptions (χ²:830.46, P < 0.0001; Brier’s Score:0.111; Harrell’s C-index:0.891). Statin use was more likely among adults with type 2 diabetes (OR 8.55, 95% CI 6.50–11.25), older age (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.65–2.09), higher total cholesterol (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.17–1.51), smoking (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.2–2.50), and male sex (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.16–1.99). Statins were less likely with higher HDL-C levels (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.60–0.79).

Discussion

Among uninsured patients at a charitable clinic, lipid screening rates were markedly low despite longitudinal engagement with the clinic (median 4 visits per returning patient). Fewer than one-third of adults and ∼3% of children/adolescents completed lipid testing, substantially lower than national averages [8]. Even among statin-eligible adults, just over half received a statin, and fewer were on high-intensity therapy. These findings highlight shortfalls in preventive cardiovascular care among uninsured populations.

Structural barriers likely contribute. Uninsured patients face competing priorities, including medication affordability, transportation barriers, food insecurity, and limited healthcare access. Despite these barriers, free clinics offer valuable opportunities for preventive care. In this clinic, over 60% of patients had multiple visits and a short interval to lipid testing, indicating that longitudinal ASCVD-risk assessment and treatment are feasible.

The PREVENT models identified a smaller cohort than the PCE at a 7.5% risk threshold. However, the 2026 AHA/ACC dyslipidemia guidelines now recommend lower treatment thresholds (ASCVD-risk ≥ 3%) [9], which would likely increase PREVENT-based eligibility and align it more closely with PCE based estimates.

Our regression analysis identified characteristics associated with statin prescriptions among uninsured patients. The high C-index suggests collinearity among established cardiovascular risk factors, like diabetes, and benefit groups. Since this model predicts prescribing behavior, its performance may reflect provider adherence to these risk factors.

This study has several limitations. Low lipid screening rates mask the extent of undertreatment. The observed statin use among our eligible cohort (57%) may be inflated compared to prior studies of uninsured (33.6%) and insured patients (53.2%) [10]. Risk scores were derived from data obtained at varying timepoints. Data may exclude labs, prescriptions, or diagnoses obtained externally. This single-site study may not generalize to all uninsured populations.

In summary, uninsured patients at a charity clinic had limited lipid screening and suboptimal statin use despite substantial cardiometabolic risk. Future research should identify scalable strategies to overcome structural barriers and reduce the disproportionate burden of ASCVD in uninsured populations.
