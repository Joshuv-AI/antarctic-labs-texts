Self-reported physical activity and risk of primary knee replacement for osteoarthritis: a competing risks cohort analysis
Authors: Hao Peng, Qianying Cai, Dongning Liu, Vicky Duong, Win Min Oo, Changqing Zhang, David J Hunter, Yongqiang Zheng, Kai Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether baseline self‑reported physical activity (PA) is associated with the risk of primary knee replacement (KR) for osteoarthritis (OA) remains uncertain, particularly across radiographic severity.MethodsIn 2,534 Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) participants with radiographic knee OA (RKOA; Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) ≥ 2) who completed the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) at baseline, we examined the association between PASE tertiles (low, moderate, high) and OA‑related primary KR over 108 months. We modeled OA‑KR as the event of interest and non‑OA primary KR and pre‑KR death as competing risks. We fit both cause‑specific and subdistribution hazard models, adjusted for demographics, BMI, race/education, economic status (income categories), WOMAC pain, knee alignment, bilateral involvement, and prior knee injury/surgery. Because KL grade violated the proportional hazards assumption and interacted with PASE (P for interaction < 0.001), analyses were stratified by KL grade (2 vs. > 2). PASE was self‑reported and cross‑sectional (baseline only).ResultsOver nine years, 372 participants underwent primary OA‑KR. The effect of physical activity on OA-KR risk was strongly modified by baseline radiographic severity. In subdistribution hazard models, participants with KL grade 2 and moderate physical activity had a substantially lower risk of OA-KR compared to those with low activity (adjusted HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20–0.73). In contrast, among those with KL grade > 2, moderate activity was associated with a higher risk (adjusted HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.08–1.97). In both strata, high levels of activity conferred no evidence of benefit or harm (KL grade 2: HR 0.86, 95%CI 0.49–1.50; KL grade > 2: HR 1.33, 95%CI 0.94–1.88). Cause-specific models yielded similar results.ConclusionThe association between baseline self‑reported PA and OA‑KR depends on radiographic severity. For individuals with KL grade 2, moderate PA was associated with lower KR risk; for KL grade > 2, moderate PA was associated with higher KR risk. Clinically, PA advice may be framed by severity: encourage moderate-intensity, low-impact activity and strength/neuromuscular training in earlier disease, and emphasize load management (low-impact modes, pacing, symptom monitoring) and weight management in advanced disease within shared decision-making. Because PASE is self‑reported and measured once, causal inference is limited; longitudinal and wearable‑based assessments are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09254-y.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Physical activity, Knee replacement, Radiographic severity

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent chronic disease, causing pain, joint stiffness, and disability in adults, especially in older adults and the working population [1, 2]. Approximately 250 million people worldwide are living with OA, with knee joints most commonly affected, accounting for nearly 85% of the global OA burden [1, 3]. Despite the established benefits of nonoperative management strategies such as physical therapy, weight management, and exercise programs in OA management, these approaches remain underutilized [1]. Emphasizing their role can lead to better patient outcomes, reduce the need for surgical interventions, and contribute to a holistic approach to addressing OA.

Knee replacement (KR), including total and partial knee replacement, serves as an effective, widely used intervention for end-stage knee OA, effectively reducing pain and improving physical activity levels [4]. However, the invasive nature of KR and potential complications underscore the need for strategies to prevent OA patients from progressing to the point where surgery is required [5]. Though effective therapies to halt OA progression are currently lacking, early identification of patients at a higher risk and timely interventions could offer a viable approach. KR stands as a significant indicator of knee OA progression, considering the often low correlation between imaging changes and symptom improvement [6]. While KR is a critical endpoint, it is important to consider it within the broader scope of OA progression. The FDA’s accelerated approval process for disease-modifying OA drugs (DMOADs) underscores the potential of surrogate biomarkers to predict long-term outcomes like KR, suggesting the need for a multifaceted approach to assess OA progression [7].

Physical activity, encompassing exercise, work, and housework, involves bodily movement generated by skeletal muscles, resulting in energy expenditure [8]. While exercise therapy has been shown to offer substantial benefits in managing OA, the role of physical activity as a risk factor for KR due to OA remains undefined, reflecting differences in exposure measurement (self‑report vs. objective), case definitions, and analytic approaches (e.g., ignoring competing risks) [9]. Some studies have identified a greater likelihood of total knee or hip replacement among former elite athletes, suggesting that repetitive high-impact loading or prior trauma may contribute to the risk of KR [10]. We also note epidemiologic links between heavy manual work (e.g., kneeling, squatting, lifting) and knee OA/KR documented in prior studies [11, 12]. In contrast, other research has found no correlation between physical activity and the risk of KR [13–15]. Given the ubiquity of OA guidelines advocating increased physical activity [16], understanding the potential effects of physical activity on the risk of KR is vital from a public health standpoint.

The aim of this study is to investigate whether self-reported physical activity at baseline is a risk factor for primary KR in patients with knee OA. Leveraging the OAI with centralized radiographic assessment, long follow-up, and adjudicated arthroplasty information allows us to examine whether baseline PA is associated with OA-KR while explicitly modeling death and non-OA KR as competing events. Considering the well-documented benefits of physical activity for overall physical and mental health [17, 18], our hypothesis is that higher levels of physical activity could act protectively, thereby reducing the risk of primary KR in patients with OA.

Materials and methods

Study design

The Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), a multi-center prospective cohort study, provides the data for our analysis. It focuses on individuals in the US who either present with symptomatic and radiographic knee OA or are at a high risk of developing these conditions. Detailed information on the study’s protocol can be accessed at https://oai.epi-ucsf.org/datarelease/docs/StudyDesignProtocol.pdf. The OAI was approved by the Committee on Human Research, the Institutional Review Board (IRB) for the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), and the IRBs at each clinical site. This current work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria [19].

Study population

Initially, the OAI comprised 4,796 participants aged between 45 and 79 years. From this pool, we identified 2,550 participants with radiographic knee OA (RKOA, defined by Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) grade ≥ 2 using central X-ray readings) in at least one knee. Our study integrated data from both the progression and incidence cohorts. While all participants demonstrated radiographic OA, it is essential to note that not all exhibited clinical symptoms. Out of these, 2,534 participants who completed the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) questionnaires at the baseline were selected for our analysis. If KR occurred, we used the pre‑operative diagnosis recorded in OAI to classify events as OA‑related vs. non‑OA (e.g., post‑traumatic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, “Other/Unable to determine”). Non‑OA primary KR and pre‑KR death were treated as competing risks; no KR events were excluded for indeterminate diagnoses.

Physical activity assessment

We evaluated physical activity using the PASE, a validated tool for individuals with knee OA [20, 21]. Furthermore, studies have shown the PASE to exhibit consistent and reproducible outcomes when used in a knee OA population, underscoring its reliability [22, 23]. This scale comprises questions regarding household chores, occupational activities, and leisure activities conducted in the preceding week. A total score (ranging from 0 to 793, with higher scores signifying greater physical activity) was computed by multiplying the time dedicated to each activity by its empirically derived weight and then summing these values across all activities. Participants were categorized into tertiles based on their PASE scores: those below the 33rd percentile were classified into the “low” physical activity group, those between the 33rd and 67th percentiles were classified as “moderate”, and those above the 67th percentile were categorized as “high” physical activity group.

Covariates

To address potential confounding factors effectively, several factors, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), race, education level, economic status (self‑reported annual household income), KL grade, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index (WOMAC) pain scores (range: 0–20), knee alignment, numbers of involved joints (two-side or one-side), history of knee injury and history of knee surgery were considered potential covariates based on previous literature and the understanding of OA disease [24–29]. Including these variables helps to eliminate confounding effects, ensuring that the observed associations are more likely to reflect true effects rather than artifacts of omitted variable bias. Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race, and education level were collected at baseline. KL grade was assessed by a centrally trained radiologist or rheumatologist at baseline and was categorized as 2 and >2 in this study [27]. For participants who underwent knee replacement, the KL score corresponding to the replaced joint was used for categorization. For others, the KL score from the clinically and radiologically determined worst knee was used. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m) at baseline. BMI was chosen as a covariate in our analytical model based on its widespread use in knee OA research, its unified representation of body composition, and its clinically interpretable categories. Knee pain was evaluated by the WOMAC pain scores (Version LK 3.1), chosen for its comprehensive representation of functional limitations in OA patients. Based on literature recommendations, WOMAC pain scores were categorized into two groups: WOMAC pain (< 5) and WOMAC pain (≥ 5) [30]. Knee alignment was assessed by a weight-bearing anteroposterior radiograph on a long limb x-ray at baseline. It was measured in degrees, with positive values representing varus alignment and negative values representing valgus alignment. Malalignment is defined by a mechanical axis deviation of 2 degrees or more in either the varus or valgus direction on a long limb X-ray. An alignment less than 2 degrees in either direction is considered neutral [27].

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the time from baseline to the first OA‑related primary KR. Competing events were pre‑KR death and non‑OA primary KR. Participants without OA‑KR were censored at 108 months (OAI maximum follow‑up). We estimated cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) for each event type. In our competing risk framework, the main event of interest is KR, specifically attributable to OA. This determination was anchored on OAI dataset variables “V99ELKPODX” for the left knee and “V99ERKPODX” for the right knee. KR cases were classified as OA-related when these variables indicated a pre-operative diagnosis of “OA.” Conversely, events that preclude the occurrence of the primary OA-related KR event are identified as competing risks. For the purposes of our study, these competing risks included death before any KR and primary KR due to conditions other than OA, such as rheumatoid arthritis, acute fractures, or trauma. This methodology, consistent with the principles described by Austin et al. [31], considers a competing risk as any event that inhibits the primary event. To ensure accuracy, KR cases with indeterminate diagnoses were systematically excluded from our core analysis to maintain data precision and reliability.

Statistical analysis

We summarized the characteristics by physical activity levels (PASE score as low, moderate, or high), presenting categorical variables with counts and percentages, and continuous variables with means and standard deviations (SDs). We employed CIFs to estimate the marginal probability for each competing event, culminating in the final assessment at 108 months, thus illustrating the proportion of patients experiencing KR or pre-KR death over time. The association between the physical activity levels and KR was investigated using cause-specific Cox proportional hazards (Cox-PH) regression models and the competing risk regression models [32]. We examined the proportional hazards(PH) assumption with Schoenfeld residuals and assessed the linearity of the continuous PASE score with martingale residuals. Additionally, to evaluate the potential interaction between physical activity and KL grade, we incorporated an interaction term in the interaction models. The observed violation of the PH assumption for KL grade and significant interaction effects (P for interaction < 0.001) between KL grade and physical activity levels prompted a subgroup analysis, stratifying subjects into KL grade 2 and KL grade >2 groups. Violation of the PH assumption can lead to misleading and erroneous findings, necessitating adjustments to maintain the integrity of the analysis [33]. Several methods are available to address this non-proportionality, with stratification being one of the most direct [33, 34]. We chose to stratify our survival model by the KL grade, the risk factor violating the PH assumption. This stratification allows for separate baseline hazards for each stratum (KL grade 2 and >2), thereby providing a more accurate analysis of the relationship between physical activity and knee replacement risk across different severity levels of OA. For multivariable analyses, we applied several models with different covariates. We adjusted for baseline covariates including age, sex, race, education level, BMI, economic status, WOMAC pain (< 5, ≥ 5), knee alignment (malignment or not), numbers of involved joints (two-side or one-side), history of knee injury and history of knee surgery; these formed the basic covariates set across all models. In 2,534 participants, 372 OA-KR events yielded an events-per-variable of 17.4, well above the threshold of 10, indicating low risk of overfitting [35]. Bootstrap validation (100 resamples) produced a shrinkage factor of 1.01, further confirming model stability [36].

We prespecified alternative parameterizations of PASE: (i) quartiles and (ii) continuous using restricted cubic splines (five knots). Because PASE captures the prior 7 days and may be subject to recall and seasonal variation, we additionally adjusted for the season of the baseline visit (spring/summer/autumn/winter derived from the baseline date). To address bilateral pathology, we conducted a knee-level analysis including 4,992 knees from 2,496 participants with bilateral KL grading, fitting a marginal Cox proportional hazards model with cluster-robust (participant-level) standard errors to account for within-person correlation. Finally, to handle missing covariate data, we used multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 5) under a missing‑at‑random assumption, re‑estimated the primary models in each imputed dataset, and combined estimates using Rubin’s rules [37]. All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1. We report all confidence intervals (CIs) at the 95% level, and all tests are two-sided with a 5% significance threshold.

Results

This study encompassed 2534 RKOA patients with baseline PASE data, with a collective mean age of 62.57 (standard deviation, SD: 9.00) years. The cohort was predominantly female, constituting 57.9% of the population. Average BMI was calculated at 29.62 kg/m2 (SD: 4.80), while the mean PASE score stood at 156.45 (SD: 81.31). Participants with higher PASE scores were generally younger, with the high tertile averaging 58.4 years and a smaller proportion above 60 years (39.6%). Females were less prevalent in this group at 48.7%. A gradient in socioeconomic status was evident, with those in the higher PASE tertile displaying greater educational (86.9% above high school level) and income achievements (27.9% earning over $100K).

Clinical outcomes varied with PASE scores; the high tertile reported higher knee injury and surgery rates (39.0% and 56.6%, respectively) and a greater readiness to consider knee replacement surgery at 72 months (74.3%). Pain assessment via WOMAC scores and the occurrence of KR due to OA or other causes also differed across tertiles, with the low PASE group showing higher rates of adverse outcomes (Death or KR not due to OA) not associated with OA (7.9%).

Cumulative incidence of KR and pre-KR death

During the nine-year follow-up, 412 patients underwent KR, 372 participants underwent primary KR due to OA, and 40 patients received KR but not due to OA. Of the 40 KR not related to OA, the predominant pre-operative diagnoses were categorized as ‘Other’ or “Unable to determine” (25/40), with secondary reasons including ‘Post-traumatic arthritis (old trauma)’ (14/40), and one case was due to ‘Rheumatoid arthritis’. Competing risk analysis of KR following OA

In the competing risk analysis, PASE tertiles showed distinct hazard ratios (HR) when adjusted for covariates (Table 2). Initial models adjusting for covariates (Model 2) did not show an association between PASE tertiles and the risk of KR due to OA. However, an interaction was detected between PASE tertiles and baseline KL grade (Model 3), revealing that the effect of PASE was dependent on the baseline KL grade (P for interaction < 0.001 for moderate PASE). Specifically, among patients with KL grade > 2, those with moderate PASE had an increased risk of KR (subdistribution HR 3.88, 95% CI: 1.92–7.85). Conversely, among patients with KL grade 2, moderate PASE was associated with a reduction in risk (subdistribution HR 0.38, 95% CI: 0.20–0.71). No significant effects were observed for the high PASE in either KL stratum. These findings were robust and consistent across both cause-specific and subdistribution hazard models.

Sensitivity analyses

Results were robust to all checks. Re‑parameterizing PASE (quartiles and restricted cubic splines, 5 knots), adding season at baseline, modeling both knees with a marginal Cox model (clustered by participant), and using multiple imputation (m = 5) yielded the same pattern as the primary analysis: moderate PASE remained protective in KL grade 2 and harmful in KL grade > 2; estimates for high PASE were smaller and generally non‑significant. The PASE×KL interaction persisted across specifications, and spline curves mirrored the categorical results (Tables S1–S4; Figure S1).

Discussion

In our study of RKOA patients, we observed that higher PASE scores were typically seen in younger individuals who also exhibited higher socioeconomic status. Interestingly, these higher PASE scores were associated with an increased incidence of knee injury, surgery and a greater willingness to consider knee replacement surgery after 72 months. Despite this, our findings indicated a complex relationship between activity levels and KR outcomes. While moderate PASE scores were linked to a lower risk of knee replacement in patients with a KL grade of 2, this protective effect was reversed with a KL grade higher than 2, where moderate PASE scores suggested an increased risk.

These findings underscore the multifaceted relationship between physical activity, knee pathology, and clinical decision-making in RKOA. However, our study’s narrative would be incomplete without discussing the impact of nonoperative management strategies. The underutilization of these approaches—spanning physical therapy, weight management, and other non-invasive interventions—could have accelerated the transition towards surgical interventions in our OA population, thereby influencing our outcomes. This notion is supported by the fact that higher activity levels might reflect a patient population with access to, and willingness to engage in, nonoperative management strategies, potentially delaying the need for KR. Our study also highlights the multifaceted nature of OA progression and the decision-making process for KR. Acknowledging the FDA’s use of surrogate markers in the approval of DMOADs, our results underscore the importance of considering both symptom severity and structural damage when evaluating the need for KR. Our analysis contributes to understanding the impact of physical activity on KR decisions, underlining the need for thorough patient evaluations in OA management.

Our analysis suggests that there is a complex relationship between activity levels and primary KR outcomes in OA patients, a finding in line with certain previous studies but contradictory to others. For instance, a cohort study conducted by Wang [38] demonstrated an increased risk of primary KR due to OA with escalating levels of physical activity. In a Singapore Chinese cohort study, the researchers also found moderate physical activity of more than 5 h/week was associated with increased KR risk [39]. However, a prospective cohort study by Skou [40] indicated no association between physical activity and KR risk in OA patients. Contrary to their findings, our results elucidate a more nuanced relationship, suggesting that the impact of physical activity levels on KR risk may vary with the radiographic classification of OA (KL grade 2 vs. >2). Furthermore, these results are noteworthy as they contradict a common perception in the community that physical activity may accelerate joint deterioration, leading to premature KR, and hence, individuals should restrain their physical activity.

These discrepancies could result from differences in physical activity measurement methodologies, variations in study design, or population characteristics. Although the PASE questionnaires have been validated for older adults with knee OA [21], self-reported questionnaires may not accurately represent the true intensity and type of physical activity, which can influence the impact on knee joints. Incorporating data from wearable technology, such as advanced pedometers or activity trackers, alongside self-reported questionnaires could offer a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of physical activity. Such an approach not only captures the quantity but also the quality of movements, thereby addressing potential limitations associated with traditional methods. Moreover, the applicability of comparing studies using diverse scales assigning different weights to various physical activity types remains uncertain. Additionally, some studies have utilized objective physical activity measures, such as pedometers [41], but the traditional pedometer does not account for squatting, muscle strength training and other exercises that increase the load of the knee joint. Therefore, the questionnaire for an accurate evaluation of physical activity in terms of type, frequency, intensity, and joint load seems to be relevant and important for the study of the risk factors of OA.

Our study emphasizes that physical activity can be considered safe for patients with OA with a KL grade of 2, which is significant, as the fear of joint failure might otherwise discourage physical activity. This is important as physical activity has other benefits, including overall health improvement and potential OA symptom management [17]. Furthermore, using joint replacement as a composite endpoint allows us to take into account both the symptoms and the structural state of the joint, offering a comprehensive understanding of the disease’s progression. In addition, our study highlights the importance of individualized patient assessments. For instance, while higher physical activity levels are generally advocated for their systemic health benefits, our data suggest that there may be a threshold beyond which increased activity could exacerbate joint degradation in patients with advanced RKOA (KL grade higher than 2). The significant KL×PASE interaction (p < 0.001) indicates that while moderate PASE lowered OA-KR risk in KL = 2, both moderate and high PASE similarly increased risk in KL >2, suggesting that disease severity modifies how activity relates to surgical outcomes. In considering the broader implications of these findings, it is vital to reflect on the role of physical activity counseling in clinical practice. For patients with RKOA, particularly those at an advanced stage, health professionals might consider recommending tailored exercise regimens that maintain joint health without accelerating pathology.

PA’s effect on KR likely varies by type/dose and joint status: high‑impact or repetitive loading and malalignment may exacerbate cartilage/bone stress in advanced RKOA, whereas moderate, low‑impact PA may support muscle strength, neuromuscular control, and weight management in earlier disease. Importantly, the exercise literature shows benefits for pain and function across OA severities; our endpoint is surgery, which integrates clinical decision‑making beyond structural change alone. Our findings, therefore, do not contradict exercise recommendations; they support framing PA by radiographic severity.

As per our understanding, this is one of the few cohort studies utilizing the Fine and Gray model to evaluate the relationship between physical activity levels and primary KR in OA populations. We chose the competing risk model over conventional ones to gain comprehensive insights from the data and to incorporate death occurrences before any KR and primary KR not due to OA into the analysis. Competing risks are common in survival data analysis. In our study, we opted for a competing risk assessment over the Cox Proportional Hazards model, understanding that even a 4% occurrence of death can serve as a significant competing event against KR, ensuring our findings’ broader applicability and capturing a more genuine reflection of KR risk amidst potential competing events. By employing the CIF to compute the marginal probability of a specific event based on its cause-specific probability and overall survival probability, we can avoid the upward bias often observed when using the Kaplan-Meier survival function to estimate the crude incidence of outcomes [31, 32].

Limitations of the study

The strength of our study lies in a large-scale prospective clinical cohort, long-term follow-up, a substantial number of OA patients confirmed by imaging, and KR data confirmed by imaging follow-up rather than self-reported information. However, our findings should be interpreted with caution due to some limitations. Firstly, the use of self-reported PASE scores to classify physical activity tertiles may introduce bias such as misclassification, despite the questionnaire being validated [42]. This could potentially obscure subtle differences in the relationship between physical activity and OA outcomes. We acknowledge that the quality of exercise performance can significantly vary among individuals. Incorrect execution of exercises may not only fail to benefit but also potentially exacerbate musculoskeletal conditions. This variability could indeed affect the outcomes of our study, and future research should aim to control and document the quality of exercise performance more precisely. Importantly, this study is a prospective cohort study without a control group. Such a design inherently lacks the comparative nature that a control group would provide, potentially leading to biases in estimating the true effect. Without a control group, there could be confounding variables that have not been controlled for or identified, which may influence the results. Furthermore, selection bias is a concern as participants in cohort studies are often not randomly selected and may represent a particular demographic or clinical profile that does not generalize to the broader population. Recall bias may also influence the results, particularly when participants are asked to remember past behaviors or experiences. Residual confounding (e.g., higher PA correlating with healthcare access, motivation, and candidacy for KR) and reverse causation (symptom dynamics influencing activity and surgical choice) might exist as well. Thirdly, our study relied on baseline physical activity data. Thus, the impact of changes in physical activity over time on KR incidence couldn’t be analyzed, although studies have shown the level of physical activity does not change over the typical course of the disease progression [43, 44]. PA may change with symptoms over time, and we lacked repeated measures; this limits causal interpretation, especially for KL grade 2, who may have had minimal symptoms at baseline. Additionally, in longitudinal cohort studies like ours, participants entering with varying levels of OA severity can introduce variability, potentially complicating the interpretation of outcomes and progression rates. Also, factors such as physical condition and physician bias could influence the use of KR as an OA progression indicator despite adjusting for numerous covariates. Our findings provide insight into knee OA outcomes within a specific demographic, yet caution should be exercised when extrapolating to broader populations. Future research should explore these relationships across diverse groups to ensure broader applicability and validate our results.

Conclusions

Baseline self‑reported PA was differentially associated with OA‑KR by KL severity: moderate PA related to lower risk in KL grade 2, whereas moderate PA related to higher risk in KL grade > 2. These findings argue for framing PA advice by radiographic severity, emphasizing low‑impact and strengthening strategies within shared decision‑making. Because PA was self‑reported at a single time point, and surgery is a decision endpoint, causal inferences are limited. Interventional and longitudinal wearable‑based studies that consider activity type/dose, alignment, and symptom trajectories are warranted.
