Quality of Allergic Rhinitis Content Across Social Media Platforms
Authors: Emily Kwon, Lucy Revercomb, Alexandra Filipkowski, Annie Xu, Brandon Gold, Rachel Kaye, Wayne D Hsueh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo assess the quality and engagement of allergic rhinitis‐related short‐form content on social media.MethodsA search across TikTok, Instagram Reels, Facebook Reels, and YouTube Shorts was conducted for allergic rhinitis‐related hashtags. Posts were categorized by content category, author, and popularity. Content was analyzed with the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool for Audiovisual Material (PEMAT‐AV) to assess understandability, Global Quality Scale (GQS) to measure quality, and Accuracy in Digital‐health Information (ANDI) to measure accuracy. Videos were independently assessed by two reviewers per platform; a third reviewer resolved discrepancies.ResultsFour hundred and sixty videos were analyzed. Most (69.1%) were educational and authored by medical professionals (38.5%) or lay individuals (48.9%). YouTube had the greatest proportion of medical professional content (56.7%). Instagram scored highest for PEMAT understandability (72.0%) versus YouTube (70.9%), TikTok (61.1%), and Facebook (45.9%) (p < 0.001). YouTube scored highest for PEMAT actionability (55.6%) versus Instagram (48.2%), TikTok (45.3%), and Facebook (35.4%) (p < 0.001). YouTube had the highest average GQS (3.12) compared with Instagram (2.70), TikTok (2.21), and Facebook (2.09) (p < 0.001). YouTube also had the highest average ANDI score (2.80) compared with Facebook (2.19), TikTok (1.91), and Instagram (1.68) (p ≤ 0.026). On multivariable analysis, medical professionals were associated with greater GQS, ANDI, PEMAT understandability, and PEMAT actionability scores (all p < 0.001).ConclusionsAmong the platforms studied, Instagram and YouTube content just barely met the PEMAT understandability threshold (≥ 70%). However, none of the platforms on average contained adequately actionable or completely accurate content. YouTube Shorts host the greatest proportion of medical professional content and the highest‐quality content relative to other platforms.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: allergic rhinitis, ANDI, GQS, hay fever, PEMAT‐A/V, seasonal allergies, short‐form content, social media

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is one of the most common upper respiratory conditions, affecting nearly 50 million individuals in the United States [1]. Symptoms such as nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and impaired sleep contribute to decreased quality of life, reduced productivity, and substantial healthcare utilization [1]. With the rise in the use of technology and social media, many patients are increasingly seeking online platforms and short‐form videos for advice on symptom management, often in lieu of professional medical advice.

Short‐form video platforms have grown rapidly as channels for health information dissemination. TikTok, released in 2016, now has 136 million users in the United States and 82.2 million daily active users [2]. Other platforms have similarly risen in engagement: nearly 800 million users watch Facebook Reels, 2.8 billion users watch Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts collectively has had over 5 trillion views since inception [3]. The accessibility, popularity, and oftentimes utilizations of layman's language in short‐form content allow medical and health‐related content to reach wide audiences—regardless of accuracy. Previous studies in otolaryngology have highlighted concerns about the validity and reliability of short‐form content [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. These studies express concerns over the spread of false medical information and emphasize the variability in content quality across different online platforms. Layperson‐generated content often receives wide exposure but can be inaccurate or offer limited practical guidance, while videos by medical professionals are usually more reliable but less widely disseminated and may use jargon difficult for broader audiences to understand.

Evaluating the quality of AR‐related content on these platforms is particularly important given AR's high prevalence, reliance on evidence‐based therapy, and potential for misinformation to affect treatment decisions and symptom management. Validated instruments such as the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool for Audiovisual Materials (PEMAT‐A/V), the Global Quality Scale (GQS), and Accuracy in Digital‐health Information (ANDI) allow systematic evaluation of understandability, actionability, quality, and accuracy of health information [6, 9, 10]. While these tools have been utilized to assess existing social media content on other medical conditions, no prior work has systematically assessed AR content across multiple short‐form video platforms.

Our study evaluates the quality, accuracy, understandability, actionability, and engagement of AR‐related short‐form videos on TikTok, Instagram Reels, Facebook Reels, and YouTube Shorts, with comparisons by platform and content creator type.

Materials and Methods

To ensure no prior search history biased the content portrayed to researchers, new social media accounts were created. The following list of AR‐related search terms were selected, based on similar content analysis studies, to maximize the amount of AR content captured across platforms: AR, hay fever, seasonal allergies, and allergies [11]. From April 13 to 20, 2025, a search was conducted across Facebook Reels (April 13–15), Instagram Reels (April 13–20), TikTok (April 20), and YouTube Shorts (April 13) by two researchers (L.R. and A.F.). The first 30 videos satisfying inclusion criteria under each search term were collected, for a total of 120 videos per platform. Videos were included if they (1) presented information on AR, (2) had audio or text, and (3) provided information in English. After screening search results, duplicate results were excluded.

Four hundred and eighty videos were collected. At the time of video selection, the number of current views, likes, comments, saves, and shares were collected. Two researchers performed categorization of the videos. In the event of discordance, a third researcher would review the video to establish consensus.

Content was categorized by video date, length, and type (lifestyle, educational, advertisement, comedy, and other). Lifestyle videos depicted AR's impact on an individual's life, while educational videos provided information about AR. Advertisements promoted a product, physician/medical group, or other marketable resource, and comedy videos were primarily intended to be humorous. Author demographics included type (lay individual, lay influencer, medical professional, and company) and gender (woman, man, nonbinary, and other). “Other” included companies or news networks accounts where gender could not be determined. Lay individuals had < 10,000 followers and lay influencers had > 10,000 followers, consistent with previous definitions [5]. Medical professionals were self‐identified (e.g., physicians, pharmacists, nurses, physical therapists, physician assistants, clinics, or hospitals). Gender was inferred from names, pronouns, and account content, and assigned based on the predominant author when multiple authors were present.

The PEMAT‐AV was used to assess the understandability and actionability of the videos [9]. Each of 13 categories for understandability and 4 categories for actionability were scored as 0 (disagree), 1 (agree), or N/A (not applicable). Scores were calculated as percentages by dividing the total points achieved over the categories evaluated per video. Understandable and actionable were defined as PEMAT scores ≥ 70%. The GQS was used to assess the overall quality of AR content [10]. Videos were scored using the GQS, ranging from 1 (low quality, poor information flow) to 5 (excellent quality, excellent information flow) (Table S1). Videos were independently scored by two reviewers per platform, with a third physician reviewer resolving discrepancies as needed. The ANDI score was also used to assess the factual accuracy of video content in comparison to medical guidelines and evidence [6].

ANDI scores ranged from 0 (completely inaccurate) to 4 (completely accurate) (Table S2). Scores were assigned by a physician reviewer for videos containing informational content (n = 347); videos without gradable content were excluded from ANDI scoring only and were included in all other analyses.

Author type distribution across platforms was compared using Pearson's chi‐square test and Fisher's exact test, with pairwise post hoc comparisons and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjustment. Single factor analysis of variance was used to compare mean outcome scores (understandability, actionability, GQS) by platform, content category, and author type, with Tukey–Kramer post hoc testing. Univariable regression assessed associations between video characteristics (views, likes, comments, shares, content category, video length, and author type) and outcomes. Variables significant in univariable analysis were included in multivariable regression models. Analyses were conducted in Microsoft Excel version 16.94 (Microsoft Corporation) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The study was exempt from institutional IRB approval (Pro2025002606).

Results

Four hundred and sixty videos met inclusion criteria and were analyzed from TikTok (n = 116), Instagram Reels (n = 112), Facebook Reels (n = 112), and YouTube Shorts (n = 120). Across all platforms, there were a total of 209,101,558 views, 11,712,735 likes, 166,603 comments, and 1,216,456 shares across all videos in the sample. On average per video, TikTok had the highest engagement metrics, including views (mean = 975,263), comments (mean = 1094), likes (mean = 79,876), shares (mean = 6360), and views per day (mean = 12,204), followed by Instagram (Table 1). Most videos were educational (69.1%), followed by lifestyle (20.2%), comedy (5.9%), advertisements (4.1%), and other (0.7%). Most videos were authored by women (52.6%), followed by men (39.8%), and others (7.6%).

Medical professionals accounted for 62.5% of YouTube, 32.1% of Instagram, 29.3% of TikTok, and 28.6% of Facebook videos. YouTube had a significantly higher proportion of medical professionals than TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook (all p < 0.001). Lay influencers accounted for 64.3% of Instagram, 50.9% of TikTok, 17.0% of Facebook, and 7.5% of YouTube videos. Both TikTok and Instagram had significantly higher proportions of lay influencers than YouTube and Facebook (all p < 0.001), while TikTok and Instagram did not differ significantly from each other (p = 0.055). Significant differences in PEMAT understandability, actionability, GQS, and ANDI scores were observed across social media platforms (p < 0.001) (Figure 2). Instagram (72.0%) and YouTube (70.9%) both had significantly higher understandability scores than TikTok (61.1%) (both p < 0.001). All platforms demonstrated significantly higher understandability scores than Facebook (45.9%) (all p < 0.001). Regarding actionability, YouTube demonstrated the highest score (55.6%), followed by Instagram (48.2%), TikTok (45.3%), and Facebook (35.4%). Both Instagram and YouTube had significantly higher scores than Facebook (p = 0.04 and p < 0.001, respectively). Regarding GQS, YouTube demonstrated the highest score (3.12), followed by Instagram (2.70), TikTok (2.21), and Facebook (2.09). YouTube had significantly higher GQS scores than all other platforms (p < 0.001). Instagram's average GQS was also significantly higher than TikTok (p = 0.004) and Facebook (p < 0.001). Regarding ANDI, YouTube received the highest score (2.80), followed by Facebook (2.19), TikTok (1.91), and Instagram (1.68). Educational content had higher GQS scores compared with lifestyle (3.01 vs. 1.35, p < 0.001) and comedy posts (1.04, p < 0.001) but not advertisements (2.56, p = 0.13). Medical professional‐authored posts had the highest understandability scores (69.9%), exceeding those from lay influencers (62.1%), lay individuals (46.3%), and company accounts (60.3%) (all p ≤ 0.001) (Figure 4). Lay influencers demonstrated higher understandability than lay individuals (p < 0.001) but did not differ from company accounts (p = 0.89). Lay influencers had greater actionability than lay individuals (p < 0.001), and company accounts outperformed lay individuals (p ≤ 0.01). ANDI scores were highest for medical professional‐authored posts (3.06) and scored significantly higher compared to company (2.02), lay individuals (1.63), and lay influencers (0.86) (all p < 0.001). On multivariable analysis, across all platforms, compared with educational content, lifestyle content was significantly associated with lower understandability, actionability, and quality (all p < 0.001) (Table 2). Comedy content similarly was associated with lower actionability and GQS (both p < 0.001). Longer video length was only associated with greater GQS (p < 0.001). Compared with medical professionals, companies were associated with lower understandability (p = 0.002), GQS (p < 0.001), and ANDI score (p < 0.001). Lay individuals were associated with lower understandability, actionability, GQS, and ANDI. Lay influencers were associated with lower GQS and ANDI (all p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis by social media platform demonstrated similar findings (Table S5).

Discussion

Otolaryngology has seen rapid growth in physician use of social media for patient education [12]. Short‐form video in particular has become a major source of health information due to its quick, engaging format. AR is highly prevalent and frequently searched online by patients seeking symptom management and treatment guidance [11, 13, 14, 15]. Given patients' increasing reliance on these platforms, evaluating the quality, accuracy, and educational value of AR‐related content is imperative. This study conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of AR videos across four widely used short‐form platforms, evaluating quality, accuracy, understandability, and actionability. To our knowledge, this is the only study comparing AR video quality across all four platforms [11, 15, 16].

Among 460 videos, AR content received over 200 billion views, reflecting extensive dissemination. Most content, however, was created by lay individuals and influencers (48.9%). Medical professional presence was underrepresented, particularly on TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook, highlighting an opportunity to increase presence on those platforms. While some studies have similarly found underrepresentation of healthcare content by medical professionals, others have found a majority shared by medical professionals, with variation across topics and platforms [17, 18].

There is little consensus on consistent short‐form platforms that demonstrate the highest quality content [5, 6, 7, 8, 19]. When evaluating content quality, only YouTube and Instagram met the PEMAT understandability threshold. Mean GQS scores were modest (range: 2.09–3.12), not qualifying as “good quality” patient materials, and mean ANDI scores ranged from 1.68 to 2.80, falling below the threshold for mostly accurate content. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating YouTube content as generally higher quality than other platforms, though still falling short of good quality; however, many studies examined long‐form YouTube content, whereas the present study focuses solely on YouTube Shorts [4, 13, 20, 21]. Although prior studies report TikTok videos as lower quality and reliability than YouTube, neither platform achieved a GQS score consistent with good quality or an ANDI score consistent with complete accuracy [6, 17]. While most content was educational, PEMAT, GQS, and ANDI were applied to all categories, which may have contributed to lower scores, as these tools were developed to evaluate educational health information.

Engagement metrics were not associated with higher content quality, indicating that the most popular content does not necessarily represent the highest quality. Longer video duration was independently associated with higher GQS scores overall. Longer videos may allow creators more time to provide context and explain health information. Notably, no platform met the PEMAT threshold for actionability, underscoring a persistent gap between information dissemination and actionable guidance in social media‐based health education. This limitation may reflect the constraints of short‐form video formats, which typically range from 15 to 60 s and are not as well suited for comprehensive discussions or step‐by‐step guidance compared with long‐form content like traditional YouTube videos, Instagram posts, or Facebook pages. Nevertheless, given the high levels of daily engagement with short‐form content among the general population, maintaining a presence on these platforms remains important for healthcare messaging.

Additionally, content created by nonmedical uploaders was associated with lower accuracy, quality, understandability, and actionability compared with content produced by medical professionals. These findings are consistent with prior studies in orthopedics, internal medicine, and otolaryngology [6, 7, 20, 21]. However, some studies have observed no significant differences between lay and medical professional content on certain platforms, suggesting that medical professional authorship alone may not guarantee higher‐quality content [22].

Social media content production is an important vehicle to educate patients and address misinformation regarding AR. Based on the significant number of views, the general public is actively engaging with health‐related content on social media, highlighting the importance of providing accurate and high‐yield information. Standardizing messaging based on clinical guidelines from professional organizations may help minimize the spread of nonfactual information. More physicians may engage with social media users by creating verified accounts, posting AR‐related content, or interacting with viral posts to dispel misinformation. For example, there are platform‐specific features such as TikTok duets, which allow users to create split‐screen videos alongside another creator's video [19, 23]. This format facilitates a space to offer endorsements or critiques of information. It also allows medical professionals to answer clinical questions and engage in larger public discussions on popular AR topics. Previous studies have demonstrated the value of incorporating social media engagement and misinformation identification into medical education, as many trainees report difficulty distinguishing accurate from false information [24, 25]. Incorporating structured training and clear guidelines for evaluating health information on social media can better equip the medical professional community at all training levels to combat misinformation.

This study has several limitations. Although videos were selected methodically, TikTok, Facebook, YouTube, and Instagram use proprietary search algorithms that modify content recommendations based on prior viewing history. By creating new accounts and collecting all videos from each platform within a limited time window, we attempted to minimize algorithm‐related variability of viewing history. Data collection dates varied by platform across a 1‐week period, which may have introduced bias if content trends or posting patterns shifted over the course of the collection timeframe. There are also limitations to the generalizability of our findings over time, as social media content evolves rapidly.

Conclusions

This study is the only evaluation of AR short‐form video quality across four major social media platforms. Across platforms, actionability remained inadequate, highlighting a substantial limitation of short‐form content for patient education. Videos created by medical professionals had the highest understandability, actionability, accuracy, and quality, yet comprised less 40% of the sample. Instagram and YouTube hosted the most understandable content, although both only narrowly met PEMAT thresholds. Facebook demonstrated the lowest understandability, actionability, and quality scores, while Instagram demonstrated the lowest accuracy. YouTube contained the greatest proportion of medical professional content and achieved the highest overall GQS and ANDI scores, though quality and accuracy remained moderate across all platforms. These findings highlight the need for increased healthcare professional engagement on YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok, as well as improved educational standards for short‐form health content shared with patients and the public.
