Birth size modifies longitudinal associations between midlife educational level and physical function at late-life for men and women
Authors: Vilborg Kolbrun Vilmundardottir, Alfons Ramel, Milan Chang, Thorhallur Ingi Halldorsson, Palmi V Jonsson, Vilmundur Gudnason, Mary Frances Cotch, Lenore Launer, Howard J Hoffman, Kristin Siggeirsdottir, Olof Gudny Geirsdottir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical function is an important domain of healthy ageing and previous studies have suggested socioeconomic status, including education to be influential. Also, lifestyle factors such as physical activity can play a crucial role in maintaining physical function. Furthermore, birth size as an indicator of nutritional status during gestation can add insight into the interplay between factors that influence physical functioning. Thus, examining healthy ageing from a life course perspective can broaden our scope for possible preventive measures for maintaining independence and quality of life at late-life. Our objective was to investigate the longitudinal association between midlife education and late-life physical function, over the mean time-period of 25 years, and to investigate whether birth size modifies the association.MethodsParticipants were 1604 men and women from the Age Gene/Environment Susceptibility (AGES) - Reykjavik Study who had measures from birth, midlife and late-life. Multivariate linear regression included gait speed (GS) and timed up and go test (TUG) at late-life as outcomes and midlife educational level as exposure. To examine effect modification, data was stratified by birth size as ponderal index (PI) in three groups.ResultsParticipants with primary education had slower GS of 0.05 m/s (p < 0.001) and longer TUG time of 0.66 s (p = 0.006) compared to college/university (reference). Birth size modified the association, with the low PI group having a slower GS of 0.1 m/s (p < 0.001) and taking 1.35s longer to complete TUG between educational groups (primary education vs. reference). There was no association between GS nor TUG with education in the high PI group.ConclusionOur results imply that for those born small, having lower educational level is associated with having worse physical function at late-life, partly through less physical activity throughout the life-course.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06462-y.Keywords: Life course perspective, Physical functional performance, Healthy ageing

Introduction

Maintaining adequate physical function into older age is a main factor in facilitating healthy ageing [1, 2]. Typically, physical function decreases substantially in midlife due to age-related physiological changes in the human body [1, 3–5], manifesting as muscle deterioration, increased fat to muscle ratio, decreased bone mass and more rigid and vulnerable joints [4–6]. These changes may lead to reduced functional ability, such as decreased balance and difficulties with walking and getting up from sitting, all measures of independence in daily activities [7, 8].

Thus, loss of functional ability can have adverse results over the life-course, including increased risk of falls and disability, social isolation, loss of independence and can even lead to nursing home admission and increased risk of mortality [1, 3, 6].

Many factors throughout the life-course can influence the maintenance of physical function, including physical activity level and socioeconomic status (SES) [9, 10]. Higher physical activity throughout life is positively associated with better physical function in older adults [11–13]. Further, educational level as a marker of SES has been associated with both physical activity and physical function [9, 13–15]. Thus, lower educational level can be considered to possibly predispose older adults to less physical activity and worse physical function.

Moreover, birth size, as an indicator of nutritional status during gestation, may influence physical function at older age [16–22]. Wherein biological differences of those born small have been implicated, such as suboptimal development of the musculoskeletal system in utero, as leading to lower muscle mass and worse physical function in older age [16–22]. Altogether, based on the hypothesis that being born small may indicate biological differences, thus increasing susceptibility to exposures across the lifecourse and possibly resulting in worse outcomes, wherein increased allostatic load has been implicated [23, 24], the current study sought to examine physical functioning in individuals exposed to different educational levels and across different groups of birth size. Moreover, recently and in line with the focus of the United Nations’ current Decade of Healthy Ageing on socioeconomic factors, experts in the field of developmental programming of health and disease (DoHaD) have called for life-course research where measures of the social environment are included and concluded on [25].

However, to the best of the authors knowledge no studies have looked at the association of socioeconomic factors and physical functioning in this specific context. Socioeconomic factors were important for the health of previous generations of older adults and remain so still today [2].

Thus, to elucidate the longitudinal association between educational level and physical functioning in older adults the current study looked at educational level at midlife (as an indicator of life-course SES) and physical functioning in late-life, over the mean time period of 25 years. Moreover, we sought to investigate whether birth size acts as an effect modifier on the abovementioned association. Hypothesising that lower educational level is associated with worse physical function and that being born small, as an indicator of worse nutritional status during gestation, may strengthen this association.

Methods

Design and participants

Analysis was based on data from the AGES-Reykjavik Study, as well as data from birth registers in Reykjavik, that were previously collected for a subset of participants in the Reykjavik Study [26].

The Reykjavik Study was initiated by the Icelandic Heart Association (IHA) in 1967 and continued to 1996, recruiting inhabitants of Reykjavik, at midlife, born in 1907–1935 (n = 30795) at the mean age of 50 years (range 34 to 79), many of whom were born in the country side. Data on birth weight and length were collected from archived midwife records in Reykjavik for a subset of those born in Reykjavik in 1914–1935 (n = 4828) and enrolled in the Reykjavik Study [26]. Of those 4828 participants, 4775 were singletons and 1699 were enrolled into AGES-Reykjavik in 2002, a random sample of survivors from the Reykjavik Study (n = 5764) at the mean age of 75 years (range 66 to 90), as previously described by Harris et al., [27].

Participants in the current study were singletons born in Reykjavik between 1914 and 1935 who participated in both the Reykjavik Study at midlife and the AGES-Reykjavik study at late-life, with available birth register data (n = 1699). Exclusion of participants included those with missing data for physical function measures (n = 90) and birth length (n = 5), leaving the final analytical sample at 1604 participants. An overview of included participants in the current study is provided in Additional File 1 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Compared to AGES-Reykjavik participants who had no birth data (Additional File 2, Supplementary Table 1), our participants were younger, had more education, reported more PA between ages 20–59 and at late-life, and had better physical function.

AGES-Reykjavik was approved by the National Bioethics Committee in Iceland which serves as the Institutional Review Board for the Icelandic Heart Association (approval number: VSN-00-063), and by the National Institute on Aging Intramural Institutional Review Board in the United States. Informed consent was signed by all participants.

Physical function

Physical function outcomes were measured in late-life. Normal gait speed (m/s) was measured over a 6-meter (6-m) track, using a standardized protocol [28]. Participants were instructed to stand still with their feet touching a taped line and then to walk at a normal pace along a 6-m track after given a command of “Go”. Timing started at the first footfall and stopped with first footfall after crossing the end line at 6-m. The fastest time of two trials was recorded in seconds and calculated as gait speed (GS) in meters per second (m/s).

The Timed Up and Go test (TUG) measured the time (seconds) it took to rise from a chair with a seat height of 45.5 cm, walk three meters, turn around, walk back, and sit down again [29, 30]. Participants wore their normal footwear and used customary walking aids, if needed. No physical assistance was given. Participants were instructed to start with their back against the chair with their arms resting on the chair’s arms, and at the command of “Go”, to get up and complete the test as described above. Before timing the performance, the participants completed one round of the test to get familiarized with it.

Educational level

Information on educational level was derived from a self-reported questionnaire answered at midlife. Educational level was defined as the highest level of completed education, split to four categories: primary school or less (six years or less); secondary education (nine years or less); college education (around thirteen years); and, university education (fourteen years or more). Due to small numbers in the third (college) and fourth (university) categories these groups were combined as previously described by Groffen et al. [31] so that three educational categories remained for the analyses.

Birth size

Information on infant sex, birth weight to the nearest 50 g (g), and birth length from crown to heel in centimeters (cm) was gathered from midwifes’ birth records in the National Archives of Iceland. Ponderal index (kg/m3) was calculated from birth weight and length and divided to quartiles. Quartiles were split into three groups of birth size for analyses: small ≤ 25th percentile (≤ 24.0 kg/m3); normal = 25-75th (> 24.0–28.0 kg/m3); and, large ≥ 75th (> 28.0 kg/m3), a method previously described by Muller et al. [32].

Covariates

Physical activity (PA)

Midlife PA was assessed by a self-reported questionnaire, where participants were asked a yes/no question whether they had engaged in regular PA between the ages of 20 to 59 years old.

Late-life PA was assessed by a self-reported questionnaire, where participants were asked how many hours per week they participated in moderate–vigorous intensity PA in the past 12 months. Predefined answers were never, rarely, occasionally (weekly but < 1 h), moderate (1–3 h per week) and high (> 4 h per week), and provided examples included badminton, golf, biking, swimming, heavy gardening, weightlifting, hiking/mountain climbing, fast walking/heavy housework, rowing, aerobics, jogging and running.

For our analysis we combined midlife and late-life PA into a lifetime PA variable, with four categories: Never or little PA, PA when younger, PA in late-life and PA always (in midlife and late-life).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS for Windows version 29.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and the level of significance was set at p < 0.05 (one-sided). Data were checked for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and are shown as mean and standard deviation (SD). Groups, i.e., primary, secondary and college/university education were compared using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and Chi-Square test for categorical variables.

Multivariate linear models were used to assess the association of education with physical function (GS and time to complete TUG test) at late-life, with the highest educational level group used as a reference. Model 1 was adjusted for age at late-life and sex and model 2 was additionally adjusted for lifetime PA, as those factors were significantly different between educational groups. Additionally, covariance was tested for numerous variables that were not included in the final analysis, including type II diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, cognitive status, smoking, alcohol consumption, use of statins, number of medications, height, body mass index and waist circumference (Additional Files 3–4, Supplementary Tables 2–3). Previous studies from the AGES-Reykjavik cohort have reported how these variables were measured [27].

Further, analysis was stratified by the three previously described groups of birth size to assess the effect modification of birth size on the association of education with physical function at late-life, therein allowing for examination of the association in groups of different birth size [33]. Statistical adjustments were made for age and sex (model 1) and additionally for lifetime PA (model 2).

Supplemental analysis

Supplemental analysis included examining the relationship between the effect modifier (birth size) and the outcomes (Additional File 5, Supplementary Table 4) and the exposure (Additional File 6, Supplementary Table 5). Further, we analyzed the data stratified by sex as, previous studies have done when looking at DoHaD [21, 24, 34], shown in Additional Files 7–11 (Supplementary Tables 6–10).

Discussion

Considering the maintenance of physical function as a major component of healthy ageing from a life-course perspective, the current study showed small but significant associations between educational level at midlife and physical function at late-life. Where lower educational level was associated with worse physical functioning and PA explained one third of the association. Furthermore, birth size modified the above-mentioned association. For those born small (low PI group), there was a significant association between educational level and both outcomes, and PA partly explained the association, whilst the results were not significant for those born larger.

Our results are in line with previously studied associations between lower education and worse health outcomes in the AGES-Reykjavik cohort [31, 35, 36] and PA was an important factor for the maintenance of physical function in older age [36–38]. The association of lower education with worse health-related outcomes, including physical function is likely mediated through both behavioural and biological factors [9, 23, 39]. Indicated pathways in the association of SES with health outcomes include increased inflammation and allostatic load in individuals with lower SES, possibly mediated by increased psychological stress and occupational exposures [23]. Focusing on physical function, Stringhini et al. estimated that by age 60, four to seven years in men and three to six years in women of good physical functioning was lost, attributable to lower SES [9]. Furthermore, individuals with lower SES may have less access to and/or limited opportunities to engage in PA throughout the life course, thus making good physical functioning habits less likely to carry over into late-life [14, 15].

As hypothesized, those with lower education had slower GS and longer time to complete the TUG test. Further, we observed effect modification by birth size, indicating a stronger association between educational level and physical functioning for those born small. Interestingly, Barker et al. concluded that being born small can make individuals more vulnerable to the effects of having lower SES [24]. Further, previous studies have proposed that small birth size is associated with physiological influences on adult body composition such as altered fat distribution and reduced muscle mass [17, 18, 22, 40]. Thus, being born small may influence muscle formation and impact growth throughout the life-course, resulting in less muscle mass and strength [17, 18, 20, 22, 34]. Thus, a possible explanation for our observed effect modification may be that such physiological influences may make individuals more vulnerable to being physically inactive or being exposed to occupational exposures throughout the life-course, resulting in slower GS and longer time to complete the TUG test at late-life in those born smaller. Hence, ensuring access to PA throughout the life-course, across SES levels may help maintain physical function in later life, especially for those born smaller. Overall, our results align with the United Nations’ Decade of Healthy Ageing initiative, which focuses on enhancing social equity to further enable healthy ageing [2].

Previous research in the field of DoHaD focusing on physical functioning have found mixed results on whether the associations of birth size can be sex specific [6, 16, 17, 22, 34]. Similarly, the current study shows inconclusive results when analysis was stratified by sex.

Having two different outcome measures of physical function in late-life (GS and TUG test) could be considered a strength to our study, as we see similar associations between education and both measures, both before and after stratification by birth size. Other strengths include having a large sample size (n = 1604) and being able to capture life-course measures. Limitations include not having data on gestational age nor information on SES of the participants’ parents, as both measures can be associated with birth size and parental SES with educational level [16, 20, 21]. Furthermore, it is possible that our study is subject to survival bias, as those who had low educational level or small birth size might not have survived to late-life [41], hence are not a part of this study as our study population is limited to participants in the AGES-Reykjavik study. However, it’s worth noting that it was previously reported that the birth size of the total original Reykjavik study sample was similar to the birth size in the sample used in this study [42]. Today, the Icelandic population generally has good access to education and a high educational level, thus those with lower education in subsequent generations can be considered a minority [43]. Furthermore, the educational level groups presented in this study no longer represent the general population, as primary education was defined as six years or less of education but presently school is mandatory for ten years, from six to 16 years of age. Thus, our results might not be completely generalisable to modern generations of Icelanders. However, educational level is considered a marker of SES and has been associated with healthy ageing, thus modern gradients in education are likely to comprise similar associations [44].

In conclusion, our results imply that for those born small, having lower educational level is associated with having worse physical function at late-life, partly through less PA throughout the life-course.
