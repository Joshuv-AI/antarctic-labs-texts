Feasibility and acceptability of DMPA-SC self-injection in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Aderaw Anteneh, Megan M. Lydon, Katherine M. Anfinson, Fikreselassie Getachew, Erica Felker-Kantor, Tihut Mulugeta, Teferi Teklu, Rahel Demissew, Holly M. Burke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA) in a prefilled auto-disabled subcutaneous injectable contraceptive (Uniject™) can be administered by community health workers and clients. Self-injection of DMPA-SC is an evidence-based practice endorsed globally by the World Health Organization and approved in several countries. The Ethiopian Ministry of Health is interested in introducing DMPA-SC self-injection to expand family planning options. This research aimed to generate information on the safety, acceptability, and feasibility of the introduction and scale-up of DMPA-SC self-injection in Ethiopia.MethodWe used mixed-method research. Four hundred women aged 18–49 interested in DMPA-SC self-injection were enrolled in the study. Participants were recruited from six public health facilities with high family planning client load in Addis Ababa. In-depth interviews were conducted with 15 family planning providers trained on self-injection and provided DMPA-SC from six public health facilities. Participants’ self-injection competency was assessed using a standardized checklist at enrollment and follow-up (three months post-enrollment). Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and the qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsSelf-injection competency among women at baseline (95%) was high and at three months follow-up (83%). Competency levels did not differ by sociodemographic characteristics. Participants reported increased confidence and comfort with self-injection during the three-month follow-up compared to baseline; 74% said they were confident to self-inject during enrollment compared with 93% at follow-up. 84% of participants said they were satisfied or very satisfied with DMPA-SC self-injection. Providers saw the benefit of offering DMPA-SC self-injection because it saves clients time, money, and the burden of having to come to a health facility. Providers suggested that DMPA-SC self-injection be added to the method mix and offered through community distribution by health extension workers.ConclusionResults showed that women can competently self-inject DMPA-SC and that it is highly acceptable to clients and providers. If made available, most women would like to continue with the method and would recommend it to others. DMPA-SC self-injection has the potential to support the Ethiopian Ministry of Health in reaching its FP2030 targets. The results from this study further support the introduction and scale-up of DMPA-SC self-injection in Ethiopia.

Background

Injectable contraceptives are the most popular modern contraceptive method in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. They are effective for pregnancy prevention, are less burdensome than a daily pill, and can be used discreetly without others knowing [1]. Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) is a type of injectable contraceptive that is delivered subcutaneously (DMPA-SC) in a prefilled, auto-disabled Uniject™ injection system, known by the brand name Sayana® Press (Pfizer, New York, NY, USA). DMPA-SC has gained recognition as an easy-to-use injectable contraceptive. This method is suitable for administration by community health workers (CHWs) and by clients themselves [1]. Self-injection of DMPA-SC is an evidence-based practice that is endorsed globally by the World Health Organization (WHO) and approved in a growing number of countries. Studies from high and low to middle-income countries have shown that family planning (FP) users are able to self-administer DMPA-SC safely and effectively [2–16]. Further, WHO has recommended DMPA-SC self-injection as part of their guidelines on self-care interventions for health [17]. Self-care interventions, which reduce the need to travel or have contact with health facilities and workers, are particularly relevant during epidemics (e.g., Ebola) or pandemics (e.g., COVID-19).

Provider-administered DMPA-SC was first introduced in Ethiopia in 2019 in the private sector. The Ethiopian Ministry of Health (MOH) is interested in expanding DMPA-SC for self-injection in the public sector to expand contraceptive options, address unmet need and to improve access for women. According to the 2016 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), 84% of women aged 15–49 years used a modern contraceptive method obtained from the public sector with injectables as the most popular choice (23%). Despite injectables being the preferred method of choice, the EDHS reported high discontinuation rates (38%) within one year of use [18]. Previous studies in African countries showed that DMPA-SC self-injection increases continuation compared with provider-administered injection and those with low levels of education can also administer self-injection safely and effectively [3]. Furthermore, qualitative research conducted in Oromia, Ethiopia in 2012 demonstrated high levels of perceived acceptability of DMPA-SC among women who use modern contraceptive methods [19]. The purpose of this research was to generate information on the safety, acceptability, and feasibility of introducing and scaling up DMPA-SC for self-injection in Ethiopia.

Although numerous global studies have explored the safety, feasibility, and acceptability of DMPA-SC self-injection (SI), these findings cannot be directly applied to the Ethiopian context without proper adaptation to local circumstances. Factors such as socio-cultural norms, health system capacity, and literacy levels may influence the successful implementation of DMPA-SC SI. This study represents the first implementation research on DMPA-SC SI conducted in Ethiopia. Its results are being utilized to support evidence-based decision-making and to guide the phased introduction of DMPA-SC SI by the MOH. Additionally, implementing this approach is expected to enhance women’s reproductive health outcomes by reducing unmet family planning needs and promoting more client-centered family planning services.

Methods

Setting

The MOH selected six public health facilities in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, as pilot sites for DMPA-SC self-injection. The selected sites are Primary Health Care Centers (PHCCs) with large catchment areas that serve high volumes of clients seeking FP services.

Study design

A mixed-methods, research study was employed consisting of quantitative surveys among women aged 18–49 years who were FP family planning users and in-depth interviews (IDIs) with FP providers from the six study sites. Providers were eligible to participate in the IDIs if they were 18 years or older, had participated in the MOH DMPA-SC training, and trained at least one client on DMPA-SC self-injection (N = 15). Women were recruited during their routine family planning visit. Those who were eligible (age 18–49 years, lived in the Sub-city of one of the study sites, did not plan to relocate from Addis Ababa in the coming 4 months, willing to provide contact information for the follow-up visit and willing to sign informed consent) and chose to be trained to self-inject DMPA-SC were invited to participate in the study and completed an enrollment survey. Women who competently and safely self-injected under provider observation were given one unit of DMPA-SC to self-inject 3 months later at home. The study team followed-up with these participants after their re-injection date had passed to conduct a survey on client experience and observe self-injection competency on a surrogate.

Sample size and sampling characteristics

A sample size of 400 was calculated to achieve precision of 5% for a 95% confidence interval around the point estimate for the primary outcome – proportion of users who demonstrated they could competently and safely self-inject at 3 months. Using information from previous studies [11, 12, 20, 21] we used conservative point estimates of 75–80%. The sample size calculation also considered potential loss to follow-up and discontinuation of DMPA-SC within three months. These parameters were adjusted to accommodate various scenarios, including differences in self-injection proficiency, as well as rates of loss to follow-up and discontinuation. Based on comparable studies conducted in other Sub-Saharan African countries, which have reported low rates of loss to follow-up and discontinuation (less than 20%), we have adopted similar estimates in our calculations [7, 11, 12].

The target sample size (n = 400) was distributed proportionately across the six study sites according to study facility catchment area populations of women of reproductive age in 2021 (Table 1).

Sample sizes for the qualitative interviews were based on purposive nonprobability sampling, which relied on the concept of saturation, or the point at which no new information or themes are observed in the data. Research has shown that saturation can occur within the first 12 interviews conducted in a relatively homogeneous group when the objective of the research is to understand common perceptions and experiences [22]. As there are only 2–3 facility-based providers at each of the six study sites, all were invited to participate in the qualitative IDIs.

Data collection

Enrollment and baseline data collection occurred between August and December 2021. New and continuing contraception users who were seeking FP services were informed of DMPA-SC and the research by the provider. Those who expressed interest in learning more about the study were introduced to a research assistant who verified eligibility, administered the informed consent and conducted the enrollment survey. Providers then trained participants how to self-administer DMPA-SC. Providers assessed participants’ self-injection competency and safety using a standardized observation checklist. The checklist was developed based on similar studies in Malawi, Uganda, and Senegal [3, 6, 7, 11, 12]. Participants were classified as competent if they correctly performed all five critical steps (selecting an injection site, mixing the liquid by shaking the device vigorously, pushing the needle cap and port together to activate the device, pinching the “skin” at the injection site to form a tent and pressing the reservoir slowly to inject) when injecting themselves. Women unable or unwilling to self-inject were offered to continue their standard MOH FP session and were discontinued from the study at this time. Women who were competent were given one unit of DMPA-SC to self-inject after three months and were instructed to put the used units in a puncture-proof container and then to bring it back to the health center when visiting the health facility for their next appointment. A 3-month post enrollment follow-up survey was conducted with each participant between November 2021 and April 2022. Participants were followed-up to determine if they self-injected on time without any prompts, to mimic real service delivery contexts. Follow-up surveys were conducted at a location preferred by the participants. Participants were asked to administer an injection on a surrogate (condom filled with sugar) while research assistants assessed their self-injection competency and safety using the same standardized observation checklist. Interviews with providers were conducted at health facilities during the follow-up period. The IDIs explored provider experiences and perceptions of self-injection contraception and recommendations for training women to safely self-inject. Interviews were conducted in Amharic, audio-recorded, transcribed and translated into English. Interviews lasted on average 67 min.

Data analysis

The analysis included basic descriptive statistics of participant characteristics at enrollment, disposition of participants by the 3-month follow-up, and key outcome variables. For the primary outcome and some of the secondary outcomes, we present point estimates and 95% confidence intervals. Measures of association were conducted through chi-squared tests and assessed for significance at the p < 0.05 level. No sampling weights were used for this analysis. We relied on the proportional allocation as described above to represent the client volumes across the study sites. Analysis was conducted using Stata v.16. Primary analysis results and a subset of secondary results were verified by a second analyst using independent dual programming.

Qualitative data were analyzed through a rapid thematic analysis approach, first, summarizing individual transcripts using a structured template in Microsoft Excel. Two analysts populated this table by reviewing and extracting data from each transcript and summarizing the data for each theme and including relevant illustrative quotes. Second, the analysis team examined the summaries within each theme to identify commonly occurring sub-themes along with supporting quotes, which were organized in a new table. Narrative summaries were written from this table by three analysts and shared with the broader study team for input and validation.

Ethical approval

The Ethiopian Public Health Institute’s Institutional Review Board (EPHI IRB) and PSI’s Research Ethics Board (PSI REB) reviewed the study protocol, data collection forms, and informed consent forms. The study investigators, all research assistants, and any other staff working directly with study participants had completed research ethics training before the implementation of the study. All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Client experiences

The FP providers screened 419 medically eligible women who were interested in DMPA-SC self-injection. Four hundred women completed the DMPA-SC self-injection training. Of the 400 women, 381 demonstrated competence to self-inject during baseline and were eligible for follow-up. The study team reached 343 of these participants for follow-up, resulting in a 10% loss-to-follow-up rate.

Participant characteristics

Table 2 presents participant demographic characteristics. Participants’ average age was 28 years and about one-third (30%) were under 25. Over half (55%) had not attained education beyond primary school. Most participants were married (91%), had 1–2 children (69%) and identified as Orthodox Christian (69%). At the time of enrollment, 50% of participants reported current use of injectables and 20% reported they were not currently using any form of contraception. Some had previous experience giving an injection of unspecified medicine (6%).

Self-injection competency during enrollment and follow-up

Participants demonstrated a high level of competence to self-inject after initially learning how to self-inject at enrollment and 3 months later (Fig. 1). The enrollment competency rate was 95% (95% CI: 92.7–97.1). Those who were not competent in self-injection include the 15 participants who completed self-injection training but ultimately decided not to attempt self-injection. Injection competency remained high at follow-up such that 83% (95% CI: 78.7–87.0) of participants (281/338) who demonstrated injection technique were observed to do so correctly. Five participants were reached by phone for follow-up and, therefore, were unable to demonstrate the injection technique.

Experience of DMPA-SC and re-injection

At the time of the follow-up visit, 70% of participants had administered their 3-month injection of DMPA-SC. During the follow-up visit, participants were asked separate questions about how confident and comfortable they were self-injecting at enrollment and at re-injection 3 months later. Compared to enrollment, participants reported feeling more confident and comfortable self-injecting at follow-up, such that 71% said they felt at ease or very at ease and 93% said they felt somewhat or very confident self-injecting at follow-up (Table 4). Participants reported similarly high rates of confidence and certainty about being able to handle self-injection in the future. Among those who had reinjected at the time of follow-up, most (77%) reported no challenges.

Storage and disposal of DMPA-SC units and client satisfaction with DMPA-SC self-injection

Table 4 depicts women’s experience of DMPA-SC unit storage, disposal, and satisfaction with DMPA-SC self-injection. At the time of the follow-up visit, 27% of participants had disposed of their DMPA-SC unit (Table 4). Among those who had disposed of their unit, 19% first placed it in a container, mainly describing the container as a hair oil bottle or container. Those who had not yet disposed of their unit explained that this was mainly because they wanted to bring it back to the health facility or were waiting for their next appointment (73%) or because they had not self-injected (26%). Almost half of participants brought their unit back to the health center while 37% reported disposing of it with their regular trash and 14% in a pit latrine (Table 4). There were no reports of accidental needle injury by the used or unused DMPA-SC units.

The majority of participants were satisfied with DMPA-SC self-injection and would recommend it to a friend. Overall, 84% of participants said they were satisfied or very satisfied with DMPA-SC self-injection (Table 4). The majority (85%) said they would recommend DMPA-SC self-injection to a friend or family member. Most reported they would continue using the method if it was made available (78%) and expressed a preference for accessing it at health facilities (95%) followed by pharmacies or drug shops (55%) and from urban health extension professionals (UHEPs) (54%).

Provider experiences

All fifteen FP providers who had been trained on DMPA-SC self-injection and offered the method to clients in the context of the pilot introduction, participated in an IDI. All were female, most were in their 30 s and most were nurses who had 1–3 years of experience working in family planning service provision (Table 5). All providers had experience offering the intramuscular version of DMPA (DMPA-IM); prior to the study, most had given more than 40 injections per month. The number of provider participants from each study site ranged from two to three depending on whether the facility was a high-volume facility.

Provider recommendations on DMPA-SC self-injection

The qualitative results support the findings from the quantitative results that there was no significant difference in self-injection competency by clients’ socio-demographic characteristics.“Almost everyone can give self-injection to themselves.” Another provider added, “actually, it is not as we feared, they are not educated but it is not difficult for them to self-inject.” - FP provider.

“Almost everyone can give self-injection to themselves.” Another provider added, “actually, it is not as we feared, they are not educated but it is not difficult for them to self-inject.” - FP provider.

Providers widely recommended DMPA-SC self-injection be added to the method mix. They were strongly in favor of the method, stating that it saves clients time, money, and burden. They described women as being “busy” and therefore appreciating a time-saving option. They also noted that fewer health center visits would reduce the burden and cost to women in securing transport and childcare.“It would also be a huge comfort to be able to cut down on her visits to the health facility. Her energy is conserved. It also helps her save time.” - FP provider.“I would be very much happy if mothers use this once it passes all the necessary steps. I would be happy if I myself use it.” - FP provider.

Most of the providers estimated that training each client on DMPA-SC self-injection took approximately 20 to 40 min. Providers explained that the duration of training varied based on clients’ needs and comprehension levels. One provider noted,

“there are some clients who immediately understand it; some clients accept it instantly. But there are clients who need too much explanation”.

They identified some factors influencing the length of training, such as clients with children present, which could extend training time—and previous experience with self-injection, with diabetic clients often needing less instruction due to their familiarity with the process.

Several providers also explained that the increased time they spent with clients to train them on DMPA-SC SI created burden on the pre-existing client flow. However, after the initial training, the method reduces burden on providers as well. One provider illustrated this by saying that

“as to me such things reduce the burden of providers. The problem that you face is at the first step; I mean training her,”

Providers noted additional benefits of DMPA-SC self-injection that made the method a good option for clients. These included more privacy afforded to covert family planning users, reduced exposure to contagious diseases at the health facility, and a lower hormone level than DMPA-IM.“Girls who aren’t married may feel shame to come here to use family planning; there is no need to come for Depo injection every three months. It [DMPA-SC self-injection] reduces such things.” - FP provider.

Some of the providers believed that the current disposal strategy is appropriate. However, some providers suggested giving clients a safety box to dispose of their used units and health extension workers (HEWs) to collect the unit for appropriate disposal.

Many providers also felt DMPA-SC should be offered through community distribution, explaining that it would allow for greater reach, enabling more clients to benefit from the method.“If it is well implemented in Addis Ababa, it might not be difficult to do there [in rural area] …. I would be happy if it is done there by the [health] extension workers.” - FP provider.

“If it is well implemented in Addis Ababa, it might not be difficult to do there [in rural area] …. I would be happy if it is done there by the [health] extension workers.” - FP provider.

The providers suggested DMPA-SC self-injection as a good option for young girls who do not have access to family planning services at the facilities because they afraid to be seen at the facilities by people whom they know.“As I told you earlier those teenagers don’t come to our health center. They are at school and other youth centers. So, I would be happy for it to be available in the places where they [teenagers] are found.” - FP provider.

At the same time, some providers had concerns about community-distribution. Some were open to this option but wanted to ensure clients would receive good training. Others had concerns about the quality of counseling and training if offered through pharmacies:“…. Generally, I don’t recommend the self-injection to be available at pharmacies unless there are people who are trained on it and able to train clients. These private pharmacies sell the emergency pill without telling a client the advantages and the disadvantages. If you go buy this emergency pill, do they tell you the side effect? Nearly all providers recommended training HEWs to offer DMPA-SC self-injection training in the community noting that they are capable and well-positioned to provide the method. The majority of providers expressed confidence that trained urban health extension professionals (UHEPs) and rural HEWs could effectively offer the method. Providers also explained that HEWs make household visits which would further reduce women’s burden of traveling to the health center and reach those who may not otherwise seek care from the health facility. One of the providers described that:“It [Involving HEWs] increases the number of self-injection users. We train our clients when they come to the health center, but health extension professionals do everything going to clients’ home. The main objective of DMPA-SC is minimizing clients travel to health center. So those who can’t come to a health center also can get the service.”

Discussion

This study’s purpose was to explore the safety, feasibility, and acceptability of DMPA-SC self-injection in Ethiopia. The findings revealed that DMPA-SC self-injection was an acceptable form of contraception among most clients and all FP providers participating in this study. Additionally, most participants showed high competency to self-inject when initially trained and at the 3-month follow-up.

Results from bivariate analysis showed no difference in self-injection competency by socio-demographics. This finding corroborates what has been found in other studies. A recent study in Uganda reported that self-injection proficiency was not associated with age of the client [23]. Given that nearly half the study population had little to no education but was able to self-inject correctly, expansion of DMPA-SC self-injection to areas of Ethiopia with less educated populations may be feasible, as our qualitative findings also suggest.

A possible reason for the slight decrease in competency after 3- months might be related to forgetting the steps that should be done to self-inject, as they were only trained three months before the follow-up visit. However, these findings are comparable to other studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa. For example, 90% of participants in Senegal achieved competency at enrollment, and 80% were competent at 3 months. In Uganda, 98% of participants demonstrated competency at enrollment and 88% at 3 months [11, 12].

A prospective cohort study in Ghana found that clients were more likely to prefer self-injection after having several opportunities to use the method over time [24]. Our findings also showed that participants reported feeling more confident and at ease with self-injection over time, indicating that they can overcome initial fears about self-injection. This supports the idea that if women can self-inject repeatedly, they can manage the challenges and become more comfortable with the method [25]. Most participants reported no challenges with reinjection. This suggests that women can self-inject DMPA-SC at home if given appropriate training [2, 15]. Providers who participated in our study suggested more training for clients at the initial stage to increase competency further, reduce reinjection challenges, and support continuation. Taking time to provide high-quality family planning counseling, including an opportunity for clients to practice on a surrogate and asses their competence before they take home a unit, are important considerations when developing training materials and guidance for health providers. The message given to clients about how to manage DMPA-SC self-injection by providers is important to increase uptake and reduce challenges of DMPA-SC self-injection [26]. A previous study in sub-Saharan Africa has showed that DMPA-SC has the power to increase contraceptive prevalence [27]. In line with this, the providers in our study were also supportive of adding DMPA-SC self-injection to the method mix to meet FP needs, especially the needs of young women who had no access to FP.

Most participants were satisfied with DMPA-SC and would recommend it to a friend; and most said they would continue to use DMPA-SC self-injection if available. Others who were satisfied may not want to continue because they want to get pregnant or are no longer at risk of pregnancy. Previous studies in countries in sub-Saharan Africa also documented that DMPA-SC self-injection increases continuation rates compared with provider-administered DMPA-SC [1, 3, 14]. The intention to continue DMPA-SC self-injection by many of the clients may be related to the quality of the training delivered by providers during the recruitment [28]. In this study, providers were able to use strategies to address clients’ fear of pain involved with self-injecting or of using a new method for the first time. Providers would engage with clients in a personal way, spend time telling them about the benefits of DMPA-SC, highlight the time it would save them, compare the needle size to the DMPA-IM, and discuss anticipated side-effects. These strategies may have helped make clients feel more at ease because the provider took the necessary time to address the client’s fears and concerns.

Most participants did not dispose of their unit by the time of follow-up because they were waiting to bring it back to the health facility or they had not been injected yet. In this study, the research team contacted participants after their reinjection date had passed but the reinjection window includes up to 4-weeks after this date. As such, participants could still reinject after the follow-up visit and remain within their reinjection window. Most of the participants who disposed of the used DMPA-SC unit did not put it in a container prior to disposal, suggesting some concern about disposal practices. Similarly, in a two-country study examining DMPA-SC waste management practices, it was shown that 35% in Uganda and 61% in Malawi did not return their used units to the health facility as instructed but mainly disposed of them in a pit latrine [9]. At the same time, there were no reports of accidental needle stick injuries in these studies nor during this study in Addis Ababa, indicating that the disposal options selected by participants were safe for them and their families. Challenges related to disposal can be improved during implementation with ideas shared from providers in this study, and from the experiences of self-injection programs in other countries. For example, study providers proposed HEWs to collect the units and that providers could offer clients a disposal container to take away with them to manage this issue. A cost-effectiveness analysis found that through a health system perspective, self-injected DMPA-SC, inclusive of providing women with a disposal container for used units, was cost-effective relative to health worker-administered DMPA-IM in Uganda [29].

Providers felt that an initial investment of their time in training clients to self-inject was worthwhile and would lead to a reduction in their workload in subsequent months. Providing DMPA-SC units to clients after a comprehensive training to take home and self-inject by themselves can reduce the workload of providers [30]. Providers in the study said they would recommend DMPA-SC self-injection continue to be offered in Ethiopia; a result similarly found in other studies including in Uganda [31]. Like previous research conducted in Oromia, Ethiopia in 2012, we found a high level of acceptability for DMPA-SC SI among women and health providers [19].

Finally, both clients and providers supported DMPA-SC self-injection training and distribution by health extension workers which will likely expand access to the method. This is in line with studies in different African countries that community workers are satisfied with DMPA-SC self-injection implementation [30, 32] and that DMPA-SC SI increases access to FP if community distribution is practiced [6]. Generally, community health workers are better accepted to provide FP services compared with other providers as they are very close to the community [33].

This study’s results present strong evidence supporting the introduction and scale-up of DMPA-SC self-injection in Ethiopia. However, the study has a few limitations worth mentioning. First, the research was conducted in urban settings, limiting generalizability to the rural context. At the same time, previous studies show that DMPA-SC self-injection is acceptable and feasible in rural areas and remote populations in Burkina Faso, Niger, Malawi, Senegal, and Uganda [8, 34]. Second, this study was conducted over a short period with only one 3-month follow-up. More resources and time would have allowed for a 12-month follow-up to assess the difference in continuation and competence for a longer period of time. Other studies including those with longer follow-up periods, have demonstrated higher continuation rates among those who self-inject DMPA-SC, further supporting our recommendation to introduce DMPA-SC self-injection in Ethiopia [3, 10, 13, 15]. Because obtaining ethical approvals to include individuals under 18 for this particular study were complex, we excluded women under 18 years of age. However, our qualitative findings emphasized the importance of including those under 18 years in future DMPA-SC self-injection in programs to ensure equitable access and inclusion.

Conclusions and recommendations

The MOH in Ethiopia aims to expand women’s access to and choice of modern family planning methods. To do so, they are committed to ensuring a broad contraceptive method mix in both the public and private health sectors. Despite progress made to address the unmet need for modern contraceptive methods, the unmet need remains high. The potential impact of DMPA-SC self-injection is substantial, particularly in the public sector where many women obtain their family planning methods. The need for expanded access to modern contraceptives is made even more urgent in the face of natural disasters, pandemics, disease outbreaks and conflicts which strain an already fragile health system. Interventions such as DMPA-SC self-injection promote women’s ability to exercise self-care and can lighten the burden on the health system by putting family planning directly into the hands of those who need the services the most. The introduction of DMPA-SC self-injection will also help advance the MOH’s progress on reaching FP2030 goals by increasing new family planning users and continuation rates.
