Ultrarapid Purification of Manganese-52 from Chromium-52 Targets via Potentiostatic Anodic Dissolution and Chelation Ion Chromatography
Authors: Qi Zhao, Junyi Wang, Junhua Xu, Mengxian Liu, Kaimin Shih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Manganese-52 (52 Mn, t 1/2 = 5.6 days) is an attractive positron-emitting isotope for integrated positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. Its availability, however, is constrained by the challenge of achieving rapid, high-purity separation from cyclotron-irradiated chromium-52 (52Cr) targets. This study presents an ultrarapid, two-step purification strategy that couples potentiostatic anodic dissolution with single-column chelation ion chromatography. Electrochemical analysis indicates that the Mn target undergoes rapid oxidation and is dissolved as ammine complexes in an ammonium/ammonium chloride buffer (pH 8) at 0.7 V (vs Hg/HgO), whereas the Cr target remained almost passivated and trace Cr leached was precipitated on the target surface as Cr­(OH)3 under identical conditions. The sharp decrease in anodic current density, induced by the distinct dissolution behavior of Mn/Cr targets, provides a convenient real-time endpoint for assessing complete Mn dissolution. The resulting analyte was then loaded directly onto iminodiacetate resins (Diaion CR11) and eluted with HCl, converting the ammine complexes to hydrated Mn2+. Simulated experiments achieved a Mn chromatographic recovery of 98.65% with >99.99% purity within 30 min. This integrated approach offers significant advantages in processing speed and recovery yield over conventional methods, demonstrating high potential for the production of clinical 52 Mn.

Introduction

Simultaneous positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI), first reported in 1990, is a remarkably powerful modality technique that enables the concurrent acquisition of morphological MRI sequences and functional PET information within a single scan. Currently, this dual-modality imaging relies on the coadministration of two chemically distinct contrast agents, typically [fluorine-18]-labeled radiopharmaceuticals for PET and gadolinium-based complexes for MRI. However, these agents often exhibit disparate pharmacokinetics and tissue interactions, leading to a spatial and temporal mismatch between PET and MRI signals, which ultimately constrains their diagnostic synergy. To address this limitation and minimize contrast dosages, manganese (Mn) isotopes have emerged as ideal alternatives for integrated PET/MRI: Mn­(II) possess favorable paramagnetic properties (d 5 configuration), while 52 Mn provides suitable decay characteristics, e.g., positron emission energy (E β+) of 0.24 MeV, positron emission intensity (I β+) of 29.4%, and a half-life (t 1/2) of 5.69 days. The combination of 55 Mn (stable) and 52 Mn radiolabeled with macrocyclic ligands, such as tetraxetan, has shown promising results in tracking stem cells in the brain, functional β cells, neural cells, and cancer cells.

The production of 52 Mn typically involves cyclotron irradiation of chromium (Cr) targets. To avoid the formation of long-lived impurities, such as 49V, 47Ti, and 54 Mn (t 1/2 = 312 days), highly enriched 52Cr targets are preferred to produce noncarrier-added (n. c. a.) 52 Mn via the 52Cr­(p, n)52 Mn reaction. Postirradiation, 52 Mn must be isolated from the Cr target and purified to pharmaceutical-grade standards, as residual Cr raises toxicological concerns. Conventional purification workflows generally begin with concentrated acid digestion, , followed by separation techniques such as solvent extraction, , coprecipitation, or, most frequently, multicolumn anion-exchange chromatography using hydroalcoholic chloride media (97% ethanol +3% 11 mol/dm3 HCl). ,, The latter exploits the selective adsorption of MnCl4 2– anions onto anion-exchange resins, whereas Cr3+ remains cationic and passes through without retention. However, this approach has several critical limitations: (1) extensive Cr codissolution during acid leaching complicates downstream purification steps, (2) limited complex stability of MnCl4 2– often leads to breakthrough losses (Mn recovery yield of 60–80%, rarely exceeding 90%), and (3) the evaporation/reconstitution steps required for phase transition to hydroalcoholic media significantly prolong processing time.

To overcome these limitations, this study introduces an integrated purification concept that couples potentiostatic anodic dissolution (electrooxidation) with single-column chelation ion chromatography. In contrast to conventional concentrated acid digestion, potentiostatic anodic dissolution offers a controllable and mild route , to drive metal oxidation/dissolution with superior selectivity. , Specifically, the substantial differences in the anodic dissolution behavior between Mn and Cr in an ammonium chloride–ammonia buffer solution (abbreviated as ABS, pH 8) enable their highly selective separation. Mn is rapidly oxidized and stabilized as soluble ammine complexes, whereas Cr remains strongly passivated. Importantly, the sharp decrease in current density serves as a clear endpoint indicator of the completion of Mn dissolution. The resulting Mn-bearing anolyte is then directly loaded onto chelating resins for further purification and simultaneous conversion to the chloride form. Simulation experiments demonstrate that this two-step workflow achieves a high Mn recovery yield (chromatographic Mn recovery of 98.65% with >99.99% purity) and rapid processing (dissolution-to-elution workflow within 30 min), highlighting significant advancements in recovery efficiency and processing time over established methodologies.

Experimental Section

Materials

It should be noted that due to the unavailability of cyclotron-irradiated 52Cr targets and a specialized radioactivity laboratory, natural Mn and Cr targets were utilized as surrogates to simulate the purification process.

Mn and Cr targets (purity of >99.99%) were supplied by Zhongke Yannuo (Beijing) New Material Technology Co., Ltd. Each target consisted of a 1 mm-thick Mn or Cr sheet bonded onto a 2 mm-thick Pt backing using an electrically conductive adhesive. These targets were cut into 2 cm × 1 cm slices without mechanically polishing. The chelating resin, Diaion CR11 (iminodiacetic acid, Na-form, 16–45 mesh), was acquired from Tianjin Shuanglian Technology Co., Ltd. ABS (0.1 mol/dm3) was prepared by dissolving NH4Cl (5.07 g, purity 99.5%, Thermo Scientific) and ammonia solution (0.36 mL, 28%, TCI) in 1 L of deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm), with the solution pH adjusted to 8. A control electrolyte comprising NaOH (1 × 10–6 mol/dm3) and NaCl (0.1 mol/dm3) was also prepared. All other reagents used were of analytical grade and used as received.

Electrochemical Tests

Electrochemical experiments were conducted in an H-type, dual-chamber glass cell using a PARSTAT MC multichannel potentiostat (PMC-200, Princeton Applied Research). A cation-exchange membrane (CMI-7000S, Membrane International Inc.) separated the anode and cathode chambers. The working electrodes, Mn/Cr targets, or Pt backing were vertically clamped and immersed in 100 mL of ABS or NaOH solution. A Pt counter electrode (1 cm × 1 cm) and an Hg/HgO reference electrode (Φ = 6 mm, 1 mol/dm3 KOH, Shanghai Chuxi Industrial Co., Ltd.) were employed. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were carried out from 0 to 1.5 V (vs Hg/HgO) at a scan rate of 10 mV/s. Potentiostatic anodic dissolution was performed at a constant potential of 0.7 V (vs Hg/HgO) for 10 min with current–time data recorded every 0.5 s. Aliquots (2 mL) of the anolyte were sampled at specific intervals, filtrated, heated at 60 °C for 20 min (CR 25 reactor, Rocker Scientific Co., Ltd.), and then acidified with 5% HNO3, prior to metal concentration analysis via an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (Agilent 5110 ICP-OES, Agilent Technologies).

Characterization of Leachates and Target Materials

Anolyte leachates of ABS and NaOH solution were filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters (RephiQuik) and diluted to a Mn concentration of approximately 1 × 10–4 mol/dm3 with the corresponding background electrolyte. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra of the diluted anolyte leachates were collected at room temperature on a Bruker ELEXSYS E500 (X-band, 9.85 GHz). The static magnetic field was swept from 3001.6 to 4001.2 G, with a modulation amplitude of 8 G at 100 kHz (first-harmonic detection). Baseline correction was applied during the acquisition. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra of the diluted anolyte leachates were recorded on a UV-1900i spectrophotometer (Shimadzu). Surface crystal phases of target materials were characterized by grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) at an incident angle (ω) of 0.2° and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) on D8 Advance (Bruker). Surface morphology and elemental mapping were performed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Gemini 500) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, Aztec UltimMax 100). Chemical states were probed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher K-α). Binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV from adventitious carbon.

Chelation Ion Chromatography

A glass chromatography column (Φ = 1.3 cm) was packed with Diaion CR11 to a bed height of 10 cm and preconditioned with three bed volumes (BVs) of deionized water. A 100 mL mixed feed, composed of 50 mL of each leachate from 10 min anodic dissolution of Mn and Cr targets, was loaded onto the column at a flow rate of 20 mL/min via a peristaltic pump (BT100-2J, LongerPump). Following a rinse with 40 mL of deionized water, the column was eluted with 20 mL of HCl solution (0.1 mol/dm3) at the same outflow rate. Each 5 mL fraction was collected in disposable centrifuge tubes. After heating and acidification, the metal concentrations in collected fractions were analyzed by ICP-OES. The recovery yield for Mn (E Mn) was calculated using 1 EMn=CeVeC0V0×100% where C and V represent the metal concentration and volume of the elution (subscript e) and initial feed (subscript 0), respectively. Additionally, the NH4 + content in each fraction was measured with an ion-selective electrode (ISE) connected to a pH/ISE benchtop meter (Weihai Jingxun Changtong Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.).

Results and Discussion

Anodic Dissolution Behavior and Target Characterization

LSV was employed to identify the optimal potential window for selective anodic dissolution. Figure a compares the LSV profiles of Mn and Cr targets in ABS and NaOH solution (both at pH 8). The coordinate is the anodic current density (j A), normalized to the geometric area of the immersed electrodes. In the ABS medium, the Mn target exhibits substantial j A values, reaching approximately 4 mA/cm2 at the open-circuit potential, demonstrating spontaneous dissolution and self-corrosion. The j A increases monotonically with potential without distinct oxidation peaks, suggesting an irreversible, diffusion-controlled dissolution process. A black film observed on the Mn target after scanning to 1.5 V likely corresponds to reoxidation of Mn­(II) to insoluble MnO2. In contrast, the Cr target in ABS exhibits very low j A values (<0.1 mA/cm2) below 0.9 V, reflecting its strong passivation. The minor j A values detected in this region are likely ascribed to localized corrosion at inherent surface defects or Cl–-induced pitting. Beyond 0.9 V, a sharp increase in j A values suggests the transpassive breakdown of the passive layer, resulting in Cr­(III) oxidation to soluble Cr­(VI). Compared to ABS, no spontaneous dissolution of the Mn target was detected in NaOH solution at open circuit, and the j A values for the Mn target in NaOH solution were almost threefold lower than those in ABS across the 0–1.5 V range, indicating markedly slower dissolution kinetics in NaOH solution. Throughout the potential window, the j A values for both targets significantly exceeded the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) threshold (highlighted in light blue), confirming the anodic dissolution processes as the dominant electrochemical pathway. Based on these observations, a potential of 0.7 V (vs Hg/HgO) was selected for potentiostatic anodic dissolution, as it allows for rapid and selective Mn dissolution without insoluble MnO2 formation, whereas the Cr target remains largely inert.

Figure b presents the potentiostatic anodic dissolution of Mn and Cr targets at 0.7 V (vs Hg/HgO) in ABS. Mn concentration increased steadily to approximately 50 mg/dm3 over a 10 min anodic period, with the j A values decreasing from 27 to 23.5 mA/cm2. Except for the initial 0.5 min, the linear relationship between Mn concentration and the square root of leaching time aligns well with the Cottrell relation, suggesting the diffusion-controlled transport of Mn­(II) from the electrode surface. In stark contrast, the Cr target showed neither measurable dissolution, below the ICP-OES detection limit (D. L.) of 10 ppb, nor significant j A values (approximately 0.04 mA/cm2), confirming its sustained passivation.

To elucidate the discrepancy in j A observed between ABS and NaOH solution, EPR and UV–vis spectroscopy were utilized to identify Mn speciation. As shown in Figure c, it is apparent that the characteristic six-line hyperfine patterns are attributed to the high-spin Mn­(II) in both spectra. The g-values are essentially identical (almost 2), confirming that Mn remains in the +2 oxidation state. However, a slight reduction in the isotropic hyperfine constant (A iso) was observed in ABS relative to the NaOH solution. Actually, NH3 is a stronger σ-donor ligand and engages in greater covalency with Mn­(II) than water, which decreases the electron spin density at the Mn nucleus. UV–vis spectra (Figure d) further support the difference in Mn speciation: the d-d absorption bands in NaOH solution correspond to the octahedral aqua complex Mn­(H2O)6 2+ (commonly simplified as Mn2+), whereas in ABS, these absorption bands undergo a modest blue shift (1–4 nm), indicative of stronger ligand fields arising from NH3 coordination with Mn­(II). This spectral shift suggests the formation of mixed-ligand complexes, Mn­(H2O) x (NH3)6–x 2+, , consistent with previously reported analogous shifts in Ni­(II) systems transitioning from aqua to ammine complexes. In summary, these spectroscopic results clearly demonstrate the variation in Mn­(II) speciation between ABS and NaOH media, which likely contributes to the distinct j A values.

The target materials were characterized before and after 10 min of anodic dissolution to evaluate surface changes. Initially, both Mn and Cr targets appeared homogeneous. After 10 min of anodic dissolution, the Cr target darkened, and a clear greenish-brown film formed on its surface (Figure a). SEM images revealed that both targets became roughened and uneven after anodic dissolution, with elemental analysis indicating a significant rise in O atom on the Cr surface (Figure b). EDS mapping images display the homogeneous distribution of Mn/Cr and O on the target surface before and after anodic dissolution (Figure S1 of the Supporting Information). GIXRD analysis (Figure c) showed that the Mn target before and after anodic dissolution exhibited similar prominent diffraction at approximately 43.0°, corresponding to the (3 3 0) crystal planes of metallic Mn. The absence of other characteristic peaks is likely due to texture effects or the preferred crystallographic orientation. The main component on the Cr target surface was identified to be Cr. Notably, a clear hump (around 20°) was found for the Cr target after anodic dissolution, which might be attributed to amorphous Cr­(OH)3. This observation was corroborated by complementary PXRD results (Figure d). XPS analysis provided additional surface compositional and chemical-state information within approximately 5 nm of the target surfaces. XPS survey indicated that no other metal contaminants were present in the targets (Figure S2 of the Supporting Information). As depicted in Figure e, the Mn 2p3/2 spectra were deconvoluted into three peaks, corresponding to MnO (641.3 eV), Mn2O3 (643.4 eV), and an associated satellite peak (646.2 eV) for MnO due to the shakeup mechanism. , It is found that the compositional changes in the Mn target before and after anodic dissolution can be negligible, while Cr 2p3/2 spectra shifted significantly after anodic dissolution: the metallic Cr0 peak (573.6 eV) diminished, while binding energy peaks corresponding to Cr2O3 (575.8 eV) and Cr­(OH)3 (577.3 eV) became predominant. − This clearly demonstrates the transformation of metallic Cr0 into Cr­(OH)3. The O 1s spectra were deconvoluted into peaks representing lattice oxygen (OL, 529.5 eV), oxygen vacancies and hydroxide (OV, OM–OH, 531.2 eV), , surface-adsorbed hydroxyl species (Oad, 532.5 eV), and bound molecular water (Ow, 534.6 eV). It is clear that the Ow peak appears after the anodic dissolution on the Cr target, which is likely associated with the hydration effect of amorphous hydroxide. Depth profiling indicated that this hydroxide layer had a thickness of less than 20 nm (Figure S3 of the Supporting Information).

Integrating the electrochemical and characterization results, Figure f schematically summarizes the anodic dissolution processes: under anodic conditions (0.7 V) in ABS, the Mn target is rapidly oxidized to soluble Mn­(II) and stabilized as mixed-ligand Mn­(H2O) x (NH3)6–x 2+ complexes. Conversely, Cr target undergoes limited oxidation due to its robust passivation. Minor oxidized Cr, probably caused by localized corrosion at defects or Cl–-induced pitting, rapidly precipitates as a very thin layer (<20 nm) of insoluble Cr­(OH)3 on the target surface, owing to its extremely low solubility product (6.3 × 10–31). Column Separation

A 100 mL mixed feed comprising 50 mL of each of the ABS leachate of Mn and Cr targets was loaded onto the preconditioned chelating resin (Diaion CR11) column. The column separation profile is depicted in Figure . ICP-OES analysis of the effluent fractions confirmed that >99.99% of the dissolved Mn was effectively retained on the resin, while Cr concentration remained below the DL of ICP-OES throughout the entire column separation process. Despite the potential risk of trace Cr contamination during anodic dissolution, the Diaion CR11 column exhibited a strong and nearly irreversible affinity for Cr­(III), preventing breakthrough contamination and ensuring high Mn purity. − Mn recovery yield exceeded 98% upon elution with a single 20 mL of 0.1 mol/dm3 HCl solution, with coeluted Cr remaining below the DL of ICP-OES (<10 ppb). The slight Mn loss observed is likely due to the trace leaching during the rinsing step or incomplete elution. Beyond metal separation, the removal of NH4 + is critical, as it can interfere with radiopharmaceutical labeling effciency. It is clear that the NH4 + concentration dramatically decreases from approximately 200 mg/dm3 in the feed to <10–2 mg/dm3 in the eluent phase (lower than the DL of ISE). These results highlight that the single-column chelation chromatography serves a dual purpose: (1) high-efficiency purification from trace Cr and (2) ligand transformation from the Mn–NH3 complexes in ABS medium to its hydrated forms in an aqueous medium, which is directly compatible with clinical labeling protocols.

Simulated Mn Purification Process

Figure illustrates a conceptual flowsheet for the purification of 52 Mn from a cyclotron-irradiated 52Cr target via potentiostatic anodic dissolution coupled to single-column chelation chromatography. According to International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) data, a 1 cm × 2 cm 52Cr target irradiated with 11 MeV proton beams typically results in a 52 Mn-enriched surface layer approximately 22 μm thick. The Cottrell fit in Figure b predicts that potentiostatic anodic dissolution at 0.7 V in ABS (pH 8) can effectively leach more than 99% of the produced 52 Mn within approximately 10.6 min. Therefore, a simulated dissolution experiment was conducted: 11 min of Mn anodic dissolution followed by 4 min of Cr dissolution to mimic the dissolution process from the 52 Mn-enriched layer to the Cr matrix. ICP-OES analysis of the resulting anolyte indicates a dissolved Mn concentration of 54.19 mg/dm3, with Cr impurities remaining below 10 ppb. Significantly, the j A values remained >20 mA/cm2 during Mn dissolution and then abruptly dropped to <0.1 mA/cm2 upon completion. This sharp decline provides a robust, real-time electrochemical signal for the Mn dissolution endpoint, effectively preventing unnecessary Cr leaching and shortening the dissolution time. Subsequent chromatography steps, including loading, rinsing, and eluting, were completed within 8 min, achieving a chromatographic Mn recovery yield of 98.65% (ratio of Mn collected in the elution fraction to that in the column feed) with a chemical purity exceeding 99.99%. Consequently, the entire dissolution-to-elution workflow was finalized in under 30 min.

Conceptual flowsheet of 52 Mn purification from the 52Cr target through anodic dissolution and chelation ion chromatography. The green box indicates a closed-loop strategy for recovering 52Cr via acid washing and electrolytic deposition.

Given the high cost (∼$8/mg) of isotopically enriched 52Cr, efficient target recycling is economically beneficial. As indicated in the closed-loop strategy (green box in Figure ), the Cr­(OH)3 layer can be efficiently stripped with 2 mol/dm3 H2SO4. While the trace amounts of Cr leached (<10 ppb in four anodic dissolution) in this study were insufficient to demonstrate practical electrodeposition for 52Cr recycling, the resulting leachate is inherently compatible with mature electrolytic plating techniques in H2SO4 media for 52Cr recycling. Furthermore, a five-cycle replication test of the anodic dissolution and column separation was performed. Results yielded consistently stable dissolved Mn concentration (>48 mg/dm3) and chromatographic Mn recoveries (92–96%), demonstrating the recyclability and separation performance stability. However, in practical radiopharmaceutical purification, the column recyclability must be carefully considered and verified, as radiation-induced degradation of the resins can lead to potential loss of 52 Mn.

Comparison with Reported Purification Strategies

Table summarizes previously reported Mn purification strategies from Cr targets and highlights their inherent limitations. Traditionally, the target dissolution is the rate-limiting step, commonly performed via concentrated mineral acid leaching. The literature indicates that cyclotron-irradiated Cr targets often require digestion in concentrated HCl (>9 mol/dm3) for at least 30 min to ensure complete Mn dissolution. Such prolonged acid leaching treatment inevitably leads to extensive Cr codissolution, complicating downstream separation. Oxidative leaching using HNO3/H2O2 can improve dissolution kinetics but introduces additional redox steps: initially oxidizing both Mn and Cr, followed by selective reduction of Mn­(IV) to Mn­(II), thereby increasing contamination risk and operational complexity. In contrast, this study employs potentiostatic anodic dissolution in mildly alkaline ABS (pH 8). In this system, Mn is rapidly and selectively dissolved, Cr remains essentially passive, and trace Cr­(III) is leached promptly and deposits as Cr­(OH)3 on the target surface. More importantly, monitoring j A values allows for the precise, real-time determination of the Mn dissolution endpoint. This feature significantly shortens the processing period and minimizes Cr contamination at the source.

Regarding downstream separation, conventional Mn2+/Cr3+ fractionation using cation-exchange chromatography often suffers from poor selectivity and low Mn recovery, particularly at high Cr3+ concentrations. Oxalate-assisted cation-exchange chromatography can enhance selectivity but at the cost of lower Mn recoveries. Alternative methods that oxidize Cr­(III) to Cr­(VI) (e.g., CrO4 2–) while keeping Mn in the divalent state, in principle, offer high selectivity. Unfortunately, the quantitative conversion of Cr­(VI) is chemically challenging, and the strongly oxidizing Cr­(VI) species can degrade the resin, leading to Cr breakthrough. Furthermore, Cr­(VI) is toxic and should be avoided in clinical applications. Chemical precipitation routes are likewise unattractive because coprecipitation losses even drive Mn recovery below 30%. The separation of MnCl4 2– and Cr3+ using anion-exchange resin remains the most widely adopted approach. , Nevertheless, it faces two critical limitations: (1) the moderate stability of Mn–Cl complexes limits Mn recovery yield (rarely exceeding 90%) and (2) each feed before loading into the chromatography column requires solution reconstitution and the acidic leachate and elute be evaporated to near dryness and reconstituted in a hydroalcoholic medium to promote MnCl4 2– formation. These laborious evaporation–reconstitution steps can prolong the total processing time by more than 30%. In comparison, the single-column chelate ion chromatography used in this study eliminates the needs for evaporation/reconstitution steps. The Mn­(II)–NH3 complexes are directly retained by the Diaion CR11 resins, and Mn2+ is then eluted almost quantitatively in HCl medium for subsequent radiochemical labeling.

Figure compares the performance of reported Mn purification methods achieving Mn purity above 95%, plotting recovery yield against total purification time. Since purification times required are seldom explicitly detailed in the literature, and several key operational parameters are either missing or not directly comparable across studies (e.g., target mass, Mn concentration, and flow rate), standardized, conservative estimates for common unit operations were applied to enable a consistent comparison: 30 min for acid leaching, 30 min for solution evaporation/reconstitution, and 20 min for a single-column separation sequence. These estimates are intended solely for cross-method comparison, and actual processing times will depend on the specific experimental setup and operating conditions. The total Mn recovery in this study is conservatively estimated at 98% (assuming an anodic dissolution efficiency of 99.9%). In summary, the integration of potentiostatic anodic dissolution with chelate ion chromatography demonstrates exceptional efficiency relative to conventional purification approaches, extremely high recovery yield (>98%), and a shorter processing period (<30 min).

Limitations of the Simulated System

In this study, nonirradiated natural Mn and Cr targets (bonded on a Pt backing) were employed as surrogates to evaluate the feasibility of the proposed radiochemical purification strategy. In practical applications, cyclotron 52Cr targets are manufactured in several forms (e.g., Cr foils, pressed pellets, or electroplated layers on metallic backings) and are exposed to intense proton beam irradiation and beam heating. This process can introduce several effects that are not reproduced in our simulated system, e.g., microstructure (e.g., density, porosity, and grain size), surface morphology, lattice defects, and even radiation/thermal damage to target materials. Such variations may influence the absolute j A values and dissolution kinetics under potentiostatic control. Nevertheless, the fundamental dissolution selectivity of the proposed method arises from the intrinsic difference in the electrochemical behavior between Mn and Cr in the ABS medium: the rapid oxidation of Mn into soluble complexes, while the strong passivation of the Cr target within the selected potential window, rather than the target manufacturing forms and radiation history. Consequently, the observed dissolution selectivity is expected to remain reliable as the transition to practical cyclotron targets proceeds. In addition, although these simulated experiments demonstrate the chemical feasibility and superiority of the purification process, the further radiochemical validation utilizing actual cyclotron-irradiated 52Cr targets remains essential to assess the rigorous requirements for clinical specifications, including radiochemical purity, radionuclide purity, and chemical purity.

Conclusion

This study presents a rapid and highly effective method for purifying Mn from a Cr target by integrating potentiostatic anodic dissolution with single-column chelation ion chromatography. Electrochemical analysis demonstrated that the Mn target exhibits markedly enhanced dissolution kinetics in ABS medium (pH 8) compared with NaOH solution. Potentiostatic anodic dissolution at 0.7 V showed the rapid and selective dissolution of Mn target, while Cr remained passivated due to the formation of an extremely thin Cr­(OH)3 layer after anodic dissolution. The large difference in anodic dissolution behavior between Mn and Cr provides a convenient, real-time indicator of the endpoint Mn dissolution by monitoring j A values. This feature can minimize both dissolution time and potential Cr contamination. Subsequent chelation ion chromatography using Diaion CR11 facilitates high-efficiency separation of trace Cr impurities and promotes the conversion of the Mn­(II)–NH3 complex into its hydrated forms for radiopharmaceutical labeling. The entire “dissolution-to-elution” workflow was completed within just 30 min, yielding an estimated Mn recovery yield of >98% and chemical purities above 99.99%. Compared to existing acid digestion and multicolumn separation methods, this integrated strategy offers substantial practical advantages of reducing required processing time and enhancing recovery, which are critical to increasing 52Mn utilization and lowering 52Mn cost.
