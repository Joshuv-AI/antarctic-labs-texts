Association between serum cotinine levels and urinary incontinence in adults in the United States: a population-based cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Chengdong Shi, Lei Yang, Guoqiang Zeng, Hongliang Cao, Fangqiu Yu, Shanyu Sha, Yuantao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental tobacco smoke (ETS) exposure has been shown to be associated with a variety of diseases, but evidence regarding the association between it and urinary incontinence (UI) is limited. Cotinine, a metabolite of nicotine in the human body, can more accurately quantify the level of human exposure to tobacco smoke. The study utilized data from seven survey cycles (2007-March 2020 Pre-pandemic) of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) program. Weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis, subgroup analysis, interaction tests, smooth curve fitting, and threshold effect models were used to analyze the relationship between serum cotinine and UI. Additionally, a 1:1 nearest neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) method was employed to minimize the impact of confounding factors. Before and after PSM, serum cotinine levels were higher in individuals with UI than those without (P < 0.05). Both before and after PSM, UI was positively correlated with serum cotinine levels, with a significantly increased risk of urinary incontinence when serum cotinine levels were in the Q3 range (before PSM: OR = 1.89, 95% CI = 1.59–2.24; after PSM: OR = 1.60, 95% CI = 1.28-2.00). Smooth curve fitting before and after PSM showed an approximate J-shaped non-linear dose-response relationship between log-transformed serum cotinine levels and UI. This study indicates that among American adults, there is a positive relationship between serum cotinine levels and UI, which is also significant in self-reported non-smoking populations. Therefore, reducing exposure to environmental tobacco smoke (e.g., avoiding second-hand smoke) in work and daily life may help alleviate the occurrence of UI, and serum cotinine levels have the potential to be a tool for predicting the degree of risk of developing UI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19863-2.Keywords: Non-linear associations, Serum cotinine, Environmental tobacco smoke, NHANES, Urinary incontinence

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a prevalent urogenital condition characterized by the involuntary and uncontrolled release of urine [1]. The predominant classification standards are stress urinary incontinence, urge urinary incontinence, and mixed urinary incontinence [2]. The prevalence of UI has been rising in recent years, with an estimated 350 million people affected worldwide. The prevalence of the phenomenon differs among various areas, demographic groups, and economic strata [3]. Obesity, diabetes, smoking, and old age are the primary risk factors for UI. Timely detection and control of these risk factors are especially crucial as they might decrease the occurrence and associated problems of UI, therefore enhancing patients’ psychological well-being and overall quality of life [4, 5].

Smoking severely impacts human health, causing varying degrees of harm to multiple organs and systems. For instance, smoking increases the risk of respiratory diseases (such as nasopharyngeal cancer, lung cancer, and pneumonia), cardiovascular diseases (such as coronary heart disease, arrhythmias, and stroke), and urogenital and endocrine-related diseases (such as reduced sperm count, cervical cancer, bladder cancer, premature ovarian failure, and diabetes) [6–8]. This presents substantial obstacles to public health systems and human well-being. Currently, a growing body of epidemiological research suggests that environmental tobacco smoke (ETS), including second-hand smoking, has emerged as an independent risk factor for a range of disorders [9]. ETS primarily consists of second-hand smoke and the burning of tobacco products in the environment. A 4-year longitudinal study on 24,985 adult women in China identified smoking as a significant risk factor for UI [10]. Additionally, in a review on smoking and urological health, David Mobley et al. mentioned that smoking is associated with various urological diseases, including incontinence, urological tumors, kidney stones, cystitis, and more [11]. Furthermore, a case-control study showed that, even when there were no significant differences in age, parity, weight, and estrogen levels, smoking status was associated with an increased odds ratio for stress urinary incontinence in women [12]. Moreover, a study by Takashi Kawahara and colleagues indicated that current smokers exhibited a higher prevalence of male lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) [13]. Nevertheless, research about UI has predominantly concentrated on active smoking, while there have been comparatively few studies investigating the correlation between exposure to ETS exposure and UI. In addition, these studies on ETS primarily rely on questionnaires, leading to a degree of subjectivity that inevitably affects the research results. As a result, certain researchers have suggested the utilization of a measurable biomarker, called serum cotinine, to accurately represent the extent of environmental tobacco smoke exposure in populations. Cotinine is a metabolite of nicotine in the human body, with a half-life of about 15–20 h, and it can be detected in serum, saliva, urine, and other bodily fluids. A study by Gen-Min Lin et al. found that among inactive smokers, subjects with urine cotinine levels > 7.07 ng/ml had a higher risk of heart failure compared to those with urine cotinine levels ≤ 7.07 ng/ml [14]. In addition, a cross-sectional survey of the US population showed that serum cotinine has a dose-response relationship with diseases such as kidney stones, coronary heart disease, and diabetes [15]. Nevertheless, there is a limited amount of evidence available about the correlation between UI and serum cotinine. Hence, this study uses serum cotinine concentration as an objective indicator to quantify ETS exposure, aiming to explore the association between ETS and UI among American adults.

Methods

Study population and design

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a major program of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) aimed at assessing the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the program has been approved by the NCHS and the Institutional Review Board of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and all participants provided written informed consent.

This study utilized data from seven consecutive survey cycles of the NHANES program (2007-March 2020 Pre-pandemic). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (A) participants without UI information (n = 32,936), (B) participants missing serum cotinine information (n = 15,522), (C) Participants missing covariate information on body mass index (BMI), creatinine, etc., where the missing values were less than 5%, and (D) participants with variables that might affect UI and serum cotinine levels, including pregnancy (n = 318), cancer (n = 3,065), stroke (n = 402), and kidney failure (n = 850). Ultimately, 10,195 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the participant selection process

Assessment of environmental tobacco smoke exposure

Based on the household interview questionnaire, we categorized individuals who had smoked less than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime as non-smokers. Individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime but were not currently smoking were classified as former smokers. Lastly, individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and had smoked on some days in the past 30 days were considered current smokers. Cotinine is a metabolite of nicotine and can serve as an indicator of both active smoking and environmental tobacco smoke exposure [16]. Serum cotinine is measured by an isotope-dilution high-performance liquid chromatography/atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometric (ID HPLC-APCI MS/MS) method. Based on the findings of Benowitz et al., smoke exposure is classified into three categories: unexposed (serum cotinine level < 0.05 ng/mL), low exposure (0.05-2.99ng/mL), or heavy exposure (serum cotinine level ≥ 3 ng/mL), using the ideal cut-off point of 3 ng/mL [17, 18].

Definition and assessment of urinary incontinence

The presence of UI was ascertained using a self-administered survey questionnaire that had been employed in previous studies. Participants were considered to have urinary incontinence if they answered “yes” to any of the following questions: (A) “During the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine with an activity like coughing, lifting or exercise?” (B) “During the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine with an urge or pressure to urinate and you couldn’t get to the toilet fast enough?” (C) " During the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine without an activity like coughing, lifting, or exercise, or an urge to urinate?”

Covariates

Based on previous studies, covariates that may influence the relationship between ETS and urinary incontinence were adjusted for age, sex (male and female), race (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other races), education (less than high school, high school graduate/GED or equivalent, and high school or higher), poverty impact ratio (PIR, < 1, 1–3, and > 3), history of hypertension, history of diabetes mellitus, BMI (< 25 kg/m2, 25–30 kg/m2, ≥ 30 kg/m2), waist circumference, physical activity, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, hypercholesterolemia, history of cardiovascular disease, and alcohol consumption [19]. Hypertension was defined as self-reported hypertension use of oral antihypertensive medications or having three or more measurements with a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg out of four measurements [20]. Diabetes was characterized as either self-reported diabetes, the utilization of antidiabetic medicines or insulin, or having a glycated hemoglobin level exceeding 6.5% [21]. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as self-reported hypercholesterolemia or the use of cholesterol-lowering medications or having a total cholesterol level ≥ 240 mg/dl. Cardiovascular disease was defined as self-reported angina, heart failure, coronary heart disease, or heart attack. Physical activity was assessed based on the questionnaire: “Do you engage in any vigorous-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities that cause a large increase in breathing or heart rate, such as running or playing basketball for at least 10 minutes continuously?” and “Do you engage in any moderate-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities that cause a small increase in breathing or heart rate, such as brisk walking, cycling, swimming, volleyball or playing golf for at least 10 minutes continuously?” If participants answer ‘yes’ to either of these questions, they are classified as engaging in strenuous or moderate physical activity accordingly. If they answer ‘no’ to both questions, they are classified as engaging in inactive physical activity [22]. The classification of alcohol consumption status was as follows: non-drinker (having consumed less than 12 drinks in a lifetime), former drinker (having consumed more than 12 drinks in a lifetime but not consumed any alcohol in the past year), low-to-moderate current drinker (having consumed 1–2 drinks per day in the past year), and heavy drinker (having consumed at least 3 drinks per day in the past year).

Statistical analysis

Considering the complex sampling design of the NHANES study, this analysis included appropriate sampling weights to provide nationally representative estimates. For the statistical analysis, continuous variables that did not conform to a normal distribution were represented by the median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were reported as frequency and weighted percentage. Due to the skewed distribution of serum cotinine, a log10 transformation was performed to approximate a normal distribution, and it was then analyzed as a continuous variable. A weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the correlation between serum cotinine and UI, as well as the correlation between self-reported smoking status and UI.

Additionally, the relationship between serum cotinine and UI was examined in non-smoking individuals. The adjusted models of the study are as follows: Model 1: Unadjusted for covariates; Model 2: Adjusted for age, gender, race, poverty-income ratio, and education level; Model 3: Adjusted for age, gender, race, PIR, education level, BMI, waist circumference, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, cardiovascular disease (CVD), physical activity, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine. The study examined trends using serum cotinine categories as continuous variables in the models. Additionally, subgroup analysis and interaction tests were conducted to observe the association between serum cotinine levels and UI across different subgroups.

Subsequently, to mitigate differences between UI and non-UI populations, this study employed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) to minimize potential biases and confounding variables in observational research [23]. Using the matched data, the study applied a multivariable logistic regression model to analyze the relationship between serum cotinine levels and UI. Interaction tests and subgroup analyses were also conducted. Ultimately, the process involved applying smoothed curve fitting and threshold effect analysis to characterize the non-linear relationship between serum cotinine levels and UI before and after PSM.

Statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.3.2) and Empowering Stats (version 4.2). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

This analysis included a total of 10,195 participants (weighted N = 629,492,77), among whom 5,979 reported urinary incontinence (58.52%), and 4,216 reported no urinary incontinence (41.48%). The population comprised 6,095 females, 59.23%, and 4,100 males, representing 40.77%. The median age of the population was 50 years, with a majority of individuals being non-Hispanic White. Table S1 presents the sample characteristics, divided based on serum cotinine levels and self-reported smoking status. Individuals with serum cotinine levels ≥ 3 ng/mL were often without diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia, frequently engaged in vigorous physical activity, and were more susceptible to UI.

Associations between serum cotinine levels and smoking status with the prevalence of urinary incontinence

Weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between serum cotinine levels and UI. In Model 1, log10-transformed cotinine levels were significantly positively associated with UI (OR = 1.16, CI: 1.12–1.21). After adjusting for covariates, the association between log10-transformed cotinine levels and UI remained significant in Models 2 and 3. In Model 3, when serum cotinine levels were categorized, compared to the lowest serum cotinine level (Q1), the highest quartile of serum cotinine level (Q3) was significantly positively associated with UI (OR = 1.93, CI: 1.38–2.69, P trend < 0.001). Furthermore, this study examined the correlation between self-reported smoking status and UI. In Model 3, compared to non-smokers, current smokers (OR = 2.22, CI: 1.82–2.69) and former smokers (OR = 2.03, CI: 1.76–2.34) were positively associated with UI (P trend < 0.001). Moreover, we examined the correlation between serum cotinine levels and UI in self-reported non-smokers. It was found that in Model 3, each unit of increased log-transformed serum cotinine levels was associated with a 13.0% increase in the risk of UI (OR = 1.13, CI: 1.04–1.22), P = 0.0042). Meanwhile, Table 1 shows that in Model 3, compared to the lowest serum cotinine level (Q1), the highest quartile of serum cotinine level (Q3) was significantly positively associated with UI (OR = 2.05, CI: 1.41–2.97), P trend = 0.013) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis and interaction tests

The subgroup analysis showed a significant association between serum cotinine levels and UI in each subgroup categorized by gender, age, BMI, physical activity, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes (P < 0.05). Significant relationships were found only among persons without diabetes and cardiovascular disease in the subgroups divided by these conditions. The results of the interaction tests indicated that factors such as gender, age, BMI, physical activity, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease did not significantly modify the relationship between serum cotinine levels and UI (p for interaction > 0.05). Conversely, elevated levels of cholesterol may affect the favorable correlation between the two variables (p for interaction < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Non-linear associations between serum cotinine levels and the prevalence of urinary incontinence

The study employed smoothing spline fitting to identify a non-linear correlation between UI and log-transformed serum cotinine levels, demonstrating an approximately J-shaped connection (Fig. 3). The threshold effect analysis revealed a critical point in the relationship between serum cotinine concentration and UI. This critical point was at a serum cotinine concentration of 0.1 ng/ml. Above this threshold, there was a positive association between UI and log-transformed serum cotinine levels (OR = 1.93, CI: 1.38–2.69, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Following propensity score matching (PSM), the study included 1,139 participants in both the urinary incontinence group and the non-urinary incontinence group. The baseline features of the two groups were effectively adjusted (Table 3). After PSM, it was observed that individuals with urine incontinence exhibited elevated serum cotinine levels. Additionally, a greater percentage of participants had serum cotinine levels exceeding 3.0 ng/ml. Results from the multivariate logistic regression analysis showed a significant positive association between greater cotinine levels and UI (OR = 1.12, CI: 1.06–1.19, p < 0.001). After adjusting for covariates in Model 3, each unit of increased log-transformed serum cotinine levels was associated with a 12.0% increase in the risk of UI (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06–1.19, p < 0.001). When treating serum cotinine levels as a categorical variable in Model 3, compared to the lowest serum cotinine level (Q1), the highest quartile of serum cotinine level (Q3) was significantly positively associated with UI (OR = 1.61, CI:1.28-2.00, P trend < 0.001). Also, in the association between self-reported smoking status and UI, in Model 3, current smokers (OR = 1.62, CI: 1.25–2.10) and former smokers (OR = 1.79, CI: 1.43–2.25) were positively associated with UI (P trend<0.001) (Table S2). Finally, in the self-reported non-smoking population, it was found that in Model 3, each unit of increased log-transformed serum cotinine levels was associated with a 17.0% increase in the risk of UI (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.05–1.31, p = 0.0055) (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis showed that serum cotinine levels were significantly associated with UI in each subgroup stratified by gender, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia (P < 0.05). In other stratified subgroups, significant associations were observed only in participants without diabetes and cardiovascular disease, those aged ≥ 40, with BMI ≥ 25, strenuous physical activity, and with low-moderate alcohol consumption. The interaction test results showed no statistically significant differences in the relationship between serum cotinine levels and UI based on gender, age, BMI, physical activity, alcohol consumption, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. This suggests that these factors did not have a significant impact on the positive association between serum cotinine levels and UI (p for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 4). At last, smoothing curve fitting was performed again on the data after PSM, revealing an approximately J-shaped non-linear association between the two variables (Fig. 5). Threshold effect analysis indicated the optimal inflection point at a serum cotinine level of 0.125 ng/ml (Table S3).

Discussion

This study observed a significant positive correlation between high ETS exposure (high serum cotinine concentration) and the occurrence of UI. Notably, this relationship remained significant in the analysis of self-reported non-smokers and the population after PSM. Additionally, a J-shaped dose-response relationship may exist between log-transformed cotinine concentration and UI. These findings suggest that the degree of ETS exposure is strongly associated with UI and that serum cotinine levels have the potential to be a tool for predicting the degree of risk of developing UI.

ETS exposure has varying degrees of impact on multiple diseases. A meta-analysis by Wang YT and colleagues on the impact of second-hand smoke on disease in the Chinese population found that those exposed to second-hand smoke had approximately 1.52 times the probability of developing bladder cancer compared to those unexposed. This impact was 1.37 times higher for liver cancer and 1.92 times higher for circulatory system diseases [24]. Similarly, our study shows that the degree of exposure to ETS is associated with the occurrence of UI. However, tobacco smoke contains over 4,500 harmful chemicals, and its mechanism of action in the development of UI is complex.

Regarding the potential mechanisms by which ETS exposure might influence UI, the following four aspects were considered. First: pelvic floor blood flow dysfunction. The harmful substances in tobacco smoke have been suggested to directly harm the endothelial cells in blood vessels, leading to the gradual development of a pro-atherosclerotic environment in the vessel walls, thereby impairing vascular function [25]. The long-term impact of these toxic substances on vascular conditions can lead to arteriosclerosis and occlusion in multiple areas of the body, particularly affecting the branches of the iliac artery. This results in reduced microcirculatory blood flow to the lower urinary tract, such as insufficient perfusion to the bladder and pelvic floor muscles. Ischemia of the bladder and pelvic floor muscles can lead to cellular hypoxia and accumulation of metabolic byproducts, causing oxidative stress reactions, decreased bladder compliance, and sensitization of bladder detrusor contraction responses. These conditions contribute to detrusor overactivity [26, 27]. Moreover, research has demonstrated that prolonged exposure to tobacco smoke ultimately causes neurotoxic and muscular degenerative alterations, leading to diminished muscle functionality, poor brain communication, and decreased ability to regulate urinary function [28]. Second: oxidative stress damage. Components of cigarette smoke (such as acrolein and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons) can impair mitochondrial function in various cells, leading to oxidative stress responses [29–31]. Meanwhile, peroxynitrite compounds can inhibit protein synthesis and alter protein function through free radical-mediated nitration reactions while damaging lipid components and cell membrane structures. These effects can further impair mitochondrial function, producing more reactive oxygen species and creating a vicious cycle [32]. Additionally, the nitric oxide (NO) in smoke can react with oxygen radicals to produce nitrated radicals, damaging muscle, nerve, and urothelial structures. When the body’s repair mechanisms cannot compensate, these factors lead to cell necrosis and apoptosis, contributing to the development of UI [33]. Third: impaired pelvic floor muscle function. Research has indicated that being exposed to tobacco smoke can result in impaired function of the skeletal muscles [34]. On the one hand, the constituents of tobacco smoke influence the process of protein synthesis and its functioning, affecting both muscle strength and muscle mass [35]. This observation has also been demonstrated in investigations using human and animal models. Toshiaki Nakatani and colleagues, through studies on rat models exposed to different levels of cigarette smoke, found that after high-dose exposure to cigarette smoke, the cross-sectional area of the soleus and extensor digitorum longus muscle fibers in rats decreased [36, 37]. On the other hand, ETS exposure reduces skeletal muscle fatigue resistance by affecting oxygen delivery, mitochondrial function, and oxidative capacity [38]. Although there is a lack of particular research on the effects of ETS exposure on pelvic floor muscles, it is hypothesized that the various components of tobacco smoke may have a comparable influence on muscle groups in the pelvic floor, such as the urethral sphincter, potentially resulting in the development of UI. Fourth: initiation of the inflammatory response. Prolonged exposure to carbon monoxide (CO) in tobacco smoke results in a condition of reduced oxygen supply (hypoxia) and heightened anaerobic metabolism, which can lead to harm to the endothelial cells, inflammation, and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [39–41]. These factors can further exacerbate vascular atherosclerosis, sensitize bladder afferent nerves, and induce fibrosis [42]. For instance, IL-1β can mediate fibrosis and stimulate smooth muscle proliferation through the serum/glucocorticoid-regulated kinase-activated T cell nuclear factor transcription factor 2 (SGK-NFAT2) signaling pathway, leading to bladder hypertrophy, decreased compliance, and worsening symptoms such as urinary frequency and urgency [43].

In conclusion, exposure to ETS can potentially contribute to the occurrence and advancement of UI by affecting the blood vessels, nerves, muscles, and other associated conditions. In addition, further research is required in the future to investigate the more detailed mechanisms by which it affects UI.

Limitations and advantage

This study extracted nationwide data and incorporated sample weights to form a sample representing the U.S. population. Additionally, the regression analysis included most covariates for adjustment to reduce confounding and subgroup analyses, and interaction tests were conducted to confirm the robustness of the results. Furthermore, PSM was performed based on sample data to further enhance the stability of the findings. However, this study has several limitations. The survey data in this study primarily targets the U.S. population, so further research is needed to evaluate the results for populations in other countries or regions. Also, as a cross-sectional study, it is unable to establish the cause-and-effect relationship between serum cotinine levels and urine incontinence. Environmental-Wide Association Studies (EWAS) is a research method that can effectively describe the associations between exposure groups and diseases. It is hoped that this method will be used in future research to further explain the results. Furthermore, the cotinine concentration was measured using a single serum sample at a one-time point in the NHANES survey, which does not capture the trend of participants’ exposure to smoke. In addition, although many confounding factors were adjusted for in this study, it is still impossible to completely rule out the influence of other variables (such as harmful substances in ETS exposure like perfluoroalkyls, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, phenols, etc., and other prescription drugs). on the results. Finally, several variables in this study were self-reported and collected through questionnaires, raising concerns about recall bias and misclassification bias.

Conclusion

This study indicates a non-linear positive dose-response relationship between serum cotinine levels and UI, and this relationship is also significant in self-reported non-smoking populations. Reducing exposure to environmental tobacco smoke (e.g., avoiding second-hand smoke) in work and daily life may help alleviate the occurrence of UI. Also, serum cotinine levels have the potential to be a tool for predicting the degree of risk of developing UI. The results compensate for previous studies that still need to be validated with larger cohorts.
