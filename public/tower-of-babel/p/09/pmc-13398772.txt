Associations of skeletal muscle mass and strength with arterial stiffness in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Naoki Koike, Yoko Irie, Yuji Doi, Ryosuke Kemmotsu, Moe Kusushita, Yasuna Sakai, Tomonori Okita, Hiroyuki Sho, Ryoko Inui, Yukiko Tabuchi, Tetsuyuki Yasuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSarcopenia is prevalent in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and may contribute to increased cardiovascular risk. However, the associations of skeletal muscle mass and strength with arterial stiffness in T2DM remain unclear. This retrospective cross‐sectional study included 281 hospitalized people with T2DM. Arterial stiffness was assessed by brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), and skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) and handgrip strength (HS) were evaluated. Associations with baPWV were examined using correlation and multivariable linear regression analyses. baPWV was inversely associated with both SMI and HS. In multivariable analyses, lower SMI (β = −0.247, P = 0.009) and lower HS (β = −0.212, P = 0.002) were significantly associated with higher arterial stiffness after adjustment for established cardiovascular risk factors. Both skeletal muscle mass and strength were associated with arterial stiffness in individuals with T2DM. Assessment of both parameters may provide complementary information regarding vascular risk.Keywords: Brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity, Muscle, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Arterial stiffness is a major determinant of cardiovascular disease in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) is a widely used noninvasive measure of arterial stiffness and predicts cardiovascular events in this population 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 .

Sarcopenia‐related traits, including reduced skeletal muscle mass and strength, are highly prevalent in people with T2DM, partly due to impaired insulin action, chronic hyperglycemia, and physical inactivity 5 , 6 , 7 . These abnormalities may have greater clinical relevance in T2DM than in the general population, as they are closely linked to metabolic dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Previous studies have shown that reduced muscle mass or strength is associated with increased cardiovascular risk; however, the underlying vascular mechanisms remain incompletely understood 8 , 9 .

Arterial stiffness may represent an intermediate vascular phenotype linking impaired muscle health to cardiovascular risk. Although several studies have reported associations between skeletal muscle mass or strength and arterial stiffness in general populations 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , data in people with T2DM are limited. In particular, associations of both skeletal muscle mass and muscle strength with arterial stiffness have not been sufficiently investigated within the same T2DM cohort, despite their distinct physiological roles.

Therefore, we investigated the associations of skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) and handgrip strength (HS) with baPWV in hospitalized people with T2DM, evaluating these parameters in separate models because of their strong correlation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This single‐center, retrospective cross‐sectional study included consecutive hospitalized people with T2DM admitted primarily for glycemic management to Osaka International Medical & Science Center (Osaka Keisatsu Hospital) between 2021 and 2024. Eligible patients underwent assessments of body composition and baPWV during hospitalization. Patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus, acute infectious or inflammatory diseases, active malignancy, or current systemic corticosteroid use were excluded. Body composition was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 770; InBody Co., Seoul, Korea). Measurements were performed in the morning under fasting conditions during hospitalization. SMI was calculated as appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by height squared (kg/m2). Muscle strength was evaluated by HS using a handheld dynamometer, and the maximum value was used for analysis. baPWV was measured in the supine position using an automated waveform analyzer (FORM‐5, Fukuda Colin Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and the higher of the two values was used for analysis because arterial stiffness may differ between sides, and the higher value was considered to better reflect vascular impairment.

Clinical and laboratory data, including age, sex, duration of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio [UACR]), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid levels, history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), smoking status, and medication use, were obtained from medical records. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Variables with skewed distributions (duration of diabetes, baPWV, UACR, and triglycerides) were logarithmically transformed before statistical analyses. Pearson's correlation analyses and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed using the log‐transformed values of these variables. In the multivariable analyses, baPWV was used as the dependent variable. Because of potential multicollinearity between SMI and HS, these variables were entered into separate multivariable models. All models were adjusted for clinically relevant covariates, including age, sex, systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), duration of diabetes, smoking status, history of CVD, HbA1c, eGFR, UACR, and BMI.

A two‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Osaka International Medical & Science Center (approval number: 2094) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained using an opt‐out approach, and no written informed consent was required because no patient‐identifiable data were included in this study.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Among the 433 people with T2DM screened during the study period, 281 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. The cohort had a mean age of 64.1 ± 15.1 years, and 69.0% were men. The median duration of diabetes was 11.0 [2.8, 20.0] years, with a mean HbA1c level of 9.9 ± 2.3% and a mean BMI of 25.7 ± 5.2 kg/m2. Seventy‐eight patients (27.8%) had a history of CVD. The mean SMI and HS were 7.0 ± 1.2 kg/m2 and 31.5 ± 9.9 kg, respectively, and the median baPWV was 1730.0 [1443.0, 2069.0] cm/s. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Multivariable analyses

SMI and HS were strongly correlated (r = 0.75), indicating potential multicollinearity; therefore, these variables were entered into separate models. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations of SMI and HS with baPWV (Table 3). In the model including SMI, SMI was significantly associated with baPWV (β = −0.247, P = 0.009) after adjustment. Similarly, HS was significantly associated with baPWV (β = −0.212, P = 0.002) after adjustment for the same covariates.

DISCUSSION

In this cross‐sectional study of hospitalized people with T2DM, we found that both SMI and HS were significantly associated with arterial stiffness assessed by baPWV. These associations remained significant after adjustment for established cardiovascular risk factors, including age, blood pressure, lipid levels, renal function, and glycemic status. Importantly, SMI and HS were evaluated in separate multivariable models to minimize multicollinearity, and both were significantly associated with arterial stiffness after adjustment for established cardiovascular risk factors.

Previous studies in general population settings have reported inverse associations between skeletal muscle mass or strength and arterial stiffness measured by pulse wave velocity 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 . Importantly, we evaluated both skeletal muscle mass and muscle strength within the same T2DM cohort and found that each parameter was significantly associated with arterial stiffness after adjustment for established cardiovascular risk factors. Because skeletal muscle mass and muscle strength reflect distinct aspects of muscle health, assessment of both parameters may provide complementary information regarding vascular risk in individuals with T2DM.

Sarcopenia‐related traits are common in people with T2DM and may contribute to vascular dysfunction through impaired insulin action, chronic hyperglycemia, low‐grade inflammation, and physical inactivity 7 , 14 , 15 . Reduced muscle mass and strength are linked to insulin resistance, which may promote endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffening. Arterial stiffness reflects cumulative vascular damage and precedes overt cardiovascular events and may link skeletal muscle impairment to cardiovascular risk. However, reverse causality, whereby vascular aging contributes to reduced physical function, cannot be excluded. Skeletal muscle may influence vascular function through myokines 16 , 17 , 18 .

Unexpectedly, HbA1c and LDL‐C levels showed inverse associations with baPWV. These findings should be interpreted with caution because patients with higher HbA1c or LDL‐C levels tended to be younger and had a shorter duration of diabetes in our cohort. In addition, treatment intensification, reverse causation, and selection bias may have contributed to these unexpected associations. Additional sensitivity analyses adjusting for GLP‐1 receptor agonist and SGLT2 inhibitor use, as well as analyses excluding users of these agents, yielded similar results to the primary analyses (data not shown). Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, baPWV is influenced by blood pressure and does not directly reflect central arterial stiffness, which should be considered when interpreting the present findings. Second, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Third, the study population consisted of hospitalized Japanese patients with T2DM, which may limit generalizability. Fourth, residual confounding may remain despite multivariable adjustment. Information regarding physical activity, exercise habits, and nutritional status was not available and therefore could not be included in the analyses. Residual confounding related to these factors cannot be excluded. In addition, muscle strength was assessed only by handgrip strength and may not fully represent overall muscle function 19 . Furthermore, because skeletal muscle mass index and handgrip strength were analyzed in separate multivariable models owing to their strong correlation, the present study cannot fully establish the independence of their associations with arterial stiffness.

In conclusion, both skeletal muscle mass and muscle strength were associated with arterial stiffness in hospitalized individuals with T2DM. These findings suggest that arterial stiffness may be involved in the relationship between skeletal muscle health and cardiovascular risk in T2DM. Assessment of both parameters may provide complementary information regarding vascular risk. Future prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings, clarify underlying mechanisms, and evaluate their generalizability to broader T2DM populations.
