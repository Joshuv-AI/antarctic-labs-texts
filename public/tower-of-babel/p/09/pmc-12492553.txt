Effect of constructivist teaching in the improvement of midwives’ interpretation ability in fetal monitor graphs
Authors: Zhuli Chen, Qiang Yao, Ming Huang, Guoyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

For the content that needed to be taught emphatically, 87.88% considered typical graph analysis the most important content. Background

Safety of mother and baby is the primary goal of obstetricians and midwives. Accurate interpretation of fetal monitoring results is particularly significant in dealing with safety issues for mother and baby. The “WHO recommendations: Intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience” advocates the use of fetal heart monitoring to evaluate fetal status [1].

Electronic fetal monitoring (EFM), also known as cardiotocography, is used to continuously monitor the fetal heart rate and uterine pressure changes. As the most routine and important means of assessing fetal status during delivery, fetal monitoring aims to detect potential fetal hypoxia during labor so that timely interventions can be taken to reduce perinatal or neonatal mortality. In clinical practice, regulating the application of EFM and accurately interpreting the fetal monitor graphs are major focuses. Byford et al. [2] found that the incidence of hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy reduced significantly between 2003 and 2011 during the implementation of the education program in fetal surveillance monitoring. Education concerning fetal monitoring should be available to all personnel involved in intrapartum care and should be repeated regularly in order to sustain such improvement.

Previous studies have shown that the interpretation of EFM results is quite different among the observers [3, 4]. These differences are mainly in the identification and classification of deceleration, evaluation of variability, and classification of suspicious and abnormal EFM results [5]. Fetal monitoring education is particularly important. Providing obstetrics department staff the training concerning the use of EFM can improve the accuracy and reliability of their interpretation of EFM results. Obstetrics department staff who receive the training in interpretation of EFM results are more likely to accurately identify fetal distress and make appropriate clinical decisions. This can improve outcomes for both mother and baby, including reduced incidences of caesarean section, and neonatal morbidity and mortality. One of the limitations of EFM is that it can create false alarms, or misconceptions about fetal distress, leading to unnecessary interventions such as caesarean section or instrumental delivery [6, 7]. Providing the training can also help reduce the risk of unnecessary interventions. There are different education programs on fetal monitoring and the education effects are unsatisfactory [8, 9]. Phased learning is not enough to deal with the actual clinical work [10].

Constructivism, first proposed by Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget, is a theory emphasizing the initiative of learners. Constructivism proposes that learning is a process in which learners generate meaning and constructivist understanding based on original knowledge and experience, and this process is often completed in social and cultural interaction. In ancient times, philosophers such as Socrates, Aristotle, and Confucius proposed the prototype of the constructivist teaching [11], and then the constructivist teaching gradually developed and improved. Constructivist teaching emphasizes student-centered. A constructivist teacher serves as an organizer, instructor, helper, and facilitator during the teaching process. By using scenarios, collaborations, and conversations, the teacher could inspire students’ initiative, enthusiasm, and pioneering spirit. This could enable students to effectively construct knowledge they have learned.

This study hypothesized that midwives’ ability to interpret the EFM results could be improved by means of constructivist teaching. This study aims to investigate the effect of constructivist teaching in the improvement of midwives’ interpretation ability regarding fetal monitor graphs. This study can provide empirical support for the theoretical teaching of fetal monitoring by midwives, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of fetal monitoring interpretation.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All research methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University [2024 Medical Scientific Research for Ethical Approval No. (098)]. Informed consent signature was waived upon the approval of the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University.

Verbally informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from all participants.

Study design

A total of 66 midwives who received the midwifery training from our hospital participated in this study during March 2023 to March 2024. Midwives who met the following criteria were included in this study: (1) had completed the 3 months of midwifery training provided by our hospital; and (2) had no language communication barriers. Midwives who did not consent to participant in this study or who did not consent to receive the training were excluded from this study.

The 66 midwives came from different hospitals in China and possessed different levels of educational attainment and working years. In constructivist teaching, the 66 midwives were divided into 33 groups and each group consisted of 2 persons. The test results before and after the constructivist teaching were collected and analyzed.

Pre-training and post-training assessments were conducted on the 66 midwives. According to the expert consensus on the interpretation of EFM results, a two class hours of theoretical training was provided offline by a professor (a corresponding author in this study) based on the 7 key points for fetal monitoring: (1) risk factors for the pregnant woman; (2) uterine contractions: excessively frequent or excessively rare; (3) baseline fetal heart rate; (4) fetal heart rate variability; (5) fetal heart rate accelerations; (6) fetal heart rate decelerations; and (7) comprehensive evaluation (correctly identifying the fetal heart rate tracings which are classified as categories I, II, and III). A total of 8 fetal monitoring graphs from clinical practice were collected as teaching cases, and the interpretation opinions of professors in the obstetrics department of our hospital on each collected fetal monitoring graph were considered as the identification standard of the graphs. Before the training, participants discussed the 8 fetal monitoring graphs and recorded the results of the discussion in the pre-training assessment form. After the theoretical training, the participants interpreted the same 8 graphs and provided their decisions on the fetal monitoring graphs in the post-training assessment form. The interpretation results of the participants before and after the training were analyzed.

This study is hypothesis-driven research. It hypothesized that constructivist teaching has a good effect in fetal monitoring training. A questionnaire designed by the researchers was used to investigate the midwives’ understanding and weakness regarding fetal monitoring knowledge and skills (Supplementary file 1). The questionnaire survey was conducted 1 month after the training was completed. The questionnaire was produced by Wenjuanxing, an online questionnaire survey platform, and is divided into 3 parts: (1) The training provided by their respective employers, including the content, frequency and method of training; (2) Fetal monitoring knowledge and skills. Items in this part were designed based on literature review, fetal monitoring education content and the researchers’ clinical and teaching experience. There are 17 items in this part, all of which concerned basic knowledge. There are 14 single-answer questions (maximum 5 points per item) and 3 multiple-answer questions (maximum 10 points per item). The questions were pre-tested in our obstetrics department; and (3) Expected method of fetal monitoring training, including expected frequency, content, and electronic training plan.

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 software was used for data analysis. For paired data that was not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed for comparison. The data was expressed as the median (25th percentile, 75th percentile). The paired sample t-test was used to compare the measurement data between groups. A statistically significant difference was identified by P < 0.05.

Teaching effects

Table 4 shows that the statistically significant differences were found in the scores for identification of high-risk factors, interpretation of uterine contractions, interpretation of baseline fetal heart rate, interpretation of fetal heart rate variability, interpretation of fetal heart rate decelerations, and comprehensive evaluation for fetal monitoring training between pre- and post-training (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found in the scores for interpretation of fetal heart rate accelerations (P > 0.05). This shows that constructivist teaching had positive effects in the improvement of the midwives’ interpretation ability in uterine contractions, baseline fetal heart rate, fetal heart rate variability, fetal heart rate decelerations, and comprehensive evaluation, but had no significant improvement in interpretation of fetal heart rate accelerations.

Expectations on the method of training concerning fetal monitoring

Details of the participants’ expectations on the method of training concerning fetal monitoring are shown in Table 5. In fetal monitoring training, different respondents had different expectations on the frequency, content, benefit and function of training. For training frequency, most respondents hoped to receive the fetal monitoring training once every 6 months, accounting for 83.33%. However, a small number of respondents wanted to be trained once or twice a year. For training content, most respondents wanted to receive training on typical graph analysis, accounting for 75.75%. More than 70% of respondents wanted to know about Chinese and foreign guidelines, expert consensus and cutting-edge information. However, there was a low demand for training in basic concepts and principles. In terms of training benefit, most respondents believed that typical graph analysis and exercise were the biggest gains. In addition, the basic concepts and basic principles were also the most rewarding part. However, the sense of benefit from cutting-edge information was low. Cutting-edge information includes the development and application of artificial intelligence and machine learning in EFM [12]. Evans et al. [13] have developed the fetal reserve index (FRI) to interpret the EFM results. However, it might be difficult for study participants to understand. Among the content that needed to be taught emphatically, typical graph analysis and exercises were considered to be the most important content. Basic concepts and principles were also important to learn. More than 60% of respondents believed that cutting-edge information should also be taught as a priority. For the functional requirements of small programs concerning fetal monitoring training, respondents generally hoped that it should have a variety of functions. Interpretation of Chinese and foreign guidelines, theoretical learning, lectures, theoretical assessment, interpretation of fetal monitoring graphs, and question bank of case analysis of fetal monitoring received higher expectations.

Discussion

In this study, constructivist teaching was applied to the EFM training for midwives. It attached importance to learners’ cumulative learning on the basis of existing knowledge, the play of learners’ subjective initiative, learners’ differences, knowledge transfer in different situations, and reflective learning. Constructivist teaching was suitable for EFM training for midwives.

The mean scores received by study participants in the training assessments rose from 62.35 ± 8.33 (pre-training) to 71.36 ± 8.57 (post-training). However, a single training session could not reach 100% mastery rate. A total of 4 questions in the post-training assessment received results lower than 50%. These 4 questions involved definition of non-stress test and oxytocin challenge test, items of fetal biophysical score, acceleration of fetal heart rate after non-term pregnancy movement, and placement of the fetal probe. The defects in mastery of the basic knowledge of fetal monitoring was obvious. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen basic knowledge training to consolidate the foundation of knowledge.

In terms of the method and content of fetal monitoring training, lectures, classroom learning and case analysis are the main training methods. A randomized controlled trial study showed that attending a short (1.5 h) in-service fetal monitoring training improved midwives’ knowledge and interpreting skills of fetal monitoring. The provision of in-service fetal monitoring education courses is sporadic and often less than recommended, which requires attention [14]. In our study, the participants believed that they gained the most from the training content concerning typical graph analysis, basic principles, and cutting-edge information. They felt they received insufficient fetal monitoring training from their respective employer. For example, they received little training content on cutting-edge information and physiological knowledge of fetal monitoring. It is advised to increase the fetal monitoring training, concerning fetal physiological knowledge, to increase the depth of their understanding of fetal monitoring [15, 16], thereby reducing the incidence of perinatal complications. The training concerning cutting-edge information of fetal monitoring should also be strengthened in order to expand the breadth of knowledge.

Ugwumadu et al. [8] believe that the mastery of basic knowledge of EFM (normal baseline rate, variability, acceleration, active/quiet sleep cycle, no deceleration, and correct calculation of contraction frequency) should be as high as 100%. Clinical obstetrics staff shall undergo regular training and assessment concerning fetal monitoring. In terms of training, obstetrical care professionals should focus on identifying and responding to normal and abnormal EFM results, including the role of prenatal factors associated with adverse perinatal outcomes. Those who fail to achieve a 100% of mastery level should receive mandatory retraining and be retested at appropriate intervals, and should not work independently in obstetric wards. The results of Beckley et al. [17] showed that obstetricians could not answer approximately 20% of the questions in the questionnaire survey, even after receiving specially developed computer-based training and 30% of the questions put forward by midwifes concerned the interpretation of EFM results. The results of our study are consistent with the findings of Beckley et al. [17]. This may indicate that some midwives lack the ability to identify abnormal fetal monitoring graph results, which may be related to the training content and methods. Fetal monitoring training is the most commonly used intervention to improve the interpretation of EFM results [18, 19]. As the implementer and observer of EFM, midwives’ ability to identify the abnormal EFM results plays a significant role in ensuring the safety of mother and baby. Therefore, fetal monitoring training should be strengthened in the education of midwives.

Training programs for midwives using EFM are critical to ensure the effective and safe use of the EFM. These programs typically include teaching, practice, and ongoing assessment and feedback. Lectures may include topics concerning birth physiology, fetal heart rate patterns, and interpretation of the EFM tracking. Practice can include simulation scenarios for healthcare staff to practice interpreting fetal monitoring graphs, and making clinical decisions based on the data. Ongoing evaluation and feedback can include regular assessment of the fetal monitoring skills of the midwives and providing feedback and coaching to improve their work performance.

This is an innovative empirical study combining constructivist teaching with conventional teaching methods. The results of this study indicates that constructivist teaching is applicable for training concerning fetal monitoring. However, this study has some limitations. First, the sample size of this study was not large. Sample size can be increased in future studies to obtain more reliable data. Second, post-training data was collected shortly after the training was completed. There were no evaluations of long-term clinical behaviors or effect tracking. Third, the 66 midwives in this study came from different hospitals and had different educational attainments and working years. The collected data was anonymous. The assessment results before and after the training were analyzed, but factors that might be associated with the differences in assessment results, such as size and level of hospital, work experience, and educational attainment, were not investigated. Future studies can investigate the influence of these factors on learning and testing results.

Conclusions

Based on a questionnaire survey, this study revealed the urgent need for fetal monitoring training in medical education, especially for correct interpretation of typical graphs and practical manipulations. As medical technology continues to advance, attention to cutting-edge advances continues to increase. The design and implementation of fetal monitoring training should focus on improvement of practical manipulation skills, combined with the analysis of typical graphs, in order to improve the trainees’ interpretation and analysis abilities. The mini program of fetal monitoring training should have a variety of functions to adapt to personalized learning demands of different trainees.

This study explored the effective training model of fetal monitoring from the perspective of midwives. After investigating the frequency, content and method of training, we put forward a reference scheme suitable for China’s fetal monitoring training. The constructivist teaching encourages students to take the initiative to raise questions in the learning process, stimulating their subjective initiative. The results of the assessments before and after the training have shown that constructivist teaching has a significant effect on improving midwives’ interpretation ability of fetal monitor graphs. This innovative training method provides a new way of training in medical education and demonstrates its great potential for improving the health of mother and baby.

Based on the results of empirical research, we firmly believe that constructivist teaching has significant advantages in fetal monitoring training. We will continue to promote constructivist teaching to improve midwives’ professional knowledge and skills. We also hope midwifery education can attract more attention and contribute to the improvement of the health of mother and baby.
