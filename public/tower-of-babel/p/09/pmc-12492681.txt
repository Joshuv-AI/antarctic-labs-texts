The sustainability of practice-based research networks across the globe – insights from a worldwide qualitative study
Authors: Anna Dania, Zsolt Nagykaldi, Ari Haaranen, Jean W. M. Muris, Philip H. Evans, Pekka Mäntyselkä, Chris van Weel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPBRNs emerged from partnerships between academics and primary care practitioners and functioned as primary care “laboratories”. In two previous scoping literature reviews, we presented the facilitators and barriers to building PBRNs linked to their internal and external environments. This article presents key insights from interviews with PBRN leaders worldwide about the sustainability of their networks.MethodsWe used the consultation exercise component of the scoping review methodology to generate complementary/additional results to our previous studies. We conducted 56 semi-structured interviews with a purposive sample of PBRN leaders using the contact information included in our earlier scoping reviews. We then expanded the sample to achieve balance and saturation in terms of PBRN developmental stage maturity, structure, focus, governance and involvement of other stakeholders. We applied inductive thematic analysis to 55 interviews (one was inaudible) and derived key elements, subthemes, and main themes from the codes.ResultsThe overarching thematic framework yielded 4 main themes, 18 subthemes, and 72 key elements. Main themes were Internal Environment, Stakeholders at the Intersection between the Internal and External Environment, the Impact and Value of PBRNs, and the External Environment.We found that PBRN leaders associated network sustainability with sufficient infrastructural support, collaborative relationships, a learning team environment culture, and agile management. They also considered that support and advocacy from organisations with global influence was essential for their sustainability. They also indicated that PBRNs have a positive impact on academic faculty, clinician education, primary care practice, community health and healthcare policy.ConclusionWhile PBRNs have found various ways to develop and thrive and have influenced the primary care field, there remains a strong need for strategic development of network relationships, research portfolios, stable infrastructure support and intensified advocacy to further consolidate their role within the broader healthcare landscape nationally and globally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13159-w.

Background

Practice-Based Research Networks (PBRNs) are research “laboratories” of primary care [1, 2]. They emerged from collaborations between academics and practitioners in the field, working together to generate and implement practice-based evidence, shared learning, and quality improvement in primary care [2–6].

The roots of practice-based research in family medicine trace back to the late eighteenth century (1788–1791) when Edward Jenner and other generalists linked early causes to later health effects through patient observations. Nearly a century later, before formal colleges and research institutes existed, pioneers like James Mackenzie and William Pickles in the UK at the end of nineteenth century, followed by John Fry (UK), F.J.A. Huygen (The Netherlands), and Curtis G. Hames (US) in the mid-twentieth century, demonstrated that practicing family physicians could produce valuable scientific insights into disease and treatment through rigorous observation and research [2, 7].

PBRNs emerged and co-evolved with the discipline of family medicine/general practice over five decades ago. In the UK, general practitioners in Birmingham began collecting morbidity data, leading to the establishment of systematic recording in 1966/67. This initiative was formally named the Weekly Returns Service (WRS) in 1966/67 and continues to operate today [8]. In the Netherlands, Huygen and colleagues established the CMR (Continuous Morbidity Registration) in Nijmegen to study the morbidity of their patients [9]. The network is now integrated into the FaMe-net PBRN [10]. In the US, the FMIS [11], COOP [12], and ASPN PBRNs emerged in the late 1970 s to study health problems common in primary care. The latter two PBRNs are still active—ASPN evolved into the AAFP NRN [13, 14] and later DARTNet [15]. Examples of notable early network studies include the WRS study on the incidence of common infectious diseases in 1985 [16]; the 22-year cohort study of CMR on cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in patients with type-2 diabetes in 1995, among the first to report increased mortality and reduced life expectancy in primary care [17]; and the ASPN research on common primary care conditions and dissemination of findings to enhance practitioner practice, reported in 1989 [18].

Since these initial efforts, research-motivated physicians, academics and researchers have collaborated to build the evidence base of family medicine and transform primary healthcare, through findings that are directly relevant to clinicians and can be integrated into practice. PBRNs further this mission by fostering relationships of trust, collegiality and reciprocity while developing infrastructure, databases, and a learning environment. Their ecosystem is a hub for communication, interaction, and knowledge sharing and accelerates the implementation of innovation through a collaborative, adaptive, community-centered milieu. PBRNs employ a wide variety of research methodologies, including surveys, observational studies, pragmatic randomized controlled trials, continuous quality improvement, and participatory methods [19, 20].

Their research began historically with the causes of common problems in primary care and population health, and later expanded into practice improvement, comparative effectiveness, patient-centered outcomes, and health policy reform. Throughout their journey, they creatively combined research methods with theoretical frameworks to improve research and the implementation and translation of research. Their ties to primary care practices and the communities they serve enable them to translate clinical research findings into practice like no other, including phase II and III clinical trials and observational studies. To accomplish this, they use systematic approaches to quality improvement, test dissemination and implementation outcomes, and facilitate the integration of state-of-the-art practices into healthcare policy [21].

Over the past 50 years, PBRNs have evolved globally and have become a cornerstone of primary care research in various health care systems by providing access to practices and data [19, 22]. This has led to supportive policies and growth, with about 130 active networks in the US by 2024 and 18 in Australia by 2021 [23, 24]. However, most countries lack stable infrastructural support for PBRNs, as researchers face limited awareness and funding from academic and research bodies. Consequently, their development has often depended on trial and error and accumulated experience, rather than on business or strategic planning, which would entail a clear allocation of resources and could promote organizational sustainability [2, 5, 25–27]. This study builds on previous research that highlights the central role of PBRNs in advancing primary care research worldwide and seeks to clarify the resources needed to sustain them, given their established importance to primary care [19, 22].

In two previous scoping literature reviews we presented the facilitators and barriers of building PBRNs, linked to their internal and external environment. These scoping reviews yielded a substantial body of knowledge about the establishment phase of networks [19, 22]. However, fewer studies provided information about the sustainability of PBRNs, especially on a global scale.

The aim of this study was to explore how PBRN leaders operated in various countries to make their networks more sustainable. This article summarises the most prominent lessons learned from interviewing PBRN leaders worldwide, focusing on approaches for sustaining their networks, through a qualitative study using thematic analysis.

Methods

Our study leveraged the consultation exercise component of scoping review methodology [28]. Two authors, (AD) and (ZN), developed a guide for participant interviews based on previous findings from our scoping reviews (Additional file 1). The interview topics were related to the sustainability of PBRNs, but we also left room for emergent information.

We initially developed a purposeful sample of 34 PBRN leaders identified from publications included in our scoping reviews [19, 22], aligned with the geographic distribution of PBRNs in those reviews. We later added nine interviewees when needed to capture insights on the development of long-standing and/or transformed networks of this sample. As interviews progressed, it became evident that a broader sample was needed to achieve balance and saturation regarding aspects such as development stage, maturity (e.g., capacity for sophisticated research), sustainability, structure (e.g., network of PBRNs), focus (e.g., sentinel surveillance, health disparities), governance type (e.g., whole system approach, top-down), stakeholder engagement (e.g., community groups), and EHR data use/management. The co-authors agreed to continue expanding the sample as long as new insights emerged. Ultimately, this approach yielded 56 semi-structured interviews with PBRN leaders and, in a few cases, coordinators, out of 57 individuals invited (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Chart flow diagram of the recruitment of interviewees. Participants were invited via email. The interviews were conducted on Skype between 4/25/2018 and 12/4/2018, and their length varied between 30 and 128 min. Video calls were recorded twice, including a digital audio recorder, to minimize the risk of unrecorded or poorly recorded sections. Nevertheless, among the 56 interviews, one interview recording was inaudible and therefore excluded. This resulted in a final sample of 55 interviews. These 55 interviews were professionally transcribed by the firm “Sterling Transcription” (www.sterlingtranscription.co.uk) in the UK and reviewed for minor discrepancies by two authors (AD and AH).

All participants consented verbally to the interview and their consent was recorded. All activities related to the acquisition and management of data from human subjects complied with the directives of the EU General Data Protection Regulation (2016/679) [29]. We separated interview data from identifiers using participant codes and implemented standard data storage in protected files and access safety protocols.

Through an iterative process, all co-authors identified key segments of the interview texts related to the sustainability, continuity, and evolution of each network. Differences in interpretation regarding meaning/content and classification were resolved by consensus or by seeking the opinion of another co-author. Subsequently, two researchers (AD) and (AH) applied inductive thematic analysis to derive key elements, subthemes, and main themes from the codes using NVivo (Version 12) and agreed on their grouping based on consensus with a third (ZN). Through implementing this researcher triangulation, we reduced bias risks and enhanced the validity of our findings.

The coding process we applied included the following steps: Gathering original narratives from the interviews—> Structuring and grouping collected narratives—> Developing components (e.g., “Research integration in GPs/FPs training curriculum”)- > Grouping components—> Developing key elements from groups of relevant components (e.g., “Reorientation of FP/GP education and professional competencies: Trainees and residents engaging in PBRN research”)- > Grouping key elements—> Developing subthemes from groups of relevant key elements (e.g., “Impact on clinician education and skills”)—> Grouping subthemes—> Synthesising main themes (e.g.,“ Impact and value of PBRNs”).

As this paper complements our previously published two scoping reviews [19, 22], we applied a similar typology for the main themes, subthemes and key elements when the content of the codes was identical, while we applied new codes to what emerged as new concepts. The overarching thematic structure of this study and those of prior reviews are available in Additional file 2, Table 1.

Results

We analysed 55 in-depth semi-structured interviews covering 50 PBRNs, including 17 in the USA, 5 in Canada, 3 in the Netherlands, 10 in the UK, 7 in Australia, 2 in France, and one in each of the following countries: Singapore, Spain, Italy, Switzerland, Norway, and Belgium. The networks were at different stages of evolution and maturity. Our analysis yielded 4 themes, 18 subthemes, and 72 key elements (see Table 1).

We found the following main themes that included subthemes, and key elements that also included facilitators and barriers for the sustainability of PBRNs: Internal Environment, Stakeholders at the Intersection between the Internal and External Environment, Impact and Value of PBRNs, and External Environment. We derived qualitative codes and presented them by grouping the most interconnected codes to enhance clarity, avoid redundancy, and to help readers appreciate the strength of connections that were established in our analysis. Internal environment

Here, we present the information gathered from the subthemes Academic participation and attitudes, Practitioner contribution and motivation, and Network infrastructure and management. Each of these parts also includes insights from the subtheme Evolution, which includes factors that have influenced sustainable development and the characteristics of the evolving environment of PBRNs, which interface with most of the key elements found in our analysis. In this subsection, we presented the key elements of Health information technology (HIT) use and health data management, which are part of the main theme of Internal Environment, alongside with the key elements HIT and HIT vendors and Regulatory environment. Although the latter two typically belong to the main theme External Environment, we included them here to avoid repetition, as they share interconnected aspects with those of HIT use and health data management.

Academic participation, attitudes and benefits

Most interviewees emphasized the strong organisational ties between PBRNs and an academic faculty that supported practice-based research and the sustainability of PBRNs. Academics actively participated in various committees, provided fundraising and infrastructural support to the networks, and fostered collaboration with other healthcare stakeholders. They also transferred expertise to non-academic members by cultivating a research-oriented culture and promoting quality improvement (QI) in practice. PBRNs effectively functioned as ‘research laboratories’ for family medicine/primary care. They sometimes developed into research hubs for faculty members within the PBRN, but also for other academics, generating scholarly output for researchers and the field, and bridging the gap between academic research and practice. Most prominently, shifts in faculty priorities and financial support challenged academic participation.“The university setting of primary healthcare has worked hand in glove with the College of General Practitioners in developing primary healthcare. So, being a university related network gave it a lot of visibility. It became very quickly the reference base of what family medicine is all about, what the role of primary healthcare is, what the health problems were, so that the network and its data became, in that way, a reference base, and that had an important - that was an important factor in leadership […] the leadership was a reference base and a leadership in research.”

Practitioner attitudes and motivation

Statements from PBRN leader’s indicated that the most motivating factors for practitioners to engage were both intrinsic motivation to be part of a greater effort to improve patient care and the altruistic nature of the discipline. Furthermore, active participation was nurtured primarily by the relationships built within networks (e.g., collegiality, mutuality), and communication intertwined with learning experience and a range of QI measurement and implementation activities, (e.g., intellectual exchange, learning communities, capacity-building activities, and convocations). The assurance of readily accessible expertise and shared resources, which practices found helpful, even when addressing non-research-related problems, was equally important. Involving the practitioners in academic discussions about research and implementation was considered a very strong motivating factor, as this learning environment led to studies relevant to practice needs, innovation, and QI in patient care. This resulted in improved skills, job satisfaction, and professional development of those aspiring to become researchers. Recognized professional development and QI audit cycles were two components that intersected with intrinsic motivation and reciprocal benefits. Moderate monetary compensation for research activities was associated with acknowledging the value of practitioner contributions. Member discouragement stemmed from disrupted or limited communication with practitioners, particularly regarding research agenda and progress. It also arose from a singular interest in scholarly output, contributing to negative practitioner attitudes and burnout.

Clinician researchers, clinician champions, practice facilitators and liaisons, and a core of research engaged-committed practitioners, or in some cases research-designated practices, also helped maintain sustainability.

Various agendas needed to be aligned with the networks’ mission and values, such as patient-centered, community-driven, data-driven, and other healthcare stakeholder-driven agendas. In these cases, networks engaged their stakeholders in advisory roles in committees and boards that were vital for achieving their goals. Many interviewees also reported distinct organizational foci on Electronic Health Record (EHR) data, QI audits, clinical trials, shared-learning, or emphasized QI in care.

Challenges of PBRN governance and management included conflicting research interests, turnover within practices requiring continuous capacity building and recruitment efforts, and communication problems with practices, especially in large networks.

Aligning the network's focus with the changing healthcare landscape (e.g., national healthcare system, demographic shifts and population health needs) and financial resources (e.g., funder interests), and reorganizing governance and research capacity to accommodate these changes were deemed highly important, albeit challenging.“An important part of the success is very pragmatic interaction with how society develops, how healthcare develops, how research develops.”

HIT use and health data management, regulatory environment, HIT vendors

Data gathered by PBRNs were used for analysis, research, teaching, and care management. Recording and pooling data from network practices facilitated data-driven business models, QI audits, learning health systems, and collaborations with other PBRNs.

In some PBRNs, network sustainability was found to be supported when their operations were augmented by collaborations with HIT vendors and improved EHR software. In this context, technological solutions were developed, such as data coding, recording/registration, standardization, connectivity, extraction, aggregation, creation of queries for research, and matching data from multiple sources.

PBRN-engaged software vendors tended to offer tools for clinical decision-support, QI and learning activities, and data extraction. Automated data extraction typically reduced practitioner research burden but, in some cases, also their personal engagement in research studies. The involvement of HIT vendors, as key stakeholders, posed some challenges to networks, including high data extraction costs and increased workload for practitioners due to complicated EHR software that hampered research engagement.

In addition, networks faced challenges with health data regulations, which sometimes hindered the use of large datasets due to concerns about patient privacy and confidentiality. We have also identified contested data ownership across practitioners, researchers, EHR vendors, and the national government as a limiting factor. PBRN leaders frequently emphasized the value of methodological solutions to overcome these barriers.

Quality of relationships, communication, and learning environment for PBRN stakeholders

Collaboration emerged as a core value across almost all PBRNs. Sustainable networks incorporated “well-connected people who are like-minded and well-liked.” Interviewees expressed that motivation to stay engaged stemmed from the cultivation of personal synergies and collaborative relationships based on mutual respect, trust, and goodwill, which supported team spirit and respected everyone’s opinion.“Collaboration is really about synergy and it really makes a huge difference. I think, as I look back, the main thing was the synergy that we developed […] to work together to produce something that was really core.”

“Collaboration is really about synergy and it really makes a huge difference. I think, as I look back, the main thing was the synergy that we developed […] to work together to produce something that was really core.”

Emphasizing inclusivity and fostering learning communities were considered crucial to achieving an overall improvement in care quality and had a tangible impact on network sustainability. Building learning communities and providing continuous education formed the core mission of many networks. While learning communities appeared to elicit greater motivation than formal training, PBRN leaders underscored the importance of accessible training opportunities for all members. Maintaining flexibility in research engagement was also vital.

Training and empowering the practitioners enhanced their experience and their networks’ research and fostered the development of clinician-researchers, research champions, and research-oriented clinicians who substantially contributed to their network’s longevity.

Communication strategies within successful networks often included a blend of in-person meetings and asynchronous exchange, which were enhanced through strategic use of venues and events. These promoted the maintenance of relationships and learning environments through training, insightful sharing, reflection on research, and governance.

Depending on the network's context or trajectory, this shared learning approach extended to trainees and residents, patients, and other stakeholder groups. Financial sustainability and business models

Networks received financial support from a variety of sources, often from an array of sources per network. Academic and professional organisations provided directed contributions and various external stakeholders also funded projects that were aligned with their interests. Increased research capacity and conducting research that was relevant to real-world problems in practice, contributed to their recognition and success with research grants and the overall sustainability of the network. However, overwhelming reliance on grant-based funding tended to pose a risk to the sustainability of the network’s infrastructure, operations, member and research participant incentives, learning and skill-building activities, and overall continuity.

These challenges contributed to the development of two kinds of network business models that helped PBRNs find new funding opportunities: data-driven and capacity-driven. Data-driven approaches often utilized “big data” or the reuse of data for multiple studies. Capacity-driven approaches focused on various types of lending the network’s expertise or leveraging specialized knowledge or resources for external projects. Other models focused on network revenue from research, training, QI grants, commercialization of network products, special agreements with academic faculty, and financial support from industry partnerships. Additional models stemmed from collaborations with research-oriented institutions (e.g., Clinical and Translational Science Awards), hospitals, and community partnerships.

Stakeholders at the intersection between the internal and external environment

In this section of the results, we present the information gathered from the subthemes Engagement of patient and community stakeholders and Other healthcare stakeholders. These two distinct groups lie at the intersection of the internal and external environments and, through their engagement, influence the activities of the networks.

Interview participants highlighted a noticeable and gradual shift in research culture after the 2000s toward increasing appreciation of patient and community groups as active research partners. These partnerships were instrumental to remove the barriers between community stakeholders and clinicians and helped bridge the gap between academia and the community. Patient/community-relevant research approaches enriched research outcomes and promoted responsive patient/community-centered care improvement in practices, bolstering the impact and sustainability of PBRNs.“PBRNs I think have always been interested in doing patient-centered research, and patient centeredness, and patient-centered research, and community-based participatory research have been around […] before so it's not a new idea.”

“PBRNs I think have always been interested in doing patient-centered research, and patient centeredness, and patient-centered research, and community-based participatory research have been around […] before so it's not a new idea.”

Building sustainable relationships with these groups was based on transparency and trust, supported by training and empowerment of community stakeholders, and in some cases compensation for significant contributions, which, in turn, promoted the exchange of ideas, knowledge, and innovations within the network's internal and external learning environment.

PBRNs engaged in reciprocal partnerships with a diverse range of stakeholders, including private health entities and hospital systems, government agencies, community health organizations, educational institutions, and professional bodies. These partnerships were often created around governance, research agenda or project-specific collaborations, and derived value from integrating diverse stakeholder perspectives to amplify project success.

In some cases, policymakers and funders worked with networks to shape the PBRN research agenda, albeit in a variably collaborative manner, depending on their priorities. These push–pull interactions yielded not only research support but, in some cases, infrastructural funding pivotal for the sustainability of PBRNs as well. Various challenges arose when research trends, shaped by policymakers or funders, moved scholarly activity in directions less applicable to primary care settings. Several leaders shared the opinion that policymakers did not always develop a continuing relationship with the networks and that some funders did not prioritise primary care research. Nevertheless, the importance of these collaborations was acknowledged, even without direct engagement, and leaders stressed the need for advocacy to ensure that funders and policymakers recognise the value of PBRN research to drive practice- and community-level change. A gradual increase in collaborations with these stakeholders was a distinctive sign of the developmental trajectory of PBRNs.

External environment

In this section of the results, we present the information yielded from the subthemes Overall academic and research culture in family medicine in each country, The national healthcare landscape, institutional/governmental support, national/state policy and external funders, Professional organisations, Relationships and collaborations with other networks. The subtheme HIT and HIT vendors and the key element Regulatory environment have been presented alongside the key element HIT use and health data management, as explained above.

Relationships and collaborations with other networks

Our interviewees suggested that sustainable PBRNs were viewed increasingly as exemplars by newer networks. In addition, sustainable networks utilised their experience to establish independent or interconnected networks or networks of PBRNs. A movement to build inter-PBRN partnerships seemed to have resulted in increased research capacity, the development of diverse datasets, stronger and more varied research portfolios, greater replicability, generalizability and validity of results, more enhanced research skills, and a broader sharing of knowledge and resources. These higher-level collaborations served as pathways to sustainability for the partnering networks and became an alternative or complementary to the network’s internal expansion and were indicative of the evolutionary trajectory of PBRNs.“For us, the growth strategy is more in networking with successful networks, rather than trying to increase the number of sites that we recruit.”

“For us, the growth strategy is more in networking with successful networks, rather than trying to increase the number of sites that we recruit.”

They also acted as power-multipliers that enhanced the research track record and reputation of member networks and contributed to advocacy and influencing health policy. However, these meta-networks required more robust infrastructural investments, more sophisticated management, and a diversified research and innovation portfolio.

PBRN leaders often stressed the importance of creating learning communities among PBRNs, fostering learning experiences at the individual and network levels that underpinned PBRN sustainability by generating more practice-relevant knowledge and resources in member practices. However, they also underlined the importance of maintaining smaller networks that remain connected with health challenges in their local communities.

Institutional/governmental support, the national/state policy, national healthcare landscape, and overall academic and research culture in family medicine in each country

Longstanding and sustainable networks developed strategic ties to national-level research stakeholders and established a mature standing in the national healthcare context. They secured governmental support directly or through national initiatives relevant to their missions and established a research culture in family medicine/primary care that advanced the culture of networking. Invariably, improvements in the national healthcare system structures, institutionalised research education of GPs/FPs, and a cultural shift toward recognizing the value of family medicine and primary care research across the broader academic community were also considered facilitators.

Limited appreciation of the value of primary care and research within academic and healthcare cultures, coupled with competing priorities for funding within these contexts, were identified as barriers hindering the progress of PBRNs and synergies with them. As mentioned earlier, PBRN leaders underscored the need for advocacy that emphasizes the value of PBRNs in primary care to stakeholders nationally and also within academia, where PBRN research is still not fully understood or appreciated.

Professional organisations: support and expectations

Many of our informants stated that PBRNs found notable support for their sustainability in professional organisations, which were often affiliated with specific networks or incorporated them as sub-divisions, aligned with their overall mission. This type of strong organisational support helped networks gain recognition and trust, which enabled their sustainable continuity. Professional groups often endorsed networks, elicited member involvement, built trust among its members in PBRN activity, provided human and financial resources, and influenced the research agenda. Discussion

In this article, we present insights from the evaluation of 55 interviews with PBRN leaders around the world, aiming to complement our previous literature reviews [19, 22] with newly identified key elements related to the long-term sustainability of networks. To our knowledge, this is the first study that investigated the experience of PBRN leaders around the world in order to determine what promotes or hinders the sustainability of their networks.

Our study confirmed the importance of certain key elements crucial for both the establishment and long-term sustainability of PBRNs. Additionally, we identified novel elements specifically tied to the long-term viability of PBRNs. Key elements presented here were distilled from the most significant topics identified in the narratives of our interviews. These lessons may be valuable to networks in countries with a long PBRN history but also to those regions that have less developed primary care research and PBRNs. Our extensive and also geographically-wide qualitative inquiry found that the sustainability and vitality of networks depended mainly on sufficient infrastructural support. Strategic research planning and agile management enabled adaptability to align networks with changes in the broader environment and emerging needs, contributing to sustainability. Inclusive and creative relationships, as well as continuous collaborative learning, were also fundamental to ensuring sustainability. Effective bilateral communication between academics and practitioners throughout the lifecycles of research projects was considered essential to maintain sustainability in networks with all governance types.

Our results confirmed the findings of our prior scoping reviews concerning the importance of structural funding to establish and maintain a network [19], as well as, persistent efforts in data QI. Additionally, unresolved issues related to data ownership within networks [19], previously reported, remain, while regulatory barriers appear to have increased over time [22].

Our findings also affirmed previous reviews that academic partnerships were vital for the growth of PBRNs from the start and throughout their trajectory, and even when networks become self-sustainable [22]. We also learned that academic contributions can enhance PBRN activity through synergies, specifically when the value and accomplishments of networks are properly recognized. Prior successful collaborations among faculty in the US and UK offer useful insights about these synergies [27, 30–33]. However, academics can also impede PBRNs due to conflicting priorities, limited funding, and non-supportive educational frameworks. These findings are aligned with our prior studies and other publications [22, 23, 32]. Our study identified advocacy needs within the broader academic community.

Our research underscores the substantial importance of collaborative, inclusive relationships and team spirit in the internal environment of networks and practitioner motivation was directly linked to the qualities and properties that emerged from these relationships along with participation in learning communities. These collaborative relationships were as important as intrinsic motivation of the practitioners mentioned in our prior review [19]. In addition, strategically planned events were significant for the sustainability of networks by fostering strong ties and intellectual exchange between members and PBRN leaders and building PBRN collaborations. Accessible training for members is a fundamental facilitator of sustainable relationships and compensation for research participation was also recognized as a necessary acknowledgment of contribution to research, as suggested elsewhere [19, 20, 34–36].

In prior scoping review, we found that later PBRNs adopted a variety of business models from their inception to ensure infrastructural stability [19]. In this study, we found that networks employed business models for maintaining sustainability. Moreover, the international literature also indicates that networks have often implemented business plans to respond to financial challenges and support future activities [26, 37–39] or engaged in research partnerships with other stakeholders or networks to increase their sustainability and impact [6, 15, 31, 32, 40–43]. The integration of research expertise developed within smaller networks into larger and high-impact networks or network partnerships, was in many cases an alternative to network expansion and a hallmark of the network evolution. Many of these PBRN partnerships have been challenging to maintain and we gained valuable insights into their struggles. Previous studies showed that favourable conditions for strong collaborative relationships include continuous funding and institutional support [5, 23, 30, 32] and generating tangible benefits through flexibility and resiliency in structure and network agenda that can respond to emerging needs [12, 14, 18, 27, 30, 32, 44]. Hindering factors include inequalities in relationships with PBRN-partners or insufficient capacity to maintain established relationships [23, 32, 45, 46]. However, these findings did not fully explore the facilitators and barriers of building PBRN partnerships, which necessitates further study to identify them and assess their long-term impact based on local and global experience.

Past research showed a pronounced trend in PBRNs toward conducting research with, rather than on, the community [45–48]. We observed this trend in our study, but it was not universally present across all interviewed networks. While our prior reviews, we noted a growing involvement of this stakeholder category, beginning in the mid-90s and increasing substantially from the 2010s onward [22]. According to our sources, this approach tended to be associated with more mature PBRNs. Additionally, we learned about extensive relationships with another category of stakeholders, those interested in the outcomes of PBRN research. These relationships have helped PBRNs evolve into more inclusive and adaptive organizations and increased their value and impact. Reorganizing governance and research agendas to balance internal and external stakeholder needs for securing funding was necessary but challenging. As noted in previous work, each additional stakeholder helped enhance research outcomes and learning [25, 30, 45, 49–51] but also introduced challenges in terms of bridging interests, cultivating understanding, and ensuring reciprocal benefits [20, 25, 30, 32, 35, 49–53]. There were similar challenges that we identified in our study when networks expanded or created meta-networks. Despite the benefit of infrastructural and institutional support, these expanded networks were hampered by the complexity of achieving stakeholder alignment and the need to respond to scientific and regulatory challenges. Prior publications provide congruent evidence but with distinct stakeholders, which tend to be less diverse than those we encountered in our study [15, 18, 27, 32, 54, 55]

Our informants emphasized the vital role of advocacy in garnering support for PBRNs and stressed the need for funders, policymakers, and healthcare stakeholders to recognize the value of PBRN research as a catalyst for significant changes at the practice, community and policy levels in primary care. This is consistent with the needs identified in the previous decade [2, 45, 52] and also in recent studies [26, 30, 45]. Our study suggests that sustainable PBRNs function as “convening centers” or hubs where diverse stakeholder interests intersect, resulting in meaningful research outcomes for all involved, and they serve as hubs for knowledge sharing and implementing innovations. These activities especially benefit from direct engagement with communities and patients [4, 18, 20, 22, 44, 46, 50, 56, 57], which also create an ideal environment for evaluating innovations. Convener networks possess innate strengths that enable them to adapt to new healthcare contexts and needs [22], and they have greater capacity for multi-faceted research and innovation [21, 22, 46, 47, 50, 58]. Their history shows that they can be observatories of community or public health [9, 45, 59], but they also have the capacity to be repositories of transformative knowledge, good practices, and practical solutions in primary care/family medicine. PBRNs can identify emerging problems and transform them into research topics and priorities. They also possess the capacity to generate rapidly in response to urgent needs, such as those that arise in a pandemic. This underscores the significance of PBRNs in addressing emergent healthcare challenges and supporting timely decision-making and patient care. PBRNs have also evolved into hubs of collaborative learning and demonstrate capacity as learning health systems [60] that facilitate patient-centeredness and, most recently, as developers of patient-centered AI applications [61]. With these capabilities, PBRNs became valuable assets that are integral to the process of primary healthcare development, especially in countries with well-developed primary care/family medicine and advocacy for PBRNs.

The Practice-Based Research Network (PBRN) model excels at organizing primary care research by leveraging the primary care base. It aligns with family medicine’s core values and principles, including patient-centered care, continuity and equity of care, professionalism, cooperation in care, and community and science-oriented care as defined by WONCA [62]. Furthermore, grounded in a community-centered approach, PBRNs focus on real-world healthcare challenges by engaging diverse patient populations and utilizing routine clinical data. Their organizational model incorporates interdisciplinary knowledge from medicine, psychology, and sociology, ensuring research is both practical and culturally relevant. By emphasizing participatory methods and bridging the gap between research and practice, PBRNs promote actionable insights and innovations that improve patient care, support the holistic focus of family medicine and improve health outcomes in the community [63, 64].

Thus, primary care and other organisations with global influence may advocate for the role and importance of PBRNs in national health systems and primary care as PBRNs are aligned with their core values. Our interviewees emphasized this need for their networks’ sustainability and highlighted the importance of drawing insights from the experience of WONCA, NAPCRG, and other research-related groups to foster PBRN activity, including regions with less developed primary care research and PBRN groups [4, 65]. In these regions, newly evolving PBRNs can tap into the substantial pool of global experience and accelerate their growth by applying lessons from well-established PBRNs. However, in these settings, some approaches proposed here, for example, intensive relationship-building, may not be encouraged in the academic environment, and external factors such as long distances and poor infrastructure may hinder intensive exchange.

One drawback of PBRNs may be found in their traditional focus on clinical practice, which often excludes public health functions such as surveillance, large-scale health promotion and preventive measures, which are central to the broader vision of primary health care as described in the Astana Declaration [66]. However, some networks have specialized in sentinel surveillance, such as the Weekly Returns Service WRS [67] or have been involved in large-scale health prevention interventions, for example, the FaMe-net [68]. To address this gap, PBRNs can expand their scope by fostering multi-sectoral collaboration with sectors like education, housing, and social services to tackle social determinants of health and enhance comprehensive outcomes. While principles such as equity, community empowerment, and integrated care are already inherent in PBRN efforts [57, 63], broadening their application would further strengthen their role and recognition within global primary healthcare systems.

PBRNs, despite certain challenges, represent an early and enduring collaboration model between academia and clinical practices, emphasizing community focus, adaptability, reciprocal stakeholder relationships, and shared learning. Principles like academic support, frontline collaborations, and patient engagement have shaped institutions such as Academic Resource Centers (ARCs) and Academic Health Science Centers (AHSCs) in the US [69], Academic Health Science Networks (AHSNs) in the UK [70], and Research Translation Centers (RTCs) in Australia [71], each with distinct missions. ARCs support research within affiliated universities. AHSCs focus on cutting-edge science and innovation. AHSNs aim to integrate research with healthcare but lack a primary care focus. RTCs apply research in rural and remote settings but have limited engagement with primary care. Unlike these later models, PBRNs prioritize practice improvement and a strong emphasis on primary care and community, making them uniquely suited to address the needs of their communities and beyond.

Limitations

Despite the 55 interviews that we have analysed our sample was limited in bandwidth and unable to provide a complete representation of all PBRN types and geographic locations due to the large number of PBRNs worldwide. The sampling was thus mainly focused on Western Europe, North America, and Australia. However, we made every effort to sample from all types of PBRNs that we have come to know during our investigation. Our research could not identify differences between rural/remote and urban networks or those in higher- or lower-resource settings, as this was beyond the scope of the study. Instead, we focused on the “common ground” among the widest variety of PBRNs and contexts found in similar studies in international literature, which constitute the most valuable and significant contribution of this study.

Our inductive thematic analysis may have been influenced by findings from previous scoping reviews and existing PBRN knowledge, which is inevitable as we used prior findings and literature reviews to develop it. Although the use of thematic analysis in this study led to a deeper knowledge and understanding of previous structures, some important elements may be underrepresented by the structure of the interview guide or the summary of rich data that emerged from our analysis. This interpretation bias is always possible in qualitative analyses and we have tried to reduce it by presenting detailed tables [72].

This study used Skype video calls for face-to-face interactions and capturing underlying social cues. However, technological challenges, including poor Internet connection and noise, necessitated multiple interviews for some leaders [73] and rendered one recording unusable, despite the double recording. Additionally, despite the use of professional transcription services and review by two authors to address minor discrepancies, the transcriptions may still be subject to individual interpretation bias.

Conclusion

PBRNs managed to thrive and develop over extended periods of time by systematically investing in infrastructure support, strategic research planning, agile management, inclusive and collaborative relationships, and ongoing learning communities. This points to the primary need for intentionality in planning and building network relationships, infrastructure, and project portfolios. While PBRNs have achieved impactful results in a number of research and health domains, such as generating high-quality practice-based evidence, clinician knowledge, community health and guidelines, and policy, a need for a greater advocacy remains paramount, which can be achieved by engaging policymakers, funders, healthcare stakeholders, and the broader academic community to improve network sustainability.
