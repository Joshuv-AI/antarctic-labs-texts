Straining During Defecation or Gut Environment
Authors: Eikichi Ihara, Atsushi Nakajima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: cardiovascular disease, chronic constipation, defecation patterns

Chronic constipation not only impairs quality of life but has also been associated with adverse long-term outcomes.1 In particular, accumulating evidence suggests that chronic constipation is linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular events.2,3 Although prior studies have established an association between constipation and cardiovascular risk, the extent to which specific defecation patterns and/or stimulant laxative use independently contribute to cardiovascular outcomes, beyond the presence of constipation itself, remains unclear.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Matsuzawa et al4 investigated the association between defecation patterns and laxative use during hospitalization and subsequent cardiovascular events in patients with acute coronary syndrome. In this study, to capture the multidimensional aspects of bowel habits beyond the conventional definition of constipation, the authors evaluated not only average daily defecation frequency but also unique abnormal defecation patterns, including the percentage of nondefecation days, consecutive nondefecation days, and maximum daily defecation frequency. Notably, the study demonstrates that both low average daily defecation frequency and a high proportion of nondefecation days, which are expected markers of constipation, were associated with an increased risk of the primary outcome; a composite of the first occurrence of all-cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal ischemic stroke, and nonfatal hemorrhage stroke. Surprisingly, a high maximum daily defecation frequency (≥5/day), which may reflect diarrheal patterns, was also independently associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events. This intriguing finding raises important questions regarding the role of abnormal defecation pattern beyond constipation per se in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease.

Although the precise mechanisms underlying the association between defecation abnormalities and cardiovascular events remain to be fully elucidated, two major pathophysiological pathways may be considered. One involves the hemodynamic consequences of repeated Valsalva maneuver induced by straining during defecation. Although the Valsalva maneuver is widely used in clinical medicine for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, it also occurs during defecation and induces transient fluctuations in blood pressure and heart rate through changes in intrathoracic pressure and autonomic reflexes, which may contribute to cardiovascular events.5 The other pathway relates to alterations in the gut environment, including changes in the gut microbiota and microbiota-derived factors.

Because of the retrospective nature of this study, the extent of straining during defecation was not assessed. Such information would be indispensable for clarifying the mechanisms linking defecation patterns to cardiovascular outcomes. Increased straining during defecation may provide a plausible explanation for the elevated risk observed in patients with a high maximum daily defecation frequency (≥5/day). In this context, defecation posture warrants particular attention. Defecation is strongly influenced by body position. In healthy volunteers, approximately one-third exhibited dyssynergic defecation and one-half were unable to expel artificial stool in supine position, whereas most demonstrated normal defecation patterns and effective stool expulsion in the sitting position.6 Furthermore, studies comparing different toileting positions have shown that squatting significantly reduces both the time required for satisfactory bowel emptying and the degree of straining compared with sitting positions, with lower toilet seats also associated with reduced straining compared with standard-height toilets.7 Taken together, the magnitude of straining during defecation likely vary substantially depending on posture and toileting conditions, such as bedpan use, portable toilets, Western-style toilets, and traditional Japanese squat toilets. Therefore, information on defecation posture and toileting conditions may help provide important insights into why patients with a high maximum daily defecation frequency (≥5/day) exhibited an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. In addition, as acknowledged by the authors, the lack of data on the Bristol Stool Form Scale represents another important limitation, as stool consistency is closely related to the degree of straining. Whether the Bristol Stool Form Scale independently contributes to cardiovascular risk, in addition to defecation frequency, warrants further investigation.

As the authors discussed, alterations in the gut environment, including changes in the gut microbiota and microbiota-derived factors, represent another plausible mechanism linking abnormal defecation patterns, beyond constipation per se, to cardiovascular disease. Among microbiota-derived factors, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) has emerged as a key mediator. Initial evidence from experimental studies demonstrated that TMAO promotes the development of coronary artery disease.8 Subsequent human cohort studies confirmed that TMAO production depends on intestinal microbiota metabolism and that elevated circulating TMAO levels are associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events.9 Additional prospective cohort studies in patients with recent first-ever ischemic stroke have further shown that higher plasma TMAO levels are associated with an increased risk of subsequent cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, recurrent stroke, and cardiovascular death.10 In these studies, circulating TMAO levels were also significantly associated with a higher proportion of proinflammatory intermediate CD14++CD16+ monocytes, suggesting a link with systemic inflammation. Moreover, TMAO has been shown to enhance platelet hyperreactivity and thrombogenic potential, thereby contributing to the development of cardiovascular disease.11 Taken together, TMAO may represent a crucial mechanistic link underlying the observed associations in this study, although it was not directly assessed. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether serum TMAO levels are altered in patients with chronic constipation or whether therapeutic modulation of constipation can influence the serum TMAO levels and subsequent cardiovascular link. Further studies are warranted to clarify the role of TMAO in the development of cardiovascular disease.

Another important finding of this study is that stimulant laxative use was independently associated with an increased risk of the primary outcome. Given that both low and high defecation frequencies are significantly associated with adverse outcomes, large fluctuations in bowel habits may be detrimental. A stimulant laxative, which can induce abrupt changes in defecation frequency, may contribute to this risk. With the increasing availability of newer agents, such as secretagogues and bile acid transporter inhibitors, it will be important to clarify how different therapeutic strategies influence cardiovascular outcomes in future studies.

Taken together, this study highlights that not only constipation itself but also the pattern and dynamics of defecation may play a critical role in cardiovascular risk after acute coronary syndrome. Both defecation-related hemodynamic stress and alterations in the gut environment likely contribute to this association, suggesting that bowel habits should be considered as an integrated physiological process rather than a simple measure of frequency. Future studies incorporating detailed assessments of straining, stool consistency, gut microbiota, and microbiota-derived metabolites will be essential to disentangle these mechanisms and to establish optimal therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing cardiovascular risk through modulation of defecation patterns.
