Study on the impact of urban underground public space expansion on near-ground carbon monoxide concentrations
Authors: Guoliang Zhuang, Peiyuan Qiu, Linke Pang, Huaqiao Xing, Jiawei Shen, Yuxin Wang, Wenbo Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs global urbanization intensifies and land resource constraints become increasingly severe, the development of underground public space (UPS) has emerged as a crucial strategy for optimizing urban spatial structures and enhancing carrying capacity. However, the impact of the mechanism on near-ground carbon monoxide (CO) concentrations remains unclear, and most relevant studies lack a systematic quantitative assessment. Therefore, this study designs an analysis framework to examine the impact of urban UPS on near-ground CO concentrations based on multi-year large-scale Point of Interest (POI) data. Then taking Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan in China as case studies, spatial partition statistics and a panel fixed-effects model are conducted to explore the impact of urban UPS expansion on near-ground CO concentrations and the urban heterogeneity with data from 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022. The results demonstrate that the UPS development has a positive effect in reducing CO concentrations. In particular, the panel fixed-effects model reveals a significant negative correlation between the number of in Underground Point of Interest (UPOI) and CO concentration levels. However, significant differences exist among cities: Shanghai and Chengdu show a steady decline in CO concentrations as the unit POI increases, with a more significant emission reduction effect in high-growth zones. Jinan has the strongest marginal emission reduction per unit POI, but the overall decrease in CO concentrations in its high-growth zones is the smallest due to topographical constraints, industrial structure, and the current stage of UPS development. This study reveals the net environmental benefits of UPS development from the city system scale, enhances the understanding of the complex environmental relationship between underground and ground, and provides a scientific basis for differentiated underground space planning and air pollution control in different cities.Keywords: Underground public space, CO concentration, Spatial analysis, Urban heterogeneitySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Global urbanization continues to drive economic prosperity and social development while significantly intensifying urban space resource shortages and ecological environmental pressures1,2. In high-density built-up environments, land resource constraints increasingly present a critical bottleneck restricting sustainable urban development3,4. Consequently, utilizing underground space has become an essential strategic choice for major global cities, aiming to optimize the spatial structure and enhance operational efficiency5,6. The concept of underground space is quite broad, encompassing both municipal infrastructure, such as civil defense and underground pipelines, and public shallow underground spaces accessible to the public for daily activities7–9. This research focuses on the latter, namely the functional spaces that are open to the public, allow for extensive participation, communication, and interaction, including subway networks, underground commercial streets, sunken plazas, underground parking lots, etc., but excluding non-public use parts like underground production and storage facilities, and deep municipal projects. The extensive development of such underground public space (UPS) has effectively addressed ground-level land use conflicts and optimized urban functional layout10–12. The near-ground atmosphere is the most direct spatial layer for daily human activities and respiratory exposure; therefore, fluctuations in its air quality are strongly associated with resident health risks, the comfort of travel environments, and urban livability13. However, the rapid expansion of UPS lacks comprehensive recognition or systematic evaluation regarding its potential impact on the urban ground environment system, particularly near-ground environmental quality.

Academic attention to this issue has gradually increased, evolving from theoretical exploration to empirical analysis. Early studies mainly focused on qualitative analysis, seeking to clarify the potential impacts of UPS development. For instance, Qian et al. used qualitative analysis to state that the UPS development, by relocating some urban functions underground, can free up more land for landscape greening and improve the urban environment14. Admiraal et al. emphasized that the UPS development can promote land intensification utilization and enhance urban resilience15. Lai et al. found that UPS plays a positive role in mitigating extreme climate events and reducing carbon emissions16. Yuan et al. further suggested that centralizing underground infrastructure and commercial facilities can reduce private vehicle demand, decrease energy consumption, and promote urban development17. These studies primarily focus on theoretical elaboration and framework construction, lacking quantitative empirical verification of influence mechanisms and effect scales.

With the advancement of technical methods, recent scholars have gradually adopted empirical approaches, such as on-site monitoring and numerical simulation, to explore the specific impact of UPS development on the surface environment. For instance, Feng et al. discovered that the construction of underground space in soft soil areas would cause surface subsidence18. Abdullah et al. compared the pollutant monitoring data before and after the opening of the subway system implementation with normalization index analysis, finding that the UPS development is conducive to reducing air pollutant levels19. Similarly, Xiao and Zheng respectively conducted research on the environmental effects of subway construction in different regions through comparative analysis and numerical simulation. Both studies concluded that subway-centered UPS helps to reduce the concentration of atmospheric particulate matter, thereby improving the urban air quality20,21. Yang and Su, respectively, used the ENVI-met software to simulate the local impacts of underground parking lots and underground commercial streets on the urban thermal environment and air quality (with carbon monoxide (CO) concentration as the indicator)22,23. While these studies represent a methodological advancement from theoretical deduction to empirical analysis, the following issues still remain in the existing literature. First, from a research perspective, previous studies have primarily focused on the internal environments of underground spaces or micro-scale block simulations, with limited exploration of the impacts of UPS development on the near-ground environment at a city scale. Second, regarding the temporal dimension and data robustness, existing research often emphasizes short-term experiments or static assessments at specific time points, lacking analyses based on long-term, real-world data. Finally, limitations also exist in the selection of evaluation indicators; most current studies prioritize PM2.5 or thermal environment indices, while studies specifically targeting CO remain scarce.

One of the primary functions of UPS is generally considered to be the diversion of ground-level traffic flow through the traffic substitution effect, thereby reducing the intensity of surface vehicular activity and potentially contributing to variations in near-ground traffic-related pollutants. Specifically, CO originates predominantly from motor vehicle combustion emissions and is highly sensitive to fluctuations in traffic volume. Given its clear near-ground exposure properties, CO has been widely adopted to characterize the intensity of urban traffic-source pollution and its response to changes in traffic activity24,25. Consequently, CO serves as a relevant indicator for evaluating the impacts of UPS expansion. Furthermore, as the near-ground air environment is directly linked to residents’ health exposure levels and urban livability, a systematic assessment of the impact of UPS expansion on CO concentrations helps elucidate the potential comprehensive benefits of UPS development in enhancing urban environmental quality. Therefore, this study proposes a methodological framework integrating multi-source data fusion and multi-method integration, to address two core research questions: (1) the magnitude and marginal effects of UPS expansion on near-ground CO concentrations; and (2) whether these impacts are consistent or exhibit significant heterogeneity across different topographical constraints and urban development stages. First, Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan are selected as representative case studies based on gradients in topographical ventilation conditions and UPS development stages. Multi-year underground public space POI (UPOI) data and high-resolution CO inversion data are systematically collected and processed. Subsequently, a city-scale panel dataset with grid-level resolution is established through multi-source data fusion. Quantitative analysis is then performed by combining spatial partition statistical with fixed-effects models. This approach effectively controls for unobservable grid-specific characteristics and temporal disturbance factors, mitigating the interference caused by spatial heterogeneity, temporal trends, and potential omitted variables on the estimation results, thereby strengthening the robustness of causal inference. Building upon these foundations, the primary contributions of this study are threefold: (1) Within a multi-source spatiotemporal data-driven framework, it provides a systematic assessment of the impact of UPS on near-ground CO concentrations at the city scale. (2) By constructing a large-scale, long-term observational dataset, the study effectively overcomes the limitations of traditional field monitoring and numerical simulation methods in terms of cost, spatial coverage, and simulation bias. (3) Through the integrated application of multiple statistical and spatial analysis methods, the robustness and interpretability of the results are significantly enhanced, thereby providing reliable quantitative evidence to support evidence-based urban planning and sustainable environmental governance of UPS.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 describes the study area, data sources, and the methodological framework. Section 3 reports the empirical results and analyzes the spatial characteristics. Section 4 examines the underlying mechanisms and inter-city heterogeneity. Finally, Sect. 5 summarizes the main conclusions.

Overview of the study area

To systematically examine the generalizability and heterogeneity of the impact of UPS on near-ground CO concentrations, this study constructs a comparative analytical framework based on the topographic ventilation gradient and the UPS development-stage gradient. Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan are selected as representative case cities (Fig. 2). These three cities span a representative range from open plains to enclosed basins, and from mature to early stages of UPS development, thereby providing a robust empirical foundation for this research.

The study area, which encompasses three cities in China: Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan. The map was generated using ArcMap 10.2 (ESRI, Redlands, CA, USA; https://www.esri.com).

Situated on the Yangtze River Delta alluvial plain, Shanghai is characterized by flat terrain and is strongly influenced by land–sea breeze circulation, which enhances atmospheric dispersion and ventilation capacity. As one of the earliest cities to develop underground space in China, its UPS has evolved into a highly networked and integrated system. Shanghai is selected to examine the marginal impact of the continuous expansion of UPS on near-ground CO concentrations within the context of a megacity characterized by relatively favorable ventilation conditions and a relatively mature underground system26.

Located in the western Sichuan Basin, Chengdu is characterized by a basin topography surrounded by mountains, which substantially limits atmospheric dispersion in both vertical and horizontal directions. This geographical setting contributes to pollutant accumulation, thereby forming an unfavorable dispersion environment. Concurrently, Chengdu is experiencing a phase of rapid growth in UPS development27. This city provides a representative case to evaluate the effectiveness and stability of UPS expansion in regulating near-ground CO concentrations under the combined constraints of unfavorable meteorological conditions and rapid urbanization.

Constrained by the southern foothills of Mount Tai and the northern landscape of the Yellow River, Jinan has developed a ribbon-like urban spatial pattern, resulting in limited atmospheric ventilation and chronic challenges related to surface space scarcity and pollutant dispersion. Currently, Jinan’s UPS remains in its nascent stage of development, primarily serving to alleviate traffic bottlenecks and optimize urban spatial structures28. Jinan provides a representative case to evaluate the marginal effect of UPS expansion on near-ground CO concentrations within a context of strong spatial constraints.

Data sources

The POI data used in this study were obtained from the Gaode Map Application Programming Interface. Traditional collection methods for UPS data, such as field surveys, struggle to provide large-scale (city-wide) and multi-year datasets, thereby imposing substantial costs and constraints on long-term spatiotemporal analysis. In contrast, POI data are widely recognized as fundamental semantic units for characterizing urban space, and their spatial distribution and density effectively depict urban functional structures, activity intensities, and spatial organizational patterns29,30. Furthermore, POI data are characterized by extensive spatial coverage, long temporal spans, and scalable batch accessibility, which facilitate large-scale empirical analysis. As noted in the Introduction, previous studies utilizing UPOI have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach for UPS research. Consequently, this study argue that UPOI can serve as a reasonable proxy for the functional utilization intensity of UPS, whereby high-density UPOI areas typically signify a larger underground built environment and more frequent human–environment interactions. In summary, we adopt UPOI to explore the impact of UPS on CO concentrations31. POI data translate the physical presence of underground public space into quantifiable functional units, providing operational and interpretable indicators for environmental impact analysis. Accordingly, this study defines the study area as the core urban areas with relatively concentrated POI distributions for the years 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022. The selection of these temporal nodes is primarily based on two considerations. First, these years encapsulate pivotal stages of urban construction, rail transit expansion, and underground space development within the three study cities, ensuring temporal representativeness. Second, due to data gaps in 2019 that compromised data completeness and consistency, 2020 was designated as the representative year for that interval to balance data availability with the temporal continuity of the analysis. CO data mainly originates from the incomplete combustion of chemical fuels and is a typical air pollutant in urban atmospheres. Its near-ground concentration can sensitively reflect the air quality conditions in a local area, and is particularly suitable for characterizing the short-term and local impacts of traffic emissions. Unlike pollutants such as PM2.5 and O3 that are susceptible to secondary formation processes, CO has relatively inert chemical properties and strong spatial representativeness. It can more directly reflect the primary pollution impacts resulting from urban transportation activities and land use changes32,33. Therefore, in this study, the ground CO concentration was selected as the key indicator to characterize the urban environmental air quality. The relevant CO concentration data were obtained from the China High Precision Atmospheric Pollutants Dataset (ChinaHighCO). This dataset employs advanced machine learning algorithms, integrating ground-based observations, various satellite remote sensing inversion products (such as MOPITT, TROPOMI), atmospheric reanalysis data, and chemical transport model simulation results from multiple sources. It fully considers the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of air pollution and generates long-term sequence, high coverage ground-level CO concentration raster data products. The time resolution of this data is annual, and the unit is mg/m3.

Methods

Extraction of UPOI data

This study further extracted the UPOI from the aforementioned data. Initially, using ArcMap software, the POI dataset was spatially clipped according to the boundaries of the research area. Subsequently, the attribute information, such as addresses, names, and types of the POI, was matched using keywords to identify the relevant entries related to UPS34. The search keywords mainly include “underground passage”, “underground floor”, “negative first floor”, “negative 1 floor”, “subway station”, and other expressions. The complete keywords are shown in Table 2. To enhance the accuracy of identification, this study conducted multiple rounds of manual verification and algorithm optimization on the preliminary extraction results: Firstly, incorrect entries were eliminated through random sampling and manual interpretation; Secondly, potential synonyms and variant expressions (such as “B1 layer”, “underground passage”, etc.) were identified through high-frequency word analysis, and the keyword database was supplemented and iteratively searched. These steps effectively improved the accuracy of UPOI identification, establishing a reliable foundation for subsequent spatial distribution and evolution analysis.

Processing of CO data

For the CO concentration data, the spatial resolution was 10 km before 2019 and was upgraded to 1 km since 2019. To maintain the consistency of the spatial scale of the data throughout the entire research period and to ensure compatibility with the 1 km × 1 km analysis grid, this study conducted spatial resampling on the CO concentration raster data of 2015 and 2017. Specifically, in the ArcMap software platform, the bilinear interpolation algorithm was used to resample the 10 km resolution data of 2015 and 2017 to 1 km resolution35, making it consistent with the spatial resolution of the data in 2020 and 2022. The bilinear interpolation method can effectively smooth the data and is suitable for the scale transformation of continuous atmospheric pollutant concentration data, which can largely preserve the spatial distribution characteristics of the data.

Spatial partition statistics

To systematically assess the impact of UPS expansion on ground-level CO concentration and its spatial heterogeneity, this study adopts a spatial partition statistical comparison method. By constructing a unified analysis unit through Geographic Information System, quantifying key indicators, and conducting classification comparisons, it progresses from spatial pattern description to correlation feature analysis36,37. The specific process is as follows:

First, a 1 km × 1 km regular grid system covering the entire area of Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan was constructed using the “Create Grid” tool in the ArcMap platform to form the basic unit for spatial analysis. Based on this, the prescreened and confirmed UPOI for each year (2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022) were superimposed with the grid surface data of the city using spatial connection technology. The number of POIs (Count_POI) within each grid unit (GridID) was accurately counted, thereby characterizing the development intensity of underground space in that unit. To reveal its dynamic growth characteristics, we further calculated the POI increment (ΔPOI) between adjacent years for each grid. Additionally, to objectively reflect the relative growth intensity of each city in different periods and ensure comparability across cities and time periods, the grids were divided into four growth types (see Table 3): unchanged zones (ΔPOI = 0), low-growth zones (0 < ΔPOI ≤ 19), medium-growth zones (20 ≤ ΔPOI ≤ 99), and high-growth zones (ΔPOI ≥ 100). This classification standard takes into account both the continuity of the numerical intervals and the grade differences in the actual growth amplitude. It can adapt to the spatial differentiation characteristics of different cities and development stages.

Panel fixed-effects model

The panel fixed-effects model is a classic statistical method suitable for analyzing data from multiple time points and multiple observation units. Its core advantage lies in the ability to control unobservable individual characteristics that do not change over time, thereby reducing omitted variable bias. From the current application status, the fixed-effects model has been widely used in research fields such as environmental economics, urban planning, air quality, and climate change38,39. Results and analysis

Spatial pattern evolution of UPS growth

Comparative analysis of total growth in UPOI quantity

Figure 3 shows the UPOI changes in Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan from 2015 to 2022. From the figure, the UPS development in these three cities exhibits a differentiated growth trend. Shanghai has always been leading, with the number climbing from 16,626 in 2015 to 39,306 in 2022, having the largest total number and an increase rate of 136.4%. This reflects the continuous advancement and mature layout of UPS development in megacities. Chengdu, as a new first-tier city, has also steadily increased the number of UPOIs with the acceleration of urban expansion and functional upgrading. In 2022, it reached 20,593, with a growth rate of 418.2%, ranking first among the three cities. Jinan has a relatively slower UPS development due to the influence of urban morphology and natural geographical factors, but it shows a year-on-year increase. In 2015, its number of UPOI was only 1348, with a low base. With the continuous advancement of projects such as rail transit, the number reached 5811 in 2022, with an increase rate of 333.1%. The changes in the data of these three cities reflect the advancement paths of UPS construction under different city positioning and development stages, providing empirical evidence for further research.

The spatial density distribution maps of UPOI in Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan were obtained through kernel density analysis41,42 (see Fig. 4). The distribution reveals that the high-density areas of UPOI in all three cities start from the city center as the initial aggregation core. The medium growth zones and low growth zones are mostly located in the urban outskirts or in the old areas that have not yet become hotspots for underground development. The rapid growth grids in Chengdu are mainly concentrated in areas such as Qingyang District and Wuhou District, showing a trend of “expansion from the old city center to the south”. This trend is highly consistent with Chengdu’s recent “southern expansion and eastern advancement” development strategy, indicating a clear synergy between underground space development and the direction of the urban surface development. The medium-growth zones and low-growth zones are mainly concentrated around the secondary urban centers, presenting a ring-shaped diffusion pattern. The growth rate of UPOI in Jinan is mainly composed of low-growth zones and medium-growth zones. The number of high-growth zones is limited, and no obvious regional clustering characteristics have been formed. Most of the growth of UPOI occurs around traditional commercial areas or major transportation routes, and the development scale and continuity are significantly lower than those in Shanghai and Chengdu.

Analysis results based on spatial partition statistical comparison

Tables 4, 5, and 6 demonstrate that CO concentrations in areas with UPOI growth have declined across all three cities. Specifically, during the period from 2015 to 2022, the CO concentration in the “high-growth zones” in Shanghai decreased by 17.4% (0.16 mg/m3), which was significantly higher than the 15.1% (0.14 mg/m3) decrease in the “medium-growth zones”, 11.7% (0.12 mg/m3) decrease in the “low-growth zones” and 6.4% (0.09 mg/m3) decrease in the “unchanged zones”. It shows that the expansion of UPS has a positive effect on the mitigation of pollutants. The trends in each stage were also highly consistent. Particularly in the periods of 2015–2017 and 2015–2020, the “high-growth zones” showed the largest decrease (15.1% and 13.9% respectively), while the “unchanged zones” showed limited or slow decreases (such as only a 1.7% decrease from 2017 to 2022). This suggests a strong negative correlation between the degree of underground development and the reduction of CO.

Chengdu exhibited particularly notable changes in CO concentration. The CO concentration in the “high-growth zones” decreased by 32.9% (0.37 mg/m3) from 2015 to 2022, which was significantly higher than that in the “medium-growth zones”, “low-growth zones”, and “unchanged zones”. This trend was consistent in all time periods separated by more than three years, with the decline in CO concentration being “high-growth zones” > “medium-growth zones” > “low-growth zones” > “unchanged zones”, reflecting the significant effectiveness of the rapid expansion of UPS in reducing emissions.

Jinan presented a distinct pattern from Shanghai and Chengdu: although the CO concentration in various regions of Jinan generally showed a downward trend, the overall concentration level remained relatively high, and the degree of decline was inversely related to the growth rate of UPS. The “unchanged zones” had the largest decline rate, at 51.2% (0.81 mg/m3); followed by the “low-growth zones”, with a 48.7% decrease (0.73 mg/m3); the “medium-growth zones” saw a 45.8% drop (0.66 mg/m3); while the “high-growth zones” had the smallest decline rate, at 45.5% (0.65 mg/m3). The results for other years also showed a similar trend, that is, “the faster the growth rate, the less the CO concentration decreases”. These results indicate that the potential emission reduction effect of UPS expansion on near-ground CO concentrations in Jinan is subject to the combined influence of multiple structural constraints, and therefore the associated environmental benefits have not been fully realized in high-growth zones.

However, it is worth noting that the above methods based on spatial partition statistical comparison may have certain statistical limitations due to differences in regional environmental conditions and inconsistent development stages. For instance, in Jinan, the decrease in CO concentration in the “high-growth zones” was actually lower than that in the “unchanged zones”. This might be influenced by factors such as the initial pollution level, construction disturbance, geographical ventilation conditions, or policy coverage differences, thereby masking the actual regulatory effect of underground space development on local CO concentration.

The model employs multi-temporal gridded data and simultaneously controls for both individual and time fixed effects. This approach enables a more precise assessment of the marginal impact of UPS expansion on near-ground CO concentrations across specific regions and time periods. All estimated coefficients for the core explanatory variable are negative and statistically significant, indicating that UPS expansion reduces CO concentrations. Specifically, the coefficient for Shanghai is -0.0003, implying that for every one-unit increase in UPOI, the CO concentration decreases by an average of 0.0003 mg/m3. For Chengdu, the coefficient is -0.0013, suggesting a stronger marginal emission reduction effect than in Shanghai. Jinan exhibits the largest absolute coefficient, indicating that its per-unit POI emission reduction effect is the most pronounced among the three cities.

Regarding model goodness-of-fit, the within R2 values for all three cities remain relatively low, particularly for Shanghai. This primarily reflects the statistical characteristics of the fixed-effects model. Individual fixed effects absorb time-invariant spatial heterogeneity across grids, while time fixed effects control for macro-level common shocks at the annual level, thereby limiting the remaining within-group variation that can be explained by the explanatory variables. For high-spatial-resolution air quality data, atmospheric dispersion processes, instantaneous meteorological disturbances, and micro-scale environmental differences introduce strong randomness, further reducing the explainable within-group variation. Nevertheless, the estimated coefficients for UPOI remain significantly negative across the three cities, and the overall F-statistics are significant, indicating that UPS expansion exerts a consistent marginal impact on near-ground CO concentrations. Furthermore, the F-test for poolability is significant, demonstrating that individual heterogeneity is statistically important and that the fixed-effects specification effectively controls for unobservable time-invariant confounding factors.

To further verify the stability of the model fit, this study plots the distribution of regression residuals for the three cities. As shown in Fig. 8, the residuals for each city exhibit a unimodal and approximately symmetric distribution, with no evident skewness or heavy tails. This indicates that the model’s error structure is generally stable and that the estimation results are free from significant systematic bias. Overall, the residual tests support the suitability of the fixed-effects model for this study.

Discussion

One of the most important findings of this study is that there is significant heterogeneity among cities in the environmental impact of UPS development. Shanghai and Chengdu showed a stable decrease in CO concentration with the increase of unit UPOI, and the emission reduction effect in the high-growth zones was particularly prominent, which is consistent with the theoretical expectations. However, the overall reduction in near-ground CO concentrations within Jinan’s high-growth zones remains relatively limited. This pattern indicates that, in Jinan, the potential emission reduction effects of UPS development are systematically attenuated by multiple structural constraints, primarily operating at three structural levels. First, topographical and meteorological conditions constrain the efficiency of pollutant dispersion. Located between the Mount Tai range to the south and the Yellow River to the north, Jinan’s ‘shallow-dish’ topography results in a high frequency of calm winds and inherently limited atmospheric dispersion capacity. In the high-density central urban area, the concentrated building clusters further obstruct ventilation, effectively offsetting the emission reduction benefits generated by UPS. Second, the influence of industrial and energy structures is significant. Compared to Shanghai and Chengdu, Jinan and its surrounding regions possess a higher proportion of traditional heavy industries, where industrial coal combustion and manufacturing processes serve as primary sources of CO. These high background concentrations of industrial emissions likely mask the marginal reduction effects brought about by UPS development. Finally, Jinan’s UPS is still in its nascent stage, characterized by a small overall scale and fragmented, non-networked spatial layouts. It has yet to form a continuous and efficient integrated system of underground transportation and commerce. Consequently, its capacity for systematic diversion of surface traffic remains limited, making it difficult to achieve the stable and substantial emission reduction effects observed in Shanghai and Chengdu in the short term.

Comparative Analysis of Influence Mechanisms and Existing Research. The expansion of UPS significantly reduces near-ground CO concentrations. To further interpret this relationship from a mechanistic perspective, this study draws on theories of urban spatial structure and travel behavior, together with existing empirical evidence. It suggests that the observed association may be explained by two complementary pathways: the traffic substitution effect and the functional substitution effect. First, the traffic substitution effect refers to a spatial diversion mechanism whereby underground transportation facilities shift a portion of surface motor vehicle traffic to the underground, thereby directly reducing ground-level vehicular emissions. As core components of UPS, facilities such as underground rail transit and underground road systems effectively alleviate surface traffic congestion and idling emissions, contributing directly to the mitigation of near-ground CO concentrations. This mechanism is supported by several international studies. For instance, Gendron-Carrier et al.43, utilizing macro-level data from 58 countries, found that well-developed underground transportation infrastructure leads to an average 4% reduction in particulate matter concentrations in city centers. Similarly, empirical research by Abdullah et al. in Iranian cities further demonstrated that the construction of metro systems significantly reduces various transportation-related pollutants, including CO19. Second, the functional substitution effect posits that urban functions such as commerce and leisure progressively migrate underground. Integrated underground pedestrian networks and commercial systems consequently reshape residents’ travel behavior and the spatial organization of daily activities. By reducing the need for short-distance surface trips at the source, this mechanism replaces a portion of surface travel demand. It further alleviates traffic congestion and inefficient energy consumption caused by conflicts between pedestrians and vehicles on surface streets. This effect has also been validated by empirical studies in developed North American cities. For instance, the Montreal Underground City in Canada successfully diverts substantial surface pedestrian traffic underground by linking metro stations with subterranean commercial facilities. This configuration significantly mitigates surface traffic pressure and indirectly reduces associated energy consumption and emissions44.

Limitations and Future Research Directions: First, constrained by the non-public nature of large-scale 3D underground space data, this study utilizes the number of POIs as a proxy variable for UPS development intensity. While this approach effectively reflects spatial distribution and density, it cannot precisely characterize the physical scale (e.g., floor area, volume, and depth) or functional attributes (e.g., actual pedestrian and vehicle flow) of development. Future research could integrate multi-source data—such as mobile phone signaling, remote sensing imagery, and metro ridership—to construct a more sophisticated 'development–activity intensity’ composite index, thereby more accurately quantifying the actual utilization intensity of underground spaces. Second, the two-way fixed effects model primarily identifies average effects across time and space. In the absence of high-resolution meteorological and traffic data, its capacity to capture local instantaneous disturbances and micro-scale nonlinear processes remains limited. Future work could incorporate meteorological variables (e.g., precipitation and wind speed) and real-time traffic data, while also combining field monitoring, UAV-based surveys, or Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations45,46. Such multi-scale approaches would help reveal the specific processes and mechanisms by which underground spaces influence near-ground CO concentrations, thereby strengthening the robustness and interpretability of research conclusions.

Conclusions

This study proposes a research framework that integrates multi-source data fusion with multi-method analytical coupling. Shanghai, Chengdu, and Jinan are selected as representative case cities, and a panel dataset is constructed based on POI data and CO raster data for the years 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022. By employing spatial partition statistical comparisons and panel fixed-effects models, this research quantitatively evaluates the impact of UPS expansion on near-ground CO concentrations. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) UPS exhibits a significant overall growth trend, with a substantial increase in the number of UPOIs across all three cities. The spatial distribution of UPOIs shows a centrifugal expansion pattern from the urban center, with high-density clusters primarily concentrated in central functional zones. This expansion trend is highly consistent with urban development axes, reflecting a coupling effect between underground development and surface urban spatial structures. (2) The expansion of UPS exerts a robust air-quality improvement effect by reducing near-ground CO concentrations. Panel fixed-effects estimates indicate that increases in the number of UPOIs are significantly associated with declines in CO concentrations, confirming the positive role of UPS development in improving urban environmental quality. (3) The impact of UPS development on near-ground CO concentrations exhibits significant city heterogeneity. Shanghai and Chengdu demonstrate a stable decline in CO concentrations associated with per-unit UPS expansion, with the reduction effect particularly pronounced in high-growth zones. In contrast, although Jinan exhibits the strongest marginal CO reduction effect per additional POI, the overall decline in CO concentrations within its high-growth zones remains the smallest among the three cities, which likely reflects constraints imposed by its topography, industrial structure, the current development stage of UPS.

Future research will focus on two critical directions to enhance the practical and operational value of the findings. First, high-precision data sources—including mobile phone signaling, remote sensing imagery, and metro ridership—will be integrated to construct a more granular 'development–activity intensity’ composite index. Second, by incorporating meteorological and traffic parameters in combination with field monitoring techniques, future work aims to elucidate the specific processes and mechanisms through which underground spaces influence near-ground CO concentrations at the micro-scale. This multi-scale approach will facilitate the mutual verification and refinement of research conclusions, ensuring a more robust understanding of the nexus between underground development and the near-ground environment.
