Therapeutic effects of cold plasma-activated water on MCF7 breast cancer tumors in a mouse model
Authors: Ghada Abd El-Reda, Manal A M Mahmoud, Fatma Abo Zakaib Ali, Fatma M Abdel-Maksoud, Asmaa M Zahran, F M El-Hossary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer is the most common cancer affecting women, and plasma-activated water (PAW) is emerging as a potential therapeutic approach. While early results are encouraging, additional research is necessary to assess its long-term effects and identify the most effective methods of application. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of PAW on an MCF7 breast cancer mouse model.MethodsA total of eighteen BALB/c mice were divided into three groups (n = 6/group): one negative control group and two tumor groups injected with MCF7 cells. After 91 days of MCF7 injection, the tumor groups received either distilled water (DW) or PAW (for 3 min) twice weekly via oral gavage for 28 days.ResultsThe results revealed that the conductivity, H2O2, and NO2- values were significantly higher for PAW than for DW, while pH was lower (P < 0.001). Hematological analysis showed an increase in neutrophil counts, neutrophil %, eosinophil %, and neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio in MCF7 and PAW groups. Conversely, there were reductions in red blood cells (RBC), platelets (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), and hematocrit (HCT) percentages, while mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) increased. Both the control and PAW groups had higher total antioxidants and levels of α-L-fucosidase, while MCF7 had elevated arginase levels. The CD44⁺/CD24⁻ and CD44⁺/CD24⁺ cell populations were highest in the MCF7 group and reduced in the PAW group. The percentage of parent cells in late apoptosis was significantly higher in the PAW group than in the MCF7 and DW groups. Histopathology analysis revealed carcinoma features in the MCF7 group, while PAW reduced these effects. Immunostaining showed increased ER-α and Caspase-3 expression in the MCF7 group, with PAW moderating expression levels.ConclusionsOverall, these findings suggest that PAW may offer promising therapeutic applications in managing oxidative stress and hindering cancer cell progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-026-01127-x.Keywords: Cold plasma-activated water, Breast cancer, Physicochemical characteristics, Histopathology, Immunohistochemistry staining

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women, standing for 25.2% of female cancer cases and 14.7% of female cancer-related deaths globally [1, 2]. Surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are the main protocols for cancer treatment. Side effects related to each method are numerous, including nausea and vomiting, alopecia, peripheral neuropathy, and myelosuppression in chemotherapy. Long treatment periods affect the mental and psychological conditions of the patients due to prolonged hospitalizations in Radiotherapy [3]. Hence, the development of research projects aimed at the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer is a matter of great concern all around the world.

Plasma-activated water is emerging as a promising therapeutic approach for breast cancer, using reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) to selectively target cancer cells while sparing normal cells. Studies have shown that PAW can induce apoptosis in breast cancer cell lines such as MCF7 and AMJ13, with notable reductions in cell viability and increased apoptotic markers like caspase-9 [4, 5]. Alkaline PAW, with its prolonged half-life and higher RONS concentrations, exhibits enhanced anticancer potency compared to acidic PAW [5].

Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate PAW’s effectiveness in reducing cancer cell viability and inhibiting tumor growth, supporting its clinical potential [5, 6]. Using argon plasma-stimulated water had the strongest effect on inducing apoptosis in cultured human breast cancer cells compared to helium and nitrogen [7]. The PAW generates a cocktail of reactive species, and oral administration of this cocktail to mice showed no toxicities even at the highest dose of PAW, after a single dose and repeated doses for 28 d in mice. The plasma solution that resulted from a 30-minute treatment of DI water using helium as carrier gas had a significant effect on the rate of apoptosis in breast (MDA-MD-231) and gastric (NCI-N87) cancer cells [8].

While results are encouraging, more research is required to explore long-term effects and optimal application methods. Therefore, this study was conducted to investigate the effects of the PAW on the MCF7 breast cancer mice model. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to examine the effects of PAW oral gavage on breast cancer treatment in vivo.

Materials and methods

Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) discharge device

PAW synthesis was conducted at the Physics Department, Faculty of Science, Sohag University (Sohag 82524, Egypt), following procedures previously detailed by Abd El-Reda et al. [9, 10]. Briefly, a custom cold atmospheric plasma system with two high‑voltage electrodes powered by an AC supply was used. A stainless‑steel needle electrode (50 kHz, 5 kV) was positioned 5 mm above the surface of 100 mL distilled water in a plastic container, generating an atmospheric air plasma jet directed at the water surface. Atmospheric air served as the working gas, and a stable power output and gas flow were maintained throughout. Water was exposed to plasma for 3 min at room temperature (26 °C), then collected and stored until use.

Physicochemical measurements

The electrical conductivity and pH of DW were measured before and after plasma activation using Adwa waterproof instruments: the EC, TDS, and temperature tester (AD31) and the pH-temperature tester (AD14), both manufactured by Adwa in Romania. Nitrite (NO₂⁻) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) were quantified as stable RNS and ROS indicators [11, 12] using colorimetric assays with Griess reagent and H₂O₂ kit (Bio diagnostic, Egypt, CAT. Numbers TA 2533 and HP 25), following the manufacturer’s procedures. On each gavage day, PAW was measured for physicochemical properties immediately before administration, for a total of 8 samples over the 4-week period (n = 8).

Ethics statement

The experiment adheres to the guidelines outlined in the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The authors confirm their compliance with the Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines [13]. All procedures in this study received approval from the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Assiut University, Egypt, under the OIE standards for the use of animals in research. The ethical approval number for this study was 06/2023/0044.

MCF7 cells and mice model

The human breast cancer MCF7 cells were purchased from VACSERA, 22,311 -Egypt, 51 Wezaret Al Zeraa, Al Agouzah, Dokki, Giza governorate, Egypt. The MCF7 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere [14]. The hormone-assisted tumor induction in non-immunocompromised mice regimen was carried out as follows: Cortisone (4 mg/mL) was administered daily for three consecutive days before tumor induction via subcutaneous injections, with each mouse receiving 0.25 mL of Solu-Cortef; subsequently, the same concentration was added to drinking water on alternating days as described by Dunpall [15]. To initiate tumor formation, mice were administered intramuscular estrogen injections (0.01 mL/kg body weight, Folon 5 mg/mL) twice daily prior to the MCF7 cell injection [16, 17]. A total of 18 female BALB/c, sexually mature, 12-week-old mice weighing 28–32 g each were obtained from the laboratory Animal House of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine at Assiut University and were maintained and monitored in a specific pathogen-free environment. All animals were acclimatized in metal cages in a well-ventilated room for two weeks prior to experimentation. The animals were maintained under standard laboratory conditions (a temperature of 23 °C, a relative humidity of 60–70%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle), fed a diet of standard commercial pellets, and provided with water adlibtum. The animals were randomly assigned to 3 experimental groups (6 mice/group): the negative control group (CN) consisted of healthy, untreated mice; and the other 2 groups (breast tumor groups) were subcutaneously injected with 50 µl PBS containing MCF7cells (7 × 105 cells per mouse) into the inguinal mammary fat pad. Randomization was performed by individually and randomly distributing animals into the respective groups without the use of computer-generated allocation software. For tumor cell inoculation, each mouse was anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection with a mixture of 100 µg ketamine and 10 µg xylazine per gram of their body weight [18]. 92 days after tumor inoculation, mice of the breast tumor (MCF7) group were subdivided into 2 different groups based on the type of water used for oral gavage: (a) DW, and (b) 3-minute PAW. Stainless steel feeding needles were used to oral gavage [19].

Each mouse received 150 µL of either distilled water (DW) or plasma-activated water (PAW) by oral gavage twice weekly for 4 weeks. Animals were monitored daily throughout the experimental period for general health status, behavior, tumor progression, and clinical signs of pain or distress. Predefined humane endpoints included body weight loss exceeding 20% of baseline body weight, impaired mobility, tumor ulceration or necrosis, severe lethargy, or any clinical condition indicating compromised welfare. Notably, animals in all groups remained clinically healthy, active, and alert throughout the study period. Investigators involved in the conduct of the experimental procedures were aware of group allocation. However, all outcome assessments, including hematological parameters, stem cell analyses, immunohistochemical evaluations, and histopathological examinations, were performed by independent investigators who were blinded to treatment allocation. No priori sample size calculation was conducted. A cohort size of six mice per group was selected to achieve adequate experimental comparability while adhering to the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Refinement, and Reduction) to minimize animal use and potential distress. This group size is consistent with those commonly reported in comparable vivo studies evaluating plasma-based therapies in murine breast cancer models. For example, Dezhpour et al. [20] utilized five mice per group, and Bakhtiyari-Ramezani et al. [21] utilized four mice per group in murine breast cancer models.

Blood sample collection

At the end of the experimental period, mice were humanely euthanized in accordance with institutional animal ethics approval. Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation, followed immediately by rapid decapitation to allow prompt collection of blood and other tissues, thereby avoiding the use of chemical anesthetics that could interfere with the biochemical and oxidative stress parameters assessed [22, 23]. To minimize animal distress, all procedures were carried out by trained and experienced personnel. Animals were gently and securely restrained to reduce handling stress, and cervical dislocation was performed swiftly to ensure rapid loss of consciousness before decapitation. Procedures were conducted according to the method described by Sultan [24]. Briefly, the mouse was securely restrained with the left hand, cervical dislocation was applied, and decapitation was immediately performed using sharp scissors. The blood from the trunk was collected in two different Eppendorf tubes: ethylene-diamine-tetra-acetic-acid (EDTA)-coated tubes and plain tubes without anticoagulant were placed on ice. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, for 15 min and the supernatant plasma and sera were transferred into clean tubes and placed at -80 °C. The serum ꭤ-L Fucosidase, Arginase, Total antioxidant, MDA, and NO2−, and the plasma H2O2, levels were measured with the colorimetric assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Biodiagnostic, Egypt).

Hematological picture

Hematological analysis of blood was performed [25] using the hematological analyzer Exigo, Boule Medical AB, Stockholm, Sweden. Hematological analysis of blood was performed, including RBCcount, white blood cell (WBC) count, HGB concentration, HCT%, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), MCH, MCHC, PLT count, lymphocyte percentage, monocyte percentage, neutrophil percentage, eosinophil percentage, and basophil percentage.

CD44/ CD24 stem cell differentiation by flow cytometer

The inguinal lymph node tissue specimen was placed in a 35 mm dish with 3 mL of PBS and washed extensively to remove blood and debris. The tissue was held with forceps and scraped using the back of a number 22 scalpel blade to pull cells off the tumor mass, with connective tissue strands removed. Scraping continued until a large slurry of cells was collected. The tumor solution was then pipetted for 3–5 min, placed into a 15 mL conical tube, and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 2 min at 4 °C. After aspirating the media, the cellular pellet was resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric analysis. The cell suspension was stained with PE-CD24 (Anti-mouse Monoclonal Antibody, Clone M1/69, Elabscience Biotechnology, Texas, USA), APC-CD44 (Anti-mouse Monoclonal Antibody, Clone G44–26, BD Biosciences, CA, USA), and Annexin V & Propidium iodide (Apoptotic Kit, Catalog No: E-CK-A211, Elabscience Biotechnology, Texas, USA) with Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), then incubated at 4 °C, protected from light. The cells were analyzed using a Becton–Dickinson FACS CANTO flow cytometer at the FCM Unit, Clinical Pathology Department, South Egypt Cancer Institute, Assiut University. Cells were gated based on light scatters, followed by positive gating of CD44 + CD24- and CD44 + CD24+ cells. The expression of Annexin V and propidium iodide was used to distinguish between necrotic cells (positive for propidium iodide only), cells in late apoptosis (positive for both propidium iodide and Annexin V), cells in early apoptosis (positive for Annexin V only), and living cells (negative for both propidium iodide and Annexin V).

Histopathological examination

At the end of the experimental period, after mice decapitation and blood collection, the tissue specimens from the inguinal mammary fat pad were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral buffer formalin. These tissue specimens underwent preparation for staining by being embedded in paraffin wax blocks, sectioned at a thickness of 3–5 μm, and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to the method outlined by Bancroft & Gamble [26]. Subsequently, all tissue sections were histopathologically examined and documented using an OLYMPUS CX43 microscope, with images captured by an OLYMPUS DP72 camera adapted to the microscope, found in the Department of Pathology and Clinical Pathology at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Sohag University. Histological scores of breast cancer were evaluated quantitatively based on the Nottingham Histological Score system [27], by investigators blinded to experimental groups. The score was given based on the amount of gland formation, nuclear features, and mitotic activity. Each of these features was scored from 1 to 3, and then the scores were added to create a final score ranging from 3 to 9 [28].

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemistry staining was performed according to the previously reported studies [29, 30]. The paraffin sections of the mammary tissue from all the different experimental groups were incubated with 3% H2O2 for 30 min at RT to block the endogenous peroxidase activity. The sections were washed with PBS (3 × 5 min). After rinsing with distilled water, the sections were incubated with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and placed in a microwave oven till boiling for antigen retrieval. After rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the sections were blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (NDS) and 0.2% Triton-X100/PBS for 2 h at RT. Then, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies Caspase-3 (1:50, catalog no. A11953-Abclonal) and ER-α (1:50, catalog no. A24421- Abclonal company). The sections were rinsed for 3 × 5 min with PBS at 7.4 and then incubated with a secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Then the sections were incubated for 5–10 min at room temperature with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and substrate-chromogen that produced a brown color at the antigen site. The slides were then counterstained with Harris hematoxylin for 30 s. The sections were dehydrated with 90% ethanol alcohol, then 100% II, cleared in xylene, and covered using DPX.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) (n = 6 per group). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, differences among groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, including histopathological lesion scores, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by the Mann–Whitney U test for pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Current work results revealed that PAW showed significantly higher conductivity, H₂O₂, NO₂⁻, and lower pH than DW. Hematological analysis indicated increased neutrophil counts, neutrophil %, eosinophil %, and neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio in MCF7 and PAW groups, with reduced RBC, PLT, HGB, and HCT% but increased MCHC. Control and PAW had higher total antioxidants and α-L-fucosidase, while MCF7 had elevated arginase. CD44+/CD24- and CD44+/CD24 + cell populations were higher in MCF7 and PAW. Late apoptosis parents’ cell percentages were significantly higher in PAW in comparison with MCF7 and DW. PAW triggers oxidative damage and late apoptosis in MCF7 tumor cells, while the more resistant CD44⁺/CD24⁻ subpopulation survives, leading to an apparent enrichment of this phenotype within the context of overall increased apoptosis.

Histopathology revealed carcinoma features in MCF7, while PAW reduced these effects. Immunostaining showed increased ER-α and Caspase-3 in MCF7, with PAW moderating expression levels.

The current study’s findings, which show significantly higher conductivity, H₂O₂, and NO₂⁻ levels, alongside a lower pH in PAW compared to DW, are consistent with previous research on PAW. Plasma discharge in water generates reactive species such as NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻, O₂, and H₂O₂, which contribute to an acidic environment with increased conductivity due to dissolved ions [31–37].

The observed reduction in RBC, PLT, HGB, and HCT% in MCF7 and PAW groups suggests anemia potentially linked to tumor-induced effects [38–40]. This contrasts with prior studies where MCF7-treated, non-immunocompromised models retained normal RBC levels, likely due to limited external tumor growth [15, 16]. In contrast, a study reported that healthy mice administered PAW as their drinking water source for 90 days (about 3 months) showed no alterations in hematological parameters, including RBC, WBC, HGB, PCV, and PLT counts [41].

The current study shows significantly elevated neutrophil counts and percentages, and neutrophil/ lymphocyte ratio in the MCF7 and PAW groups, indicating an inflammatory response often associated with tumor presence. This aligns with prior studies showing increased total WBC counts in MCF7 models, suggesting an immune response to tumor growth [38, 39]. On the contrary, rats treated with MCF7 for 70 days showed a decrease in overall WBC count [16]. A similar finding was reported in immunodeficient nude mice exposed to oral lavage with PAW for 2 min and 4 min over 2 weeks. The mice showed no significant haematological changes overall, but increased monocytes and neutrophils, suggesting PAW may enhance immune response [42].

The significantly higher levels of total antioxidants and α-L-fucosidase in both the control and PAW groups compared to MCF7 suggest a protective effect against cancer progression [43]. In this context, the reduced α-L-fucosidase level in the MCF7 group likely reflects cancer-associated suppression of this lysosomal enzyme, which is known to possess tumor-suppressive properties through its role in regulating glycoprotein turnover and related cell signaling pathways [43, 44]. In contrast, the elevated levels detected in the Control and PAW groups may indicate preservation or partial restoration of normal enzymatic activity, thereby supporting the proposed protective and anti-tumor potential of PAW treatment.

In contrast, elevated arginase levels in MCF7 cells indicate enhanced tumor aggressiveness, as arginase facilitates the conversion of L-arginine, competing with nitric oxide synthase [45]. Additionally, PAW treatment increased levels of H₂O₂ and nitrite, crucial for inducing selective apoptosis in tumor cells through the generation of peroxynitrite and singlet oxygen [46–48].

In the current study, the percentage of CD44+/CD24- and CD44+/CD24 + cells in MCF7 was 2.03% and 1.00%, respectively, with highly significant differences between MCF7 and Control (P = 0.019 and 0.017, respectively). This is consistent with that shown by other studies [49–57]. Recent evidence indicates that breast cancers remodel lymphatic vessels, enlarging and increasing the number of tumor-draining vessels, which enhances lymph flow, facilitates metastasis to lymph nodes, and may contribute to systemic spread [58]. The proportion of CD44 + CD24- cells is greater in metastatic lymph node foci than in primary breast cancer foci [59]. CD44, a receptor for hyaluronic acid, is one of the most frequently studied surface markers, expressed by nearly all tumor cells. CD24, a heat-stable antigen, is another surface marker found in various tumor types [60]. The current study’s finding of a significantly higher percentage of CD44+/CD24- cells in both MCF7 and PAW groups aligns with existing evidence that CD44 + CD24- cells are a marker for breast cancer stem cells, often linked to tumor initiation, metastasis, and a stem cell-like phenotype [55, 61]. Increased CD44+/CD24- cells are particularly associated with aggressive subtypes, such as triple-negative breast cancer, which is resistant to conventional therapies [52]. CD44+/CD24- cells, known for their cancer stem cell properties, play a pivotal role in tumor progression, therapy resistance, recurrence, and metastasis due to their strong resistance to apoptosis [51, 62].

Notably, PAW has demonstrated potential in enhancing late apoptosis in MCF7-treated mice, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy. PAW treatment significantly increased late apoptosis in CD44 + CD24- (81.67% parent cells, 0.90% total cells; p < 0.001, 0.090) and CD44 + CD24+ (98.43% parent cells, 0.57% total cells; p = 0.001, 0.054) compared to MCF7 and control groups. These results align with findings from other researchers, highlighting the therapeutic potential of anticancer drugs in inducing apoptosis in CD44 + CD24− cells [57]. Todoroki et al. [63] reported higher expression of anti-apoptotic genes, such as Bcl-2 and CFLAR, in CD44+/CD24 − cells using real-time PCR assays.

The concurrent increase in CD44⁺/CD24⁻ cells and late apoptosis following treatment with plasma-activated liquids may reflect differential susceptibility among tumor subpopulations rather than authentic stem cell expansion [64]. These liquids generate reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), inducing oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis in malignant cells [65, 66]. However, CD44⁺/CD24⁻ breast cancer stem-like cells exhibit enhanced antioxidant defenses and superior resistance to ROS-induced apoptosis compared to bulk tumor cells [67, 68]. Thus, PAW selectively eliminates ROS-sensitive non-stem tumor cells while resistant stem-like populations persist, producing apparent enrichment alongside increased overall apoptosis.

The hormone-assisted tumor induction in non-immunocompromised rats and mice was established over 71 and 91 days [15, 16]. Similarly, the current study aligns with previous findings, showing that MCF7 successfully induces an early-stage breast cancer model in non-immunocompromised mice, producing limited tumor growth and distinct histopathological changes after 120 days. The current study confirms the efficacy of the xenograft model, with MCF7 inducing fibroadenomas and adenocarcinoma stages, demonstrating breast cancer heterogeneity and mucin overexpression a hallmark influencing tumor adhesion, invasion, and immune evasion [69, 70]. Histopathologically, untreated MCF7-treated mice exhibited extensively proliferated ducts, mucin accumulation, and cribriform patterns, indicating aggressive tumor characteristics [69, 71–73]. In contrast, PAW treatment reduced these alterations, restoring tissue to near-normal morphology. This aligns with the results of Bakhtiyari-Ramezani et al. [21], who found that cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) therapy in a triple-negative breast cancer mouse model significantly reduced Nottingham histological scores and decreased the degree of mitosis and nuclear pleomorphism.

The current study demonstrates that PAW modulates key markers of apoptosis (Caspase-3) and estrogen signaling (Erα), impacting cancer progression in MCF7 injected mice. Notably, PAW-treated groups display reduced ERα and Caspase-3 staining intensity, suggesting PAW’s role in moderating these markers. In control mice, weak ERα and Caspase-3 expression in mammary tissues indicates normal regulatory levels, whereas MCF7-treated mice exhibit significantly elevated ERα and Caspase-3 levels, reflective of dysregulated apoptosis and estrogen-driven proliferation in breast cancer [74–79]. Our results showing that PAW treatment regulates the apoptotic process by moderating marker expression are strongly supported by recent literature investigating various plasma-activated liquids. Dezhpour et al. [20] found that combining plasma-activated medium (PAM) with conventional chemotherapy significantly increased the expression of cleaved-caspase-3 in MCF7 cells, which successfully initiated mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. Similarly, research by Veronico et al. [6] demonstrated that plasma-treated water solutions (PTWS) supplemented with organic molecules led to a massive production of intracellular ROS and significantly increased the levels of cleaved caspase-3 in MCF7 cells via the p-JNK signaling pathway. These mechanisms are further corroborated by Almeida-Ferreira et al. [80], who observed a significant loss of procaspase 3 (a surrogate marker for its activation into executioner Caspase-3) alongside an increased BAX/BCL2 ratio in MCF7 cells following plasma exposure. Collectively, these studies suggest that the therapeutic efficacy of PAW stems from its ability to overcome cellular antioxidant defenses and trigger regulated cell death.

While original MCF-7 cells are Caspase-3 deficient due to a 47-bp deletion in exon 3 that abrogates protein translation [81], clinical evidence indicates that Caspase-3 expression often increases in parallel with histological aggressiveness and tumor progression [82]. This paradox is reconciled by the protease’s non-apoptotic, tumor-promoting roles: it drives tumor repopulation through the “Phoenix Rising” pathway by activating iPLA2 to release PGE2 [83, 84] and stimulates angiogenesis via VEGF-A upregulation [83]. Furthermore, the anti-apoptotic splice variant Caspase-3s acts as a dominant-negative regulator; a high Caspase-3s/Caspase-3 ratio is significantly associated with chemoresistance in locally advanced breast cancer [85]. Thus, elevated Caspase-3 staining in aggressive tumors typically signals a therapy-resistant, progressive phenotype rather than productive cell death [83]. Accordingly, the finding that PAW increased late apoptosis by flow cytometry yet reduced Caspase‑3 staining is coherent: PAW likely promotes a more efficient, Caspase‑3–independent terminal apoptosis typical of MCF7 biology, while diminishing aberrant or stromal‑derived Caspase‑3 signals seen in untreated tumors [86–88].

Work limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The use of non-immunocompromised mice may have influenced tumor establishment and growth dynamics, potentially limiting the magnitude of tumor progression compared with immunodeficient xenograft models [15]​. In addition, MCF7 cells are known to exhibit relatively lower rates of proliferation and apoptosis following plasma-based treatment [89]​, which may have affected the observed therapeutic response. The relatively small sample size was selected in accordance with the 3Rs principles to minimize unnecessary animal use and distress; however, this may reduce statistical power and limit the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the four-week treatment period may not fully capture long-term therapeutic efficacy or potential tumor recurrence. Future studies should consider using immunocompromised mice, extending the observation period, and increasing the sample size, to achieve more accurate results and further minimize animal distress.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PAW exhibits unique physicochemical properties that influence both hematological and oxidative stress markers, demonstrating immunomodulatory effects, and potential antioxidant benefits. The increased populations of CD44+/CD24- and CD44+/CD24 + stem cells in MCF7 groups suggest enrichment of stem-like cell subpopulations. Notably, the percentage of late apoptosis in stem parent cells was significantly higher in the PAW treatment compared to both the MCF7 and DW groups. Histopathology showed that PAW treatment reduced carcinoma-related changes observed in MCF7 injected tissues, suggesting a potential protective effect against tumor progression. Immunohistochemical staining further supports that PAW modulates ER-α and Caspase-3 expression, pointing to regulated apoptotic and proliferative processes. Overall, these findings suggest that PAW could have promising therapeutic applications in managing oxidative stress and hindering cancer cell progression.
