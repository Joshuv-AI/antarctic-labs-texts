The triple burden: A systematic review and meta-analysis of essential medicine availability, price, and affordability across Ethiopian health facilities
Authors: Tsegaye Melaku, Dula Dessalegn, Mestawet Getachew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccess to essential medicines is a fundamental component of achieving universal health coverage, as outlined by the World Health Organization's(WHO) framework. Despite various efforts to improve access to essential medicines, there remains a gap in comprehensive data regarding their availability, price, and affordability at health facilities in Ethiopia. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the pooled estimates of availability, price, and affordability of generic essential medicines at health facilities in Ethiopia.MethodsWe conducted a complete search of electronic databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Science Direct, Web of Science, Scopus, African Index Medicus, AJOL, Google Scholar, and Organization repositories such as the Ministry of Health, Ethiopian Pharmaceuticals Supply Service) to find relevant articles on the topic from inception to August 2025. Data was analyzed using STATA version 18.0. Random-effect model meta-analysis and descriptive statistics were performed to determine the pooled availability and affordability, respectively. Statistical significance was considered at a p-value less than 0.05.ResultsA total of 46 studies, encompassing data from 2302 health facilities, were included in the final analysis. The overall mean availability of essential medicines was 63.89 % (± 19.48 SD). In public health facilities, the pooled availability was 64 % (95 % CI: 58 %–70 %), while in private facilities it was lower at 54 % (95 % CI: 42 %–66 %). The pooled proportion of unaffordable medicines was 62 % (95 % CI: 45 %–79 %). Analysis of the Median Price Ratios (MPRs) revealed that, in public health facilities, MPRs ranged from 0.55 to 5.21, with an overall median (IQR) of 1.45 (1.26–2.31). In private health facilities, MPRs ranged from 0.88 to 11.17, with a higher overall median (IQR) of 3.66 (2.12–4.10), reflecting greater price variability and reduced affordability in the private sector.ConclusionThe availability of generic essential medicines in Ethiopia was below the WHO target of 80 % in both public and private health facilities. Prices of these medicines were higher than international reference prices, with significant variations across sectors. About two-thirds of the evaluated medicines were unaffordable. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated efforts, including policy refinement, supply chain improvements, and context-specific interventions, to progressively enhance equitable access to essential medicines across all sectors.Systematic review registration number: PROSPERO CRD420251137000.

Introduction

Access to essential medicines is a fundamental human right that is recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) and is a key component of achieving universal health coverage.1., 2, 3 The WHO uses the same essential medicines definition in low-income settings as elsewhere, selecting drugs that address priority health needs with proven efficacy and safety, cost-effectiveness, and availability, but adapts decisions to local disease patterns and resource limitations.4,5 The complexities surrounding access to essential medicines, particularly generics, in healthcare facilities are multifaceted, encompassing issues of availability, pricing, and affordability that disproportionately affect vulnerable populations. Availability refers to the physical presence of medicines within healthcare facilities, ensuring that patients can readily obtain the treatments they need without facing stockouts or shortages, which can lead to interrupted treatment regimens and adverse health outcomes.6 Price, on the other hand, pertains to the cost of medicines, which can vary significantly depending on factors such as manufacturing processes, distribution channels, and market competition, with inflated prices creating financial barriers for patients, especially those with limited incomes or inadequate insurance coverage.7,8 Affordability, a related but distinct concept, considers the ability of individuals or households to purchase essential medicines without incurring undue financial hardship, taking into account their income levels, healthcare expenditures, and other essential living expenses.9,10

Globally, a significant portion of the population, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), lacks reliable access to essential medicines, with unavailability and unaffordable prices in the private sector acting as major barriers to access.11 In some of the poorest countries in Asia and Africa, the proportion of people without access to essential medicines can be as high as 50 %.12,13 Essential generic medicine availability is low to moderate in public healthcare settings serving vulnerable populations.14 Several studies report that in the public sector, generic availability ranges from 1.43 % to 68.3 %, frequently below the WHO's target of 80 %, while private facilities report availability between 31.5 % and 77.4 %.12., 13, 14, 15. Pricing studies show that median price ratios for generics span 0.2 to 19.4 in public outlets versus 0.4 to 26.2 in private ones.15 Studies reveal significant disparities in these areas, underscoring the challenges patients face in accessing necessary medications. Disease (COPD)LMICs, the availability and affordability of essential medicines for specific conditions, such as asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), are particularly poor. A study across 52 countriesfound that inhaled corticosteroids, a critical asthma medicine, were unavailable in many countries, and their prices were often unaffordable for patients.12 Similarly, cardiovascular and hypertension medicines show average availability of 54 % in LMICs versus 60 % in higher-income countries, with generic medicines (61 %) more available than branded versions (41 %).16

The Ethiopian government has undertaken initiatives to enhance access to medicines through various policies and programs, including the National Medicine Policy, the Pharmaceutical Sector Development Program, and the Health Extension Program.17,18 Despite these efforts, the availability of essential medicines remains a challenge in many regions of the country, particularly in rural areas where access to healthcare facilities and infrastructure is limited. The elevated costs associated with essential medicines also constitute a significant barrier to access in Ethiopia.19 Numerous households in the nation possess constrained financial resources, and out-of-pocket expenditures for medicines can impose substantial burdens.20,21 This situation may result in the underutilization of healthcare services and suboptimal health outcomes. Moreover, there are several factors influencing essential generic medicine access in vulnerable populations: these include policy implementation (price controls, subsidies), supply chain management (procurement efficiency, distribution), market structure (mark-ups, taxes), and facility-level characteristics (urban vs rural location, facility tier).22., 23, 24, 25., 26

Although research on the pricing, affordability, and availability of essential medicines has been conducted in Ethiopia, this information has not been systematically collated and synthesized to present a comprehensive review of the situation. To the best of our knowledge, there was no systematic review or meta-analysis that addressed the availability, affordability, and pricing of essential medicines in Ethiopia. This review aims to address this gap by conducting a systematic literature review and meta-analysis, thereby providing a comprehensive overview of the current pooled evidence concerning the pricing, affordability, and availability of essential medicines within the Ethiopian context. This systematic review aimed to analyze the availability, pricing, and affordability of essential medicines in Ethiopia. The goal is to provide current evidence-based findings to assist policymakers in improving access to essential medicines and ensuring fairness in their distribution.

Methods

Reporting

This systematic review and meta-analysis was based on a recommended methodology and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis for 202027 (Fig. 1). The results were reported based on the PRISMA statement, and the article screening and selection process were demonstrated through a PRISMA flow diagram. The study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number: CRD420251137000). A protocol was not developed for this systematic review.Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart showing the search and study selection strategy.Fig. 1

PRISMA flowchart showing the search and study selection strategy.

Search strategy

We conducted a complete search of electronic databases, such as PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Science Direct, Web of Science, Scopus, African Index Medicus, AJOL, Google Scholar, and Organization repositories such as the Ministry of Health, Ethiopian Pharmaceuticals Supply Service, to find relevant articles to the topic of interest from inception to August 2025. Articles written in English were searched for in this review. References from retrieved articles were also used to manually search for additional relevant articles. The search strings or terms stemmed from the following keywords: “availability, price, cost, affordability, essential drug, essential medicine, medicine, and Ethiopia”. In the advanced search databases, the search strategy was built based on the above-mentioned terms using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)“ and “All fields” by linking “AND” and “OR” Boolean operator terms, as appropriate (Supplementary file 1).

Study selection and data extraction

In our study, dual independent screening was performed at both the title/abstract and full-text stages. The screening process demonstrated high reliability, with a κ statistic of 0.85 for the title/abstract stage and 0.90 for the full-text stage, indicating strong agreement between reviewers. Two reviewers (TSM and MG) independently selected studies and extracted data. The following data were extracted: (1) general information of the study: title, first author, year of publication, and study design; (2) characteristics of studies: study area, survey method, number of essential medicines investigated, and names of investigated medicines; (3) outcome: availability rate of essential medicines. Reviewers resolved disagreements by discussion to consensus, and if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (DD).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools28 (Supplementary file 2). The following items were used to appraise the included studies: (1) appropriateness of inclusion criteria, (2) description of study subject and setting, (3) valid and reliable measurement of exposure, (4) objective and standard criteria used, (5) identification of confounders, (6) strategies to handle confounder, (7) outcome measurement, and (8) appropriate statistical analysis. Each item was determined by yes, no, unclear, or not applicable. For the overall quality rating of a study, more than 6 scores were considered as high, 4–6 scores as moderate, and < 4 scores as low quality.28

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The pooled availability of essential medicines was estimated using the percentage and its 95 % CI. In this review, we included studies that defined overall medicine availability as the presence of the survey medicines at the specified strength and dosage form in selected retail outlets on the day of the survey. Availability was determined by the overall availability of a selected group of medicines. The following ranges were used to describe the percentage availability29: < 30 %(very low), 30 –<50 % (low), 50–80 % (fairly high),>80 %(high). Although the affordability is estimated as the total number of days' wages the lowest-paid government worker would be required to pay to obtain a full monthly standard dose of the lowest-priced generic medicine as recommended by the WHO and Health Action International,30 because a very limited number of articles addressed based on this concept, we calculated the proportion of unaffordable essential medicines at public health facilities. The other outcome variable included in this review was the Median Price Ratio (MPR): The MPR is a measure used to compare the price of a medication in a local market to an international reference price. It is defined as the ratio of the median local unit price of a medication to the median international reference unit price, typically sourced from the International Medical Products Price Guide.31 A random-effects model was used in this meta-analysis because of anticipated heterogeneity. The heterogeneity was assessed using both the X2 test and the I2 statistics to evaluate the degree of dispersion. Substantial heterogeneity was defined as an I2 value ≥50 %. Meta-regression analysis was performed to investigate the source of heterogeneity. A Funnel plot and Egger's regression test were conducted to assess publication bias. In addition, sensitivity was performed to ensure consistency of pooled results. Data was analyzed using STATA version 18.0. Statistical significance was considered at a p-value less than 0.05.

Definitions of terms

▪Essential medicine availability refers to the proportion of medicine outlets, such as public or private health facilities, where a specific essential medicine is found on the day of the survey. It is a key indicator of access to medicines, and the WHO recommends that essential medicines should be available in at least 80 % of health facilities. These medicines must be present at all times, in adequate quantities, appropriate dosage forms, and of assured quality within functioning health systems.32▪Essential medicine affordability is assessed by calculating how many days' wages the lowest-paid government worker (LPGW) would need to purchase a standard course of treatment for a common condition. Medicine is considered affordable if it costs less than one day's wage of the LPGW. This standardized approach allows for comparisons across countries and helps identify economic barriers to accessing treatment.32▪Essential medicine pricing is evaluated using the Median Price Ratio (MPR), which compares the median local price of a medicine to an international reference price. The MPR is calculated by dividing the local median unit price by the international reference unit price. An MPR of 1 or less indicates that the local price is aligned with or lower than the international benchmark, suggesting fair pricing.32

Results

Search results

Initially, a total of 3675 articles were identified through searches of different databases. Of the identified studies, 2103 were removed before screening. One thousand three hundred sixty-eight studies were excluded after reviewing their abstracts and titles. The full texts of the remaining 204 articles were sought for retrieval, of which 103 were removed, and the remaining (n = 109) included a full assessment based on the eligibility criteria. We again excluded (n = 63) articles. Finally, the review included 46 studies conducted in Ethiopia between 2008 and 2024. Fig. 1 illustrates the process of the literature review, screening, and eligibility assessment of the study articles. Regarding the risk of bias, the overall methodological quality of the included studies was rated to be moderate to high. The majority of studies (41 out of 43, 95.3 %) were deemed to have used an appropriate sample frame for the target population and to have sampled participants appropriately. Furthermore, all studies utilized valid methods to identify the condition, measured it in a standard and reliable way for all participants, and conducted appropriate statistical analyses. The most common methodological limitation pertained to sample size adequacy. Of the 42 studies, 16 studies (38.1 %) were rated ‘No’ for having an inadequate sample size. Additionally, two studies were rated ‘No’ for not describing the study subjects and setting in sufficient detail (Supplementary file 2).

Publication bias

Funnel plots (visual inspection) and Egger and Begg rank statistical tests at the 5 % significance level were used to assess the presence of publication bias. There was no statistically significant evidence of publication bias as shown by Egger's test (β = −0.15, P = 0.884). Similarly, Begg's test confirms that there was no statistically significant publication bias (Kendall's score = −93.00, P = 0.383) (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Funnel plot showing publication bias based on medicine availability.Fig. 6

Meta regression results

Discussion

The WHO recommends that all countries voluntarily aim to achieve a minimum target of 80 % availability of affordable, safe, and quality essential medicines and basic health technologies by 2025. This target is crucial for each nation to realize Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3) and Universal Health Coverage (UHC) within their respective contexts.78 However, meeting this goal remains a significant challenge in LMICS, as evidence indicates inadequate availability and poor affordability. The findings of this review on the prices, availability, and affordability of essential medicines provide valuable insights into Ethiopia's progress toward achieving this objective.

The availability of generic essential medicines in African health facilities exhibits substantial variation across different regions and sectors. The current review shows that the pooled availability of essential medicines was approximately 64 % in the public sector and 54 % in the private sector. Conversely, numerous studies suggest that public facilities frequently demonstrate lower availability compared to private and faith-based institutions. For instance, in Malawi, the overall availability of essential medicines was recorded at 48.5 % within public facilities, 71.1 % in retail pharmacies, and 62.9 % in faith-based facilities.79 Similarly, in Rwanda, the availability of medicines was higher in private pharmacies (71.3 %) compared to public (59.6 %) and faith-based (62.8 %) sectors.80 In Ethiopia, the reduced availability of generic essential medicines in the private sector can be ascribed to the tendency of many private pharmacies to stock originator brands instead of generics. This preference for originator brands impacts the overall availability of generic essential medicines within private facilities.

The availability of generic medicines varies significantly across countries, influenced by economic status, health system infrastructure, and policy frameworks. In LMICs, targeted initiatives have sought to improve access. For example, India's Jan Aushadhi scheme has improved the availability and affordability of generic medicines by capping prices and promoting their use.81 Similarly, China's National Drug Pricing Policy has reduced the prices of essential medicines, but its implementation has led to supply chain disruptions in some regions.82 In Pakistan, the National Drug Pricing Policy of 2018 improved the availability of some medicines but also increased prices, making them less affordable for many patients.83 However, persistent challenges remain, including frequent stock-outs and limited coverage of essential drugs.81 In contrast, high-income countries generally exhibit better availability of generics, yet disparities persist in specific therapeutic areas—particularly in pediatric formulations and mental health medications.84,85 These differences could be due to variations in study scope, timeframes, the specific medicines analyzed, or the geographic regions covered. It is crucial to account for these factors when interpreting and comparing findings across studies. Due to the limited number of studies focusing on specific classes of medicines, subgroup analyses were not performed in this review.

The pricing of generic essential medicines is a critical determinant of their accessibility. In the current review, the Median Price Ratio (MPR) ranged from 0.55 to 5.21 at public health facilities, with an overall median (IQR) of 1.45 (1.26–2.31). However, at private health facilities, the MPR of essential medicines ranged from 0.88 to 11.17, with a higher overall median (IQR) of 3.66 (2.12–4.10). These findings align with global trends observed in similar studies. A broader systematic review across Asia revealed that private-sector medicine prices were consistently higher than those in the public sector.86 Similarly, a study from Peru has also demonstrated that while public sector prices were closer to international reference prices (IRPs), private sector medicines often exceed MPRs of 6, making them unaffordable for low-income populations.86 Generic medicines are typically priced lower than branded drugs, but their prices can still be higher than IRPs in many countries. For example, a study in China found that the MPR for generic medicines was 1.59, indicating that generic prices were 1.59 times higher than IRPs.87 Similarly, in Pakistan, the MPR for selected generic medicines was found to be 1.76 times the IRP, making them less affordable for many patients.88 Furthermore, in Sub-Saharan Africa, the prices of generic medicines can be even higher due to distribution mark-ups and taxes. Similar to the current study, a report from Malawi showed higher MPRs at private health facilities compared to public health facilities. The MPR for generic medicines was 2.54 for faith-based facilities, 2.70 for retail pharmacies, and 4.01 for private clinics.79 Similarly, in Kenya, the MPR for generic medicines in private facilities was significantly higher than in public facilities.89 A study in Malawi found that the median prices of pediatric essential medicines were significantly higher than IRPs, with some medicines costing up to 2.24 times the IRP.90 These high prices contribute to the poor affordability of essential medicines, even for generic versions. The poor availability of essential medicines in the public sector forces patients to rely on the private sector, where medicines are often more expensive. This further exacerbates the problem of unaffordability, as low-income patients are unable to afford the high prices of medicines in the private sector. These variations show the persistent challenge of ensuring affordability and equitable access to essential medicines, highlighting the need for strengthened pricing policies and regulatory frameworks across both public and private healthcare sectors.

Affordability constitutes a pivotal factor in determining access to generic essential medicines. It is conventionally quantified by the number of days' wages requisite for procuring a standard treatment regimen. In numerous LMICs, generic medicines persist as unaffordable for the majority of the population and represent a significant concern in several African nations due to elevated prices and limited wages. In the current review, 62 % of the evaluated medicines were deemed unaffordable within the country. Likewise, studies across developing regions highlight substantial challenges related to medicine affordability. For instance, in Asian countries, Cameron et al.26 reported that 65 % of essential medicines in Malaysia were unaffordable, whereas Khuluza et al.79 found that in Malawi, 56.3 % of prescribed medications exceeded financial capacity. Vulnerable groups such as children, the elderly, and low-income populations are disproportionately impacted by the unaffordability of essential medicines. In Malawi, the availability of pediatric formulations was deficient, with only 38.1 % of essential medicines accessible in public health institutions.90 The affordability of generic essential medicines is affected by numerous factors, including the type of health facility, the sector (public versus private), and the country's economic situation. Moreover, high prices, poor availability, inefficient supply chains, weak regulatory frameworks, lack of generic competition, and cultural and socioeconomic factors can affect the affordability.91 For example, in Rwanda, medicines were more economically accessible within public and faith-based sectors compared to private pharmacies.80 Conversely, in Kenya, elevated retail prices in private establishments rendered medicines less affordable for low-income groups.89 Addressing these factors requires a comprehensive approach that includes improving the availability of essential medicines, promoting generic competition, regulating medicine prices, reducing out-of-pocket payments, and addressing cultural and socioeconomic factors. To improve the availability, pricing, and affordability of generic essential medicines, several strategies can be implemented. Policies that promote the use of generic medicines, such as mandatory generic prescribing and substitution, can reduce costs and improve affordability. Subsidies, reimbursement systems, and social health insurance can reduce the financial burden of essential medicines on patients. Moreover, implementing effective price regulation mechanisms, such as reference pricing and value-based pricing, can reduce the prices of generic medicines and improve affordability.

Strengths and limitations

This research has several strengths. Methodologically, the study included a substantial sample size of 46 studies, from 2302 health facilities, making it a robust representation of the population under investigation. While the current review offers valuable insights, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. We did not calculate affordability based on daily wages due to the limited number of studies that reported using this criterion. Instead, we focused on describing the percentage of unaffordable medicines. Additionally, we were unable to pool the data according to medicine classes, formulations, and patient groups. It lacks the detailed analysis needed to fully understand the economic impact on individuals and the specific challenges faced by different medicine categories and patient demographics. Furthermore, only studies published in English were included, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant research published in other languages. We acknowledge that funnel plots, Begg, and Egger tests have limitations when used with proportion data exhibiting high heterogeneity.

Conclusion

The availability of generic essential medicines in Ethiopia was suboptimal and below the WHO target of 80 % both at public and private health facilities. Essential medicines were priced higher than IRPs in Ethiopia, with considerable MPR variation across sectors, with public health facilities demonstrating lower values than private health facilities. About two-thirds of the medicines evaluated were deemed unaffordable within the country. While significant progress has been made in the country, challenges such as low availability, high prices, and poor affordability persist. Addressing these challenges requires comprehensive policies, strengthened supply chains, and targeted interventions to ensure equitable access to essential medicines for all populations.
