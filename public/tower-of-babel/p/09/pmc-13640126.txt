Sustained seropositivity up to eight years after two-dose inactivated hepatitis A vaccination in Turkish children: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Özlem Aydemir, Mehmet Köroğu, Aziz Öğütlü, Oğuz Karabay, Aslı Vatan, Sema Çetin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTürkiye introduced a two-dose inactivated hepatitis A vaccination program in 2012 as part of the national childhood immunization schedule. However, data on the long-term persistence of vaccine-induced immunity in Turkish children remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the durability of anti-HAV IgG responses following routine childhood hepatitis A vaccination.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted including 551 children who completed the two-dose hepatitis A vaccination schedule and 481 age-matched unvaccinated children. Vaccination status and anti-HAV IgG antibody levels were obtained from electronic hospital records. Serological testing was performed using a chemiluminescence microparticle immunoassay. Anti-HAV IgG levels ≥ 1 mIU/mL were considered protective.ResultsAnti-HAV IgG seropositivity was 97.8% among vaccinated children. Seropositivity rates remained high over time but showed a gradual decline from 99.4% in the first year to 90.0% in the eighth year after vaccination. Despite this decrease, the majority of vaccinated children remained seropositive throughout the follow-up period. In contrast, seropositivity was 38.7% in the unvaccinated group, reflecting natural exposure to hepatitis A virus in the population. Because of the retrospective cross-sectional design, longitudinal antibody changes, clinically confirmed hepatitis A cases, and possible silent HAV infections could not be systematically evaluated.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that a two-dose inactivated hepatitis A vaccination schedule provides sustained serological protection for up to eight years in Turkish children. Although anti-HAV antibody levels gradually declined over time, seropositivity rates remained high throughout the study period, suggesting persistence of immunological protection. The maintenance of immune memory despite decreasing antibody concentrations may contribute to continued protection. Further long-term prospective studies evaluating both humoral and cellular immune responses are needed to better define the duration of protection and assess the potential need for booster vaccination.

Introductıon

Acute hepatitis A virus (HAV) infection is a globally distributed infectious disease that remains a significant public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Although typically self-limiting in children, HAV infection may lead to clinical disease in adolescents and adults, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality [1].

The global incidence of hepatitis A varies considerably around the world, but is more common in low- and middle-income countries. This variation is largely attributed to differences in public health infrastructure and the implementation of routine childhood vaccination programs in developed countries [2]. It is estimated that more than 100 million new HAV infections develop worldwide each year. According to the prevalence rates in the world, there are three different endemicity patterns: high, moderate and low [2].

In Türkiye, HAV infection remains endemic, although the incidence of acute hepatitis A has been decreasing. A shift in the age distribution of hepatitis A infection toward adolescence and young adulthood has been observed in recent years [3]. Viral hepatitis caused by HAV is more common in childhood, but can be seen in all ages and genders. HAV is transmitted from person to person mainly through the fecal-oral route and household transmission [4]. HAV infection is asymptomatic in approximately 70% of children under 6 years of age, while approximately 70% of adolescents and adults are symptomatic. Hospitalization rates are generally 11% to 22%, with rates reaching approximately 40% among adults. Mortality rates due to HAV infection are 0.3% overall and ≥ 1.8% among those > 50 years of age and those with chronic liver disease [5–8]. Today, the incidence of childhood HAV infection is decreasing in many countries due to improved socioeconomic conditions, access to clean water, and adequate sanitation [9]. However, the absence of HAV exposure during childhood may lead to higher susceptibility and a shift in disease incidence to later life, resulting in an increase in symptomatic cases, as age is a risk factor for the severity of HAV infections [9] .

Considering the main transmission route of Hepatitis A infection, preventing the virus from spreading through food, water and the environment is the most important control method, while another method of protection is vaccination.

WHO recommends including HAV vaccination in national vaccination programmes for children ≥ 12 months of age in countries with high and moderate epidemiological endemicity [2]. Both live attenuated and inactivated hepatitis A vaccines have been developed; however, live attenuated vaccines are mainly used in China and a limited number of Asian countries, whereas inactivated vaccines are used in most European countries and Türkiye [10]. These vaccines can be administered alone or in combination with other vaccines (in the case of non-live vaccines) [1]. A 2-dose schedule with vaccinations at 6–18 month intervals is widely used in national immunization programs as it has been shown to provide long-term protection [11] .

Inactivated HAV vaccines are approved for administration in two-dose schedules with the first dose given at ≥ 12 months of age and a flexible interval between doses (six months to five years; usually six to 18 months) [12, 13]. However, there is increasing evidence that a single-dose HAV vaccination program is effective in preventing diseases [12]. In recent years, in some South American countries, considering the incidence of acute hepatitis A and the ease of vaccination, the use of single-dose UV programs in children has been on the agenda [11, 12]. However, this application is not currently an approved program for inactivated HAV vaccines.

Before the introduction of routine childhood vaccination, hepatitis A represented a considerable public health burden in Türkiye. Previous national surveillance data demonstrated intermediate endemicity, with a substantial proportion of infections occurring during childhood [3, 14] Following the implementation of universal childhood immunization, a marked decline in reported hepatitis A incidence was observed [14, 15]. Nevertheless, HAV transmission has not been completely eliminated, and sporadic outbreaks continue to occur, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations and among susceptible adolescents and young adults [3, 14, 15].

In Türkiye, the vaccine is administered to children born on or after March 1, 2011 in two doses at 18 and 24 months of age [16]. The effect of inactivated HAV vaccines administered in two doses in children on the incidence of hepatitis A has been shown in many countries at both national and regional levels. However, as a result of our literature review, it was seen that although there are studies on HAV seroprevalence in Türkiye, data on the long-term effectiveness and durability of vaccine-induced immunity following routine childhood hepatitis A vaccination remain limited [3, 14–18]. The aim of this study was to assess the long-term persistence of anti-HAV antibodies and vaccine-induced serological protection following routine two-dose inactivated hepatitis A vaccination in children.

Materials and methods

A total of 551 children, 297 (53.9%) male and 254 female, who were included in the vaccination program in our country and were born on or after March 1, 2011 and received two doses of the vaccine were included in the study. The unvaccinated control group was included to estimate the back ground seroprevalence of naturally acquired anti-HAV antibodies in children who had not received hepatitis A vaccination and to provide a comparison with vaccine-induced seropositivity. This retrospective observational study was conducted at Sakarya University Training and Research Hospital (Fig. 1).

Patients born after March 1, 2011, who applied to our hospital pediatric outpatient clinics and were requested for Anti-HAV IgG were retrospectively scanned from the hospital automation system and included in the study. Vaccination status was verified using electronic vaccination records available in the hospital information system. The patients’ vaccination schedules, number of doses of vaccine administered, vaccination dates and Anti-HAV IgG levels were examined from retrospective records. Individuals born before March 1, 2011 and therefore not eligible for routine childhood hepatitis A vaccination were excluded from the study. Antibody titers against Hepatitis A were studied using the chemiluminescence microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) method (Architect I2000, Abbott, USA). Protective Anti-HAV IgG titers were defined as ≥ 1 mIU/mL. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine for this study (dated 28.03.2023 and numbered 175).

Results

A total of 1,032 children were included in the study, of whom 551 (53.4%) had completed the two-dose hepatitis A vaccination schedule and 481 (46.6%) had not received hepatitis A vaccination. Among vaccinated children, 297 (53.9%) were male and 254 (46.1%) were female.

Of the patients who completed the two-dose vaccination schedule, 539 (97.8%) were anti-HAV IgG positive, whereas 12 (2.1%) were anti-HAV IgG negative. Of the anti-HAV IgG-negative patients, 10 (83.3%) had an underlying chronic disease including immunodeficiency or malignancy. Seropositivity rates and mean antibody titers according to the time of antibody measurement after vaccination

Of the 551 vaccinated children, antibody levels were measured in 170 (30.8%) during the first year after completion of the vaccination schedule, in 118 (21.4%) during the second year, in 72 (13.1%) during the third year, in 80 (14.5%) during the fourth year, in 48 (8.7%) during the fifth year, in 26 (4.7%) during the sixth year, in 17 (3.0%) during the seventh year, and in 20 (3.6%) during the eighth year after vaccination (Table 1).

Post-vaccination seropositivity rates remained high throughout the study period but gradually declined over time. Seropositivity rates were 99.4% in the first year, 99.2% in the second year, 98.6% in the third year, 97.5% in the fourth year, 95.8% in the fifth year, 92.3% in the sixth year, 94.1% in the seventh year, and 90.0% in the eighth year after vaccination (Table 1). Although seropositivity rates gradually declined over time, protective anti-HAV antibodies remained detectable in the majority of vaccinated children throughout the eight-year observation period.

Mean anti-HAV IgG concentrations also showed a declining trend over time, decreasing from 12.17 mIU/mL during the first year after vaccination to 5.5 mIU/mL in the eighth year (Table 1).

Of the 481 patients in the unvaccinated control group, 295 (61.3%) were anti-HAV IgG negative and 186 (38.7%) were anti-HAV IgG positive. The control group was included to estimate the background seroprevalence of naturally acquired anti-HAV antibodies and to provide a comparison with vaccine-induced seropositivity.

Dıscussıon

Improving socioeconomic conditions, including access to clean water and adequate sanitation, contributes to the control of HAV infection, while immunization is considered the most effective way to prevent this disease. Universal childhood hepatitis A vaccination has progressively been adopted worldwide and has resulted in substantial reductions in HAV incidence in many countries [16].

HAV vaccination has been included in routine vaccination programs in many countries around the world since 1999 [19–23]. In Türkiye, hepatitis A vaccines have been included in the routine vaccination program since 2012 to be administered at 18–24 months, and since this date, inactivated hepatitis A vaccine administration continues in the routine vaccination schedule [19].

This study aimed to evaluate long-term anti-HAV seropositivity and the persistence of vaccine-induced protection among children vaccinated within the national immunization program. Because the duration of protection following routine childhood HAV vaccination remains an important public health consideration, long-term monitoring of anti-HAV seropositivity is essential for assessing vaccine effectiveness and informing future vaccination strategies [1, 24].

To our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating long-term anti-HAV seropositivity following routine childhood hepatitis A vaccination within the Turkish National Immunization Program. In our study, 97.8% of children included in the vaccination program remained seropositive during the eight-year follow-up period, whereas the seropositivity rate in the unvaccinated control group was 38.7%. The proportion of unvaccinated children included in the control group was determined by the study design; therefore, the proportion of unvaccinated children in the study population should not be considered representative of hepatitis A vaccination coverage in the population.

The inclusion of an unvaccinated control group enabled comparison of vaccine-induced immunity with naturally acquired immunity. The substantially lower seropositivity rate observed in the control group suggests that a considerable proportion of unvaccinated children remain susceptible to HAV infection. In addition, the relatively low seropositivity observed among unvaccinated children may reflect reduced HAV circulation following implementation of the national vaccination program, suggesting a potential indirect population-level benefit of vaccination.

In the eight-year period following vaccination, it was observed that antibodies became negative or did not form at all in twelve children (2.1%) in various years. Eleven of these children had underlying chronic conditions such as malignancy or immunodeficiency, whereas only two had no identified comorbidity. Since our study was not prospective, we could not observe the changes in antibody titers of the same patients in the following years after vaccination. Therefore, we could not find an answer to the question of whether the patients with negative antibody titers did not develop an antibody response after vaccination or whether the antibody titers that did occur became negative over time. This constituted the biggest limitation of our study. In addition, because of the retrospective design of the study, information regarding clinically confirmed hepatitis A cases, asymptomatic HAV infections, and possible natural boosting events was not systematically available. Therefore, it could not be determined whether silent HAV exposure contributed to antibody persistence in either the vaccinated or control groups.

Our findings are consistent with previous long-term follow-up studies reporting seropositivity rates above 95% for up to 10 years after vaccination [12, 25, 26]. Patricia Juliao et al. evaluated patients in terms of seropositivity in the eighth and tenth years after two doses of vaccination and reported the seropositivity rate as 97.7% in the eighth year and 96.3% in the tenth year. In the same study, the seropositivity rate in the single-dose vaccine group was found to be 74.3% in the eighth year and 71.9% in the tenth year [20]. Compared with previous studies reporting seropositivity rates above 95% after 8–10 years of follow-up, the lower seropositivity observed in some of our later follow-up periods may be explained by differences in study design, sample size, laboratory methods, seropositivity thresholds, vaccine formulations, and host characteristics. In addition, a considerable proportion of seronegative children in our cohort had underlying chronic diseases that may have adversely affected vaccine responsiveness or long-term antibody persistence.

In our study, it was observed that the seropositivity rates in children who received double doses of inactivated HAV vaccine decreased over the years. Nevertheless, declining antibody levels do not necessarily indicate loss of protection. Long-term protection against HAV is believed to depend not only on circulating antibodies but also on immunological memory. Previous studies have demonstrated that vaccinated individuals may retain the ability to mount a rapid anamnestic immune response following HAV exposure even when antibody concentrations decline to very low or undetectable levels. Therefore, measurable circulating antibody concentrations may not fully reflect the duration of vaccine-induced protection [1].

Despite methodological differences among studies, anti-HAV seropositivity rates of ≥ 90% are consistently reported in vaccinated children, and current evidence suggests that protection may persist for at least 30–40 years [1]. In our study, antibody measurement was performed using the chemiluminescence microparticle immunoassay method. It was observed that the antibody levels of the patients tended to decrease over the years.

It has also been reported in studies that long-term surveillance studies are needed to evaluate the full protection period of vaccines and the need for a booster dose later in life in countries implementing national vaccination programs [1].

Strengths and limitations: A major strength of this study is that it provides, to our knowledge, the first evaluation of long-term anti-HAV seropositivity following routine childhood hepatitis A vaccination within the Turkish National Immunization Program and includes both vaccinated and age-matched unvaccinated children. The inclusion of an unvaccinated control group allowed comparison of vaccine-induced immunity with naturally acquired immunity. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design did not allow longitudinal follow-up of antibody concentrations in the same individuals. Second, asymptomatic HAV infections and natural boosting events could not be systematically evaluated. Third, cellular immune responses were not assessed, and therefore long-term protection may be underestimated when based solely on circulating antibody concentrations.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that two-dose inactivated hepatitis A vaccination provides sustained serological protection for up to eight years in Turkish children. Although anti-HAV antibody levels gradually declined over time, protective seropositivity remained high throughout the study period. Given the relatively limited duration of follow-up, longer-term prospective studies incorporating both humoral and cellular immune responses are warranted to better define the duration of protection and to assess the potential need for booster vaccination later in life.
