Prevalence of temporomandibular disorders and their association with psychosocial and behavioural factors among medical and dental postgraduate residents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: ShahrukhAli Khan, Hassan Yaqoob, Syeda Farah Jabeen, Taimur Khalid, Syed Murtaza Raza Kazmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemporomandibular disorders (TMD) are multifactorial conditions influenced by biological, behavioral, and psychological factors. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of TMD and to examine its association with psycho-emotional factors and oral parafunctional behaviors among postgraduate medical and dental residents using the diagnostic criteria for Temporomandibular disorders (DC/TMD).MethodsA cross-sectional analytical survey was conducted from May 2023 to January 2024 among 220 medical and dental postgraduate residents at a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan, selected through proportionate stratified sampling. Participants completed a structured questionnaire covering socio-demographics, DC/TMD Axis I and II, the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4, PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7), and the Oral Behaviors Checklist (OBC). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression at a 95% confidence level.ResultsThe prevalence of undiagnosed TMD was 10%, higher among females (63.64%) and dental postgraduate trainees (28.55%). BDS qualification was significantly associated with TMD (adjusted OR: 4.28; 95% CI: 1.56–11.79). Frequent symptoms included intermittent jaw pain (80%), headaches (50%), and joint noises (50%). Participants with TMD had significantly higher mean scores for anxiety, depression, distress, and OBC (p < 0.05). Each unit increase in OBC score was associated with a 1.08-fold increase in TMD odds. No significant association was observed with workload or residency year.ConclusionsTMD in postgraduate residents is linked to psycho-emotional stress and oral parafunctional behaviors, with dental residents at greater risk. Early screening, ergonomic interventions, behavioral modification, and integrated mental health support are recommended to reduce long-term orofacial morbidity in this population.Keywords: Temporomandibular disorder, Postgraduate Residents, Anxiety, Distress, Depression, Oral Behaviour

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) comprise a spectrum of musculoskeletal and neuromuscular conditions related to the masticatory muscles, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) complex, and associated structures. They are among the leading causes of non-dental orofacial pain and are recognized as the third most prevalent oral condition, following dental caries and periodontal disease [1]. A recent meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of TMDs at approximately 31% among adults, with the highest incidence between 20 and 40 years of age, and a higher predilection in females [2, 3, 4].

The aetiology of TMD is widely recognized as multifactorial, involving a dynamic interplay of biological, behavioural, and psychological factors [5]. These include trauma, parafunctional habits, psychological stressors, genetic predisposition, and occlusal discrepancies [5]. Rather than acting in isolation, these factors often interact synergistically, influencing both the onset and progression of the disorder. Currently, the biopsychosocial model is widely accepted as the most comprehensive framework for understanding TMD [6]. This model integrates biological, psychological, and social components, offering a holistic perspective on disease development and chronicity [7].

Among the biological and behavioural contributors, parafunctional habits such as bruxism and nail biting are frequently implicated in the pathogenesis of TMD. These activities involve excessive and involuntary use of the masticatory system, subjecting the temporomandibular joint and associated muscles to abnormal mechanical stress [8]. Persistent bruxism may result in muscle fatigue, inflammation, and structural damage to the joint, with repetitive loading significantly contributing to the development and exacerbation of TMD [8].

In addition to biomechanical factors, psycho-emotional influences such as anxiety, stress, and depression contribute to the initiation and perpetuation of TMD [9]. These psychological stressors often exacerbate parafunctional activities, thereby intensifying joint loading [10]. Baig et al. reported the frequency of anxiety and depression among TMD patients as 31.46% and 36.15%, respectively [11]. Additionally, Ohmi et al. established a significant association between TMD and both emotional stress and bruxism [12]. This interplay between emotional distress and oral parafunction underscores the complex nature of TMD pathophysiology and supports the need for a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management.

Clinically, TMD presents with various symptoms, ranging from non-odontogenic toothache, jaw or periauricular pain, and restricted mandibular movement to joint sounds and discomfort [13]. Additionally, extra-articular manifestations such as tinnitus, referred otalgia, headaches, and cervical muscle pain are frequently observed [14]. The recognition of pain as a principal symptom has driven a growing body of research into the epidemiology and etiopathogenesis of TMD, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and comprehensive management.

Pain-related TMD is reported more frequently among university and postgraduate students, a trend attributed to elevated academic stress. Diab et al. noted a prevalence of 73.4% among postgraduates, while Wu et al. reported 31.7% in medical cohorts [15, 16]. This heightened vulnerability is linked to intense academic pressure, especially in final-year students facing performance pressure and uncertainty regarding future careers [17]. These psychosocial stressors contribute to anxiety, disturbed sleep pattern and the development of parafunctional behaviors, which collectively act as precipitating and perpetuating factors in TMD pathogenesis [18]. As such, this population represents a vulnerable group requiring focused preventive attention.

Given the established association between psychological stress, parafunctional behaviours, and temporomandibular disorders (TMD), postgraduate residents represent a high-risk population. However, evidence on the prevalence of TMD and its psycho-emotional correlates in this group remains limited. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no loco-regional study has employed the updated Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) to explore these associations. This study aims to determine the prevalence of TMD among postgraduate residents and investigate its relationship with psychological stress and oral parafunctional habits. The findings may inform the development of targeted screening protocols and institutional interventions to alleviate the burden of TMD and enhance psychological well-being within this vulnerable cohort.

Materials and methods

A cross-sectional analytical survey was conducted from May 2023 to January 2024 among postgraduate residents at a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all the eligible participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [19]. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethical Review Committee of Aga Khan University Hospital, Karachi (ERC:2021-6760-19868).

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi software, based on a previously reported prevalence of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) of 17% among university students [20]. Assuming a 95% confidence level and an absolute precision of ± 5%, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 217 postgraduate residents. At the time of data collection, a total of 516 medical and dental postgraduate residents were enrolled in the training program. To ensure proportional representation across clinical and non-clinical departments, weighted sampling was employed, yielding a target of 217 participants. A total of 220 participants were included to ensure complete attainment of the calculated sample size.

Participants were selected using a probability-based stratified sampling technique, wherein each department constituted a separate stratum. The number of participants recruited from each department was determined proportionate to its total number of residents. Within each stratum, a sampling frame of eligible residents was obtained, and participants were approached consecutively until the required sample size for that department was achieved. The study population comprised postgraduate medical and dental residents aged 25 to 40 years, enrolled in residency levels R-I to R-VI, who voluntarily provided informed consent to participate.

Participants were excluded if they presented with notable comorbid conditions, such as congenital anomalies affecting craniofacial structures or dysfunctions of the stomatognathic system. Additional exclusion criteria included any prior history of trauma involving the head, neck, or spine, including injuries resulting from accidents, physical assault, or sports activities. Individuals with a history of orthodontic treatment, rheumatologic or autoimmune conditions, chronic sinusitis, or those who had experienced odontogenic pain within the past six weeks were also excluded. Furthermore, participants who had used medications such as antidepressants, muscle relaxants, antiepileptics, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) within two weeks prior to the study were not eligible for inclusion. All participants were screened against the exclusion criteria prior to enrolment to ensure study integrity and minimize potential confounding factors.

Data collection

Data were collected through a questionnaire (DC/TMD), a validated diagnostic instrument widely recognized for its high diagnostic accuracy. The questionnaire was disseminated via email using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture; version 11.0.3; Vanderbilt University) to residents enrolled in clinical and non-clinical departments of a tertiary care hospital. The questionnaire consisted of three parts: Demographic characterization of the population, Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) axis I and axis II [21].

Demographics

The questionnaire collected detailed socio-demographic information from the participants, this included age, gender, and basic qualification. Data was also obtained on the type of postgraduate program currently enrolled in and its status, affiliated institution. and satisfaction with the chosen specialty. Participants were further asked to report their total working hours per week, number of night duty hours, and current living arrangement (whether residing with family or in a hostel).

Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD)

From Axis I of the DC/TMD, the TMD Pain Screener and Symptom Questionnaire were utilized to assess the prevalence of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and identify commonly reported symptoms among residents. The DC/TMD demonstrates excellent sensitivity (≥ 0.86) and specificity (≥ 0.98), along with strong inter-examiner reliability (kappa value: 0.8) for assessing pain-related TMD.

From Axis II, the following scales were employed in the present study to evaluate the association between TMD and psycho-emotional factors, including depression, anxiety, distress and oral parafunctional behaviors:

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [21]: The PHQ-9 is a self-reported scale used to assess the severity of depression. In the present study, PHQ-9 severity categories were defined as mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). A score of ≥ 5 was considered indicative of depression.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [21]: The PHQ-9 is a self-reported scale used to assess the severity of depression. In the present study, PHQ-9 severity categories were defined as mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). A score of ≥ 5 was considered indicative of depression.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [21]: The GAD-7 scale was employed to quantify the severity of generalized anxiety disorder. Consistent with established thresholds, GAD-7 scores of 5–9 indicated mild, 10–14 moderate, and 15–21 severe anxiety. A score of ≥ 5 was used as the threshold for identifying anxiety in the study population.

Consistent with established thresholds, GAD-7 scores of 5–9 indicated mild, 10–14 moderate, and 15–21 severe anxiety. Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) [21]: The PHQ-4 is a brief screening tool that combines the PHQ-2 for depression and the GAD-2 for anxiety, providing an overall distress score. In the present study, PHQ-4 scores of 3–5 indicated mild, 6–8 moderate, and 9–12 severe distress. A score of ≥ 3 was used as the threshold for identifying psychological distress.

Oral Behaviors Checklist (OBC) [21]: To evaluate oral parafunctional behaviours, the OBC was utilized and stratified by severity. Scores within the 0–16 range are considered representative of normal behaviour. In contrast, scores between 17 and 24 are twice as prevalent among individuals with TMD, while scores ranging from 25 to 62 are observed 17 times more frequently. Only scores exceeding 24 are identified as a significant risk factor contributing to the clinical onset of TMD.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp.) with a 95% confidence level. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The dependent variable was the presence of temporomandibular disorder. Independent variables included age, gender, qualification (medical vs. dental), specialization, department, residency year, working hours, night duty hours, satisfaction level, anxiety (GAD-7), depression (PHQ-9), stress (PHQ-4), and oral parafunction behaviors (OBC score). Potential confounders considered were age, gender, department, and qualification, which were adjusted for in multivariable models.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Differences in continuous variables between participants with and without TMD were assessed using independent t-tests, while associations with categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square tests.

To identify factors independently associated with TMD, logistic regression analysis was performed. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were computed. In the adjusted model, the effect of each independent variable was controlled for potential confounders (age, gender, department, and qualification). Results are expressed as odds ratios (OR) or adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. This approach ensures that observed associations reflect independent relationships while accounting for confounding effects.

Results

A total of 220 residents were evaluated in this study, with a mean age of 28.38 ± 2.19 years. The cohort was predominantly female, comprising 133 (60.45%) individuals, with males constituting 87 (39.55%). The majority of the participants were postgraduate medical residents (n = 185/220 ; 84.09%), while 35 participants (15.91%) were postgraduate dental residents.

The prevalence of undiagnosed TMD among postgraduate medical and dental residents was found out to be 10% (n = 22/220). The findings reveal a higher prevalence of TMD among females (n = 14/22 ; 63.64%) and among dental residents (n = 10/22 ; 28.57%). Certain specializations, such as Operative Dentistry (n = 4/22 ; 18.18%), Anaesthesiology (n = 3/22 ; 13.64), Cardiology (n = 3/22 ; 13.64), and Orthodontics (n = 3/22 ; 13.64), exhibit a greater proportion of participants reporting TMD.

Most participants in both the TMD positive and negative groups were actively enrolled in their programs (n = 208/220 ; 94.55%). TMD was relatively evenly distributed across the different years of the training program, with a slightly higher prevalence observed in the first year (n = 57/220 ; 25.91%) and fourth year (n = 44/220 ; 20.00%). Most participants expressed satisfaction with their programs, with the majority (n = 125/220 ; 56.82%) rating their satisfaction as “partly satisfied.” However, no significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed in satisfaction levels between the two groups.

Both groups report working more than 60 h per week, including over 24 h of night duty per week. However, there is no significant difference (p < 0.05) in total working hours and night duty hours between the groups. Of the 220 respondents, 72 (32.73%) reported a history of jaw pain, comprising 57 TMD-negative and 15 TMD-positive individuals. When specifically asked about symptoms in the past 30 days, the majority of TMD-negative individuals (39/57; 68.42%) reported no pain, while 18 (31.58%) described intermittent episodes. Discussion

The present study investigated the prevalence of TMD among postgraduate medical and dental residents, with a particular emphasis on its relationship to psycho-emotional factors. The prevalence of previously undiagnosed TMD in this cohort was 10%, aligning with the estimates for the general adult population (5–12%) [22] yet markedly lower than rates reported among medical students, who are consistently identified as having greater susceptibility to TMD and oral dysfunction [23].This disparity is noteworthy as studies employing the same diagnostic criteria used in the present investigation have reported prevalence rates among medical students of up to 31.7% [16]. In comparison, those using non–DC/TMD instruments such as the Helkimo Index [24] and the Fonseca Anamnestic Index, [25] have documented even higher rates, reaching 59%–60% [26, 27]. Although these instruments are highly sensitive, they lack specificity and standardization [28]. This methodological limitation reinforces the value of our use of the standardized DC/TMD criteria, which provide greater diagnostic precision and comparability across studies.

Beyond diagnostic methods, strict exclusion criteria and demographic characteristics particularly age may also explain the lower prevalence observed in our cohort. A longitudinal study has shown that TMD prevalence peaks after the age of 30 [26]. In contrast, the mean age of participants in the present study was 28 years, indicating that many had not yet reached the age range associated with the highest risk. This age distribution may therefore have contributed to the reduced prevalence observed in our study.

Gender emerged as a significant determinant of TMD risk in our study, with females comprising 63.64% of affected individuals. This finding aligns with current evidence suggesting that women have approximately twice the risk of developing TMD compared to men [27], a disparity attributed to hormonal influences affecting muscle and joint sensitivity, differences in stress levels, and variations in pain perception [29, 30]. Estrogen and progesterone are known to modulate connective tissue properties in the temporomandibular joint, potentially increasing female susceptibility during reproductive years [31]. Nonetheless, some studies have reported a higher prevalence in men or no significant gender association [32, 33], underscoring the need for further investigation.

Temporomandibular disorder showed a notable association with professional discipline, with dental graduates (BDS) exhibiting a higher rate (28.55%) compared to medical graduates (MBBS, 6.48%), aligning with trends reported in earlier studies [34]. This is attributed to the demanding nature of dental practice, which often involves prolonged working periods in ergonomically challenging positions, and the repetitive nature of dental procedures, that strain the neck and jaw muscles and joints, increasing TMD risk [35]. Additionally, high occupational stress, time pressures, and patient management challenges may exacerbate parafunctional behaviours, such as clenching and bruxism, further increasing TMD onset [36]. With an odds ratio of 4.28, BDS holders were at a significantly higher risk compared to MBBS holders. This is further substantiated by the finding that two of the four specialties with the highest TMD prevalence, operative dentistry and orthodontics, belong to dental disciplines. This emphasizes the need for targeted preventive strategies for residents in these high-risk fields.

Although prevalence did not differ significantly across residency years, a modest increase was noted in first- and fourth-year residents. This observed trend might be ascribed to the unique challenges faced by students during these specific periods. The first year typically represents a critical adjustment phase, characterized by high stress from transitioning into a new professional environment and coping with intensified academic demands [37, 38]. The fourth year often coincides with expanded clinical responsibilities, which may similarly heighten psychological and physical stress [39].

Postgraduate training is inherently associated with demanding schedules and increased workload stresses. However, analysis of workload patterns in the present study revealed no statistically significant differences in TMD prevalence between residents working more than 60 h per week, including those undertaking over 24 h of night duty, and those with comparatively fewer working hours. While excessive working hours, insufficient rest, and sleep deprivation have been implicated in the exacerbation of TMD [40], our findings contrast with those of Kmeid et al. [41], who observed higher TMD severity scores among individuals with longer working hours. This suggests that workload intensity, in isolation, may not be a primary determinant of TMD risk. Instead, a combination of individual predisposition and external stressors such as financial pressures, family obligations, and academic demands may exert a greater influence on its development and persistence.

Clinically, jaw pain emerged as the most frequently reported symptom among participants with TMD, followed by headaches and jaw joint noises. More than half of the affected individuals described intermittent jaw pain, whereas only two reported constant pain persisting for 30 days. Approximately half of the TMD-positive group also experienced headaches and joint noises. These manifestations are well-documented in literature as common clinical features of TMD, [42–44] lending further validity to our findings. The symptom profile also reflects the multifaceted nature of TMD, which often extends beyond the temporomandibular joint to involve associated craniofacial structures.

Participants with TMD exhibited higher mean scores for anxiety, distress, and depression compared to those without TMD. Notably, 68.18% of the TMD-positive group experienced anxiety, 72.72% reported distress, and 77.27% showed symptoms of depression. These findings support the hypothesis that psychosocial factors play a significant role in the development and maintenance of TMD. High levels of anxiety can lead to increased muscle tension and parafunctional habits, [45] while depression and chronic stress can affect pain modulation, alter pain perception, and reduce coping mechanisms, making individuals more susceptible to chronic pain conditions like TMD [46]. However, in our cohort, all participants exhibit mild levels of stress, anxiety, and depression, which may have contributed to the low prevalence of TMD in our representative sample.

The relationship between psychosocial distress and maladaptive oral behaviours further strengthens this interpretation. Participants with TMD recorded higher scores on the Oral Behaviors Checklist (OBC), reflecting habits such as clenching and grinding that are closely linked to TMD onset. Consistent with our findings, Al Shaban KK et al. [33], identified oral behaviours as the most common contributing factor to TMD, with gum chewing (75%), nail or lip biting (18%), and bruxism (7%) among the most frequent. Addressing these psychological aspects through counselling, stress management programs, and relaxation techniques may therefore represent a critical component of comprehensive TMD prevention and management in postgraduate residents.

While this study offers valuable insights into the association between TMD, psycho-emotional factors, and oral parafunctional behaviors among postgraduate residents, it is imperative to acknowledge several limitations inherent in this evaluation. The cross-sectional design of the present study precludes the establishment of temporal or causal relationships between psycho-emotional factors, parafunctional behaviors, and TMD onset. Longitudinal studies would be necessary to delineate causality and assess temporal changes in symptomatology. Secondly, reliance on self-reported questionnaires may have introduced recall and reporting bias, particularly regarding psychological symptoms and parafunctional habits, potentially leading to misclassification and over- or underestimation of TMD prevalence. Clinical examination was not performed; therefore, TMD classification relied solely on questionnaire-based responses, limiting accurate case classification and preventing identification of specific clinical subtypes and their prevalence. Lastly, the single-institution design may have restricted the applicability of these findings to postgraduate residents in different geographic or institutional settings.

Despite these limitations, the findings from this study have important implications for the training and support of medical and dental residents. Given the prevalence of TMD and its associated symptoms among this population, although not significantly greater compared to other studies, there is a need for targeted interventions that address both the physical and psychological aspects of TMD. Ergonomic training to promote proper posture, stress management programs to reduce psychological distress, and interventions to address detrimental oral behaviors should be integrated into residency programs. Additionally, residency programs should consider workload adjustments and ensure adequate rest periods to prevent burnout and its associated health consequences.

Conclusions

This study underscores that temporomandibular disorders in postgraduate residents are not merely a clinical concern but a multifactorial occupational health issue, shaped by psycho-emotional stress and modifiable oral behaviours. Addressing these factors through early screening, ergonomic optimization, and integrated mental health support should be embedded into residency training to protect long-term orofacial function and overall well-being.
