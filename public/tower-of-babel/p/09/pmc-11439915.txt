Establishment of human pluripotent stem cell-derived cortical neurosphere model to study pathomechanisms and chemical toxicity in Kleefstra syndrome
Authors: Andrea Balogh, Mária Bódi-Jakus, Vivien Réka Karl, Tamás Bellák, Balázs Széky, János Farkas, Federica Lamberto, David Novak, Anita Fehér, Melinda Zana, András Dinnyés
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the present study, we aimed to establish and characterize a mature cortical spheroid model system for Kleefstra syndrome (KS) using patient-derived iPSC. We identified key differences in the growth behavior of KS spheroids determined by reduced proliferation marked by low Ki67 and high E-cadherin expression. Conversely, in the spheroid-based neurite outgrowth assay KS outperformed the control neurite outgrowth due to higher BDNF expression. KS spheroids were highly enriched in VGLUT1/2-expressing glutamatergic and ChAT-expressing cholinergic neurons, while TH-positive catecholamine neurons were significantly underrepresented. Furthermore, high NMDAR1 expression was also detected in the KS spheroid, similarly to other patients-derived neuronal cultures, denoting high NMDAR1 expression as a general, KS-specific marker. Control and KS neuronal progenitors and neurospheres were exposed to different toxicants (paraquat, rotenone, bardoxolone, and doxorubicin), and dose-response curves were assessed after acute exposure. Differentiation stage and compound-specific differences were detected with KS neurospheres being the most sensitive to paraquat. Altogether this study describes a robust 3D model system expressing the disease-specific markers and recapitulating the characteristic pathophysiological traits. This platform is suitable for testing developing brain-adverse environmental effects interactions, drug development, and screening towards individual therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72791-4.Keywords: EHMT1, Human induced pluripotent stem cells, Neurospheroid, In vitro toxicology, Kleefstra syndromeSubject terms: Cell biology, Neuroscience, Stem cells, Diseases

Introduction

Rare neurodevelopmental disorders represent a field where the current animal models are very limited in recapitulating the human developing brain, thus seriously limiting our understanding of the vital interactions of the altered fetal genotype and adverse environmental effects during pregnancy potentially contributing to the post-natal severity of the syndromes. The overall aim of this study is to demonstrate that in vitro patient-derived cellular systems are suitable tools to provide valuable information filling the gap in the fetal-environmental interactions and potentially delivering insights on druggable targets and drugs in rare neurodevelopmental diseases.

Kleefstra syndrome (KS), initially called the 9q34 subtelomeric deletion syndrome is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the euchromatin histone methyl transferase 1 (EHMT1) or the deletion of a specific region on chromosome 9 that includes EHMT1 leading to haploinsufficiency of this gene1,2. EHMT1, also known as G9a-like protein (GLP), forms a heteromeric complex with G9a and is responsible for the mono- and di-methylation of histone H3lysine 9 (H3K9) conferring repressive marks that are non-permissive for transcription3,4. KS is defined by distinct facial features, microcephaly, developmental delay, intellectual disability, low muscle tone, and communication difficulties often accompanied by psychiatric symptoms such as apathy, psychosis, autistic features, bipolar mood disorders, and regression in cognitive abilities5–7. Despite several well-characterized animal models, our understanding of how EHMT1 deficiency causes KS is limited. In Drosophila melanogaster EHMT/G9a is responsible for regulation of learning and memory, which could be restored in the mutants during adulthood by re-expression of EHMT indicating that cognitive defects are reversible8. In a mouse model, Balemans et al.9 showed that Ehmt1(+/-) mice revealed reduced activity and exploration, with increased anxiety compared to wildtype mice, and that the hypoactivity is not caused by any motor dysfunction. Furthermore, the same research group showed that pyramidal CA1 hippocampal neurons of Ehmt1+/− mice had a significant reduction in spine density, number of mature spines, and dendritic arborization, indicating postsynaptic deficit10. Yamada et al. reported that aberrant microglia activation due to upregulation of proinflammatory genes and consequent neural inflammation might play a role in the molecular pathology of Ehmt1+/− mouse brain11.

The cellular consequences of EHMT1 deficiency in epithelial cells were identified in a recent paper by Iglesias-Ortega et al. using siRNA to downregulate EHMT1 in a retinal epithelial cell line (RPE1)12. Among the key changes identified were alterations in the morphology and distribution of different subcellular structures, such as the Golgi apparatus, the lysosomes, and different cell adhesion components accompanied by impaired cell proliferation and migration. Human-induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology13 and the subsequent neuronal differentiation allow us to investigate the pathophysiology of neurodevelopmental disorders on single-cell neuronal network level14. Neuronal networks of KS-patient-derived cells exhibit network bursting with a reduced rate, longer duration, and increased temporal irregularity compared to control networks mediated by NMDA receptor subunit 1 15.

The human iPSC-derived 3D models bridge the gap between traditional two-dimensional (2D) mono-layer cultures and animal models. The 3D cultures better replicate the structural complexity of tissues found in vivo, providing a more physiologically relevant environment for cells. This includes the spatial organization, cell-cell, and cell-matrix interactions16,17. Depending on the cell type of origin, the two main human iPSC-derived 3D CNS models are categorized as organoids and neurospheres18,19. The brain organoids are derived directly from hPSC-s, and following a multilayer patterning can reach great complexity over a prolonged time enabling the study of early neuronal differentiation and brain development20,21.

Compared to organoids the neurospheres are less complex and relatively homogenous in their structures and among each other and with restricted cell types obtained from NPCs. As such, the neurospheres represent a more suitable platform for drug screening purposes and toxicology assays22.

Here, we established and characterized a cortical neurospheroid model system derived from low-passaged NPCs obtained from a KS-patient-derived iPSC, generated and characterized previously by our group carrying a heterozygous, de novo, premature termination codon mutation (Trp1138Ter) leading to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay23,24. We identified key differences in the growth behavior of KS spheroids determined by reduced proliferation and distinct cellular composition. In KS cortical neurospheres glutamatergic and cholinergic neurons were overrepresented while the catecholaminergic neurons were almost absent compared to the control.

Next, we addressed the question of how EHMT1 haploinsufficiency is affecting the drug toxicity sensitivity of KS patients. By using four different compounds, we have found significant differences in tolerance depending not only on the genotype but also on the differentiation status of the cells. Neuronal progenitor cells (NPC) were less sensitive to paraquat than their fully matured 3D counterparts, while rotenone, bardoxolone, and doxorubicin were more detrimental to the NPCs. KS spheroids showed extreme sensitivity to paraquat and manifested slightly decreased viability in response to the other compounds tested, pinpointing the importance of the developmental stage in toxicology tests. Overall, we report a well-characterized, robust model system suitable not only for neurodevelopmental studies of the KS pathophysiology but also for drug screening purposes.

Results

Mutation in EHMT1 causes reduced spheroid formation and growth arrest

To generate the 3D neurospheroid culture for the KS cell line we used our well-established protocol for toxicology assays in non-diseased, control cell lines22. Although the spheroid formation has occurred in both cell lines, while the control spheroids manifested a progressive growth during the culture, the growth of the KS spheroids was overtly arrested and their size at the termination of the 3D culture (at day 57th ) was equal to the starting size (Fig. 1a-b). To measure EHMT1 activity in TD57 spheroids we detected the expression of its product H3K9me2 using ICC (Fig. 1c-d). The lower H3K9me2 immunoreactivity detected in KS denotes EHMT1 deficiency and thus downregulated activity. To address the question if the smaller spheroid size of the KS line is the result of a lower proliferation rate and cell cycle arrest, we measured the gene expression of KI67, marker gene of proliferation, and CDKN1A, indicative of cell cycle arrest using qRT-PCR on TD57 day spheroids. We found that the expression of KI67 was significantly lower in KS than in the control (p = 0.004), while CDKN1A showed slightly higher but statistically non-significant expression in the KS (Fig. 1e). To identify whether the lower proliferation rate is evoked by the 3D culture system or is associated with impaired EHMT1 expression, we quantified the expression of both genes in the NPC stage as well. Figure 1f shows that although the trend is similar to the expression levels seen in 3D (lower KI67 expression and higher CDKN1A expression in KS) the difference is not significant. Similar changes were reported by Lee et al.25 in lung cancer cell lines where they used siRNA to silence the EHMT1. The authors found that EHMT1 knockdown reduced spheroid formation in 3D culture and caused cell-cycle arrest by increasing CDKN1A expression. Another aspect of the spheroid formation the authors investigated was the expression of the CDH1 gene encoding E-cadherin responsible for cell adhesion and they have found that its expression decreases when EHMT1 is silenced accounting for the structurally loose spheroid. To verify if cadherin expression is affected in the KS neurospheres we measured both CDH1 (E-cadherin) and CDH2 (N-cadherin) in TD57 spheroids. Conversely to the findings of Lee et al.25 in KS spheroids, there was a massive, more than one-hundred-fold increase in CDH1 expression (p = 0.02) while CDH2 was downregulated (p = 0.003) (Fig. 1e). At the NPC stage, E-cadherin was not detectable, and N-cadherin showed a similar trend to the 3D culture, being strongly downregulated in KS cells (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1f).

Next, we characterized the cellular composition of the spheroids using qRT-PCR and ICC on terminally differentiated spheroids on day 57. Concerning the distribution of the three main cell types there was no significant difference in the two cell lines neither at transcript nor at protein level. The number of MAP2-positive neurons, astrocytes (identified by AQP4 in qRT-PCR or GFAP in ICC), and oligodendrocytes (identified by CALU11 in qRT-PCR or MBP in ICC) were similar (Fig. 2a–c). Regarding the neuronal subtypes, we have found that NMDA receptor (NMDAR) subunit 1 encoded by GRIN1 is significantly overexpressed both on transcript (p = 0.002) and protein level (p = 0.02) (Fig. 2d–f). This result is consistent with the findings of Frega et al. who quantified the NMDAR1 expression in two patients with different EHMT1 loss-of-function mutations and neurons with CRISPR-engineered disruption of EHMT115. Furthermore, glutamatergic VGLUT1/2 (vesicular glutamate transporters 1 and 2), cholinergic ChAT (choline o-acetyltransferase), and catecholaminergic TH (tyrosine hydroxylase) neurons were detected using ICC and qRT-PCR. VGLUT1/2 protein expression was significantly higher in KS spheroids (p = 0.02). ChAT, although higher in KS did not reach a significant difference on protein level (p = 0.05) but it was significantly higher on RNA level (p = 0.006) (Fig. 2f). TH showed significant downregulation in KS both on protein (p = 0.02) and transcript level (p = 0.0002) (Fig. 2d-f). The expression of two additional markers was detected using qRT-PCR. Although both GAD1 (glutamate decarboxylase 1) and SLC6A4 (solute carrier family 6 member 4) showed higher expression in KS spheroids the difference was not significant (Fig. 2f). To detect and quantify the presence of pre-synaptic terminals we used immunostaining against synaptophysin (SYP) and vesicle-associated membrane protein 2 (VAMP2) (Fig. 2d). The level of synaptophysin was slightly elevated, while VAMP2 was significantly overrepresented in KS spheroids (p = 0.02) (Fig. 2e).

Neurite outgrowth assay shows growth advantage in KS neurospheres

To investigate the impact of EHMT1 deficiency in KS on neurite growth we performed a neurite outgrowth assay using TD57 spheroids by plating them on laminin-coated tissue culture plates and culture for three days and neurites were visualized by immunostaining of tubulin beta 3 (TUBB3). We were intrigued by the results as opposite to the growth behavior of the KS spheroids, the neurites of the KS were significantly longer (p = 0.002) and presented a more abundant branching than the control (Fig. 3a-c). To verify if the same difference was true for the spheroid, we measured TUBB3 expression on cryosections of TD57 spheroids. Similar to the results obtained with the neurite outgrowth assay, KS spheroids also contained significantly higher TUBB3 expression (p = 0.02) indicating longer neurites (Fig. 3d-e).

The growth and branching of neurites, among others, is determined by the neurotrophin brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)26. To investigate if BDNF is responsible for the longer neurites in KS we used immunostaining to detect BDNF expression in the neurites of TD57 spheroids. We have found that indeed, BDNF expression is significantly higher in KS than in the control (p = 0.008, Fig. 3f–g, Supp. Figure 1a) accounting for differences in the length and branching of neurites.

To quantify the excitatory synapses between neurites, we performed immunostaining against postsynaptic density protein (PSD95) and synaptophysin (SYP). PSD95, as an essential scaffolding protein during synaptogenesis and neurodevelopment involved in the stabilization of the NMDA receptors by direct binding and anchoring27. Synaptophysin is the most abundant synaptic vesicle membrane protein and facilitates the endocytosis of synaptic vesicles in central neurons28. The expression of both proteins is lower in KS, with only PSD95 reaching significance (p = 0.01) (Fig. 3h–j, Supp. Figure 1b).

Compound- and cell-type-specific toxicity effects

To assess the sensitivity of 3D neutrospheres and neuronal progenitors we exposed them to various doses of four compounds with different mechanisms of toxicity for 72 h. The herbicide paraquat (PQ2+,N, N′-dimethyl-4–4′-bipyridinium) is one of the environmental toxicants associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD) characterized primarily by the loss of dopaminergic neurons29. PQ undergoes redox cycling with cellular diaphorases such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase and nitric oxide synthase (NOS) to yield the monovalent cation PQ+30. From this redox cycle, superoxide is generated, leading to oxidative stress-related cytotoxicity. In our test system, when NPCs were treated with PQ, although the KS had an EC50 value of 61.14 µM, and the control was EC50 = 92.7 µM the difference did not reach statistical significance, and the dose-response curves were overlapping (Fig. 4a; Table 1). Conversely, the mature spheroids showed elevated sensitivity in both cell lines as compared to NPCs (EC50 = 23.7 µM for the control and EC50 = 4.8 µM for KS), with significantly lower cell viability of the KS spheroids at 30 and 10 µM (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4b). The second compound tested was rotenone, a commonly used insecticide in agriculture, known to cause mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS by inhibiting the mitochondrial complex I which, due to dopaminergic-neuron selective toxicity, contributes not only to Parkinson’s disease pathophysiology but it was also shown to exert developmental neurotoxicity in a human brain spheroid model already at non-cytotoxic concentrations29,31. The NPCs of the KS and control cell lines treated with rotenone showed overlapping dose curves, with no significant difference at any concentration tested (Fig. 4a). The EC50 values for rotenone in the case of NPCs were 0.1 µM for the control and 0.07 µM for the KS. When spheroids were exposed to the toxicant significantly higher cell death was noted at 0.1 µM and 0.01 µM rotenone treatment in the KS versus the control (p = 0.0007 and p = 0.01 respectively) (Fig. 4b). The EC50 values obtained for rotenone in 3D cultures were 0.23 µM for the control and 0.11 µM for the KS. When comparing EC50 values for NPC vs. 3D, the trend is opposite to what we measured using paraquat, as both cell lines are more sensitive at the NPC stage than the mature spheroids, although the difference was below statistical significance (Table 1).

The third compound tested was bardoxolone methyl (CDDO-Me), a synthetic triterpenoid that is a robust inducer of the Nrf2 pathway, which by inhibiting NF-κB, leads to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects32. At the NPC stage the cell lines are equally sensitive to the tested bardoxolone doses (EC50 = 0.39 µM for the control and EC50 = 0.31 µM for the KS) (Fig. 4a; Table 1), while in the 3D stage, the dose-response curve of the KS is slightly lower than the control’s, although statistical significance could be detected only at 1 µM (p = 0.04) (Fig. 4b). The EC50 values in spheroid stage for the CONT are 1.76 and 1.6 µM for the KS. Overall, the NPCs of both control and KS were significantly more sensitive than the 3D stage (p = 0.0004 for CONT NPC vs. CONT 3D and p = 0.0002 for KS NPC vs. KS 3D) (Table 1).

Finally, we used doxorubicin (DOX) as it is one of the most effective and widely used chemotherapeutic anti-cancer drugs, crucial to the treatment of a range of neoplasms while causing severe neurotoxicity33. In actively proliferating cells DOX inhibits topoisomerase II, leading to the formation of double-stranded breaks of DNA or direct intercalation with DNA, which in turn inhibits DNA duplication and transcription to mRNA34.In differentiated cells its cytotoxic effect is conferred mainly by mitotoxicity, the formation of free radicals, and lipid oxidation33. At the NPC stage, KS shows slightly higher tolerance at some of the concentrations tested although the difference is not significant and the EC50 value is 0.01 µM for both lines (Fig. 4a; Table 1). At 3D TD57 the two cell lines have similar dose-response curves, and overall, both cell lines show significantly higher tolerance (p = 0.001 for CONT NPC vs. CONT 3D; p = 0.03 for KS NPC vs. KS 3D) than in the NPC stage (Table 1), which again is in line with the different proliferation rates of NPC vs. TD57 with NPCs proliferating at a much higher pace (Fig. 4b).

Regarding the effect of PQ Naspolini et al.35 showed that PQ exposure and the oxidative stress response resulted in decreased glucose transport into neural cells associated with a compensatory increase in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity suggesting the importance of lactate as energy fuel. To address the question if EHMT1 haploinsufficiency impacts LDH expression we measured the transcript levels of the two subunits of LDH, LDHA which has a higher affinity for pyruvate, and LDHB with a higher affinity for lactate. Figure 5 shows that the expression of LDHA is significantly downregulated in the KS line in both maturation stages tested (p = 0.03 for the NPC, and p = 0.002 for the 3D spheroid), while LDHB is not affected by the mutation.

Discussion

Patient-derived, 3D-based CNS modeling of neurodevelopmental diseases represents a valuable tool for personalized medicine and drug development as it recapitulates most of the phenotypic and functional aspects of various brain regions depending on the differentiation protocol applied18,19,36. In this study, we established a 3D corticoid brain model for KS suitable for the identification of disease-related molecular and cellular changes during brain development and drug screening purposes. We found that although the spheroids form similarly to the control cell line, their growth is restricted reflected by the low expression of KI67, high CDH1, and moderately increased CDKN1A, which might be responsible for cell cycle arrest. Our result with a patient-derived model is in line with the findings reported by Fear et al. who used CRISPR single-based gene editing of iPSCs, followed by neuronal cell differentiation to generate EHMT1 haploinsufficiency to model Kleefstra syndrome37,38. The authors reported that E2F and SP1, major transcription factors driving cell proliferation were decreased while the activity of p53 driving the expression of CDKN1A was increased. In a different model system aiming to elucidate the cellular consequences of EHMT1 downregulation Iglesias-Ortega et al. used siRNA to knock down EHMT1 in the RPE1 retinal epithelial cell line12. The authors describe similar outcomes to our data, namely decreased cell proliferation rate due to cell cycle arrest. They also report reduced migration ability of siEHMT1 cells, correlating with higher vinculin and β-catenin expression, proteins responsible for focal and adherens junctions12. β-catenin forms a complex with E-cadherin and this complex is responsible for maintaining the integrity of epithelial cell-cell contact and keeps Wnt/β-catenin signals in check. We show that in KS neurospheres the mRNA level of CDH1 encoding E-cadherin is massively upregulated which can be responsible for the lower cell proliferation seen in KS cells. E-cadherin, a key cell-cell adhesion molecule, plays a significant role in regulating cell proliferation through various signaling pathways, including the Hippo pathway. The Hippo pathway is crucial for controlling organ size, tissue homeostasis, and cell proliferation. E-cadherin regulates YAP1 activity in the Hippo pathway thereby influencing cell proliferation and apoptosis39. Furthermore, it was shown that EHMT1 regulates cancer development in human gastric cancer by regulating the expression of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition marker E-cadherin which is a direct target gene of EHMT1 and overexpression of EHMT1 is associated with tumor stage and lymph node metastasis by inhibiting E-cadherin40. In addition, G9a/GLP (the corresponding protein products of EHMT2/EHMT1) inhibition induced change in morphology in cell lines accompanied by the appearance of smaller neurosphere-like cells in SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cultures41. Whether these cellular defects contribute or not to microcephaly seen in more than 55% of the KS- patients42 is not clear and needs further investigation. Our results together with the cited references demonstrate that EHMT1 has a universal role in basic cellular mechanisms like cell cycle regulation, cell proliferation, survival, and migration regardless of cell type. Furthermore, considering, that we and others showed that EHMT1 is implicated in regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition37,40, it would be intriguing to assess the incidence and progression of cancer in KS patients, which might have been overlooked previously, due to the small group size of rare disease patients.

Conversely to reduced cell proliferation and growth arrest of KS cells, in the spheroid-based neurite outgrowth assay, we observed that the KS spheroid-derived neurites were significantly longer and more abundantly branched than in the control. This is in line with the findings of Benevento et al., who showed that BDNF levels were abnormally increased in the brain of Ehmt1+/− mice due to hypomethylation of the BDNF promoter43. Similarly, in Ehmt1+/− mouse embryonic stem cell-derived neurons Bdnf was repressed by NRSF/REST, and loss of Ehmt1 by reducing the expression of NRSF/REST resulted in increased expression of REST-target genes, among them Bdnf44. We further investigated the synapthogenesis but the increase in neurite outgrowth did not lead to higher connectivity within neuronal networks as assessed by the lower expression of PSD-95 and SYP in the KS line.

Next, we aimed to determine the cellular composition of TD57 neurospheroids. While several lines of evidence indicate that reduced EHMT1 level accelerated neuronal differentiation during time course experiments37,44–46, we did not see a difference in the three main cell types (neuron, astrocyte, oligodendrocyte), as most probably by day 57 of differentiation not only the KS but also the control reached maturation. Instead, we identified major differences and shifts in neuron subtypes in favor of the excitatory neurons marked by excessive ChAT, VGLUT1/2, and NMDAR1 expression. Previously, our group reported that iPSC-derived KS neurons showed higher sensitivity of calcium responses to acetylcholine stimulation24. In the current paper, we demonstrate that mature neuropsheroids from KS also express high ChAT levels contributing to the excitatory phenotype described by Frega et al. manifested in network bursting with a reduced rate, longer duration, and increased temporal irregularity compared to control networks15. The authors show that these changes are mediated by the upregulation of NMDA receptor (NMDAR) subunit 1 encoded by the GRIN1 gene15. These results are in line with our findings regarding higher protein and transcript levels for NMDAR1 and GRIN1 in TD57 KS-patient-derived spheroids. The molecular mechanism accounting for these observations relies on the role of EHMT1 regulating NRSF/REST (repressor element-1 silencing transcription factor/neuron-restrictive silencer factor) function. It has been shown that reduced EHMT1 activity elevated NRSF/REST-target gene expression during neuronal development44 and REST is known to regulate the expression of ChAT, GRIN1, and other cholinergic genes47. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the glutamatergic neurons expressing VGLUT1/2 are also significantly enriched in KS spheroids. VGLUTs are responsible for releasing glutamate into the synaptic cleft and thus their aberrant overexpression renders them excitotoxic48. Whether excitotoxicity due to high VGLUT1/2 and high ChAT expression is part of the pathomechanism of KS is not known, but some of the symptoms like hallucinations, epileptic seizures, and poor sleep seen in these patients are most probably caused and exacerbated by the hyperexcitability due to elevated glutamate, acetylcholine and NMDRA149. The effects of excitatory neurotransmitters are balanced out by serotonin, an inhibitory neurotransmitter involved in emotion and mood. The level of serotonin is regulated by the SLC6A4 protein that transports serotonin from synaptic spaces into presynaptic neurons thus decreasing serotonin availability. So far, there are no studies investigating the serotonin-associated gene expression pathways in KS but is well documented that KS patients manifest anxiety, depression, apathy5, and psychiatric disorders correlated with high SLC6A4 expression50.

Finally, we aimed to assess the chemical-induced oxidative stress and toxicity of the control and KS cell line using an ATP-based viability assay. Data obtained so far on stress tolerance of G9a mutant Drosophila suggests that G9a has a protective function in response to multiple types of stress51. To establish the role of the EHMT1 deficiency in the stress response of the KS-patient-derived cell line, we tested the cytotoxic effect of oxidative stress (induced by paraquat or rotenone), Nrf2 pathway activation (CDDO-Me), and a chemotherapeutic drug (doxorubicin) on neuronal progenitor cells and terminally differentiated neurospheres. EHMT1 is a negative regulator in both NF-κB- and type I interferon-mediated gene induction pathways52 and thus its haploinsufficiency leads to aberrant microglia activation and consequent neural inflammation described in the Ehmt1+/− mouse brains by Yamada et al.11. Bardoxolone-methyl, via inducing the Nrf2 pathway inhibits NF-κB leading to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, and could ameliorate the inflammation-related symptoms provoked by low EHMT1 expression. We show that bardoxolone-methyl exerts a toxic effect in the nanomolar range in the progenitor cell cultures of both cell lines while the differentiated neurospheres became less sensitive, with EC50 value in the micromolar range. We observed a similar trend with doxorubicin treatment. DOX is the first-line chemotherapeutic agent for many types of cancer, and although it poorly crosses the blood-brain barrier it still penetrates the brain at cytotoxic doses leading to thinning of the brain cortex and reduced brain connectivity53. Our group has shown before that DOX had a differentiation stage-specific effect when neurospheroids at different maturity phases were exposed to the drug22. Here we demonstrated that the EC50 of DOX was 1500 fold lower for the undifferentiated progenitors compared to the fully matured neurospheres. The trend observed might be the consequence of the slower proliferation rate of neurospheres vs. NPCs which leads to less DOX intercalation within DNA thus less cell death. In general, it was shown that human iPSC-derived neurons during maturation acquire a complex anti-apoptotic network to protect against cell death and confer neuronal resilience against stress54 which explains the increased sensitivity of NPCs versus the neurospheres in response to the chemicals tested except PQ.

Concerning the effect of the ROS inducers, we found that PQ had the most deleterious effect on KS spheroids, while in the case of the other compounds, the difference in sensitivity was less profound. Our results are in line with the data published by Riahi et al. regarding the increased sensitivity of Drosophila where the loss of the epigenetic regulator G9a causes a shift in the transcriptional and metabolic responses to oxidative stress that leads to decreased survival time upon feeding with paraquat51. Naspolini and colleagues in PQ-treated rats showed that due to energy failure, lactate dehydrogenase was upregulated which suggests the relevance of lactate as energy fuel35. Our findings revealed lower LDHA expression in the KS cell line which might contribute to the low ROS tolerance of this genotype. Furthermore, in healthy rat hippocampal neurons PQ exposure provoked cytotoxicity associated with decreased cell viability and increased glutamate excitotoxicity35, which in our KS model is further exacerbated by the high VGLU1/2 and NMDAR1 expression. Moreover, the decreased LDHA level might be responsible for depression documented in some of the KS patients. The human prefrontal cortex is reported to be highly glycolytic at the resting state and further upregulates its glycolytic capacity upon brain activation, and alterations in the energy metabolism of this brain region are associated with depression55. Yao et al. showed that astrocytic LDHA regulates neuronal excitability and depressive-like behaviors through lactate homeostasis in mice56. Additional research is needed to elucidate if LDHA downregulation seen on the RNA level translates to an energy deficit contributing to and exacerbating the psychopathology seen in KS patients.

LDHA also was demonstrated to negatively correlate with E-cadherin expression particularly in cancer biology. Several studies have investigated the relationship between these two molecules, suggesting that decreased LDHA levels correlate with increased E-cadherin expression inhibiting the epithelial-to-mecenchymal transition57.

Overall we summarize our findings combined with the published results in Fig. 6. In the KS cell line, the decreased EHMT1 activity and downregulated methylation of the CDH1 promoter results in elevated E-cadherin which inhibits cell proliferation, mainly via contact inhibition of proliferation and recruitment of the Hippo pathway39,58, migration, and higher sensitivity to oxidative stress by inhibiting the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 58. Next, the LDHA is downregulated in the KS cell line, which also weakens the cells’ survival and stress response mechanisms, especially during oxidative stress conditions induced by PQ treatment which, at least partially, could be mitigated by anaerobic glycolysis51. Furthermore, the low LDHA levels might enhance the E-cadherin expression, and its negative effects on cell survival and proliferation.

Overall, these observations revealed novel insights into the altered genetic pathways and their interactions in different developmental stages of the KS 3D neuronal network models, potentially revealing druggable targets and differential sensitivity to drugs and environmental or iatrogenic toxicants. Such observations would help pharmacologists, toxicologists, and physicians to understand better the potential risks and solutions in KS or other neurodevelopmental diseases caused by defects in the methylation of histones or DNA, such as Kabuki syndrome59.

It is important to note, that depending on the size of the deleted chromosome fragment, or the position of the mutation causing the EHMT1 haploinsufficiency the genetic and epigenetic modifications and the clinical severity of the syndrome varied broadly in these patients. Hereby we extrapolate our findings with one unique KS patient cell line to other EHMT1 mutations based on the comparison with published datasets, however, the clinical phenotype variability would point to the importance of investigating the cellular level changes on a patient-by-patient manner, ultimately also indicating the need for the personalized therapy approach.

In conclusion, we established a 3D model system expressing the KS-specific markers which recapitulate the characteristic pathophysiological traits and provide a suitable platform for neurodevelopmental studies of the KS pathophysiology, developmental neurotoxicity and also for drug screening and testing individual therapeutic strategies in this rare disease.

Materials and methods

hiPSC generation and neural differentiation

The establishment of an EHMT1 mutant iPSC line from an 11-year-old female KS patient and a healthy control was carried out as published previously by our group24. The KS subject was selected from the clinical cohort of the Autism Foundation’s Outpatient Clinic, Budapest, Hungary. The study was approved by the Research-Ethics Committee of Heim Pál Childrens’ Hospital (permission number KUT-83/2013). Written informed consent was obtained from the healthy donor and the legal guardians of the KS patient before the subjects entered the study. The reprogramming of both KS-patient and healthy donor-derived samples was regulated by the ethical license issued by the Scientific and Research Ethics Committee of the Hungarian Health Science Council for “Production of induced pluripotent stem cells (IPS) from human somatic samples” with the following ID No.: IV/3935-1/2021/EKU in May 2021. All experiments were performed according to the relevant guidelines and regulations.

In brief, the PBMCs were reprogrammed by the four “Yamanaka factors” OCT3/4, SOX2, KLF4, and C-MYC using the integration-free Sendai virus gene-delivery method60. The neural differentiation using iPSC lines at passage number 10 was conducted as previously described by our group24. In brief, iPSC lines were maintained on matrigel (BD Biosciences) in mTESR1 culture medium (Stemcell Technologies) which was replaced by neuronal induction medium (NIM) (1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 and Neurobasal medium supplemented with 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine, and 5 ng/ml bFGF (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific). After one day of culturing cells were passaged and plated onto poly-L-ornithine (Sigma-Aldrich) and laminin (Roche Diagnostics) coated plates in NIM medium supplemented with 5 µg/ ml insulin, (Sigma-Aldrich), 500 ng /ml noggin (R&D Systems) and 10 µM SB431542 (Sigma-Aldrich). After the neuroepithelial cell layer was formed the cells were replated into laminin-coated dishes in NIM medium supplemented with 10 ng/ml−bFGF and 10 ng/ ml−EGF (Promokine) and cultured until neuronal rosettes formed. The neuronal rosettes were manually picked and further cultured to create a masterbank of neural progenitor cells (NPCs).

Cortical neurosphere culture

3D cortical neurosphere cultures were established as described before by our group22. In brief, single-cell suspension of NPCs in passage number 9 was plated onto a low-adherent 96-well plate with 10,000 cells/well in neuronal maintenance medium (NMM), a mixture of 1:1 vol/vol of DMEM/F12 and Neurobasal-A medium, supplemented with 1% N2, 1% B27, 1% NEAA, 2 mML-glutamine, and 50 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin. Spheroids were cultured for 57 days (terminal differentiation day 57: TD57) with half NMM medium change every third day.

Cryosectioning, immunocytochemistry (ICC), and microscopy of 3D neurospheroids

3D samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (PBS) for 1 h at room temperature (RT) and washed 3 times with PBS. Afterward, the samples were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (in PBS) containing 0.01% sodium azide at 4 °C until embedding in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura). The 16 μm parallel sections were made using a cryostat (Leica CM 1860 UV, Leica Biosystems), mounted to Superfrost Ultra Plus Adhesion Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and stored at -20 °C until use.

After 10 min air-drying, the sections were blocked for 1 h at RT with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS, supplemented with 0.01% TritonX-100. Primary antibodies were used overnight at 4 °C as follows: rabbit anti-TUBB3 (1:500, Abcam), chicken anti-MAP2 (1:2000, Abcam), goat anti-GFAP (1:400, Abcam), rabbit anti-VAMP2 (1:250, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-MBP (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), goat anti-ChAT (1:50, Merck), rabbit anti-TH (1:200, Abcam), rabbit anti-NMDAR1 (1:500, Merck), chicken anti-VGLUT1/2 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), mouse anti-SYP (1:100, Abcam), rabbit anti- H3K9me2 (1:250, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Next day, sections were washed in PBS three times, and the immune reaction was completed by Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-chicken (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-mouse (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-goat (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific) applied for 1 h at RT. The sections were covered using Vectashield mounting medium containing DAPI (1.5 µg/ml; Vector Laboratories), which labeled the nuclei of the cells. Negative controls for the secondary antibodies were performed by omitting the primary antibodies. The highest diameter middle sections with immunoreactivity were analyzed using a Leica Stellaris-8 microscope (Leica Microsystems) with Leica Application Suite X software (Leica Microsystems). All images were further processed using the GNU Image Manipulation Program (GIMP 2.10.0). Quantification of the immunocytochemistry data was performed using NIH ImageJ analysis software (imagej.nih.gov/ij) according to Tieng et al. and Kobolák et al.22,61. Briefly, confocal images were taken at ×200 magnification using the Leica Stellaris-8 system with a 20x dry lens (NA: 0.75). Using NIH ImageJ, we applied an automatic threshold to the images to distinguish immunoreactive pixels. Pixels with intensity levels above the threshold were classified as white, while those below the threshold were classified as black. This thresholding ensures that even the darker parts of the spheroid are accurately accounted for. We measured the numbers of immunoreactive pixels for neuronal, glial, and synaptic markers, as well as DAPI, in the middle sections of the four largest neurospheres for each cell line. These measurements were normalized to the number of DAPI-positive nuclei and presented as marker/DAPI ratios ± SEM (p < 0.05).

Neurite outgrowth assay

Spheroids at day 57 were placed on 0.02% Poly-L-Ornithine and 3 µg/cm² laminin (both from Sigma-Aldrich) coated 24-well plates. 72 h after placing them into the plates, spheroids were fixed with 4% PFA (Thermo Scientific) and stained for chicken anti-TUBB3 (1:2000, Chemion) and mouse anti-BDNF (1:1000, Invitrogen) followed by the secondary antibodies A488 and A647. In the other staining TUBB3 was combined with rabbit anti-PSD-95 (1:200, Abcam) and mouse anti-SYP (1:100, Abcam) followed by A647 and A594. All secondary antibodies were purchased from Invitrogen, and used at a dilution of 1:2000. Pictures were taken with ImageXpress Nano Automated Imaging System (Molecular Devices) at a magnification of 10x for neurite outgrowth measurements and 40x for BDNF, PSD-95 and SYP punctuate stainings. For the neurite outgrowth assays, 10x magnification images were merged using ImageXpress Nano Analysis Software to produce a high-quality picture that shows the entire spheroid in one view. The number of neurites was quantified with Sholl analysis using ImageJ Fiji’s Neuroanatomy plugin. The total length of neurites was measured using the neurite outgrowth module of ImageXpress Nano Analysis Software. For quantifying the BDNF, PSD-95, and SYP expression at least twenty random fields were chosen from four spheroids in such a manner to cover the full-length neurites excluding the spheroid body and the halo surrounding it. During the analysis, the integrated signal intensity of the protein of interest was normalized to the TUBB3 area.

.

qRT- PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) which includes the QIAshredder column to remove the DNA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One microgram of RNA was transcribed using the Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with dsDNase for qRT-PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The QIAgility liquid handling robot and the Rotor-Gene Q cycler (Qiagen) were used for the experimental set-up and the qRT-PCR reaction, respectively. The amplification was carried out in 15 µL final volume, containing cDNA corresponding to 5 ng total RNA, 400 ng of forward and reverse primer, and 7.5 µl SYBR Green JumpStart Taq ReadyMix (Sigma-Aldrich). Primers used in the experiments are enlisted in Supplementary Table 1. All qRT-PCR reactions were normalized using GAPDH as a housekeeping gene and analysed using the comparative 2^−ΔΔCt method62.

Toxicity profiling

NPCs were plated in NMM supplemented with 5ng/ml bFGF and 5ng/ml EGFat 30 000 cells / well in Nunc MicroWell 96-well, flat-bottom, optical coverglass base microplate (ThermoFisher), and triplicate wells were treated the following day in 100 µl NMM supplemented with graded doses of chemicals. For the TD57 spheroids, 50% of the spent medium was exchanged with NMM containing the amount of chemicals to yield the specified treatment concentrations. Cell cultures were exposed to the following toxicants for 72 h: paraquat dichloride hydrate (Carbosynth, CAS # 75365-73-0), doxorubicin hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS # 25316-40-9), rotenone (TCI Chemicals, CAS # 83-79-4), bardoxolone methyl (Selleckchem, CAS # 218600-53-4). Vehicle control was used as a regular medium supplemented with 0.1% DMSO. ATP release-based viability assay was performed using the CellTiter-Glo Cell Viability Assay kit for the NPCs (Promega) and with CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay kit for the neurospheres (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The luminescence signal was recorded with a Thermo VarioScan Flash (Thermo Fisher Scientific) plate reader.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad PRISM 8 (CA, USA). Data are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error (SE), with statistical significance accepted at p < 0.05. The detailed statistical methodology for each data analysis is given in the figure.
