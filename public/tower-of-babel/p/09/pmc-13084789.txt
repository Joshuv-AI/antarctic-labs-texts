Carbonic anhydrase 9 as a circulating biomarker and therapeutic target in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma treated with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab
Authors: Yu Sato, Takahiro Kodama, Kazuki Maesaka, Machiko Kai, Kazuhiro Murai, Yuki Tahata, Yoshinobu Saito, Tasuku Nakabori, Kazuyoshi Ohkawa, Satoshi Tanaka, Ryotaro Sakamori, Masanori Miyazaki, Kunimaro Furuta, Hisashi Ishida, Kengo Matsumoto, Seiichi Tawara, Takayuki Yakushijin, Yasutoshi Nozaki, Atsushi Hosui, Akira Nishio, Nobuyuki Tatsumi, Naruyasu Kakita, Changhoon Yoo, Moto Fukai, Akinobu Taketomi, Hayato Hikita, Tomohide Tatsumi, Tetsuo Takehara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Atez/Bev) has become the first-line therapy for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, reliable biomarkers to predict therapeutic response remain lacking.MethodsWe conducted a high-throughput plasma proteomic screen using the Olink proximity extension assay to identify predictive biomarkers in 78 patients with unresectable HCC treated with Atez/Bev. Validation was performed in an independent cohort of 89 patients. Tumorous expression of the lead candidate, carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA9), was examined using transcriptomic and single-cell analyses. Functional studies were performed in a syngeneic mouse model using Car9-overexpressing hepatoma cells, treated with anti-programmed death-ligand 1/vascular endothelial growth factor antibodies, with or without a CA9 inhibitor.ResultsHigh plasma CA9 levels were significantly associated with poor objective response rate, disease control rate, progression-free survival, and overall survival in both discovery and validation cohorts. Multivariable analysis confirmed CA9 as an independent predictor of treatment resistance and poor outcomes. Tumorous CA9 expression correlated strongly with circulating CA9 and was restricted to malignant cells. In vivo, tumors overexpressing Car9 showed resistance to Atez/Bev therapy, characterized by reduced CD8+T cell infiltration, suppressed cytotoxic gene expression, enrichment of M2-like macrophages, and pro-angiogenic signaling. The addition of a CA9 inhibitor reversed resistance and restored antitumor efficacy of Atez/Bev in this model.ConclusionsTumor-derived circulating CA9 is a predictive biomarker and functional driver of resistance to Atez/Bev therapy in HCC. Targeting CA9 may enhance therapeutic response.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Combination immunotherapy with atezolizumab and bevacizumab (Atez/Bev) is the current first-line systemic therapy for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, a substantial proportion of patients exhibit primary or acquired resistance. While tumor immune microenvironment characteristics such as CD8+T cell infiltration or regulatory T-cell exclusion have been linked to treatment response, tissue sampling is often not feasible in clinical practice. Therefore, the development of minimally invasive and clinically applicable biomarkers to predict response to Atez/Bev remains an unmet clinical need.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study identifies circulating carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA9), derived from tumor cells, as a novel blood-based biomarker predicting resistance to Atez/Bev therapy in patients with unresectable HCC. Using proteomic screening and validation cohorts, high pretreatment CA9 levels were associated with lower response rates and worse survival. Mechanistic studies revealed that tumorous CA9 promotes an immunosuppressive, angiogenic tumor microenvironment, and that pharmacologic inhibition of CA9 restores responsiveness to anti-programmed death-ligand 1/vascular endothelial growth factor therapy in vivo.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study provides a clinically actionable biomarker that can be measured in plasma to guide therapeutic decisions in HCC. Monitoring CA9 levels may enable early identification of patients unlikely to benefit from Atez/Bev, supporting a precision medicine approach. Moreover, it introduces CA9 as a potential therapeutic target to overcome resistance, paving the way for future clinical trials combining CA9 inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade and anti-angiogenic therapy.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, characterized by a high incidence and poor prognosis due to late diagnosis and limited treatment options for advanced stages.13 Despite advancements in therapeutic strategies, including surgical resection, liver transplantation, and locoregional therapies, a significant proportion of HCC cases are diagnosed at an unresectable stage, necessitating the development of effective systemic therapies.4 5

Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a promising treatment modality for various malignancies, including HCC. Among these, the combination immunotherapy of atezolizumab, an anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody, and bevacizumab, an anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) antibody, (named Atez/Bev hereafter) has demonstrated significant clinical benefit in patients with unresectable HCC.468 The phase III IMbrave150 trial reported that this combination therapy significantly improved overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with sorafenib.9 Now, Atez/Bev is recommended as the first-line therapy in a variety of clinical guidelines including the American Society of Clinical Oncology,4 the European Society of Medical Oncology,10 and the Japanese Society of Hepatology,11 unless there is no contraindication to ICI therapy. However, not all patients respond to this combination immunotherapy, and the identification of reliable biomarkers to predict therapeutic response remains a critical challenge.

Previous large-scale biomarker studies using tumor specimens from the clinical trials revealed that the “immune-HOT” and less immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment, represented by the infiltration of exhausted CD8T cells and exclusion of regulatory T cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), may be the prerequisite for better clinical outcomes with immunotherapy, including Atez/Bev.1214 However, tumor sampling was unnecessary and rarely performed for patients with advanced-stage HCC for diagnostic purposes in daily practice because of definitive diagnosis by imaging.15 In this context, blood-based biomarkers offer a minimally invasive and readily accessible means to monitor disease progression and treatment response.1517

To address the urgent need for minimally invasive biomarkers that can predict therapeutic response to ICIs in unresectable HCC, we adopted a high-throughput proteomic screening strategy using the Olink proximity extension assay (PEA) platform. This technology enables the simultaneous quantification of multiple low-abundance proteins with high sensitivity and specificity from small plasma volumes, and has been increasingly applied in recent studies for biomarker discovery across various cancers, including HCC.1822 For instance, circulating interleukin-6 (IL-6) and leukemia inhibitory factor have been associated with poor response to ICIs in HCC and other malignancies, and were identified using similar multiplex protein platforms.23 Building on these findings, we conducted an unbiased plasma protein profiling of patients treated with Atez/Bev to identify novel blood-based biomarkers associated with therapeutic resistance. Following this proteomic screen, we further explored the functional role of a lead candidate—carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA9)—in modulating tumor immunity and treatment efficacy using a syngeneic HCC mouse model.

Methods

Patients and study design

This was a prospective, multicenter observational study designed to identify blood-based biomarkers predictive of response to combination immunotherapy in unresectable HCC. A total of 167 patients were enrolled between November 2020 and January 2024 from 13 tertiary referral hospitals in Japan. Eligible patients were diagnosed with unresectable HCC, defined as tumors unsuitable for curative treatments (resection, ablation, or transplantation) due to tumor location, liver function, comorbidities, or patient preference. Diagnosis was confirmed by dynamic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI according to established imaging criteria.

Patients were included if they were ≥20 years of age, had histologically or radiologically confirmed HCC, were deemed unresectable by a multidisciplinary tumor board, had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2, and adequate hematologic, hepatic, and renal function. Patients were excluded if they had active uncontrolled infection, decompensated liver disease (Child-Pugh class C), concurrent malignancies, or other serious medical conditions that could interfere with study participation. The training and validation cohorts for Atez/Bev were defined based on the timing of patient enrollment.

Treatment and clinical evaluation

Patients received Atez (1,200 mg) plus Bev (15 mg/kg) intravenously every 3 weeks, per standard clinical guidelines. Treatment decisions were made by multidisciplinary tumor boards. Response evaluations were performed using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 and modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria every 6 weeks with contrast-enhanced CT or MRI.24 25

For RECIST V.1.1, up to two target lesions per organ and a maximum of five target lesions in total were selected, and the sum of the longest diameters of these target lesions was calculated at each assessment. Complete response (CR) was defined as the disappearance of all target lesions, partial response (PR) as a ≥30% decrease in the sum of target lesion diameters, and progressive disease (PD) as a ≥20% increase in this sum (with an absolute increase of ≥5 mm) or the appearance of new lesions. Stable disease (SD) was defined as any response that did not meet the criteria for PR or PD.

For mRECIST, only the arterially enhancing (viable) portion of HCC lesions was measured, and the sum of the longest diameters of viable target lesions was used. CR was defined as the complete disappearance of intratumoral arterial enhancement in all target lesions, PR as a ≥30% decrease, and PD as a ≥20% increase (with an absolute increase of ≥5 mm) in the sum of viable target lesion diameters or the appearance of new intrahepatic or extrahepatic lesions, with SD defined as neither PR nor PD.

The primary clinical endpoints were objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), PFS, and OS. ORR and DCR were defined as the proportions of patients achieving CR or PR, and CR/PR/SD, respectively, according to RECIST V.1.1 and mRECIST. PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to PD or death based on RECIST V.1.1, and modified PFS as the time from treatment initiation to PD or death based on mRECIST. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. The median follow-up period was 15.8 months (IQR 9.4–23.7 months).

Biospecimen collection and storage

Peripheral blood (10 mL) was collected in EDTA tubes within 7 days prior to initiating systemic therapy. Samples were centrifuged at 1,300×g for 15 min at room temperature to isolate plasma, which was aliquoted and stored at –80°C. Samples underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle prior to analysis. A separate cohort of 32 resected HCC tumor samples was used for gene expression analysis; these were collected at Hokkaido University Hospital between 2007 and 2018, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80°C.

Quantitative measurement of multiple plasma proteins using a proximity extension assay

A comprehensive secretory protein profiling was performed using the PEA technology. Plasma concentrations of secreted proteins were quantified using the Olink Target 96 Cytokine panel (Olink Holding, Uppsala, Sweden), which enables the simultaneous measurement of 92 immune-oncology-related proteins with high sensitivity and specificity (ADA, ADGRG1, ANGPT1, ANGPT2, ARG1, CAIX, CASP-8, CCL17, CCL19, CCL20, CCL23, CCL3, CCL4, CD27, CD28, CD4, CD40, CD40-L, CD5, CD70, CD8A, CD83, CD244, CRTAM, CSF-1, CX3CL1, CXCL1, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL12, CXCL13, CXCL5, CXCL9, DCN, EGF, FASLG, FGF2, Gal-1, Gal-9, GZMA, GZMB, GZMH, HGF, HO-1, ICOSLG, IFN-gamma, IL1 alpha, IL10, IL12, IL12RB1, IL13, IL15, IL18, IL2, IL33, IL4, IL5, IL6, IL7, IL8, KIR3DL1, KLRD1, LAG3, LAMP3, LAP TGF-beta-1, MCP-1, MCP-2, MCP-3, MCP-4, MIC-A/B, MMP12, MMP7, MUC-16, NCR1, NOS3, PDCD1, PDGF subunit B, PD-L1, PD-L2, PGF, PTN, TIE2, TNF, TNFRSF12A, TNFRSF21, TNFRSF4, TNFRSF9, TNFSF14, TRAIL, TWEAK, VEGFA, VEGFR-2). The assay was performed by APRO Science Group/Pharma Foods International (Tokushima, Japan). Protein expression data are presented as normalized protein expression values, which are relative, arbitrary units on a log2 scale. A complete list of the 92 proteins analyzed is also provided in online supplemental table 1.

Mouse syngeneic tumor model of hepatocellular carcinoma with anti-PD-L1 and anti-VEGF antibody treatment

A syngeneic murine HCC model was established using immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice (male, 5 weeks old, Charles River Laboratories, Japan). Mice were housed in pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, standard chow, and water provided ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Osaka University and complied with relevant ethical regulations.

Hepatoma cell preparation: Hep55.1C, a murine hepatoma cell line, was obtained from CLS (Cat# 400201, Eppelheim, Germany) and authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling. Stable Car9-overexpressing and vector control lines were generated using lentiviral particles encoding the murine Car9 open reading frame (ORIGENE, Rockville, Maryland, USA). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. At 70–80% confluence, cells were harvested and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 6.5×10⁶ cells/100 µL.

Tumor induction and treatment allocation: Each mouse was inoculated subcutaneously in both flanks with 6.5×10⁶ Hep55.1C cells (either Car9-overexpressing or control). Tumor volume was measured with calipers every 3–4 days and calculated as (length×width²)/2. When tumors reached ~100 mm³, mice were randomized (via computer-generated randomization) into treatment groups (n=7–8 tumors per group) to ensure balanced tumor volumes.

Treatment regimens:

Anti-PD-L1 antibody (clone 10F.9G2, Bio X Cell; 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally (i.p.)).

Anti-VEGF antibody (clone 2G11-2A05, Bio X Cell; 5 mg/kg, i.p.).

CA9 inhibitor S4 (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-110243; 5 mg/kg, i.p.).

Antibodies were administered every other day for a total of seven doses. S4 was given six times per week, either alone or in combination with the antibodies. Vehicle control mice received PBS. The dose of S4 was based on prior literature demonstrating in vivo efficacy in solid tumors and our own in vitro dose-response data in Hep55.1C cells.26 No significant treatment-related toxicity (eg, weight loss >10%) was observed during the study.

Study endpoints: Mice were sacrificed when tumors reached ~1000 mm³ or at predefined experimental endpoints. Tumors and surrounding tissues were harvested for histological and molecular analyses. H&E staining was performed to assess tumor architecture and necrosis. Molecular evaluations included quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) (targeting Gzmb, Cxcl9, Vegfa), western blotting for Car9 protein, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) for tumor immune profiling.

Blinding and replication: Treatment administration and tumor measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. Each experiment was independently replicated at least two times with consistent findings.

Statistical analysis

Clinical variables are summarized as medians with IQRs, unless otherwise stated. The treatment line was defined exclusively on the basis of prior systemic therapies. For comparisons between two independent groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied when data approximated a normal distribution, whereas Mann-Whitney U tests were used for non-parametric data. For comparisons across more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison procedures (eg, Dunnett’s test) was used for parametric data, and the Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc comparisons was used for non-parametric data. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Correlations between plasma CA9 levels and tumorous CA9 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, given the non-normal distributions and the influence of potential outliers. In contrast, the relationship between CA9 levels measured by Olink and ELISA was evaluated using Pearson’s product-moment correlation, as the scatter plots suggested an approximately linear association. For scRNA-seq analyses, differential gene expression between two groups (eg, CA9-high vs CA9-low malignant cells or immune cell subsets) was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test as implemented in Seurat. Tumor growth data from mouse experiments were analyzed using repeated-measures approaches (eg, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA) with suitable post hoc tests, as specified in the corresponding figure legends.

For survival analyses, OS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to death from any cause. PFS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to radiologic disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. Differences in OS and PFS between groups were assessed using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test.

Because this was an exploratory biomarker study, no formal sample size calculation was performed; cohort size was determined by feasibility and the number of eligible patients during the enrollment period. Factors associated with initial PD were investigated using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models, whereas factors associated with PFS and OS were analyzed using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Candidate covariates included plasma CA9 level, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), C-reactive protein (CRP), ECOG performance status, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, vascular invasion, albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score, and etiology, selected on the basis of clinical relevance and previous reports.27 Proteomics-derived candidate proteins were additionally evaluated together in multivariable models, as detailed in the figure legends. Other statistical procedures used are described in the individual figure legends. A two-sided p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.4.0 for Windows (GraphPad Prism, RRID:SCR_002798, San Diego, California, USA) and JMP V.17.1.0 (SAS Institute, RRID:SCR_014242, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

All other materials and methods are provided in the online supplemental material and methods.

Results

High-throughput proteomics identifies secretory proteins correlated with response to Atez/Bev treatment in unresectable HCC

To identify circulating proteins correlated with response to Atez/Bev treatment in unresectable HCC, we analyzed 92 immune-oncology-related proteins in pretreatment plasma from 78 patients with unresectable HCC using PEA technology from Olink (online supplemental table 1). Clinical background and treatment response details are provided in online supplemental table 2 and online supplemental figure 1. The discovery cohort consisted of 78 patients with unresectable HCC treated with Atez/Bev. The median age was 73 years (IQR 67–79), and 79.5% were male. Most patients had a good performance status (PS 0: 91.0%) and received Atez/Bev as first-line therapy (65.4%). The majority had preserved liver function (median ALBI score –2.43), and 53.8% were BCLC stage C. HCC etiology was viral in 55.1% of cases, and median AFP and des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP) levels were 11 ng/mL and 226 mAU/mL, respectively. In this cohort, ORR and DCR were 26.9% and 66.7% by RECIST and 41.0% and 67.9% by mRECIST, with median PFS and OS of 6.5 and 28 months, respectively. Volcano plots revealed elevated levels of circulating proteins, including CA9, IL-8, colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1), and IL-6, in patients with PD compared with those achieving disease control (non-PD) by both RECIST and mRECIST (figure 1A,B). Notably, IL-6 was previously identified as the predictor of non-responder to Atez/Bev therapy for patients with HCC by ours and other groups.23 28 In addition, high IL-8 and CSF-1 were also reported to be correlated with reduced clinical benefit of immunotherapy in other types of cancer,2931 supporting the validity of our proteomic screening. To compare the relative contribution of these circulating proteins, we next focused on six top candidate molecules from the proteomic screen (including CA9, IL-6, IL-8, CSF-1, matrix metalloproteinase 12 (MMP12) and monocyte chemoattractant protein 3 (MCP-3)) and constructed multivariable models that simultaneously included these six proteins without clinical covariates. In the discovery cohort, high CA9 levels were the only factor that remained significantly associated with worse OS in Cox proportional hazards models and with lower DCR and disease control rate by mRECIST (mDCR) in logistic regression analyses, whereas the other candidate cytokines did not retain statistical significance in these six-marker models (online supplemental figure 2). Consistent with these findings, patients with high pretreatment CA9 levels exhibited lower ORR and DCR by mRECIST (figure 1C) and significantly shorter PFS and OS (p<0.05 for both) (figure 1D–F) than those with low CA9 levels. Furthermore, multivariable regression models that additionally incorporated clinical covariates again identified high CA9 levels as an independent predictor of poor outcomes (onlinesupplemental tables 35). Taken together, these results identify CA9 as the most informative circulating biomarker among the candidates highlighted by our proteomic screening and support its role as a potential predictor of Atez/Bev efficacy in advanced HCC.

Validation assay confirms high CA9 level is associated with poor response and shorter survival in patients with advanced HCC with Atez/Bev therapy

To validate the biomarker potential of CA9, we measured plasma CA9 levels in a subset of the discovery cohort using ELISA, confirming high concordance with Olink data (online supplemental figure 3). Validation was extended to an independent cohort of 89 patients with advanced HCC treated with Atez/Bev therapy (online supplemental table 6). The validation cohort included 89 patients with similar clinical backgrounds. The median age was 73 years (IQR 69–79), and 80.9% were male. PS was generally preserved (PS 0: 91.0%), and 84.3% received Atez/Bev as first-line therapy. Liver function remained comparable (median ALBI score –2.36), and 58.4% were classified as BCLC stage C. Viral etiology was present in 51.7%, and AFP and DCP medians were 32 ng/mL and 285 mAU/mL, respectively. In this cohort, ORR and DCR were 24.7% and 60.7% by RECIST and 32.6% and 62.9% by mRECIST, with median PFS and OS of 4.5 and 15.1 months, respectively (online supplemental figure 4A–E). Plasma CA9 levels were significantly higher in PD patients than in those achieving non-PD (figure 2A,B). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for plasma CA9 in predicting PD was 0.624 (p<0.05) (figure 2C). The optimal cut-off value derived from the Youden Index was 188.7 pg/mL, which was subsequently used for survival and response stratification. Stratifying patients by a CA9 cut-off revealed significantly lower DCR in the high-CA9 group (figure 2D). Patients with high CA9 levels showed significantly shorter median PFS by RECIST (3.0 months vs 8.2 months, p<0.05) and by mRECIST (3.4 months vs 8.2 months, p<0.05), and median OS (11.3 months vs 25.0 months, p<0.05) than those with its low levels (figure 2E–G). Clinical background differences were minimal between high-CA9 and low-CA9 groups except for the ALBI score (online supplemental table 7), which was higher in high-CA9 patients. Correlations between CA9 and clinical parameters were generally modest, but statistically significant associations were observed with higher total bilirubin, higher ALBI score, lower platelet count, and higher aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels (online supplemental figure 5). These results validated that elevated CA9 levels predict therapeutic resistance and shorter survival in Atez/Bev-treated patients.

Multivariable analysis identifies CA9 as an independent predictor of clinical outcomes in patients with advanced HCC with Atez/Bev therapy

To identify factors associated with initial PD in patients receiving Atez/Bev therapy, we first performed univariate logistic regression analyses in the validation cohort. Elevated plasma CA9 levels, AFP levels, and the presence of vascular invasion were significantly associated with increased risk of initial PD, both by RECIST and mRECIST (table 1, online supplemental table 8). We then conducted multivariable logistic regression analysis. A high plasma CA9 level remained the only independent predictor of initial PD response, both by RECIST (OR 2.91, 95% CI 1.14 to 7.41, p<0.05) and by mRECIST (OR 3.00, 95% CI 1.14 to 7.92, p<0.05) (table 1, online supplemental table 8). Univariate Cox proportional hazards analyses for PFS and OS similarly showed that high CA9 levels were significantly associated with poor outcomes (p<0.05) (table 2, onlinesupplemental tables 9 10). In multivariable Cox regression (table 2), a high CA9 level was an independent risk factor for reduced OS (HR 2.48, 95% CI 1.44 to 4.29, p<0.05), and elevated AFP was also independently associated with worse OS (HR 1.83, 95% CI 1.01 to 3.30, p<0.05). For PFS, high CA9 was the only variable that remained significant in multivariate analysis (HR 2.01, 95% CI 1.27 to 3.19, p<0.05 by RECIST) (onlinesupplemental tables 9 10). We also performed an alternative multivariable analysis that included BCLC stage in place of potentially overlapping clinical factors such as PS, macrovascular invasion, and extrahepatic metastasis. As shown in onlinesupplemental tables 1113, CA9 remained the only independent predictor of initial PD and OS in this adjusted model. To further evaluate the impact of CA9 in a clinically homogeneous setting, we next focused on patients who received Atez/Bev as first-line therapy. By pooling the discovery and validation cohorts, we identified 94 first-line cases with available baseline CA9 measurements. In this pooled first-line population, patients with high CA9 levels again showed lower ORR and DCR by both RECIST and mRECIST compared with those with low CA9 (online supplemental table 14). Consistently, Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated that the CA9-high group had significantly shorter OS and PFS, whether assessed by RECIST or mRECIST, than the CA9-low group (online supplemental figure 6A–C). These findings indicate that the adverse impact of high circulating CA9 on treatment response and survival is maintained even when analyses are restricted to first-line Atez/Bev therapy. Together, these results underscore the robustness of CA9 as an independent predictor of both early treatment resistance and long-term survival outcomes in patients treated with Atez/Bev. The forest plot showed that patients with high CA9 levels exhibited higher HRs for PFS and OS and higher ORs for initial PD across most subgroups, except for the HR for OS in the Child-Pugh B subgroup (online supplemental figure 7). These results firmly established circulating CA9 as an independent biomarker of resistance to Atez/Bev therapy in unresectable HCC.

Tumorous CA9 contributes to resistance to anti-PD-L1/VEGF therapy

We investigated the source of circulating CA9 and its functional role in Atez/Bev resistance. As an exploratory analysis, we examined the relationship between tumorous CA9 mRNA expression and circulating CA9 levels in 32 HCC specimens and observed a positive trend that did not reach statistical significance when assessed by Spearman’s rank correlation (online supplemental figure 8, online supplemental table 15). Single-cell transcriptome analysis of HCCDB V.2.0, a publicly available HCC transcriptomic database, further demonstrated that CA9 expression was restricted to malignant cells, supporting the notion that the tumor compartment is the primary source of circulating CA9 (figure 3A–C; online supplemental figure 9).

To explore its functional role, Car9 (murine CA9 ortholog) was overexpressed in Hep55.1c cells (figure 3D). Overexpression of Car9 in Hep55.1c cells was maintained in the established syngeneic tumor (figure 3E) and did not affect the tumor growth (figure 3F,G). Importantly, tumors derived from Car9-expressing cells showed only a modest response and markedly reduced sensitivity to anti-PD-L1/VEGF therapy, whereas parental cell-derived tumors exhibited pronounced growth suppression with the same treatment (figure 3H,I). These findings demonstrated that tumorous CA9 plays a pivotal role in resistance to combination immunotherapy.

CA9 promotes an immunosuppressive and angiogenic tumor microenvironment

To investigate the mechanism underlying CA9-mediated resistance, we characterized the tumor microenvironment of Car9-overexpressing tumors. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of whole tumor lysates showed that these tumors had reduced expression of cytotoxic T cell-related genes (Cd8a, Ifng, Gzmb) and the CTL-recruiting chemokine Cxcl9, indicating an immune-cold phenotype (figure 4A). In contrast, mRNA levels of M2-like macrophage markers (Cd163, Cd206, Il10, Arg1) and angiogenesis-related genes (Vegfa, Vegfb, Tie2) were increased, suggesting an immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic tumor microenvironment (figure 4B,C). Other cell type-associated markers, such as Cd4, Foxp3, Ncr1, Ncam1, and Itgax, were not substantially altered (online supplemental figure 10). We also performed single-cell transcriptome analysis of tumors derived from parental and Car9-expressing Hep55.1c cells (figure 4D, online supplemental figure 11A–C). Tumors derived from Car9-expressing Hep55.1c cells showed a significantly lower number of cytotoxic T cells and lower expression levels of cytotoxic molecules, including Prf1 and Gzmb (figure 4E–G, online supplemental figure 11D,E). We also confirmed the high expression of Cd163 and Cd204 and suppression of antigen processing and presentation pathway in the TAM of Car9-expressing tumors (figure 4H–J). These findings were validated by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure 12A–C). In addition, single-cell transcriptomic analysis suggested reduced expression of genes involved in antigen presentation and T-cell activation in dendritic cells and endothelial cells, as well as increased expression of angiogenesis-related genes in tumor-associated neutrophils (figure 4K,L, online supplemental figure 11F–J). To determine whether similar CA9-associated immune programs are present in human HCC, we next analyzed the public scRNA-seq dataset GSE151530, which profiles malignant, immune, and stromal cells from primary liver cancers at single-cell resolution (online supplemental figure 13A–D). In this dataset, CA9 expression was highly enriched in malignant epithelial clusters (online supplemental figure 13E), consistent with our HCCDB V.2.0 analysis. When malignant cells were stratified into CA9-high and CA9-low groups based on median CA9 expression, Hallmark Gene Set Enrichment Analysis indicated that CA9-high tumors exhibited diminished interferon gamma response and tumor necrosis factor alpha signaling via NF-KB in both TNK-cell and myeloid clusters, along with reduced enrichment of other inflammatory and allograft rejection signatures and increased hypoxia signaling. These human single-cell data support the notion that high CA9 expression in malignant cells is associated with a more immunosuppressive, hypoxic, and less inflamed tumor microenvironment (online supplemental figure 13F–I). Finally, we investigated whether pharmacological CA9 inhibition could potentiate the antitumor activity of anti-PD-L1/VEGF therapy in vivo. In wild-type Hep55.1c tumors, adding a CA9 inhibitor to anti-PD-L1/VEGF resulted in a modest, yet statistically significant additional suppression of tumor growth compared with anti-PD-L1/VEGF alone (online supplemental figure 14A,B), which is in line with the relatively low baseline Car9 expression in this model. In contrast, when the same combination was administered to Car9-overexpressing tumors, CA9 inhibition exerted a more pronounced additive effect on tumor growth control (figure 4M, online supplemental figure 15). Taken together, these findings suggest that tumorous CA9 dampens the efficacy of anti-PD-L1/VEGF antibodies and support CA9 as a potential therapeutic target, particularly in CA9-high unresectable HCC.

Discussion

In this study, we discovered tumor-derived circulating CA9 as a novel predictor of response to the Atez/Bev combination therapy in unresectable HCC. Our study started with the high-throughput screening of potential biomarkers using innovative Olink technology, which is highly useful in biomarker research due to its ability to perform high-throughput, multiplex protein analysis with superior sensitivity and specificity. Our initial screening identified several candidate proteins predicting immunotherapy response, including CA9, IL-6, IL-8, and CSF-1. Elevated IL-6 levels are linked to a more immunosuppressive TME, often hindering the effectiveness of ICIs like pembrolizumab and nivolumab.32 We have previously reported the association of high serum IL-6 levels with the primary resistance to Atev/Bev therapy in HCC,23 which was also subsequently validated by the Korean group.28 High IL-8 levels are associated with poor responses to ICIs in cancers such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) due to their role in recruiting neutrophils and promoting angiogenesis.33 Similarly, CSF-1 contributes to immune evasion by promoting the expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, which can lead to resistance to ICIs in some cancers.34 Taken together, these findings supported the robustness of our screening results. Future combinatory assessment of these cytokines together with CA9 could help identify patients more likely to benefit from immunotherapy and improve treatment personalization.

CA9 is a transmembrane protein that plays a crucial role in the regulation of pH within tumors via hydration of carbon dioxide to produce bicarbonate and protons. The produced protons contribute to the acidification of the tumor microenvironment, while the produced bicarbonates titrate the intracellular pH of tumor cells, promoting cancer cell survival, invasion, and metastasis.35 It is well-known that hypoxia induces the CA9 expression via hypoxia-inducible factor-1α in the tumor.35 Overexpression of CA9 in tumor tissues and its aggressive phenotypes has been reported in various cancers, including renal cell carcinoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer as well as HCC.35 In HCC, tumorous CA9 expression correlates with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and is associated with poor prognosis.36

The role of circulating CA9 as a biomarker has also been explored in various cancers. The ectodomain of CA9 is cleaved by metalloproteinase ADAM17 and released into the circulation in response to hypoxia and acidosis.37 Since CA9 expression in non-cancerous tissue is rare (epithelia of the stomach, gallbladder, pancreas and intestine),37 it is considered that circulating CA9 levels may reflect CA9 expression and hypoxic state in the tumor. Indeed, our study revealed that high levels of circulating CA9 are predominantly derived from the tumor cells in HCC, as indicated by the single-cell transcriptome analysis. In HCC, patients with high serum CA9 levels had an increased mortality risk.38

The potential of CA9 as a therapeutic biomarker for anti-angiogenesis or immunotherapy response has also been reported. In metastatic clear-cell RCC, CA9 expression has been associated with shorter OS in patients treated with temsirolimus and bevacizumab.39 In the phase III neoadjuvant trial of breast cancer, addition of bevacizumab improved the rate of pathological complete response in patients with low circulating CA9 levels but did not in those with high levels.40 Moreover, bevacizumab treatment upregulated CA9 and its inhibition enhanced the efficacy of bevacizumab in the xenograft model of colon cancer and glioblastoma.41 These data suggested the potential of CA9 as the biomarker of bevacizumab treatment. Meanwhile, our mechanistic study showed that CA9 functionally contributed to the immunosuppressive microenvironment with reduced tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), which negatively affected the response to ICI. Indeed, the negative association of tumorous CA9 expression with TILs and reduced Th1 response was also reported in human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and melanoma, respectively.42 43 Moreover, targeting CA9 in the tumor microenvironment induced the sensitization of tumors to ICIs in melanoma.43 Collectively, these findings suggest that CA9 may be involved in resistance to both bevacizumab and ICI therapy, and that circulating CA9 can serve as a useful risk stratification marker for Atez/Bev efficacy in HCC rather than a definitive test to separate responders from non-responders. Importantly, our data also indicate that the discriminative performance of CA9 as a single biomarker is modest (AUROC 0.624), and a considerable proportion of patients with high CA9 levels still achieve SD, PR, or even CR with Atez/Bev. Thus, CA9 should not be used alone to decide whether an individual patient should receive Atez/Bev, but is better interpreted as a marker that enriches for subgroups with more favorable or unfavorable outcomes under this regimen.

Importantly, beyond its prognostic value, CA9 represents a promising therapeutic target. Its overexpression in the tumor microenvironment creates an immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic niche, which may contribute to reduced responsiveness to ICIs and anti-angiogenic agents. Our in vivo experiments showed that pharmacological inhibition of CA9 synergized with anti-PD-L1 and anti-VEGF antibodies to suppress tumor growth, highlighting its potential role in enhancing immunotherapy efficacy. These findings support the rationale for incorporating CA9 inhibition into combination treatment strategies for HCC. In the future, if CA9-targeted agents are clinically developed, circulating CA9 levels may also be useful as a companion diagnostic to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from adding CA9 inhibition to Atez/Bev, ideally in conjunction with other biomarkers within a multimodal prediction framework.

Limitation

Our study has the following limitations: (1) All in vivo experiments were performed in a subcutaneous syngeneic model, which we selected because it allows reliable tumor monitoring, technical feasibility, and good reproducibility, and has been widely used in previous studies on immunotherapy resistance. However, subcutaneous tumors do not fully recapitulate the liver-specific tumor microenvironment. Although we attempted to establish an orthotopic HCC model using the same cell line, tumor engraftment and growth were not sufficiently consistent to allow robust evaluation of treatment effects. Future studies employing orthotopic or other liver-specific models will be important to further validate our findings. (2) The relatively small sample size of our study cohorts was limited by the short duration following the drug’s approval. (3) Patient heterogeneity remains an inherent limitation in real-world studies. While clinical trials often employ strictly defined inclusion criteria, real-world analyses such as ours provide valuable insights into biomarker performance in routine clinical practice. Future prospective studies with larger and more homogeneous cohorts will be necessary to further validate our findings and refine the clinical utility of CA9 as a predictive biomarker in combination immunotherapy for HCC.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that tumor-derived circulating CA9 is a predictive biomarker of resistance to Atez/Bev therapy in unresectable HCC. Monitoring plasma CA9 levels could improve patient stratification, ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes for patients with HCC.
