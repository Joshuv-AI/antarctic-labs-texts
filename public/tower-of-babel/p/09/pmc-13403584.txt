Pan-genomic and pan-transcriptomic integration unveils evolutionary dynamics and regulatory diversification of the WRKY gene family in Camellia sinensis
Authors: Binjingyu Wang, Shilai Tian, Xinzhuan Yao, Yue Wan, Chao Xu, Chao Luo, Hu Tang, Zifan Yang, Muhammad Safiullah Virk, Ping Bin, Litang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe WRKY transcription factor family, characterized by its conserved WRKYGQK motif and zinc finger structure, serves as a central regulatory module in plants, mediating developmental, stress-responsive, and metabolic processes through W-box binding. In Camellia sinensis, WRKY genes act as molecular integrators that link environmental cues to the biosynthesis of quality-related compounds, underscoring the importance of understanding their evolutionary patterns for genetic improvement. However, previous research has been limited by single-reference genome approaches, which fail to capture the full spectrum of WRKY gene presence-absence variation across diverse tea plant cultivars.ResultsThis study presents the first pan-genome-wide analysis of the WRKY family in any plant species, integrating data from 22 tea plant genomes. We identified a comprehensive set of WRKY genes and classified them into core and dispensable categories, revealing extensive presence-absence variants (PAVs) that were overlooked in single-reference studies. Phylogenetic reconstruction resolved 11 subfamilies, with notable lineage-specific expansions. Mechanistically, core gene evolution was predominantly driven by whole-genome or segmental duplication under strong purifying selection, whereas dispensable genes, particularly in certain subfamilies, showed signals of adaptive evolution and were enriched through dispersed duplication. Transcriptomic analysis uncovered co-expression networks among highly expressed WRKY clusters, indicating functional redundancy and sub-functionalization in stress adaptation.ConclusionsThe findings from the present study provide insights into the evolutionary dynamics of presence-absence and copy-number variation within the WRKY family in tea plants, establishing a valuable genetic resource for enhancing stress resilience and metabolic traits in tea breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09047-y.Keywords: Camellia sinensis, WRKY transcription factors, Pan-genome, Gene family evolution, Copy number variation, Natural selection, Expression regulation

Introduction

The WRKY gene family comprises a group of transcription factors of remarkable importance in plants. Named after the highly conserved WRKY domain in their protein sequences, WRKY proteins represent the first zinc-finger transcription factors identified in plants [1]. They typically regulate diverse biological processes, including stress responses, development, and secondary metabolism. WRKY proteins are characterized by their DNA-binding domains containing one or two WRKY domains, each comprising approximately 60 amino acid residues with a conserved WRKYGQK heptapeptide at the N-terminus and a zinc finger motif (C-X4-5-C-X22-23-H-X1-H or C-X7-C-X23-H-X1-C) at the C-terminal region. These transcription factors can specifically interact with the W-box ([C/T]TGAC[T/C]) in the promoter region of target genes to modulate downstream gene expression [2]. WRKY transcription factors are widely involved in regulating defense responses, growth, and developmental processes in plants. They act as positive or negative regulators, mediating a wide range of physiological processes by responding to internal and external environmental signals, including seed dormancy and germination, seedling morphogenesis, root growth, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [3]. In tea plants, these genes are involved not only in anti-disease, drought, and low-temperature stress responses, and other anti-stress processes, but also in regulating the synthesis and accumulation of tea polyphenols, amino acids, caffeine, and other tea quality-related components. Therefore, the study of the WRKY gene family in the tea plant not only helps reveal the molecular mechanisms underlying stress resistance and quality formation, but also provides valuable genetic resources for genetic improvement and breeding [4]. The ubiquity and functional diversity of WRKY genes are evident across plant species. Genome-wide analysis has identified 74 WRKY genes in Arabidopsis [3], over 100 in Oryza sativa [5], 197 in Glycine max [6], 45 in C. sinensis [7], 55 in Cucumis sativus [8], and 81 in Solanum lycopersicum [9]. These studies have delineated three major classes and multiple subclasses of WRKY transcription factors, contributing to a deeper understanding of their functional diversity, evolutionary relationships, and roles in plant growth and stress responses [10].

Camellia sinensis is a perennial evergreen shrub cultivated worldwide for its leaves, which are processed into one of the most consumed beverages. Its genome is large and characterized by recent duplication events, contributing to a rich repertoire of secondary metabolites, including catechins, theanine, and caffeine, which determine tea quality and health benefits. WRKY transcription factors have been implicated as key regulators of these metabolic pathways as well as stress responses in tea plants, yet a comprehensive pan-genomic view has been lacking. In C. sinensis, substantial progress has been made in WRKY gene research. Early genome-wide identification using a single reference genome (cultivar ‘Shuchazao’) reported 45 WRKY genes, which were classified into three major groups and shown to be differentially expressed under cold, drought, and salt stresses [4, 7]. Subsequent studies have expanded these findings, identifying specific WRKY members that function as key regulators in the biosynthesis of quality-related compounds. For instance, CsWRKY12 was shown to interact with CsVQ4L, thereby promoting the accumulation of galloylated catechins in tender tea leaves [11]. Transcriptome analyses further demonstrated that numerous WRKY genes are rapidly induced by Colletotrichum infection, herbivore attack, and abscisic acid treatment, indicating their central roles in integrating biotic and abiotic stress signals. For instance, a study revealed that phosphate deficiency induced by Colletotrichum infection activates anthocyanin-mediated defense responses in tea plants [12], while a different study demonstrated that CsWRKY15 promotes auto-resistance under intercropping conditions [13]. Despite these advances, all existing studies have been constrained by the use of single reference genomes, which inherently fail to capture the full spectrum of genetic diversity present across tea plant populations.

With the gradual reduction in the cost of gene sequencing, the number of species with fully sequenced reference genomes has climbed exponentially over the past two decades. The growing popularity of complete genome sequences for species has made the characterization of numerous gene families on a genome-wide scale a standard process for researchers in their daily studies [14]. However, reliance on single reference genomes limits our understanding of genetic diversity. Dynamic changes, such as genome-wide size and subfamily conservatism, contain important information and play a crucial role in promoting evolutionary diversity and adaptability of organisms [15]. Structural variations, such as copy number variations (CNVs), sub-microscopic deletions/duplications of ≥ 1 kb DNA fragments caused by genomic rearrangements, are often overlooked in such studies [16]. Although CNVs have been widely characterized in pan-genomic studies, most systematic genome-wide identifications and evolutionary analysis of gene families still rely on single reference genomes.

Given the remarkable functional diversity and evolutionary complexity of the WRKY family, which is central to plant adaptation and secondary metabolism, a pan-genomic approach is indispensable for comprehensively uncovering its full genetic variation, evolutionary dynamics, and breeding potential. Unlike single-reference analyses that assume a static gene set, pan-genome approaches capture both core genes (present in all individuals) and dispensable genes (present only in a subset), thereby revealing lineage-specific innovations and adaptive potential [17]. Importantly, although dozens of WRKY genes have been functionally characterized in tea, the extent of PAVs, CNVs, and subfamily-specific expansions at the population level remains completely unexplored. To date, no pan-genome-wide analysis of the WRKY gene family has been conducted in any plant species, representing a significant gap in understanding how this critical transcription factor family evolves across species. Notably, the pan-genomic approach used here identified several previously unannotated WRKY loci in tea. Whether these loci represent lineage-specific genes or have functional relevance for stress adaptation or quality-related metabolism remains to be investigated. Highlighting these newly identified genes may facilitate future functional studies.

In the present study, we used 22 published genomic datasets of tea plants [18] to overcome the limitations of single-reference genomes and characterize the complete evolutionary landscape of the WRKY gene family. We adopted the approach of Seung-Do Lee’s group, involving scanning all genes in the pan-genome assembly and identifying Homology-based Sequence Clusters (HSCs) for gene family characterization [1]. HSCs group genes into families based on sequence similarity, allowing systematic classification of gene families across multiple genomes. To eliminate variability in the number of genes caused by differences in genome interpretation quality, the method employs the BITACORA algorithm, which can detect previously undetected copies of gene families that other tools may have missed due to low sequence similarity or assembly issues [19]. By integrating pan-genomic and pan-transcriptomic data, this study provides the first comprehensive view of the evolution of the WRKY gene family at the population level in any plant species, offering novel insights into the evolutionary dynamics of WRKY transcription factors and their potential for tea plant improvement.

Results

Identification of WRKY genes in 22 tea plant pan-genome assemblies

We scanned the 22 tea plant pan-genome assemblies to identify WRKY coding genes, including previously unannotated sequences. Each candidate was required to contain a complete WRKY domain. The known WRKY domains in Arabidopsis ranged from 21 to 62 amino acids, predominantly 57–62 aa (Supplementary file S1). Based on this, we set a threshold of ≥ 55 amino acids for WRKY domain identification. The screening results demonstrated that the number of WRKY genes we identified in the 22 tea plant genomes ranged from 109 to 148 (Supplementary files S2 and S3). The minimum observed WRKY domain length was 56 amino acids, consistent with annotated tea plant WRKY genes. As shown in Figs. 1 and 113 to 148 of the identified WRKY genes corresponded to annotated genes. Additionally, 18 to 34 newly predicted WRKY genes were identified per genome. The full-length amino acid sequences of the identified WRKY genes ranged from 56 to 768 amino acids (Supplementary File S3). For comparison, annotated Arabidopsis WRKY genes ranged from 110 to 1025 amino acids (Supplementary File S1).

The annotated genes to newly predicted genes across 22 C. sinensis cultivars based on pan-genomic scanning with the BITACORA tool. For comparison, Arabidopsis thaliana contains 74 WRKY genes [3], Oryza sativa contains over 100 [5], and Solanum lycopersicum contains 81 [9]

The wider length range of newly predicted WRKY genes may reflect several factors. First, the pan-genomic approach captures a broader spectrum of genomic regions, potentially revealing unannotated members with sequence variations, insertions, or deletions [20]. Second, the C. sinensis genome is complex, with an estimated size of 3.0-3.26 Gb and a heterozygosity of 2.8% [21], which may contribute to sequence diversity. Third, WRKY genes are known to undergo frequent genomic modifications, including insertions, deletions, and duplications [22]. These factors together may explain the observed variation in the extended length. The newly identified WRKY genes demonstrate the utility of pan-genomic scanning for discovering loci missed by traditional annotation. Importantly, these genes have not been previously annotated in the C. sinensis genome, although their orthology with WRKY genes from other plant species remains to be systematically investigated.

Core and dispensable WRKY genes

WRKY genes from 22 tea plant genomes were grouped into HSCs. The designation of a gene group as ‘core’ or ‘dispensable’ is based on its presence-absence pattern at the HSC level across the 22 genomes, not on the copy number variation of individual genes within any single genome. A total of 1905 WRKY genes were identified and divided into 101 HSCs (Supplementary File S4). This exceeds the 87 WRKY genes in the reference genome (cultivar Shuchazao) and the average of 84 per genome, indicating extensive PAVs among tea plant WRKY genes. The overall PAV landscape across all 101 HSCs and 22 genomes is visualized in a heatmap (Supplementary Figure S1). The WRKY HSCs were categorized into four conservation classes according to their PAV patterns across the 22 genomes, using the following criteria: 35 core WRKYs (present in all 22 varieties, conservation frequency = 100%), and 66 dispensable WRKYs (missing in at least one variety, conservation frequency < 100%). Of these, the dispensable WRKYs could be further subdivided into 23 soft core WRKYs (conserved in ≥ 90% of the lines (missing in ≤ 2 varieties)), 31 shelled WRKYs (present in > 10% but < 90% of the lines), and 12 lineage-specific or clouded WRKYs (present in ≤ 10% of the lines (restricted to ≤ 2 varieties)) (Supplementary file S5). The core, soft core, shell, and cloud WRKYs were named CsWRKY.CR1–CR35, CsWRKY.SC36–SC58, CsWRKY.SH59–SH89, and CsWRKY.CL90–CL101, respectively.

Among these dispensable HSCs, the number of newly predicted genes varied by category. Among the 23 soft-core HSCs, 4 were newly predicted, and all 31 shell HSCs were newly predicted (none were annotated in any existing genome assembly). Among the 12 cloud HSCs, 5 were newly predicted, while the remaining 7 were annotated in at least one genome. The shell category contained the highest number of newly predicted HSCs.

Furthermore, CNV differed between annotated and newly predicted WRKY genes. In the annotated WRKY genes, the number of core WRKYs ranged from 35 to 48, exceeding the core HSC count of 35 due to CNV and dispensable WRKYs from 36 to 48 (Fig. 2A). Among newly predicted WRKY genes, core WRKYs were only detected in 3 genomes (1 copy each), while dispensable WRKYs ranged from 0 to 7 (mean = 2.4) (Fig. 2B).

Pan-genome-based phylogenetic analysis of the WRKY gene family

Phylogenetic analysis of the 101 WRKY HSCs was performed based on conserved WRKY domain sequences. As shown in Fig. 3, these 101 tea plant pan genes were classified into 11 subfamilies (Supplementary file S6). Most of these subfamilies corresponded to previous classifications in A. thaliana [23]. The classification is based on the number of WRKY domains and zinc finger-like motifs [24]. SF7 and SF8, which correspond to WRKY proteins with two WRKY structural domains, can be further classified into four subgroups based on their zinc finger motifs (SF7a, SF7b, SF8a, and SF8b). SF7 proteins contain a C2H2-type zinc finger motif and a common WRKY motif, whereas SF8 proteins contain a C2HC-type zinc finger motif [25] and usually have atypical WRKY motifs. The SF1, SF2, SF3, and SF4 families are characterized by a single WRKY structural domain, whereas SF5, SF6, and SF9, due to the zinc finger motif variants, form independent branches. Based on these domain architectures and phylogenetic placement relative to Arabidopsis orthologs, our subfamilies can be mapped to the standard WRKY groups as follows: SF7a and SF7b belong to Group I; SF1, SF2, SF3, and SF4 belong to Group II; SF5, SF6, and SF9 belong to Group III; and SF8 represents an atypical class often associated with Group IIc. A detailed gene-wise classification is provided in Supplementary File S6. The highest gene number was observed in SF7b, followed by SF8a, SF6, and SF2. SF9 contained only a single gene. Compared with A. thaliana, three subfamilies (SF2, SF3, and SF7b) had higher gene numbers in the tea plant, three subfamilies (SF4, SF6, and SF7a) had lower numbers, and four subfamilies (SF1, SF8a, SF5, and SF8b) had similar numbers.

The tree was constructed based on the alignment of conserved WRKY domain sequences from 101 HSCs, using A. thaliana WRKY sequences as the outgroup for rooting. The 11 subfamilies (SF1–SF9, with SF7 and SF8 further divided) are indicated and were classified based on WRKY domain count and zinc-finger motif type

Protein motif and gene structure analysis

Protein motif composition and gene structure were analyzed for each HSC. As shown in Fig. 4, gene length varied among WRKY genes, primarily due to differences in intron length and number. Motif 1 and motif 2, which are the core components of the WRKY structural domains (Supplementary file S7), remained highly conserved in the vast majority of members. In some cases, variant patterns were observed: motif 1 was replaced by motif 7 in the SF8a subfamily member YH9H106624 and the SF6 subfamily member WRKYg700.t1. The newly predicted WRKY gene members generally contain the four core motifs of motif 1, motif 2, motif 3, and motif 4. Among them, two newly predicted WRKY genes (WRKYg135.t1 and WRKYg050.t1) exhibited unique motif arrangement patterns: motif 10 was inserted between motif 1 and motif 2, and motif 2 was replaced by motif 5 in WRKYg050.t1. Other motifs, except motifs 1 and 2, generally showed subfamily specificity. Motif 4 was replaced by motif 8 in some cases, primarily in SF7a subfamily members, including CSS0032925.1 and HJY087196. In addition, motif 5 was inserted between motifs 2 and 4 in SF1 subfamily member DX5H060174 and SF2 subfamily member LTDC006371. The two evolutionarily related subfamilies, SF1 and SF2, exhibit similar motif structures.

CNV, homology, gene repeat type, and transposable element analysis

To assess CNV of WRKY genes in the tea plant pangenome, gene copy number was calculated for each gene family. Among the 101 WRKY gene families, three were single-copy, and the remaining were multicopy (Supplementary file S8, Fig. 5). This distribution is consistent with duplicate gene expansion in plant transcription factor families [26].

CNV of WRKY gene clusters across 22 C. sinensis genomes. The heatmap displays the gene copy count per genome for each of the 101 HSCs

As shown in Fig. 6, the proportion of whole-genome-segmental duplications was 46.81% in core genes [27]. Transposon-associated dispersed duplications accounted for 17.4% (core), 22.2% (soft core), 26.7% (shell), and 12.5% (cloud) [28]. Proximal duplications were most numerous in shell genes (four copies), and the core genes each contained four single-copy genes. This distribution pattern is consistent with the temporal stratification hypothesis, in which ancient WGD events shape the core genome, and recent duplications contribute to species-specific innovations [29]. The distribution of duplication types across conservation categories is shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary File S9.

Distribution of duplication mechanisms in core and dispensable genes (n = 22 genomes)

Among the 1905 WRKY genes identified across 22 genomes, WGD or segmental duplications accounted for 68.40%, dispersed duplications for 25.60%, tandem duplications for 2.12%, neighboring duplications for 3.82%, and single-copy genes for 0.07% (Fig. 7). The proportions of core, shell, soft core, and cloud WRKY genes among the total identified were 34.7%, 30.7%, 22.8%, and 11.8%, respectively (Supplementary File S9).

Proportions of gene duplication mechanisms for the 1,905 identified WRKY genes (n = 22 genomes)

As shown in Fig. 8, the distribution of WRKY genes varied by chromosome. Chromosomes 13 H and 15 H contained few members, whereas other chromosomes contained several genes. Segmental duplications were the primary mechanism underlying WRKY gene expansion.

Analysis of natural selection

As shown in Fig. 9, the median Ka values of core WRKY genes were significantly higher than those of dispensable members. In contrast, median Ks values showed no significant difference (ΔKs = 0.003). The median Ka/Ks ratios for both core and dispensable groups were 0.29 and 0.33, respectively, both less than 1. Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks values for each HSC (genes in SCZ) are provided in Supplementary file S10.

Natural selection pressure analysis of core and dispensable WRKY gene clusters across 22 C. sinensis genomes based on Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks ratios. The three panels show: (upper left) Ka values, (upper right) Ks values, and (lower) Ka/Ks ratios. The significance of the difference in Ka values between core and dispensable genes was assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test (p < 0.001).

Furthermore, selection pressure varied among WRKY subfamilies (Fig. 10). The average Ka values of the subfamilies ranged from 0 to 0.23, and the corresponding Ks values ranged from 0 to 1.17. Subfamily SF4 exhibited relatively high Ka and Ks values. The initial calculation indicated an extremely high Ka/Ks value (≈ 14.6) for subfamily SF5. However, further analysis revealed that all gene pairs within SF5 exhibited very low synonymous substitution rates (Ks < 0.015), indicating that these genes originated from recent, species-specific expansion events. Thus, the Ka/Ks ratio for SF5 was not interpreted as meaningful for long-term selection pressure. Expression analysis showed that SF5 members co-expressed with stress-responsive modules (data not shown). For the remaining subfamilies, the average Ka/Ks values ranged from 0 (SF9) to 9.21 (SF6). The highest reliable Ka/Ks values were found for SF6, followed by SF8a, SF1, and SF2 (7.62 ~ 9.21). A similar pattern has been reported for stress-related transcription factors in other plants [30].

Gene-wide transcriptome analysis

Transcriptome data from 20 tea plant genomes [18] were analyzed for five tissues (buds, roots, stems, old leaves, and young leaves). Transcriptional data for all newly predicted WRKY genes in each variety were extracted, showing that more than half of these genes exhibited expression (Transcripts Per Million, TPM > 1) in at least one of the five tissues across the analyzed cultivars (Supplementary file S11). TPM values for WRKY genes were averaged across the 20 genomes. Heatmaps of the expression levels of these genes in the five tissues (right) and standard deviations (left) across the 20 tea plant genomes are shown in Fig. 11. Cluster analysis based on gene expression levels classified the WRKY transcription factors in the 20 tea plant cultivars into five distinct clusters (left tree diagram). Expression profiles within each cluster were similar across varieties.

Heatmap illustrating expression levels of WRKY genes in five tissues (right) and their standard deviation across twenty C. sinensis genomes (left)

High- and low-expression clusters both contained members from multiple subfamilies. For example, the high-expression cluster contained members of SF01 (CSS0019338), SF04 (CSS0047128), SF07b (CSS0013701, CSS0049619), and SF06 (DX5H114184), and the low-expression cluster also had a mixed distribution of SF03-SF09 (Supplementary file S6). Members of different subfamilies were present in both high- and low-expression clusters, similar to the modular regulation pattern observed in Arabidopsis WRKY genes [31]. The gene CSS0019338 in the SF01 subfamily showed significantly high expression in all five tissues (mean TPM = 172.29, standard deviation = 241.44). This expression signature suggests that CSS0019338 may be multifunctional through post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms or environmental signal integration pathways [32]. qRT-PCR validation of selected newly predicted genes is described in Sect. 2.11.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis of core WRKY transcription factors

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for core WRKY transcription factors. Among 35 core WRKY proteins analyzed, 28 were successfully mapped and formed an interaction network (Fig. 12), while the remaining seven could not be mapped. The amino acid sequences of these seven unmapped core WRKYs (CSS0005560.1, CSS0017731.1, CSS0021156.1, CSS0007384.1, CSS0022276.1, CSS0047128.1, and CSS0013638.1) are provided in Supplementary File S13. These seven proteins had no predicted interactions with other core WRKYs in the network. The network contained 28 nodes and 14 edges (average node degree = 1, PPI enrichment p < 1.0e− 16). Two hub genes, CSS0013701.1 and CSS0037160.1, exhibited the highest connectivity, each with four interactions, and both belonged to the expanded SF7b subfamily. Three clusters were identified. Proteins from the same phylogenetic subfamily tended to co-cluster. Functional enrichment analysis revealed cluster-specific specializations: Cluster 1 (red) was enriched in DNA-binding and W-box motif binding activities; Cluster 2 (green) was associated with innate immune response and defense against fungi; Cluster 3 (blue), containing the two SF7b hub genes, was enriched in jasmonic acid response and regulation of secondary metabolism.

Expression dynamics of WRKY genes under drought and salt stress

Transcriptome data from the tea cultivar SCZ under combined drought-salt stress at 24, 48, and 72 h [33] was analyzed, and the newly predicted WRKY genes were included in the analysis (Supplementary File S12, Fig. 13). Most annotated genes, including CSS0000212, CSS0005560, and CSS5905, showed significant upregulation at 24 and 48 h but down regulated by 72 h. Newly predicted WRKY genes, such as WRKYg216 and WRKYg2524, exhibited similar temporal patterns. Thus, both annotated and newly predicted WRKY genes showed early upregulation followed by reduced expression under prolonged stress. This analysis was performed on a single cultivar (SCZ) and validation across additional germplasm is will further strengthen the conclusion.

Discussion

In the present study, we innovatively established a gene family research paradigm integrating multi-omics data and systematically analyzed the evolutionary characteristics of the WRKY gene family in C. sinensis at the pan-genome level. Rather than being confined to a single reference genome, our analysis of 22 genome assemblies enabled the classification of WRKY genes into HSCs, providing a comprehensive view of both core and dispensable components of this family. We used a systematic nomenclature and renamed these HSCs as whole genes: CsWRKY.CR001-CR35 (35 core genes), CsWRKY.SC36-SC58 (23 soft-core genes), CsWRKY.SH59-SH89 (31 shell genes), CsWRKY.CL90-CL101 (12 cloud genes). This framework enabled downstream analysis, including phylogeny, gene structure, CNV, natural selection pressures, and transcriptomic profiling, to be conducted on a more complete set of loci, yielding evolutionary insights unattainable through a single reference approach. Notably, integrating transcriptome-wide data revealed transcriptional divergence and convergence across subfamilies, effectively overcoming the evolutionary signal loss inherent in single-genome studies and permitting an unprecedented perspective on the functional evolution of the WRKY family in tea plants.

Genome-wide HSCs and reference genome genes

Our pan-genomic approach identified 101 WRKY HSCs, a number substantially higher than the average of 84 WRKY genes per individual genome, underscoring the extensive PAVs within this family and highlighting the limitation of relying on a single reference genome. This magnitude of PAV is consistent with findings from recent pan-genome studies in other plant species, where significant gene content variation has also been documented for transcription factor families, including the NAC family in Hordeum vulgare and broader genomic surveys in Oryza sativa [34, 35]. This observation aligns with the known prevalence of structural variations, such as PAVs and CNVs across plant species [36], which are often associated with agronomic traits and environmental adaptation [37]. However, the extent of PAV observed in the WRKY family (35 core vs. 66 dispensable HSCs) is substantially higher than that reported for the bHLH family in Hordeum vulgare [14], suggesting that WRKY genes experience more dynamic evolutionary pressures.

The present study found that segmental duplications were the predominant mechanism for core WRKY genes, whereas tandem duplications contributed minimally across all conservation categories. This pattern mirrors findings in seed plants and angiosperms, where ancestral whole-genome duplications have been shown to drive the expansion of regulatory gene families, particularly those involved in seed and flower development [38]. Thus, the correlation between duplication type and gene conservation level observed here echoes patterns reported in other plant lineages. Accounting for PAVs and CNVs thus yields a more accurate characterization of WRKY family evolution than single-reference approaches. Three methodological advantages distinguish our pan-genomic approach from single-reference studies: (i) inclusion of WRKY genes absent from the reference genome, thereby reducing ascertainment bias; (ii) classification of core vs. dispensable genes, enabling evolutionary inferences at the population level; and (iii) calculation of selection pressures on individual HSCs, revealing differential constraints between core and dispensable members.

The discrepancy in gene counts between our study and previous reference genome-based annotations likely stems from differences in genome annotation quality [39], gene scanning methodologies, and selection criteria. The BITACORA algorithm employed here enhances the detection of conserved domain sequences, though future efforts should aim to resolve complete gene structures for these putative members. To minimize false positives and negatives, we implemented a multi-tiered validation strategy for the newly predicted genes. First, all candidates were required to contain an intact WRKY domain confirmed by Pfam scanning. Second, more than half of these newly predicted WRKY genes showed active transcription (TPM > 1) across the five tissues examined in the pan-transcriptome data, supporting their genuine expression. Third, the stress-responsive expression of representative genes was further validated by qRT-PCR experiments under cold stress. These validations collectively support the reliability of the gene predictions of the present study. For WRKY genes not expressed in the target transcriptome tissues in this study, they may be transcribed and function in other tissues and under different environmental conditions. For example, in monocotyledonous crop species, 86, 101, and 128 putative WRKY genes were identified in the reference genomes of Triticum aestivum [40], Oryza sativa [41], and Zea mays [42], respectively, and we hypothesized that the number of these genes would also change significantly when genome-wide scans were performed. Finally, the ≥ 55 aa threshold used in this study may fail to detect a minority of non-canonical WRKY members with atypically short domains. Future studies incorporating more refined sequence analysis and experimental validation could further refine the WRKY family catalog.

Biological significance of core and dispensable genes in WRKY evolution

The concepts of core and dispensable genes emerged with the release of genome-wide data from microbial genomes and have recently been adopted in genome-wide studies of plant genomes [43]. In plants, core genes are typically associated with fundamental cellular processes, while dispensable genes contribute to niche-specific adaptation and environmental responses. Although previous genome-wide studies have extensively described core and dispensable genes, most analyses have been conducted only at the genome-wide level, with few applying these concepts to specific gene families. Our pan-genomic analysis revealed clear functional partitioning between core and dispensable WRKYs. Core WRKY genes, predominantly involved in fundamental regulatory processes, exhibit strong purifying selection and widespread expression, underscoring their essential and conserved functions. This pattern is consistent with a recent pan-genome analysis of bZIP transcription factors in Cucurbitaceae, where core genes also exhibited stronger purifying selection and were preferentially retained from whole-genome/segmental duplications [44]. In contrast, dispensable WRKYs, particularly those in the shell and cloud categories, are more associated with environmental adaptation and show evidence of adaptive evolution [45], aligning with their variable presence across cultivars and suggesting a primary role in niche-specific adaptation.

This functional differentiation is further supported by our transcriptomic analyses under drought and salt stress, which revealed a biphasic expression pattern (peaking at 48 h and downregulating at 72 h). Such temporal dynamics may reflect a trade-off between stress defense and growth maintenance, a common feature of stress-responsive gene regulation.

This functional partitioning is further supported by their mechanisms of duplication. Genes from tandem duplications belonged exclusively to the soft-core category, while genes from dispersed and segmental duplicates were distributed across all conservation categories, implying that genomic context influences both evolutionary trajectory and functional constraint. To infer the biological implications of WRKY CNVs, we surveyed functionally characterized WRKY genes across plant species [46]. Based on their reported biological functions, we speculated on the biological effects of CNVs in WRKY on the tea plant. For example, expansions in SF1 are thought to be associated with increased resistance to fungal pathogens and bacterial pathogens [47]; the regulatory functions of WRKY genes in SF2 show pleiotropic alterations during plant growth, development, and response to ethylene [48]; and SF6 appears to play a central role in senescence regulation [49]; suggesting that these dispensable specific tea plant WRKY gene expansions may be directly related to environmental adaptation, in which we detected 42 shell WRKY genes, which are not annotated in any tea plant genome and are all newly annotated genes. In contrast to SF6, only one SF5 WRKY gene has been characterized in Arabidopsis, which is also involved in regulating leaf senescence and defense against pathogens [50]. It is worth noting that SF9 was identified as a single gene in the tea plant, and often this is the case where the selection of a single reference genome is likely to lead to the erroneous conclusion that SF9 is not present in the tea plant, which can be corrected by using the whole genome as a reference.

Expansion of WRKY gene family bias in C. sinensis

Gene duplication, as an essential driver of plant genome evolution, significantly increases genome complexity and functional diversity by generating additional genetic material [51]. In this study, amplification of the tea WRKY gene family was mainly driven by WGD and segmental duplication events, particularly for core genes, which show high sequence conservation. The finding that tandem duplications were exclusively associated with soft-core genes suggests a mechanism for the rapid, recent expansion of gene families involved in condition-specific responses, a pattern observed in other species, such as tomato [52]. This observation aligns with the “gene balance hypothesis,” which posits that genes retained following tandem duplication tend to be less connected and more prone to lineage-specific functional diversification, whereas whole-genome duplications preferentially retain highly connected, dosage-sensitive genes [53]. To better interpret the tea-specific expansion pattern, we compared our results with those from other woody perennials. Vitis vinifera contains 80 WRKY genes distributed broadly across subfamilies but shows limited lineage-specific expansion [54]. Similarly, Theobroma cacao possessed 61 WRKY members, without pronounced subfamily amplification [55]. Furthermore, comparison with coffee (Coffea canephora) revealed a similar trend of constrained WRKY family size and lack of drastic subfamily-specific expansion [56]. These observations suggest that constrained WRKY family sizes may be common among woody perennials. In contrast, our pan-genomic analysis identified 101 WRKY HSCs in tea, with notable expansions in subfamilies SF7b and SF8a, likely mediated by WGD and dispersed duplication events. This suggests a more recent and extensive diversification of the WRKY family in tea, possibly reflecting adaptive evolution to its perennial habit and diverse environmental pressures.

Functional implications of the WRKY PPI Network

The PPI network analysis revealed that core WRKY transcription factors in tea plants form a hierarchical and modular regulatory system. A similar modular organization has been reported for the WRKY regulatory network in Arabidopsis during early immune signaling, where distinct functional modules coordinate defense gene expression [57]. The identification of two SF7b subfamily members, CSS0013701.1 and CSS0037160.1, as hub genes underscored their central role in network topology. Their high connectivity, combined with functional enrichment in JA signaling and secondary metabolism, suggested that these hubs integrate stress responses with metabolic reprogramming - a key adaptation in perennial crops, such as tea. The division of the network into three functional clusters reflected a functional specialization that may enhance regulatory efficiency. Cluster 1 appeared to provide basal transcriptional machinery through DNA-binding activity, while Cluster 2 was specialized for immune activation. Cross-cluster interactions suggested signal integration, a feature also observed in the Arabidopsis WRKY network, where modules crosstalk to balance growth and defense. The strong purifying selection acting on SF7b hub genes (as evidenced by low Ka/Ks ratios) contrasted with the more rapid evolution of lineage-specific WRKYs, underscoring their essential roles in plant fitness. These dual roles in stress and metabolism made them promising targets for molecular breeding.

Finally, the seven core WRKYs not mapped in the network may represent tea-specific regulators with novel functions. Their absence from current databases suggested potential lineage-specific adaptations, possibly related to specialized metabolism such as caffeine biosynthesis. Future experimental validation using yeast two-hybrid or co-immunoprecipitation assays will be required to confirm their interaction partners and functional roles.

Experimental validation reinforces the authenticity and stress-responsive roles of newly predicted WRKY genes

Our cold-stress-based qRT-PCR analysis strengthened the authenticity and stress-responsive nature of newly predicted WRKY genes originating from dispensable genomic regions. The successful validation of multiple candidates underscored two key advances. First, it provided strong empirical confirmation for our integrated prediction pipeline (combining BITACORA with domain validation and transcriptional screening), demonstrating that the pan-genome approach can expand the catalog of functional genes beyond single-reference annotations. Second, it forged a critical link between genomic variation and adaptive physiology. The fact that genes identified through their variable presence across cultivars are readily induced by abiotic stress strongly supported the hypothesis that the dispensable genome is a reservoir for adaptive traits. These results confirmed that our pan-genomic framework is effective for both discovering genetic variation and prioritizing candidate genes for functional studies. The validated genes, including WRKYg013, WRKYg2524, and WRKYg2525, now represent high-confidence targets for future mechanistic investigations, such as elucidating their position in the core WRKY regulatory network or their role in regulating quality-related metabolites.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although our analysis of 22 genomes provides a far more comprehensive view than any single reference genome, the current dataset primarily encompasses modern cultivated varieties (C. sinensis var. sinensis). Genetic diversity from wild relatives, such as C. sinensis var. assamica, ancient landraces, or under-sampled geographic regions may not be fully captured. This sampling bias is a common limitation in plant pan-genome studies. For instance, a representative Oryza sativa pan-genome study was similarly dominated by cultivated accessions [35]. Therefore, some rare or ecologically specialized WRKY gene variants could be absent from our current pan-genome. Future pan-genome assemblies incorporating a broader array of germplasm resources will help elucidate the species’ full genetic landscape. The homologous sequence clustering in this study used a fixed 95% amino acid identity threshold, which is widely adopted in plant comparative genomics. Future studies could further optimize gene family clustering by employing orthology-aware methods, such as OrthoFinder, or by conducting systematic sensitivity analyses on clustering parameters. This would provide an even more refined perspective on gene family evolution.

The functional roles of newly predicted and lineage-specific WRKY genes remain computationally inferred. Notably, these functional inferences rely heavily on functional annotations of homologous WRKY genes in Arabidopsis thaliana, a herbaceous plant, and the inherent biological differences between herbaceous and woody plants may limit the predictive power of such orthology-based functional annotations for tea plant genes. The predicted associations with stress adaptation and quality trait regulation require direct experimental validation. Cis-regulatory element analysis of promoter regions was not feasible because current pan-genome assemblies primarily provide coding sequences without precise transcription start site annotations for newly predicted genes. Nevertheless, the observed stress-responsive expression (transcriptome and qRT-PCR) suggested the presence of functional regulatory elements upstream of these loci. Future work should focus on: (i) experimental validation of protein expression and subcellular localization; (ii) cis-regulatory element analysis to decipher transcriptional mechanisms; (iii) integrating WRKY PAV-CNV data with metabolomic QTL mapping to identify alleles controlling quality traits, such as catechins and theanine; and (iv) functional characterization via CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, overexpression, yeast one-hybrid, and EMSA. The validated cold-responsive genes WRKYg013, WRKYg2524, and WRKYg2525 provided ideal starting points for these investigations.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically elucidated the evolutionary features and functional diversity of the WRKY gene family by integrating pan-genomic and transcriptomic data from 22 tea plant accessions. A total of 101 WRKY HSCs were identified, a number that substantially exceeds the annotation capacity of any single reference genome, thereby confirming the prevalence of gene PAVs within the WRKY family in tea plants. Among them, core genes (CsWRKY.CR001-CR35) were retained over time due to purifying selection facilitated by functional redundancy. In contrast, dispensable genes, such as shell-layer WRKY, drove environmental adaptive evolution through dispersed duplication with positive selection. Gene duplication was the primary driver of WRKY family expansion, with 68.40% of WGDs contributing to core gene conservation, whereas dispersed duplications (25.60%) were largely responsible for lineage-specific evolution of dispensable genes. Natural selection analysis indicated that core genes were subject to stronger purifying selection (Ka/Ks = 0.29) and their functional redundancy was retained over time through WGD events. Transcriptomic data further revealed that the highly expressed gene clusters, SF01-CSS0019338 and SF04-CSS0047128, exhibited cross-subfamily aggregation, suggesting that members of different subfamilies are involved in common biological pathways through functional complementation. The integrated pan-genomic multi-omics framework established herein overcomes the limitations inherent in single-reference genome analyses. By distinguishing between core and dispensable genes, this study clarifies the biological significance of PAVs and CNVs at the gene family level, thereby advancing our understanding of WRKY family evolution and providing a theoretical foundation for mining stress-resistance genes and implementing precision breeding in tea plants. Moreover, over half of the newly predicted WRKY genes showed active expression in the transcriptome, validating the reliability of the BITACORA algorithm and supplying high-confidence candidates for subsequent functional genomics research. Future studies should integrate functional experiments to verify the biological roles of these newly predicted genes and elucidate their molecular connections with tea quality traits.

Materials and methods

Identification of the WRKY gene family in the whole genome of the tea plant

The genome sequences of 22 tea plant whole-genome samples were downloaded from the relevant literature [18]. A full genome scan of the WRKY gene was performed in full mode using the BITACORA (v1.4.2) tool [58] with E-values of both BLAST and hmmscan set to 1e-5. Hidden Markov Model (HMM) files of the WRKY structural domain (PF00010) were obtained from http://pfam.xfam.org/. The genome sequence, gene annotation model files, and annotated WRKY protein sequences were used as input to the BITACORA tool to scan the whole genome of 22 tea plants. For the protein sequence of the tea plant WRKY gene, the WRKY structural domain (PF00010) was scanned using the hmmscan tool. The identified WRKY genes were verified to contain the complete WRKY structural domains by separate hmmscan searches and screened for genes with a WRKY structural domain length ≥ 55 amino acids.

Homologous genome cluster inference

The homologous WRKY gene clusters across 22 tea plant whole-genome samples were inferred using the CD-HIT tool with 95% identity and 90% sequence coverage to identify strong representative sequences. In this study, sequence similarity-based clustering was primarily employed to delineate homologous sequence clusters for pan-genome-level presence/absence variation analysis. This approach provides each cluster (HSC) with a clear “present” or “absent” status definition, thereby forming the basis for core versus non-essential gene classification. It should be noted that this method aims to capture broad homology relationships rather than strictly distinguish between orthologs and paralogs.

Phylogenetic tree construction

Conserved WRKY structural domain sequences were identified using the hmmscan tool in the HMMER (v3.4) toolkit (http://hmmer.org/) [59]. The amino acid sequences of the WRKY structural domains were compared by Muscle (8 iterations) [60]. An unrooted maximum-likelihood tree was constructed based on the best-fitting JTT+G4 alternative model (lowest BIC score) using IQ-TREE (v1.6.12) [60]. Dendritic support was calculated by the 1000 self-help method and the SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test. Dendrogram annotation was performed using FigTree (v1.4.3).

Natural selection analysis

Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks calculations between gene pairs were performed using the ParaAT (v2.0) tool with default settings. Homologous WRKY gene pairs from the tea plant whole genome samples were used as input to ParaAT. The mean and standard deviation of Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks were calculated by the groupby function of the Pandas package in Python 3.7.

Analysis of gene structure and conserved structural domains

The exon-intron structure of the WRKY gene was retrieved from the gene-annotated GFF3 file (SCZ); for WRKY genes missing in C. sinensis, gene annotation files from other tea plant species were used. For newly predicted WRKY genes, gene structure information identified by BITACORA was used. The final gene structure information was visualized by TBtools [61]. The MEME (v5.4.1) tool was used with default settings via https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme.

Gene duplication and covariance analysis

Repeat types and covariance of WRKY genes were analyzed using the MCScanX (v1.0.0) tool [61]. Protein sequences from the whole genome of each tea plant were analyzed by all-on-all BLASTP (E-value threshold of 1e− 05), and only the first five BLASTP comparisons were retained. The BLASTP output and the GFF3 gene location file (converted to bed format) were used as input data for MCScanX. Gene duplicate types were inferred by the duplicate_gene_classifier procedure. The brief procedure was: (i) all genes were initially classified as “single-copy genes”; (ii) genes with BLASTP matches to other genes were re-labelled as “duplicate dispersed genes”; (iii) for any BLASTP match, the gene ranking of the two genes was changed to “duplicate_gene_classifier” if the difference between the gene rankings of the two genes was not significant. (iii) for any BLASTP match, if the difference in gene rank between two genes is < 20, the gene is relabeled as a ‘close neighbor duplicate’ (this threshold is the default setting in MCScanX and has been widely adopted to distinguish gene pairs likely arising from local duplication events, as opposed to tandem duplicates or dispersed duplicates); (iv) if the difference in gene rank between two genes is = 1, the gene is relabeled as a ‘tandem duplicate’; (v) anchored genes in the covariate block are relabeled as ‘WGD’. are relabeled as “WGD/segmental duplicates”. To resolve potential classification conflicts, the procedure adheres to the hierarchical priority rules built-in the MCScanX standard analysis pipeline, with the priority order being: tandem duplicate > close neighbor duplicate > WGD/segmental duplicate > dispersed duplicate. Each gene pair is uniquely assigned to the corresponding category when it meets the criteria for a higher-priority type, ensuring consistency and unambiguity in duplication-type classification. Co-linearity was visualized by the Circos program [62].

Transcriptome analysis

The raw sequencing data from five tissues (BUD, STEM, ROOT, OL, YL) of 16 tea plant genomes were obtained from the research of Zhang Xingtan’s team [18]. The TPM data of the five tissues from SCZ, TGY, LJ43, and HD were sourced from the TPIA database [63]. The data were filtered using Trimmomatic [63], aligned with Hisat2 [64], and the TPM values were calculated using featureCounts [65]. The average TPM and standard deviation of the gene expression in the pangenomes of the 20 genomes were computed. The average TPM was normalized using log2(TPM + 1). The relative standard deviation was calculated by dividing the standard deviation by the average TPM, followed by a log2(std + 1) transformation. The expression heatmap was generated using the clustermap function in the seaborn package of Python.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis

The amino acid sequences of the 35 core WRKY transcription factors were used as input for the STRING database (v12.0) with the organism set to C. sinensis. A high-confidence interaction score (≥ 0.7) was applied. Hub genes were identified as nodes with the highest degree of connectivity. Functional modules were detected using the built-in Markov Clustering (MCL) algorithm in STRING with an inflation parameter of 3.0.

Cold stress treatment and quantitative real-time PCR analysis in plants

One-year-old C. sinensis seedlings with uniform growth were selected and pre-cultured in an artificial climate chamber (conditions: 25 °C, 16/8 h light/dark cycle). For cold stress treatment, seedlings were randomly divided into two groups: the control group continued growing at 25 °C, while the treatment group was transferred to a 4 °C incubator. Fully expanded young leaves were collected at two time points: immediately before treatment (0 h) and 24 h after treatment. Leaves were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for RNA extraction. Three biological replicates were established for each time point. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with Oligo dT primers. Gene-specific primers for qRT-PCR analysis were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software (primer sequences detailed in Supplementary Material Table S13). The tea plant CsACTIN gene served as an internal control for normalizing target gene expression levels. qRT-PCR reactions were performed on a CFX384 real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR system using a 20 µL reaction volume containing cDNA template, specific primers, and SYBR Green Premix. The amplification program was set as follows: 30-second pre-denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 5-second denaturation at 95 °C and 30-second annealing/extension at 60 °C. A melting curve analysis was performed post-reaction to confirm the specificity of the amplified products. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical significance of expression differences between treatment and control groups was assessed using Student’s t-test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).
