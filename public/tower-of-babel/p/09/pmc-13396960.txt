Extensive corporal necrosis in penile ischemic gangrene: A case report of total penectomy in an octogenarian without diabetes or renal failure
Authors: Ahmed Chaabouni, Wafa Elleuch, Mohamed Trigui, Kays Chaker, Nouri Rebai, Yassine Nouira, Mourad Hadj Slimen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenile gangrene is a rare but severe clinical emergency, typically linked to advanced diabetes or chronic renal failure. We report the case of an 85-year-old male with a severe cerebrovascular history who presented with apparently localized glans gangrene. Although a standard partial penectomy was initially planned, intraoperative exploration revealed extensive deep corporal necrosis, ultimately necessitating an immediate conversion to a total penectomy. This case strongly highlights that surface clinical signs frequently underestimate deep tissue damage. Therefore, advanced preoperative imaging, particularly MRI, is crucial, and informed consent must explicitly include the potential need for total penectomy during unexpected surgical findings.Keywords: Penile gangrene, Total penectomy, Partial penectomy

Introduction

Penile ischemic gangrene is a rare and devastating condition that typically manifests in patients with profound vascular compromise, most notably long-standing diabetes mellitus, advanced peripheral arterial disease, or chronic renal failure.1,2 The underlying micro and macrovascular occlusion leads to progressive tissue necrosis, drawing strong clinical and pathophysiological parallels to ischemic dry gangrene of the lower extremities.3 In the clinical evaluation of this condition, advanced soft-tissue imaging particularly Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is critical for accurately delineating the deep, proximal extent of the non-viable tissue within the corpora.4 Ultimately, when tissue loss is extensive or conservative management is not feasible, definitive treatment demands aggressive surgical intervention, frequently necessitating a total penectomy to control systemic risks.5

Case presentation

An 85-year-old male with a medical history significant for hypertension and previous ischemic cerebrovascular accidents presented to the emergency department with a three day history of progressive blackish discoloration of the glans penis (Fig. 1). On examination, the patient was hemodynamically unstable and in a state of shock, necessitating the initiation of continuous intravenous norepinephrine for blood pressure support.

Penile gangrene of glans and distal shaft.

External genitourinary examination revealed a circumcised penis with well-demarcated, dry necrotic eschar localized to the glans. The scrotum and testicles were clinically unremarkable. A transurethral Foley catheter was successfully inserted, draining clear urine. The patient presented with leukocytosis. Initial blood cultures were negative, and both coagulation parameters and blood glucose levels were within normal limits, ruling out diabetes mellitus and primary coagulopathy. No purulent discharge was observed on examination.

Due to the patient's critical hemodynamic instability and the immediate unavailability of an emergency magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan to assess deep tissue viability, he was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for resuscitation. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis was initiated, and a pre-operative platelet transfusion was administered to optimize his surgical profile.

Given the uncertainty regarding the proximal extent of tissue viability, the patient and his family were informed that intraoperative findings could necessitate conversion from partial to total penectomy with creation of a perineal urethrostomy. Informed consent was obtained accordingly.

Once stabilized, the patient was transferred to the operating room with the initial intent of performing a partial penectomy. However, upon surgical incision and exploration, extensive and unexpected ischemic necrosis was discovered tracking deeply into both the corpus spongiosum and the corpora cavernosa (Fig. 2).

Operative Specimen: Ischemia of the glans, spongiosal and cavernosal corporal tissues.

The involved erectile tissues appeared frankly non-viable and did not demonstrate evidence of bleeding after incision, consistent with irreversible ischemic injury. No purulence or findings suggestive of Fournier's gangrene were identified intraoperatively. Tissue cultures were obtained and subsequently returned negative.

Given the lack of viable erectile tissue and the high risk of progressive sepsis, the procedure was immediately converted to a total penectomy. A perineal urethrostomy was fashioned to allow for dependent urinary diversion, and a suprapubic catheter was placed at the conclusion of the procedure to secure bladder drainage (Fig. 3).

Post-operative result of total penectomy and perineal urethrostomy.

Although portions of the penile shaft skin appeared macroscopically viable, preservation of a skin tube was not pursued because of concerns regarding the reliability of its vascular supply after complete excision of the underlying ischemic corporal tissues and the need for definitive source control in this critically ill patient.

Discussion

While ischemic gangrene of the glans penis remains a rare clinical entity, historically documented etiologies predominantly include advanced diabetes mellitus, calciphylaxis secondary to chronic renal failure, localized trauma, thrombotic events, and severe infectious processes.1 Notably, our patient lacked the classic metabolic predisposing factors of diabetes or end-stage renal disease. Instead, his extensive history of ischemic cerebrovascular events strongly suggests a severe, generalized macroatherosclerotic burden as the primary predictive factor. Furthermore, as highlighted in recent literature regarding vasopressor induced peripheral ischemia, the requirement for high dose norepinephrine to manage his initial hemodynamic instability likely exacerbated localized tissue hypoxia via intense alpha adrenergic vasoconstriction, precipitating the acute gangrenous event.6

A critical step in the initial management of these patients is accurately distinguishing pure dry ischemic gangrene from infectious wet gangrene (e.g., Fournier's gangrene), as this distinction fundamentally dictates the therapeutic trajectory. In our case, the absence of purulent discharge, the lack of intraoperative evidence of necrotizing infection, and the negative blood and tissue cultures supported a predominantly ischemic rather than infectious process.

In confirmed cases of dry gangrene, treatment options are stratified based on the extent of necrosis, ranging from conservative topical measures to partial penectomy.5 Conservative strategies are generally reserved for small, well-circumscribed lesions or for highly comorbid patients with prohibitive surgical risks secondary to diffuse arterial occlusive disease. However, definitive surgical resection becomes mandatory upon disease progression, intractable pain, or the onset of superimposed infection.

In the present case, a partial penectomy was initially planned based on the visually localized necrosis of the glans. However, the lack of immediate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) availability precluded an accurate preoperative assessment of deep tissue viability. Although MRI remains the preferred modality for evaluating the proximal extent of corporal involvement, it may not be readily available in emergency or resource-limited settings. In such circumstances, penile Doppler ultrasonography may provide useful information regarding arterial perfusion, while computed tomography may assist in excluding anaerobic gas-forming infection and evaluating local tissue extension. Nevertheless, surgical exploration remains the definitive method for assessing corporal viability when imaging is unavailable or inconclusive.

Previous studies emphasize that soft tissue imaging is crucial, as external clinical signs frequently fail to reflect the true depth of cavernosal infarction.4 Consequently, the discovery of unforeseen, extensive necrosis tracking into the spongiosal and cavernosal tissues during surgical exploration necessitated an immediate intraoperative conversion to a total penectomy.

While staged debridement with delayed reassessment has been proposed in selected patients with uncertain corporal viability, particularly in cases of calciphylaxis-related penile necrosis, this strategy was considered unsuitable in our patient. Intraoperative exploration demonstrated extensive and frankly non-viable involvement of both corpora cavernosa and the corpus spongiosum, without evidence of residual tissue perfusion. Given the patient's initial shock state and the risk of ongoing necrosis and secondary septic complications, definitive surgical management was favored over a staged organ-preserving approach.

Similarly, although preservation of the penile skin tube may provide cosmetic and psychological benefits in selected cases, the extensive underlying corporal necrosis raised concerns regarding the long-term viability of the remaining tissues. Because the primary objective was complete removal of non-viable tissue and definitive disease control, a penile-sparing reconstructive strategy was not considered appropriate in this particular setting.

This case underscores a vital clinical caveat: comprehensive preoperative informed consent must explicitly encompass the potential need for total penectomy and perineal urethrostomy, acknowledging the well documented discrepancy between visual clinical assessment and intraoperative realities in penile ischemia.7 Even when necrosis appears limited to the glans, occult proximal cavernosal involvement may be present and become apparent only during surgical exploration. Surgeons should therefore discuss the possibility of more extensive resection with patients and families whenever uncertainty exists regarding the true extent of tissue ischemia.

Conclusion

Ischemic penile gangrene is a rare but catastrophic manifestation of underlying systemic vascular disease. While early clinical recognition and advanced soft tissue imaging, such as MRI, are paramount for accurately delineating the extent of tissue necrosis, this case highlights that surface clinical findings can be deceptively localized. When timely imaging is unavailable or when conservative measures fail, urologists must be prepared for aggressive surgical intervention. Ultimately, total penectomy remains a necessary and life saving procedure when deep corporal ischemia is encountered, underscoring the critical importance of intraoperative adaptability and comprehensive preoperative patient counseling.
