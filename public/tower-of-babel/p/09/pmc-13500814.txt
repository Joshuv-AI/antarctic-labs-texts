A physics-informed foundation model for rapid high-fidelity structural response prediction
Authors: Shiqiao Meng, Ying Zhou, Bingxu Liao, Mushi Chang, Tianshu Zhang, Abouzar Jafari, Abderrahim Djerrad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate and rapid prediction of nonlinear structural dynamics is central to structural design, disaster mitigation and resilience assessment, yet conventional finite-element analysis remains too computationally demanding for real-time or portfolio-scale applications. To address this computational bottleneck, we develop SeisGPT, a physics-informed foundation model for high-fidelity response prediction across multistorey buildings with diverse structural types and topologies. Its architecture integrates structural-mechanics priors, storey-resolved structural representations and a spectral decoder that captures modal response propagation while accommodating learnable nonlinear corrections. The model is trained on more than 2 million nonlinear elastoplastic simulations derived from 270,000 automatically generated, code-compliant structural designs and 694 real-world buildings, together comprising over 10 billion response time steps. For previously unseen buildings, SeisGPT predicts displacement and acceleration histories with normalized errors below 5% and achieves an approximately 40,000-fold computational speedup relative to conventional finite-element analysis. By assimilating sparse sensor measurements, SeisGPT reconstructs full-building responses from limited observations and, in shake-table tests, shows closer agreement with measured responses than finite-element simulations, supporting structural-health monitoring and post-earthquake damage-state assessment. These results indicate that physics-informed foundation models can support high-fidelity structural-response prediction, sparse-sensor reconstruction and response-informed downstream structural assessment at inference speeds compatible with real-time and portfolio-scale use.

Introduction

Nonlinear structural dynamics provides the foundational framework for predicting how structures behave under diverse external dynamic excitations, including seismic ground motions, wind loads, traffic-induced vibrations, and mechanical impacts1–4. Rapid, high-fidelity response prediction is central to structural design, disaster mitigation, operational maintenance and digital-twin applications5–8. In practice, however, such analyses still depend largely on elastoplastic finite-element analysis (FEA)9–11, which typically requires hours to days per simulation and therefore limits iterative design, rapid assessment and portfolio-scale decision-making12–17.

To alleviate this computational bottleneck, structural engineering has increasingly adopted surrogate modeling approaches18. Early methods, including simplified analytical surrogates and regression-based approximations, offered speed but often lacked the fidelity required to capture nonlinear response, geometric complexity, and boundary-condition effects in realistic structures. These AI-driven methods can be broadly categorized into two major streams: purely data-driven approaches—such as convolutional neural networks (CNN)19–21, recurrent neural networks (RNN)22–24, long short-term memory (LSTM)25–28 networks, and Transformers29,30—which primarily utilize observational data to capture complex underlying relationships without explicitly representing physical processes; and physics-informed approaches31–37, which incorporate structural mechanics principles directly into machine learning (ML) frameworks.

Despite notable advances, several critical challenges persist in contemporary ML-based dynamic response modeling. A principal limitation is the restricted structural generalizability: existing models are predominantly data-driven or narrowly physics-informed, and are typically confined to singular typologies such as standard frames or shear walls. Such constraints overlook variations in building height, plan irregularities, material gradation, and hybrid-system connectivity, with the result that these models often fail to capture continuum-level mechanics and the nonlinear elasto-plastic response of entire building structures under external excitation, leading to substantial performance degradation when applied outside their original training domain20,34,35. Another fundamental challenge lies in the absence of universal structural knowledge and the scarcity of experimentally validated, multi-type datasets, both of which undermine the reliability of predictions across diverse structural systems35. A further obstacle is the difficulty of achieving high-resolution, spatially detailed predictions, particularly for high-rise or geometrically irregular structures, as most existing ML frameworks trade fine-scale fidelity for computational efficiency25,38,39. Finally, current approaches remain limited in their ability to integrate sparse sensor data, which prevents predictive accuracy from surpassing that of finite-element-based simulations.

To address these limitations, we introduce SeisGPT, a generalized physics-informed foundation model for predicting dynamic responses of previously unseen multistorey building typologies. Rather than treating structural response prediction as a generic sequence-modeling task, SeisGPT combines a structurally conditioned Spectral Duhamel–Green (SDG) Mixer with a mass–stiffness-aware graph neural network encoder, allowing structural-mechanics information to enter both the spatial representation of buildings and the temporal propagation of dynamic responses. The SDG-Mixer represents latent response evolution through a mass-normalized stiffness operator, differentiable spectral decomposition, Green’s-function-based modal propagation and a bounded learnable spectral correction, thereby embedding structural-dynamics principles directly into the model architecture. SeisGPT is pretrained on a large-scale structural response dataset comprising responses from 694 real-world instrumented buildings and more than 270,000 automatically generated, code-compliant structural designs, collectively yielding over 10 billion nonlinear response time steps. A sliding-window training strategy further enables the model to learn response evolution over long excitation histories while maintaining efficient inference. For deployment-oriented applications, the framework also incorporates a lightweight data-assimilation module that reconstructs full spatiotemporal structural responses from sparse sensor inputs. Together, these components provide an end-to-end framework for rapid structural response prediction, sparse-sensor response reconstruction and response-informed post-earthquake assessment across heterogeneous multistorey building systems.

Results

Structural response prediction with SeisGPT

SeisGPT is a pretrained architecture for structural-response prediction under external dynamic loading. It processes excitation histories together with essential structural descriptors, including mass and stiffness information, and is designed to generalize across floor-defined multistorey buildings with varying geometry and lateral-force-resisting organization. The architecture combines a simplified dynamic-response (SDR) module for efficient prior-response estimation with a physics-informed graph neural network (GNN) that encodes storey-level structural interactions.

As shown in Fig. 1a, SeisGPT adopts an encoder–decoder architecture. External excitation records and structural matrices are first processed through a preprocessing stage that incorporates the SDR module to generate preliminary floor-level responses. Rather than relying on standard sequence-modeling paradigms, the decoder is built around the Spectral Duhamel–Green (SDG) Mixer, a dynamics-tailored backbone that replaces conventional multi-head self-attention with a structure-dynamics-coupled spectral operator. Within this module, the network constructs a mass-normalized stiffness operator in token space and performs differentiable eigendecomposition to project latent features into a modal basis. Temporal propagation is then implemented through a modal Green’s-function transfer that represents phase advance and damping decay in underdamped structural oscillators. To account for geometric and material nonlinearities that are not fully represented by linear modal propagation, the SDG-Mixer incorporates a bounded learnable rational spectral correction. These dynamics-specific inductive biases encode wave-propagation and structural-resonance behavior while enabling global information exchange with lower parameter count and computational cost than generic attention mechanisms. A final linear projection yields multi-step structural-response forecasts. The same framework can also incorporate sparse sensor inputs to reconstruct responses at uninstrumented locations.

The SeisGPT framework comprises two downstream variants. SeisGPT-Enhanced is obtained by fine-tuning a large pretrained base model on real-building response data, improving fidelity for practical engineering applications and enabling efficient building-specific adaptation through low-rank adaptation (LoRA). SeisGPT-R follows a parallel route, with pretraining on sparsified synthetic responses and fine-tuning on sparse real-building observations, to reconstruct full spatiotemporal responses from limited sensor inputs.

Subsequently, to better reflect real-world structural dynamics, SeisGPT-Base is fine-tuned using a dataset of 694 FE models derived from existing building structures, resulting in SeisGPT-Enhanced (Fig. 1b). This fine-tuning substantially improves predictive fidelity, aligning the model more closely with practical engineering scenarios. Additionally, SeisGPT-Enhanced can be rapidly adapted to specific buildings when FE analyses are available, employing low-rank adaptation (LoRA)—an efficient parameter-tuning approach requiring minimal computational overhead.

To address the challenge of limited observational data, the framework introduces SeisGPT-R (SeisGPT-Reconstruction), a variant designed to infer complete structural response profiles from sparse sensor measurements. The model is pretrained as SeisGPT-R-Base on a sparsified version of the synthetic building response dataset, enabling it to learn structural response patterns under incomplete input conditions. It is subsequently fine-tuned on sparse response data from 694 real-world FE building models, yielding SeisGPT-R-Enhanced. This reconstruction-oriented variant estimates responses across all floors when dense instrumentation is unavailable. Its ability to recover floor-wise response histories from partial observations supports structural health monitoring, response-informed post-earthquake damage-state evaluation and data-efficient condition assessment in sensor-sparse settings.

Pretraining SeisGPT-Base on the large-scale dataset for structural generalization

SeisGPT-Base was trained and evaluated on a synthetic dataset comprising more than 2,000,000 finite-element simulations derived from three floor-defined structural systems: frame, frame–shear wall and shear wall buildings. The 270,000 corresponding finite-element models were generated through the automated design workflow described in the Methods, spanning substantial variation in height, plan geometry, wall distribution and lateral-force-resisting organization within the validated application domain. This dataset is intended to provide broad coverage of admissible floor-defined multistorey configurations for structural-response prediction, rather than unrestricted generality across arbitrary structural morphologies. Its representativeness was further examined through representative topology visualization, code-normalized drift verification and descriptor-space comparison with the real-building dataset (Supplementary Figs. 2–4). Across multiple excitation scenarios, the resulting dataset contains more than 10 billion floor-response time steps for supervised pretraining (Fig. 1b).

Separate models were trained for absolute acceleration and relative displacement prediction. For evaluation, 1000 previously unseen buildings were sampled from each structural category, yielding 3000 test structures in total. As summarized in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2, SeisGPT-Base achieved acceleration FNMAEs of 0.0226, 0.0216, and 0.0250 for frame, frame–shear wall and shear wall buildings, respectively, with corresponding displacement FNMAEs of 0.0437, 0.0416, and 0.0633. These results show that large-scale pretraining on diverse nonlinear simulations enables accurate response prediction for previously unseen buildings and loading scenarios. Additional floor-position analyses further show that SeisGPT-Base maintains consistent prediction accuracy across relative storey elevations and structural types (Supplementary Fig. 10). Complementary response-history visualizations for representative frame, frame–shear wall and shear wall structures are provided in Supplementary Figs. 11–13. To further assess the effectiveness of the SeisGPT architecture, we compared the performance of SeisGPT-Base against several baseline models, including Phy-Seisformer35, GRU40, LSTM41, TimesNet42, Timer-XL43, N-Beats44, Informer45, and TimeMixer46. Specifically, for the GRU and LSTM models, we replaced the SeisGPT-Base decoder (see Fig. 1a) with the respective model’s decoder while retaining its original encoder, resulting in two hybrid variants: SeisGRU and SeisLSTM. As shown in Fig. 2, SeisGPT achieved lower FNMAE/FNRMSE values and higher Pearson correlation coefficients than the evaluated baseline models for both acceleration and displacement prediction across the three structural types. A decoder-backbone ablation further isolates the contribution of the SDG-Mixer, showing that it improves the accuracy–efficiency trade-off relative to a Transformer decoder under otherwise identical architectural settings (Supplementary Table 7).

Fine-tuning SeisGPT-enhanced with real-world building response dataset

To better align the pretrained model with real structural behavior, SeisGPT-Base was fine-tuned on FEM response histories from 694 real buildings, of which 629 were used for training and 65 for testing, yielding SeisGPT-Enhanced (see Fig. 1b). To evaluate the respective contributions of synthetic pretraining and real-data fine-tuning, we compared three training configurations on the real-world building response dataset: A1, trained from scratch using only the limited real-building data; A2, SeisGPT-Base pretrained on the large-scale synthetic dataset and evaluated directly on real buildings; and A3, SeisGPT-Enhanced obtained by further fine-tuning A2 on the real-building dataset. Synthetic pretraining reduced acceleration and displacement FNMAE from 0.0524 and 0.1189 in A1 to 0.0227 and 0.0666 in A2, while increasing the corresponding Pearson correlation coefficients from 0.9079 and 0.8406 to 0.9781 and 0.9359. Subsequent fine-tuning yielded FNMAE values of 0.0195 and 0.0639, with R values of 0.9829 and 0.9423 for acceleration and displacement, respectively (Fig. 3a, b). On the 65-building test set, SeisGPT-Enhanced also achieved lower errors than the evaluated alternative models; for example, compared with TimesNet, it reduced acceleration and displacement FNMAE from 0.0470 and 0.1036 to 0.0195 and 0.0639 (Fig. 3c, d). These results indicate that large-scale synthetic pretraining provides transferable structural-response representations, while targeted fine-tuning further aligns the model with real structural behavior. Few-shot fine-tuning of SeisGPT-enhanced for structure-specific adaptation

In building-specific seismic response forecasting and response-informed downstream assessment, the availability of recorded response histories for individual buildings is often limited. Efficient utilization of these sparse datasets could improve predictive accuracy for future seismic events. To examine this possibility, we explored the efficacy of few-shot fine-tuning to personalize the predictive performance of SeisGPT-Enhanced for individual structures. Specifically, starting from a globally fine-tuned baseline model, we employed low-rank adaptation (LoRA) to fine-tune SeisGPT-Enhanced using between one and nine recorded seismic response histories per building from a benchmark dataset comprising 65 real-world structures. An additional, unseen seismic response sequence per building was reserved for independent evaluation. The model was explicitly trained to forecast the relative displacement responses of the buildings. It should be noted that, unlike the dataset used in the previous section, the test dataset in this experiment is constructed such that each of the 65 buildings has only one seismic response record under a single ground motion, which is used exclusively for evaluation.

As shown in Fig. 4a, adaptation with a single response sequence increased R from 0.942 to 0.962 and reduced FNMAE by about 10%, with additional response sequences leading to further reductions in error. We also tested four LoRA placements—across all linear layers, encoder only, decoder only and output layer only—and found that encoder adaptation yielded the largest improvement among the tested placements (Fig. 4b). Zero-shot prediction across different structural systems

To assess the extent of cross-system generalization, we evaluated SeisGPT-Base on structural systems that were not represented in the training dataset. Specifically, we considered three categories that differ fundamentally in material properties, stiffness distribution, and dynamic response: (i) 2000 automatically designed steel structures generating 6000 elastoplastic response records, (ii) 33 masonry structures modeled from real buildings in Abaqus producing 73 analyses, and (iii) 4 base-isolated buildings modeled in ETABS yielding 33 analyses. All evaluations were performed in a strict zero-shot setting, without fine-tuning or hyperparameter adjustment, and benchmarked against existing predictive models. Details of the steel-structure design and modeling procedure are provided in the Supplementary Information, while the macro-link constitutive scope of the masonry benchmark and representative masonry and base-isolated models are shown in Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8.

Across these structural systems, which differ from the training distribution in material behavior, stiffness characteristics and energy-dissipation mechanisms, SeisGPT-Base retained predictive accuracy in the zero-shot setting. It achieved R/FNMAE/FNRMSE values of 0.9675/0.0340/0.0535 for steel structures, 0.8654/0.1002/0.1471 for masonry structures and 0.8841/0.0847/0.1254 for base-isolated structures (Fig. 5a–c). In all three categories, these values exceeded those of the evaluated baseline models; for example, Timer-XL achieved R values of 0.8175, 0.5294, and 0.7147 for steel, masonry, and base-isolated structures, respectively. The performance difference relative to in-distribution reinforced-concrete cases indicates that the learned structural-response representations extend beyond the specific system types encountered during training. Sensitivity to temporal resolution (Δt): We quantified zero-shot sensitivity to sampling interval by recomputing displacement histories for 4000 structures across Δt values ranging from 0.001 to 0.100 s, without retraining or recalibration. Performance peaked at the training resolution of Δt = 0.020 s, where SeisGPT-Base achieved R = 0.9595, FNMAE = 0.0460, and FNRMSE = 0.0663. The model remained accurate under modest resolution shifts, with R values of 0.9519 at Δt = 0.030 s and 0.9401 at Δt = 0.040 s, but degraded at coarser intervals, falling to R = 0.8805 at Δt = 0.080 s and 0.8478 at Δt = 0.100 s, with FNMAE increasing to 0.1020 and 0.1145, respectively (as shown in Supplementary Table 9 and Fig. 5e). These results delineate the practical operating range of a model trained at Δt = 0.020 s and indicate that, when deployment data are available at substantially different resolutions, training directly at the target Δt is preferable for optimal accuracy.

Pretraining data scale: We further examined the effect of pretraining dataset size on downstream predictive accuracy. Increasing the pretraining data fraction from 50% to 100% improved the Pearson correlation coefficient from 0.8551 to 0.9596, while reducing FNMAE from 0.1569 to 0.0461 and FNRMSE from 0.4318 to 0.0664. Although the trend is not strictly monotonic at very small fractions, the overall pattern indicates that predictive accuracy improves with larger pretraining datasets. These results provide quantitative evidence for a data-scaling effect in pretrained structural-response models and motivate further expansion of both synthetic and real structural datasets (Supplementary Table 10 and Fig. 5f).

Discussion

This study introduces a physics-informed domain-specific foundation model that combines the fidelity of nonlinear simulation with the speed required for rapid structural assessment. Across 3000 previously unseen in-distribution buildings, SeisGPT-Base achieved acceleration FNMAE values of 0.0226, 0.0216, and 0.0250 for frame, frame–shear wall, and shear wall structures, respectively, with corresponding displacement FNMAEs of 0.0437, 0.0416, and 0.0633. On the real-building dataset, SeisGPT-Enhanced further reached R values of 0.9829 and 0.9423 for acceleration and displacement, while end-to-end inference remained 5.96 × 103 to 6.07 × 104 times faster than CPU-based nonlinear FEA. Together with its ability to reconstruct unmeasured responses from sparse sensors, these results indicate that SeisGPT addresses two long-standing bottlenecks in operational structural assessment: computational latency and sensing incompleteness.

The results further show that the model learns transferable representations of structural dynamics. Without case-specific retraining, SeisGPT-Base achieved R values of 0.9675, 0.8654, and 0.8841 on steel, masonry, and base-isolated structures, respectively, exceeding the evaluated baselines in the corresponding zero-shot settings. Model quality also improved with pretraining scale, with R increasing from 0.8551 to 0.9596 as the data fraction rose from 50 to 100%, while the reconstruction experiments showed that mid-height instrumentation yields the most informative inputs for full-building response inference. These numerical results suggest that large-scale physics-consistent pretraining can improve structural-response prediction across heterogeneous building systems.

Several limitations temper these contributions. First, the training dataset, although extensive, is confined to code-compliant and numerically stable structures within the validated domain of floor-defined multistorey buildings. As a result, highly irregular geometries, atypical detailing, and other extreme design-space tails remain under-represented. Nevertheless, the accepted synthetic dataset shows substantial descriptor-level overlap with the real-world building dataset and satisfies explicit code-normalized drift verification, indicating that the main limitation concerns extrapolation breadth rather than the realism of the dominant training distribution. Second, because the reference data are generated by conventional nonlinear solvers, the present framework is validated primarily within response regimes that remain well posed under standard nonlinear time-history analysis (NLTHA). The IDA-based evaluation extends this scope to failure-adjacent converged response and drift-based damage-state quantities up to the onset of instability, but post-instability collapse progression, contact, and debris-governed kinematics remain outside the validated domain. Third, predictive fidelity degrades at coarse temporal resolution, implying minimum telemetry requirements or the need for explicit adaptation to legacy monitoring systems. Finally, the framework does not yet provide calibrated uncertainty estimates or downstream risk metrics required for safety-critical deployment.

These boundaries motivate several directions for future work. Expanding datasets to encompass irregular and near-collapse behaviors, as well as soil–structure interaction, torsional irregularities and nonstructural coupling, would strengthen generality. Enriching cross-system evaluations, particularly for damping and isolation technologies, would clarify the limits of transferability. On the methodological side, calibrated uncertainty quantification and test-time adaptation strategies are needed to improve robustness under sensor outages, temporal drift or post-damage distributions. Prospective studies that connect waveform-level accuracy to actionable engineering outcomes—such as control decisions, occupancy assessments and resilience metrics—will be important for assessing end-to-end value in operational engineering workflows.

Methods

Numerical module: dataset acquisition and processing

We constructed a nonlinear time-history response dataset for model training, validation, and testing using NLTHA. The dataset comprises external excitation records, automatically generated structural models, real-building finite-element models and their corresponding response histories. The following subsections describe the excitation selection, building-model generation, NLTHA simulations and dataset splitting procedure.External excitations selection: To support evaluation across multiple dynamic loading scenarios, the external excitation database includes seismic ground motions, stochastic white noise, subway-induced vibrations, and impulsive loading. A total of 26,000 dynamic records were assembled, with the majority sourced from realistic seismic ground-motion records49 spanning predominantly mid-frequency ranges (approximately 1–10 Hz). Amplitude scaling was consistently applied to maintain realistic intensity distributions aligned with established seismic design codes and structural engineering guidelines. All excitation records were uniformly sampled at a time interval of 0.02 s.Building models: A total of 270,000 detailed macro-scale building models were developed for this study, encompassing four principal structural categories: 150,000 frame structures, 60,000 frame–shear wall structures, 60,000 shear wall structures, and 694 models derived from real-world buildings. These models span a wide spectrum of typologies—including high-rise, mid-rise, and low-rise configurations—and incorporate diverse functional programs, such as residential, office, and commercial types. The integration of diffusion-based generation with physics-informed post-optimization enabled automated production of realistic, code-compliant structural models at scale. Beyond procedural generation and code-based member design, the accepted synthetic structural dataset was further validated through three complementary analyses: visualization of representative structural topologies and three-dimensional finite-element realizations, code-normalized verification of governing inter-storey drift ratios across the accepted dataset, and descriptor-space comparison against the real-world building dataset. The generation of each building geometry and configuration model adhered to a structured process that maintained realistic relationships between parameters while allowing for controlled variability. To cover a broad design space, the range of values and combinations was intentionally extended beyond typical correlations, allowing for a more comprehensive exploration of the input space. This approach enhances the model’s generalizability by training it on a diverse set of building configurations.Key geometric parameters include floor height (ranging from 2.8 to 4.5 m), slab thickness (80–150 mm), and shear wall thickness (200–400 mm). The dimensions of columns and beams were automatically designed per design codes, with calculations for axial compression and flexural strength to ensure compliance. If any generated dimensions were found to be unreasonable, they were regenerated. The reinforcement ratio for all structural elements was automatically calculated according to the applicable design standards. To enable large-scale, diverse, and structurally valid building model generation, we developed a dedicated generative design pipeline built upon a family of customized diffusion models: ArchiFlux, StructFlux, and BeamFlux. The training data consisted of semantically labeled architectural drawings from real-world buildings, in which key components—including partitions, windows, doors, beams, columns, and shear walls—were annotated using distinct color channels to facilitate visual and algorithmic parsing. These annotated plans formed a structured dataset for training our domain-specific generative models. Each model in the suite serves a specialized function: ArchiFlux synthesizes floor plans with spatial segmentation aligned to functional zoning; StructFlux generates the spatial arrangement of primary load-resisting elements such as columns and shear walls; and BeamFlux defines the distribution of secondary framing components. The outputs from these models are then post-processed and evaluated to ensure structural coherence, such as alignment between vertical and horizontal load paths and adherence to minimum clearances and layout feasibility.

SeisGPT core deep learning model

To represent the physical laws governing structural vibrations within the neural architecture, the SeisGPT framework adopts a physics-informed encoder–decoder design. At the core of the decoder is the Spectral Duhamel–Green (SDG) Mixer, which couples structural dynamics with deep representation learning through a mechanics-informed spectral operator. The overall architecture consists of four primary components: (i) a multi-stream feature embedding and gated fusion module, (ii) a topology-aware GNN structural encoder, (iii) the SDG-Mixer backbone for dynamics-driven latent propagation, and (iv) a physical-space decoder. Response feature decoder (RFD): The RFD is implemented as a stack of SDG-Mixer blocks, forming a dynamics-grounded backbone for structural response prediction. Its design is motivated by a fundamental principle of structural dynamics: long-range dependencies in response histories are primarily governed by modal interactions and impulse-response propagation. The SDG-Mixer blocks operationalize this principle through a learnable token-mixing mechanism that constructs a sample-specific spectral coupling space defined by the structural stiffness and mass matrices, within which Green-function–based damped propagation is performed. A stable rational correction is further incorporated to account for nonlinear effects and potential model mismatch.

The decoder begins with an embedding stage that maps heterogeneous input tokens into a unified latent representation, expanding the channel width to CC=256 and the latent resolution to dd=4096. To stabilize optimization, root-mean-square normalization (RMSNorm) is applied before spectral mixing. Overall, the RFD provides a structure-conditioned mechanism for global mixing based on structural-dynamics principles. By integrating valid-floor stiffness–mass coupling, Green-function-inspired damped spectral propagation and a stable rational spectral correction, the SDG-Mixer forms a physically interpretable decoder backbone for rapid response prediction across heterogeneous building configurations.

Fine-tuning with SeisGPT: While SeisGPT was designed for general structural response prediction, it could be fine-tuned to enhance accuracy for a specific building, provided prior structural response data was available. During the fine-tuning process, the model was adapted to better predict responses for the target building, ensuring improved prediction accuracy for that specific structure. This adaptation was achieved by introducing LoRA layers to the linear layers of SeisGPT-Enhanced. During fine-tuning, all original model parameters, except those in the LoRA layers, were frozen. Only the small parameters in the LoRA layers were updated during training. This approach allowed SeisGPT to efficiently adapt to the structural characteristics of the target building and provide more accurate responses, enhancing the model’s performance in real-world applications where building-specific data was available.
