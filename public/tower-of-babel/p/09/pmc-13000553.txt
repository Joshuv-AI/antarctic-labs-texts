Acquired smooth muscle hamartoma mimicking scrotal filariasis: A rare case report
Authors: Syahjihan Amrullah, Wahjoe Djatisoesanto, Dimas Panca Andhika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmooth muscle hamartoma (SMH) is a rare benign lesion characterized by an overgrowth of smooth muscle fibers, typically originating from the arrector pili muscles in the dermis. We report the case of a 39-year-old male sailor with a progressively enlarging scrotal for several years, with rapid enlargement within the past year. The mass was intermittently painful, especially during physical exertion. The patient underwent successful surgical excision with bilateral orchidopexy. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the diagnosis of ASMH. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. This case highlights the importance of considering ASMH in the differential diagnosis of scrotal masses. Conservative testis-sparing surgery can result in excellent outcomes.Keywords: Smooth muscle hamartoma, Scrotal mass, Scrotal filariasis, Orchidopexy

Introduction

Smooth muscle hamartoma (SMH) is a rare benign lesion characterized by overgrowth of smooth muscle fibers, most commonly arising from the arrector pili muscles in the dermis.1 It typically presents congenitally on the lumbosacral region, buttocks, or proximal extremities, but occurrences in the scrotal region are very rare.2 Acquired SMH of the scrotum has been reported in only a few cases worldwide, highlighting the rarity of this condition.3 Clinically, SMH can mimic conditions like Becker's nevus or congenital melanocytic nevus, making diagnosis challenging. Histopathology, showing interlacing smooth muscle bundles in the dermis, is the gold standard for diagnosis.4

This case is notable due to the rare presentation of an acquired smooth muscle hamartoma (ASMH) in the scrotal region, mimicking conditions like testicular tumors and scrotal elephantiasis, especially given his travel history to regions endemic for lymphatic filariasis. Such overlapping features posed diagnostic challenges, requiring imaging and histopathology for accurate diagnosis. Smooth muscle hamartomas are benign dermal proliferations, divided into congenital or acquired types.5 Usually asymptomatic, acquired smooth-muscle hamartomas are indolent and are treated mainly for symptoms or cosmetic reasons. A previous report also noted an unusual presentation of shins with hidrosis, highlighting the varied clinical spectrum of ASMH.5 We present this case to emphasize the diagnostic challenges of scrotal ASMH and add to the limited literature on this rare condition.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old male visited the urology clinic with progressive scrotal swelling for years, with rapid enlargement in the past year. It caused pain during activity or lifting. He is a sailor, with no previous medical treatment or STI history, and reports a bead-like inguinal enlargement. Initial clinical evaluation suspected a testicular tumor, with differential diagnosis including scrotal elephantiasis, especially given the patient's travel history to African regions endemic for lymphatic filariasis (Fig. 1).

(A) Preoperative presentation of scrotal enlargement (B) spermatic cord and testis during surgical procedure.

The patient was referred for a contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT. CT abdomen–pelvis with contrast demonstrated a very large, well-circumscribed scrotal mass with predominantly low-attenuation (fluid-density) contents and no obvious solid enhancing component on the provided images. The lesion caused marked scrotal enlargement with displacement/compression of adjacent scrotal structures, without clear extension into the abdominal cavity (Fig. 2).

CT scan of the scrotal mass.

Laboratory tests showed tumor markers within normal limits: serum β-HCG, AFP, and LDH, ruling out common testicular germ cell tumors. Hematology revealed no significant anemia or cytopenias, but mild leukocytosis and neutrophilia likely reactive to the underlying mass. HbA1c was elevated, indicating possible undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes.

The patient underwent an elective, clean surgical procedure by a urologist under general anesthesia. The preoperative diagnosis was suspected paratesticular tumor, with postoperative considerations of paratesticular tumor versus scrotal lymphedema. The operation involved tumor excision and bilateral orchidopexy (Fig. 3). A sub-inguinal incision was extended to the scrotum, revealing extravasation of serous fluid but no infiltration into the spermatic cord or testis. The right testis and cord were intact and free from tumor. The cystic mass, measuring about 20 × 20 cm, was excised en bloc with overlying skin (Fig. 4). Hemostasis was secured.

Excised scrotal mass.

(A) Immediate postoperative wound presentation (B) Wound appearance at 1-week follow-up.

Orchidopexy was performed bilaterally with layered closure using absorbable sutures (Vicryl 3-0 and Monosyn 3-0). A handshoe drain was inserted, and scrotal support applied postoperatively. Three tissue samples were sent for analysis. Postoperative care included IV fluids, ceftriaxone, tranexamic acid, sodium metamizole, scrotal support, and wound care starting two days after surgery. The patient was planned for continued observation and drain removal as indicated.

Histopathological analysis of the excised scrotal mass identified three specimens: scrotal skin and dartos muscle, tunica vaginalis, and a large mass. Microscopic exam showed no malignancy. Immunohistochemistry at RSDS Hospital confirmed smooth muscle hamartoma. The final diagnosis was a post-excision scrotal smooth muscle hamartoma. The patient was advised to continue routine check-ups and return if new symptoms appear.

Discussion

Acquired Smooth muscle hamartoma (SMH) is a rare benign growth of dermal smooth muscle fibers, usually congenital on the trunk or extremities. ASMH in the scrotal area is extremely rare, with fewer than 20 cases in the literature.5 Our patient had a progressive scrotal mass, initially suggesting a testicular tumor or scrotal elephantiasis, especially given his work as a sailor in Africa, where lymphatic filariasis is endemic. Similar cases have shown filarial granulomas mimicking testicular tumors, requiring careful histopathology to prevent overtreatment.6,7

What makes this case notable is not only the rarity of scrotal ASMH but also the diagnostic complexity compounded by endemic exposure risk, mimicking filariasis clinically. Furthermore, the mass had grown rapidly within one year after a period of slow progression, an atypical course for most benign scrotal lesions. Tumor marker evaluations, imaging, and ultimately excisional biopsy with immunohistochemistry were essential to establish the benign nature of the lesion, thereby averting unnecessary radical orchiectomy. Management consisted of complete surgical excision with preservation of the testes and bilateral orchidopexy, resulting in favorable postoperative outcomes.

Prognosis for ASMH is excellent, with low recurrence rates following complete resection. Our case highlights the necessity of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis for scrotal masses, especially in endemic areas, and emphasizes the role of histopathology and IHC in confirming rare benign entities such as ASMH.

Conclusion

Acquired smooth muscle hamartoma (ASMH) of the scrotum is a rare benign condition that can mimic malignant or infectious scrotal issues, especially in those with travel to endemic areas. This case highlights the importance of thorough evaluation using imaging, tumor markers, and histopathology to avoid misdiagnosis. Our patient underwent successful surgical excision without harming the testes, and bilateral orchidopexy was done to preserve testicular function. The positive outcome shows that conservative, testis-sparing surgery is feasible and suitable for benign scrotal masses. Greater awareness of ASMH can improve diagnosis and surgical planning.
