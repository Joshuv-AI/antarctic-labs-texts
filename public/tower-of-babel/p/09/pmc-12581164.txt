Extracellular iron and the lung microbiome in smoking and COPD

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost microorganisms that make up the lung microbiome use several iron acquisition strategies for survival. Iron levels and iron-associated proteins are higher in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of smokers with and smokers without COPD associating with disease severity. In this study we assessed whether or not smokers with and smokers without COPD who have increased airway iron display a specifically altered airway microbiome.Methods16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing of BALF from individuals (n=181) enrolled in the bronchoscopy substudy of the SubPopulations and InteRmediate Outcome Measures In COPD Study (SPIROMICS) was paired with measured levels of BALF iron, the iron storage protein ferritin (ex-ferritin) and the host-iron siderophore lipocalin-2 (LCN-2) in matched participants.ResultsOverall, iron, ex-ferritin and LCN-2 levels were not associated with ecological diversity metrics in the BALF of participants enrolled in this cohort. However, in a differential analysis, specific taxa were found to be enriched in never-smokers with low BALF ex-ferritin levels and also in smokers with high BALF iron levels. Of participants with COPD, an unclassified taxon, Bacteroidetes-OTU0063, was enriched in those with a high BALF iron level and those with a low BALF LCN-2 level. Furthermore, COPD participants with a high number of historical exacerbations and a high BALF LCN-2 level showed enrichment for Streptococcus and Leptotrichia. Leptotrichia was also enriched in participants with COPD and a high number of historical exacerbations with elevated BALF iron and BALF ex-ferritin levels. In addition, symptomatic individuals with COPD who had a high BALF ex-ferritin level had enrichment with Moraxella.ConclusionThe above data suggest that abundance of iron and iron-binding proteins may be linked to the presence of both oral commensals and potentially pathogenic microorganisms in the lower airways of individuals with COPD.

Introduction

COPD is a chronic progressive inflammatory disease of the lung and a leading cause of death worldwide [1]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have highlighted a role for disturbed iron metabolism in COPD susceptibility [2]. Higher levels of iron and iron proteins are found in the lung tissue, airspace macrophages [3, 4] and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of smokers and individuals with COPD [4, 5]. Importantly, prior studies have shown an association between these lung iron parameters and poor clinical outcomes in COPD [5], but the mechanisms by which increased airway iron and iron-associated proteins mediate these outcomes remain to be determined.

Iron is essential for almost all life and is the most widely used metal in biological processes. This dependence on iron for most organisms makes it a valuable commodity in the battle between host and pathogen. An important innate immune mechanism of the host is to sequester iron, thus blocking access to this metal by microorganisms, a strategy known as nutritional immunity [6]. Ferritin, an important component of nutritional immunity, is an iron storage protein that serves to sequester intracellular free iron in a safe, soluble and non-toxic form, making it less available to invading pathogens [7]. Intracellular ferritin can also be secreted into the extracellular space (ex-ferritin) [8], where it represents a potential source of iron [9, 10] and may function as a short-range cell-to-cell carrier of ferric iron [11, 12]. In response, many bacteria have evolved strategies to capture iron; one such strategy is the use of siderophores – iron chelators that can be located on the bacterial cell surface or secreted to capture iron. Siderophores bind iron with such high affinity that they can compete for iron with lactoferrin, transferrin and ferritin, removing iron from these host proteins [13, 14]. Such bacterial iron capture strategies may, in turn, be thwarted by the host through the retention of iron intracellularly or through the secretion of proteins such as lipocalin-2 (LCN-2) [15]. In addition to its role in blocking bacteria siderophore-mediated iron acquisition, LCN-2 is a beta-defensin peptide that binds to and transports lipophilic molecules [16] and is elevated in several disease states [17], including COPD; for example, studies have found increased levels of LCN-2 in the blood, sputum and BALF of people with COPD [18–20].

COPD progression is marked by exacerbation events, episodes of increased respiratory symptoms which accelerate lung function decline and increase mortality [21]. These exacerbations are commonly attributed to respiratory infection in the lower airways and often require antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory interventions. Importantly, some of the most common bacterial pathogens found in the lower airways of individuals with COPD, including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis [22, 23], require iron for survival and growth [24–26]. In addition, several observational studies have already linked alterations in airway microbial communities with disease progression, severity and long-term morbidity in COPD [27–29].

Restricting access of microbes to iron limits their growth, but this is directly countered by the dysregulated iron metabolism that leads to increased iron in the lower airways in COPD. In this study, we sought to examine whether or not the levels of lower-airway iron or iron-associated proteins found in the extracellular space are associated with specific bacterial communities in the COPD lung. We measured the levels of iron, extracellular ferritin (ex-ferritin) and LCN-2 in the BALF of individuals with COPD enrolled in the SubPopulations and InteRmediate Outcome Measurements In COPD Study (SPIROMICS) and integrated these data along with paired 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing data previously reported from this cohort [29].

Methods

Cohort participants

SPIROMICS (ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT01969344) is an ongoing longitudinal multicentre observational study that recruited 2981 participants, aged 40–80 years, across four strata: never-smokers (≤1 pack-year of tobacco-smoking history), ever-smokers (current or former smokers with ≥20 pack-years) without airflow obstruction and participants with mild/moderate and severe COPD [30]. SPIROMICS includes a diverse range of clinical sites across the United States, ensuring broad geographic representation. Sex was included as a biological variable, with both female and male subjects participating. Each participating SPIROMICS site is approved by its respective institutional review board and all subjects provided written informed consent [5, 30]. A subgroup of participants (n=215) with post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) >30% of the predicted value and without an exacerbation in the preceding six weeks were further enrolled in a bronchoscopy substudy and underwent bronchoscopy [5, 30, 31] (table 1). Exacerbation events were analysed relative both to the baseline visit and to the bronchoscopy visit (0–12 months before visit).

Extracellular iron and iron-binding protein measurements

BALF samples were processed, aliquoted and stored at −80°C [31]. Historical measurements of iron and ferritin used in this study have been described previously [5]. Briefly, iron was measured via graphite furnace atomic absorption spectroscopy (GFAAS) and ferritin via ELISA using the Abcam Human Ferritin ELISA Kit (cat. #ab200018), which detects both ferritin heavy chain and ferritin light chain. BALF LCN-2 levels were measured by ELISA using the Human Lipocalin-2/NGAL DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, cat. #DY1757). Associations between the levels of iron, ex-ferritin and LCN-2 in individual BALF samples were tested using nonparametric Spearman correlation analysis.

16S RNA gene sequencing analysis

Separately stored, previously unthawed aliquots of whole BALF were processed for bacterial microbiota analysis [29]. Briefly, total DNA was extracted from BALF cell pellets (5 mL of whole BALF centrifuged at 13 000 rpm), as well as bronchoscope suction channel flushes (background control). Extracted DNA was used for 16S rRNA gene sequencing performed by the University of Michigan's Microbiome Core on an Illumina MiSeq targeting the V4 region, as previously described [29]. Also included for sequencing were negative extraction controls (sterile PBS) and other potential sources of background signal, including elution buffers and empty plate wells [29]. A touchdown PCR amplification strategy [32] including a mock reference community (ZymoBIOMICS, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) was utilised, and normalised pooled libraries generated for sequencing using the 500-cycle MiSeq V2 Reagent kit. All generated sequence data (BALF, background/negative extraction controls) were processed together and potential contaminant sequences were identified and removed using decontam [33]. The 16S rRNA gene sequence reads were processed using mothur (version 1.40.5) [34], aligned to the SILVA reference alignment (release 102) [35] and classified using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) 16S rRNA reference training set (version 16) [36]. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were defined using a threshold of 97% similarity [29].

Data analysis and availability

Cohort characteristics were described using mean (sd), median and n (%) for normal continuous, non-normal continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The sequence read count table, taxonomy table and phylogenetic tree file (picante) [37] were imported into R for analysis using phyloseq (version 1.26.0) [38]. The OTUs were filtered by average relative abundance >0.1% (final total of 125 OTUs analysed). For the enrichment analysis, participants were grouped according to “high” or “low” levels of BALF LCN-2, BALF total iron and BALF ferritin, or according to COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score, using the median values (table 1) as the cut-off between low and high. For the 16S rRNA gene sequencing data, we used the vegan package in R to construct Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances [39, 40] for β-diversity and Shannon index for α-diversity. Taxon-level differential abundance was determined by DESeq2 [41], which utilises a negative binomial generalised linear model with Wald's test [41] with a false discovery rate of <0.2 [42]. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.0.3. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing data are available in the Sequence Read Archive of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under project number PRJNA673153. Codes used for the analyses presented in the current paper are available online (https://github.com/lungmicro-RCSI/Spiromics).

Results

Iron, ex-ferritin and LCN-2 levels are not associated with diversity metrics but are associated with specific taxa in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of all study subjects

We first evaluated the lower-airway microbiota and their association with BALF iron, ex-ferritin and LCN-2 levels (figure 1a) in all subjects (nonsmokers, smokers and those with COPD) by using available paired data from those enrolled in the bronchoscopy substudy of SPIROMICS. Overall, BALF iron and ex-ferritin displayed some correlation (r=0.35; p<0.0001) and BALF ex-ferritin and BALF LCN-2 showed weak correlation (r=0.18; p=0.01); no correlation was observed for BALF iron and LCN-2 (r=0.05; p=0.48) (figure 1b, supplementary figure 1a–c). Using median cut-off points of 157 ng·mL−1 for BALF elemental iron, 26.71 ng·mL−1 for BALF ferritin and 9.57 ng·mL−1 for BALF LCN-2, we divided all participants into either “high” (higher than the median) or “low” (lower than the median) iron groups (supplementary figure 1d). We found that there were no significant differences in α- or β-diversity metrics in individuals with iron, ex-ferritin or LCN-2 levels that were higher or lower than the median values (figure 1c–h). Next, we looked to identify taxonomic differences between all participants with high or low BALF iron, ex-ferritin and LCN-2 levels. In individuals with high or low iron ex-ferritin levels, no enriched taxa were present (figure 1i–j). However, in those with high BALF LCN-2, Streptococcus-OTU0019 was significantly enriched. By contrast, in those with low BALF LCN-2, Pseudomonas-OTU0029 was enriched (figure 1k).

We next sought to evaluate if never-smokers (n=24) with high BALF iron, ex-ferritin or LCN-2 levels displayed taxonomic differences when compared with those with lower BALF iron indices. In a differential analysis, no taxonomic differences were observed in never-smokers based on total iron levels, or LCN-2 levels; however, never-smokers with low BALF ex-ferritin levels showed an enrichment for Staphylococcus-OTU0012, Pseudomonas-OTU0025, Pseudomonas-OTU0029 and Cupriavidus-OTU0080, as well as another 12 genera (figure 2a).

Iron, ex-ferritin and LCN-2 levels are associated with specific taxa in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of smokers enrolled in SPIROMICS

Next, we assessed if ever-smokers (n=84), defined as current (n=38) or former (n=46) smokers with ≥20 pack-years smoking history without airflow obstruction, with high or low BALF iron, ex-ferritin or LCN-2 levels displayed taxonomic differences when compared with those with lower BALF iron indices. In ever-smokers without obstructive disease, Leptotrichia-OTU0127 was enriched in those with low total iron, while Cloacibacterium-OTU0120, Lactobacillus-OTU0196 and Alcanivorax-OTU0190 were enriched in those with high total iron (figure 2b). No enrichment was observed in ever-smokers with high or low BALF ex-ferritin or BALF LCN-2 levels (figure 2b).

Symptom severity and airway iron and iron proteins are associated with specific genera of bacteria in the airways of individuals with COPD

We have previously shown that BALF iron and ferritin levels are higher in patients with COPD and in smokers without COPD than in nonsmoker control participants [5]. Similarly, here we also show that BALF LCN-2 levels are significantly higher in COPD participants than in either never-smokers (p=0.009) or ever-smokers (p=0.02) (supplementary figure 2a, table 1), with no significant difference between COPD participants who were current or former smokers (supplementary figure 2b). Higher log2 BALF LCN-2 was also associated with reduced FEV1 (b: −0.019; p<0.001; adjusted value for age, smoking status and sex) (supplementary figure 2c). Having seen these relationships between iron indices and a diagnosis of COPD, we next examined the relationship between the airway microbiome and a higher iron, ferritin or LCN-2 level in the BALF of participants with COPD (n=80). An unclassified taxon Bacteroidetes-OTU0063 was enriched in those with COPD and a high BALF iron level (figure 2c). This taxon was also enriched in individuals with COPD and a low BALF LCN-2 level. In participants with COPD, enrichment of Moraxella-OTU0037 was seen in those with a high BALF ex-ferritin level and Cupriavidus-OTU0080 was enriched in those with a low BALF ex-ferritin level (figure 2c).

Next, the COPD subgroup was stratified by symptom burden as assessed by CAT score, using a score of 10 as the cut-off. Higher BALF LCN-2 levels were significantly associated with higher symptom burden, as assessed by CAT questionnaires, in a manner similar to that for BALF iron and BALF ex-ferritin levels [5], whereby participants with a CAT score of ≥20 had significantly higher BALF LCN-2 levels than those with a CAT score of 0–9 (supplementary figure 2d). In assessing microbiome changes in individuals with a high and those with a low CAT score, there were no significant differences in α- or β-diversity by CAT score between the two symptom groups (supplementary figure 3a–i). However, COPD participants with a high CAT score and high BALF total iron had enrichment of Tropheryma-OTU0021, Selenomonas-OTU0067 and Bacteroidetes-OTU0063 (figure 3a). Those with a high CAT score and high BALF ex-ferritin had enrichment of Moraxella-OTU 0037 (figure 3b). Individuals with higher CAT scores and high LCN-2 levels were enriched with Tropheryma-OTU0021 and Bacillales-unclassified_OTU0017 (figure 3c). Individuals with a high CAT score and low LCN-2 level were enriched in Bacteroidetes-unclassified_OTU0063 (figure 3c). By contrast, those with a low CAT score and high LCN-2 level were enriched with Stenotrophomonas-OTU0180 and Thermus-OTU0231 (figure 3f). Those with low CAT scores showed no significant bacterial enrichment associated with BALF ex-ferritin or total iron concentrations (figure 3d,e).

Exacerbations and airway iron and iron proteins are associated with specific genera of bacteria in the airways of individuals with COPD

Finally, to assess if these findings had further relevance to clinical outcomes in COPD, we looked at the relationship between the lower-airway microbiota, iron indices and disease exacerbations. We have previously shown that BALF ex-ferritin and iron levels are elevated in participants who have a high number of historical COPD exacerbations [5]. Importantly here, BALF LCN-2 levels are also associated with one or more historical exacerbations (any exacerbation event that occurred in the 12 months preceding the bronchoscopy), compared with individuals with no exacerbations (supplementary figure 2e; p=0.001). Comparing those who had experienced one or more historical exacerbations with those who had not, there were no significant differences in α- or β-diversity between high and low BALF iron, ex-ferritin or LCN-2 levels (supplementary figure 3j–r). In a differential analysis, individuals with COPD with one or more historical exacerbations with high BALF ex-ferritin and high total iron or high LCN-2 levels showed enrichment of Leptotrichia-OTU0050 (figure 4a–c). Individuals with one or more historical exacerbations and a high level of total iron were also enriched for Prevotella_unclassified-OTU0030 (figure 4a) and those with one or more historical exacerbations and a high LCN-2 level showed enrichment for Prevotella-OTU0045 and Streptococcus-OTU0019 (figure 4a,c). However, those who experienced one or more historical exacerbations and had a low BALF LCN-2 level showed enrichment of several taxa, including Bacillus-OTU0973, Alistipes-OTU0229, OTU0243, Porphyromonas-OTU0390 and Jeotgalicoccus-OTU0098 (figure 4c). In participants who had no historical exacerbations prior to enrolment, those with low LCN-2 levels and those with high total iron were significantly enriched with Bacteroidetes-unclassified_OTU0063 (figure 4d,f). Those with a high BALF ex-ferritin level who had no historical exacerbations were enriched in Moraxella-OTU0037 and Tropheryma-OTU0021 (figure 4e). Finally, those with no historical exacerbations and a low BALF ex-ferritin level showed enrichment of Cupriavidus-OTU0080 species (figure 4e).

Discussion

In this analysis, we attempt to decipher if there is an association between iron levels in the extracellular space of the lung and a specific microbial signature. Although no direct associations were observed between iron levels and α- or β-diversity metrics across all 181 participants, a sub-analysis stratified by disease status revealed several notable findings. Using clinical outcomes such as physiological evidence of COPD, symptom burden and exacerbation history, we show that individuals with COPD with a history of exacerbation and high BALF LCN-2 levels had greater enrichment of several taxa including Leptotrichia, Streptococcus and Prevotella, common oral commensals. Importantly, this study is the first study to measure BALF LCN-2 in a large cohort of individuals with mild–moderate COPD and, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to link iron status in the lower airways with a specific microbial signature. SPIROMICS is enriched for spirometrically mild-to-moderate disease, a deliberate design feature to discover factors that lead to disease progression. As a result of this unique design, our studies suggest that components of the lung microbiome and abundance of iron in combination may relate to outcome in milder COPD, highlighting their potential collaborative roles in disease pathogenesis.

In our study, individuals with COPD and a history of exacerbations who had higher levels of LCN-2, iron and ferritin in the lower airways also had greater enrichment of Leptotrichia, facultative anaerobes that can be found in the oral cavity. In a previous SPIROMICS substudy, BALF neutrophil percentage was positively associated with Leptotrichia (in particular OTU50), which was positively associated with exacerbations >1 year post bronchoscopy [28]. The role of the level of iron or LCN-2 on Leptotrichia growth, biology or potential to establish infection and disease remains mostly unknown. However, the appearance of this oral commensal in the lower airways is consistent with findings from others who have demonstrated airway enrichment with common oral commensals in individuals with mild COPD and in smokers with preserved lung function [27–29]. Here, other oral commensals such as Prevotella and Streptococcus were also enriched in individuals with COPD and high BALF LCN-2 levels, particularly in those with historical exacerbations. Prevotella and Veillonella were also abundant in the airways of never-smokers with lower BALF ex-ferritin levels. However, whether enrichment of these bacteria is the cause of or the result of increased airway iron proteins and previous exacerbations remains unknown.

In all participants, regardless of disease status, streptococcal enrichment is associated with high levels of LCN-2. This genus was also significantly enriched in individuals with COPD with one or more historical exacerbations who had high BALF LCN-2 levels. Streptococci are Gram-positive, facultative anaerobes and are generally commensals in the upper respiratory tract. Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae) is one of the most abundant species isolated from the COPD lung, associated with 10–15% of exacerbation events [43]. Streptococcus-OTU0019, previously identified in this 16S rRNA dataset as S. salivarius via culture isolation of primary isolates, is negatively associated with lung function in mild-to-moderate COPD [29]. S. salivarius is considered an opportunistic pathogen and its presence in the lower airways has also been associated with other chronic lung diseases [44]. While little is known regarding the effects of S. salivarius on host-iron metabolism, S. pneumoniae induces LCN-2 production in the nasal mucosa, alveolar macrophages and airway secretions of mice and uses siderophore-independent methods to acquire iron from the host (e.g. lactoferrin) [45]. Conversely, Pseudomonas was enriched in all participants with low BALF LCN-2 levels. Pseudomonas is a genus of Gram-negative bacteria, whose species includes the respiratory pathogen P. aeruginosa, which in the sputum is linked to severe exacerbations in COPD [46]. Whether or not these associations with a high or low LCN-2 level render these individuals more susceptible to COPD progression remains an open question. Furthermore, whether these associations with LCN-2 are directly linked to the role of LCN-2 as an iron siderophore, as a general bacteriostatic response to bacterial infection or as a transporter of lipophilic molecules cannot be ruled out and remain to be determined. This is highlighted by the weak association between iron levels and LCN-2 levels in the BALF. How iron and LCN-2 impact the composition of the airway microbiome, or, alternatively, how disturbances in the airway microbiome may affect LCN-2 expression and airway iron levels, and how these interactions may thus influence increased occurrence of lower respiratory infections leading to exacerbation also require further investigation.

In this study, symptomatic COPD participants with at least one historical exacerbation and a high ferritin level in the airways displayed enrichment for Moraxella. This genus of Gram-positive bacteria includes Moraxella catarrhalis (M. catarrhalis), a commensal of the upper respiratory tract that can gain access to the lower airways in COPD. M. catarrhalis uses lactoferrin and transferrin as sources of iron in the absence of siderophores [47], enabling it to acquire iron for growth and proliferation. There is also evidence to suggest that iron deficiency may promote M. catarrhalis invasiveness [48]. However, whether the Moraxella OTU enriched in this study is M. catarrhalis is yet to be confirmed.

Other interesting genera of bacteria were also identified in this study, including some with intriguing iron links, such as Stenotrophomonas, a genus of Gram-negative environmental bacteria that includes the species Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, which is resistant to heavy metals, including iron [49], and Tropheryma, a genus of Gram-positive bacteria, of which the species Tropheryma whipplei may thrive under conditions of iron loading [50]. While the above genera of bacteria are not classically considered commensal bacteria, Stenotrophomonas is found to be enriched in the trachea and Tropheryma has been detected in the BALF of healthy individuals [51, 52]. The above findings overall propose a potential link between iron abundance in the lower airways of individuals with COPD and shifts in lung microbiota composition towards bacteria that 1) can adapt to higher concentrations of iron, 2) are likely to become more invasive during iron scarcity or abundance or 3) can acquire iron from siderophore-independent iron-uptake mechanisms.

While this study provides novel insights on the role of iron and iron-related proteins in the lung microbiome, particularly in the context of COPD, using 16S rRNA sequencing does not provide any information on the function of these organisms and is limited to bacteria. More in-depth analyses of the microbiome can be performed with new technologies such as shotgun whole metagenome and RNA metatranscriptomic sequencing [53]. Another limitation to our study is that BALF LCN-2, iron, and ferritin levels were measured at a single time point, which does not inform us about the stability or changes of these measurements over time in each individual, or if they fluctuate at the time of an exacerbation or infection. As the SPIROMICS cohort is predominantly composed of those with mild COPD, the occurrence of exacerbations is relatively low and may not be representative of the broader COPD population [54]. How these findings relate to the microbiome of induced sputum from these individuals has yet to be examined [55].

Many studies have documented the effects of iron deficiency or iron overload on the gut microbiome [56], and some have assessed the role of iron in respiratory infections [57, 58]. To the best of our knowledge, this report is the first to link iron indices in the lower airways with the lower-airway microbiome in COPD. This offers a compelling perspective on the influence of nutrient availability and microbial communities within the COPD lung. Furthermore, this sets the stage for additional mechanistic studies that could greatly improve our understanding of nutritional immunity in the lower airways as well as the microbiome-mediated mechanisms driving early COPD pathogenesis, and thus potentially identify strategies to halt disease progression.
