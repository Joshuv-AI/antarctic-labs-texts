Percutaneous coronary intervention or conservative therapy for chronic total coronary occlusions (CTOs)? Evidence from the ISCHEMIA trial
Authors: Cristina Madaudo, Giuseppe Vadalà, Giuseppe Astuti, Alfredo Ruggero Galassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of chronic total coronary occlusions (CTOs) remains one of the most debated areas in interventional cardiology because of its technical complexity and the relatively high risk of procedural complications. Although the effectiveness of percutaneous coronary intervention for CTOs (CTO–PCI) in improving symptoms has been demonstrated, its impact on hard clinical outcomes remains controversial. The International Study of Comparative Health Effectiveness with Medical and Invasive Approaches (ISCHEMIA Trial) and the ISCHEMIA CTO sub-study provide important insights: in patients with stable coronary artery disease and moderate-to-severe ischaemia, an initial invasive strategy does not reduce cardiovascular mortality or myocardial infarction compared with optimized medical therapy. However, the trial was not designed to specifically assess the effectiveness of CTO–PCI, and only a minority of enrolled patients had a CTO, which was revascularized in only few cases. In addition, the ISCHEMIA trial excluded high-risk patients who are frequently encountered in everyday clinical practice, such as those with left main coronary artery disease, severe symptoms, or recent acute coronary syndromes. This article critically analyses the results of the ISCHEMIA Trial in the context of CTOs, comparing them with evidence from dedicated CTO–PCI studies, and proposes an integrated decision-making model based on symptom control, objective demonstration of inducible ischaemia and myocardial viability, multimodal imaging, and discussion within the Heart Team.Keywords: Chronic total occlusion, Coronary revascularization, ISCHEMIA Trial, Optimized medical therapy

Introduction

The International Study of Comparative Health Effectiveness with Medical and Invasive Approaches (ISCHEMIA Trial) is one of the most influential studies addressing the management of patients with chronic coronary syndrome. The trial demonstrated that early coronary revascularization in 5179 patients with documented atherosclerotic coronary artery disease on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and moderate-to-severe inducible ischaemia does not reduce the primary end-point of cardiovascular death or the incidence of myocardial infarction when compared with an initially conservative strategy based on optimized medical therapy (OMT).1

Nevertheless, the trial confirmed a clear benefit of revascularization in terms of reduction of angina-related symptoms in symptomatic patients whose symptoms were not adequately controlled by OMT. It should also be emphasized that the trial population was not fully representative of patients commonly encountered in everyday clinical practice, as individuals with left main coronary artery disease and those with more severe angina were excluded from enrollment. In addition, crossover from the initially conservative arm to the invasive arm occurred in approximately 15% of patients in the OMT group and was not counted as an adverse event. This was consistent with the study protocol, which allowed patients assigned to an initial conservative strategy to undergo coronary angiography and revascularization within 30 days if clinically indicated, without this being considered an end-point event.1

Within this framework, the management of patients with chronic total occlusions (CTOs) remains particularly challenging, especially when deciding whether to pursue coronary revascularization, for several reasons: (i) percutaneous coronary intervention for CTOs (CTO–PCI) is technically more complex than non-CTO–PCI; (ii) CTO–PCI is associated with higher complication rates compared with non-CTO procedures; and (iii) while the effectiveness of CTO–PCI in reducing symptoms is well established, its impact on hard clinical outcomes remains controversial.1,2

Although patients with CTOs often resemble those enrolled in the ISCHEMIA trial, it must be acknowledged that ISCHEMIA was not designed as a CTO-specific study. Of the 5179 patients enrolled, only 1470 (28.3%) had a CTO.3 Therefore, a direct extrapolation of the trial results to the CTO population is not straightforward and requires specific considerations. Notably, the ISCHEMIA CTO sub-study showed that patients with CTOs had a greater extent of inducible ischaemia, were more symptomatic, and experienced worse outcomes at follow-up, irrespective of treatment allocation.3

From this perspective, the ISCHEMIA trial confirmed that coronary revascularization is more effective than pharmacological therapy in reducing symptom burden and improving functional capacity, as objectively assessed using the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ), EQ-5D, and the Duke Activity Status Index. The invasive strategy was associated with a modest but statistically significant improvement in overall quality of life (QoL), particularly among patients who were more symptomatic at enrollment [mean difference +1.4 points (95% CI 0.2–2.5, P≈0.02) in the SAQ Summary Score]. This benefit was more pronounced in patients with baseline angina (SAQ Angina Frequency <80), in whom the invasive strategy resulted in a mean improvement of +3.7 points (95% CI 1.6–5.8, P < 0.01) compared with conservative management. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the two strategies in patients with rare or no angina at baseline (SAQ Angina Frequency >80). These findings are consistent with the existing literature supporting CTO–PCI as an effective tool for angina relief.4-6

Moreover, in the context of an ageing population, it will become increasingly important—across cardiovascular medicine—to consider QoL end-points as being as relevant as hard outcomes. Future clinical decision-making should increasingly acknowledge that patients aim not only to live longer, but also to live with a better QoL.4

A further important contribution to understanding the role of revascularization in CTOs comes from a pre-specified sub-analysis of the ISCHEMIA trial based on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA).3 In this sub-study, more than 3100 patients evaluated by the core laboratory were classified according to the presence or absence of a CTO, which was identified in nearly half of the analyzed population.3

The results indicate that, even in the presence of a CTO, an initial invasive strategy does not significantly reduce the composite end-point of cardiovascular death or myocardial infarction compared with a conservative strategy. Similarly, when compared with conservative management, the invasive strategy in patients with CTO is associated with a significant improvement in angina, dyspnoea, and QoL, with a greater perceived clinical benefit than that observed in patients without CTO. However, several aspects deserve emphasis: (i) patients with CTO exhibit a greater extent of inducible ischaemia compared with those without CTO; and (ii) successful CTO revascularization is associated with a reduction in cardiovascular death and myocardial infarction, driven predominantly by a decrease in spontaneous myocardial infarctions.3

Effective CTO percutaneous coronary intervention (CTO–PCI) is associated with a significant reduction in ischaemic burden, which in turn correlates with improved long-term clinical outcomes. Although these findings do not derive from a randomized comparison between successful and unsuccessful CTO treatment and therefore must be interpreted with caution, they suggest that the benefit of revascularization in CTOs may emerge primarily when the procedure is successful and when the occluded territory contributes substantially to the patient’s ischaemic and symptomatic burden. Notably, these data are fully consistent with previously published evidence.7

Overall, the ISCHEMIA CTO sub-study reinforces a key message: CTO revascularization should not be proposed systematically but is best indicated in symptomatic patients with documented ischaemia and a plausible potential for functional recovery. From this perspective, CTO–PCI remains a selective option, capable of providing tangible clinical benefit when embedded within a careful, individualized decision-making process and when performed in dedicated, high-expertise centres.8

Management of patients with CTO in clinical practice after the ISCHEMIA trial

The most recent European consensus document issued by the European Association of Percutaneous Cardiovascular Interventions (EAPCI) and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI), in collaboration with the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), confirms that the indication for CTO revascularization should be based on a particularly thorough clinical and instrumental assessment, integrating symptomatic status, functional evaluation, and advanced imaging findings.8 Persistent symptoms such as angina—or more frequently dyspnoea—despite OMT remain the main drivers of clinical decision-making.

Beyond symptom assessment, objective demonstration of inducible ischaemia in the myocardial territory supplied by the occluded vessel is crucial. This can be achieved using the various functional imaging modalities currently available, including stress echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), myocardial perfusion scintigraphy (SPECT), and positron emission tomography (PET) with Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (^18F-FDG). When the CTO-related myocardial territory shows regional wall motion abnormalities, assessment of myocardial viability becomes particularly important. Contrast-enhanced CMR is considered the gold standard for both ischaemia quantification and the evaluation of scar burden and myocardial viability. A stress-induced perfusion defect involving at least two myocardial segments, or late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) affecting less than 25–50% of the myocardial wall thickness, are strongly suggestive of potential functional recovery after revascularization and of a favourable long-term prognostic impact.8

CTO revascularization in patients with left ventricular dysfunction

CTO revascularization in patients with impaired left ventricular (LV) function remains a controversial topic, with limited dedicated evidence. Some prospective studies have suggested a potential clinical benefit in this high-risk population. In a multicentre cohort of 132 patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 40% followed for a median of more than 23 months, procedural success was achieved in 82.5% of cases, with an acceptable safety profile and, most importantly, a significant reduction in hospitalizations for heart failure (HFH).9 Although no substantial differences emerged in terms of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, or non-fatal myocardial infarction, the lower incidence of HFH supports the hypothesis that restoration of coronary flow in patients with LV dysfunction may stabilize the clinical course.

Similarly, in a multicentre study enrolling 839 patients undergoing elective CTO–PCI and stratified according to LV function, those with LVEF ≤35% accounted for approximately 8.6% of the population and exhibited a high angiographic success rate (91.7%), comparable to that observed in patients with preserved LV function.10 At a mean follow-up of approximately 16 months, event-free survival from major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) was similar across groups. This finding suggests a favourable effect of revascularization in patients with severe LV dysfunction, who generally have a worse prognosis than those with normal or mildly reduced LVEF. Moreover, in patients with LVEF ≤35%, successful revascularization was associated with a significant recovery of LV function, with an increase in LVEF from 29.1% ± 3.4% to 41.6% ± 7.9% (P < 0.001).10

Effective CTO revascularization appears to confer a greater benefit, in terms of cardiovascular death and myocardial infarction, in patients who experience a marked reduction in inducible ischaemia within the CTO-related territory—particularly those with LV dysfunction—compared with patients who achieve only modest ischaemic reduction and have preserved ventricular function.11

The ISCHEMIA-HF sub-study further supports this concept. Patients with a history of heart failure or LV dysfunction had a significantly worse prognosis than those with preserved LV function, and only in this subgroup did an invasive strategy demonstrate a clear clinical benefit. In contrast, among patients without LV dysfunction, revascularization did not provide advantages over medical therapy alone. Notably, the benefit of the invasive approach increased progressively as LV function worsened.12

Overall, although not definitive, these data suggest that in patients with left ventricular dysfunction—particularly in the presence of viable myocardium—CTO revascularization may reduce heart failure burden and promote recovery of contractile function. This reinforces the importance of careful patient selection through advanced imaging and multidisciplinary evaluation.10

CTO revascularization as an integral component of a complete percutaneous revascularization strategy

A substantial body of evidence supports complete revascularization over incomplete revascularization in both chronic and acute coronary syndromes. However, none of these studies specifically addresses the optimal approach to CTOs. Conversely, current guidelines on chronic coronary syndromes (CCS)13 acknowledge that the presence of a CTO represents one of the main risk factors for incomplete revascularization, largely because CTO treatment requires a multidisciplinary approach and is not performed in all centres.

In this regard, the SYNTAX Extended Survival Study has provided a decisive clarification: long-term prognosis is driven less by the revascularization modality [PCI vs. coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)] than by the degree of revascularization completeness.14 At 10-year follow-up, patients undergoing PCI with complete revascularization exhibited mortality rates comparable to those of patients treated with CABG. In contrast, incomplete percutaneous revascularization was associated with a significantly higher mortality, particularly when the degree of incompleteness was substantial. The study also confirmed that surgical revascularization is more frequently complete than percutaneous revascularization.

Nevertheless, in real-world practice, a non-negligible proportion of patients with multivessel disease present with comorbidities or specific anatomical conditions (e.g. porcelain aorta, lack of suitable venous or arterial conduits, prior stroke) that confer an unacceptably high surgical risk. In such cases, a percutaneous strategy aiming at complete revascularization becomes highly desirable.

Today, the use of intravascular imaging techniques—such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT)—advanced calcium plaque modification devices (ACPMD), and mechanical circulatory support systems has become essential to improve procedural safety and effectiveness. Supporting this selective and personalized approach, important evidence comes from the contemporary European Registry of Chronic Total Occlusion (European Registry of Chronic Total Occlusion, ERCTO), which analyzed 15 329 CTO revascularization procedures.15 Severe calcification, present in approximately 20% of lesions, when treated using ACPMD, was associated with significantly higher procedural success and a significantly lower rate of MACCE compared with treatment of severely calcified lesions without ACPMD (MACCE 2.2% with ACPMD vs. 4.1% without ACPMD).15

Limitations of the available evidence

Despite the growing interest in CTO revascularization, the currently available evidence still has important limitations. Dedicated randomized trials are few and often characterized by heterogeneous study populations; patient enrollment is frequently difficult and slow; and there is a high rate of crossover between treatment groups, which reduces statistical power and complicates interpretation of results. The most symptomatic patients or those with more complex coronary anatomy are often excluded from randomization. In addition, many studies have not systematically incorporated the assessment of myocardial viability or inducible ischaemia using advanced imaging techniques, which are now considered central elements in the diagnostic workup of these patients.

Observational registries provide larger and more representative datasets, but they remain subject to selection bias, particularly with respect to anatomical complexity, operator expertise, and baseline clinical characteristics. Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for further adequately powered prospective studies based on protocols that incorporate standardized imaging assessment and structured clinical follow-up.

Conclusions

The ISCHEMIA Trial, although not specifically designed to address CTOs, has played a pivotal role in redefining the management of stable coronary artery disease by placing OMT at the centre of care and promoting a more selective use of invasive strategies. However, two ISCHEMIA sub-studies—those focusing on CTOs and on heart failure—suggest a potential benefit of percutaneous revascularization over medical therapy in patients with CTOs and in those with left ventricular dysfunction.

In these two areas of particular interest, future dedicated studies may provide more robust evidence to further refine patient selection and treatment strategies. Finally, once an indication for CTO revascularization has been established—whether in the context of isolated CTO or multivessel disease—the procedure should be performed by experienced operators in specialized centres, where multidisciplinary expertise and advanced technologies are available to ensure optimal procedural safety and clinical outcomes.
