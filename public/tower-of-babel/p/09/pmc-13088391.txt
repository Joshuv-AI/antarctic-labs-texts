Engineering NKG2D ligand affinity transforms EGFR-targeted NK cell engagers into high-potency effectors against pancreatic cancer
Authors: Seul-Gi Lee, Myungjee Lee, Hye-Min Lee, Ga-Hyun Son, Sang-Rok Yoon, Byeong-Ho Chae, Dae-Seong Kim, Kyung-Mi Lee, Yong-Sung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) often escapes T cell–mediated immunity through impaired major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) antigen presentation, contributing to its limited responsiveness to T cell–based immunotherapies. Because natural killer (NK) cells are capable of eliminating MHC-I–low tumor cells, redirecting NK cytotoxicity represents a promising strategy for these immune-cold tumors such as PDAC. Among activating NK receptors, NKG2D has been widely exploited in NK cell–engaging platforms through the incorporation of NKG2D ligands (MICA/B and ULBP family members). However, the impact of NKG2D ligand (NKG2DL) identity and binding affinity on NK cell engager potency has not been quantitatively defined.ResultsWe engineered a panel of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–targeted, heterodimeric IgG1 Fc-based αEGFR×NKG2DL immune cell engagers (ICEs), each pairing a panitumumab-derived EGFR arm with one of six human NKG2DL extracellular domains (MICA, MICB, ULBP1, ULBP2, and the ULBP6 allelic variants ULBP0601 and ULBP0602). All ICEs bound specifically to EGFR and NKG2D, and NKG2D binding affinity correlated directly with NK cell cytotoxic potency across PDAC cell lines expressing different EGFR densities. Among native ligands, ULBP0602 exhibited the strongest NKG2D binding and highest killing activity. Yeast display–based affinity improvement of ULBP0602 yielded the variant ULBP6#2, which showed approximately a 13-fold improvement in affinity due primarily to slower dissociation kinetics. The affinity-improved αEGFR×ULBP6#2 induced up to an 11-fold reduction in EC₅₀ and greater maximal lysis than the parental construct, along with enhanced IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion by NK cells. In NK-humanized NSG mice bearing PDAC xenografts, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 achieved markedly greater tumor growth inhibition compared with αEGFR×ULBP0602 in both PANC-1 (89% versus 57%) and BxPC-3 (60% versus 25%) models, without observable toxicity or weight loss.ConclusionsThese results support an affinity–activity relationship for NKG2D-engaging ICEs within the EGFR-targeted, Fc-competent format evaluated here. Notably, improving ULBP6 affinity enhanced NK cell effector function and increased antitumor efficacy in NK-reconstituted PDAC models. Collectively, our findings provide a design framework for engineering high-potency, tumor-tethered NKG2D engagers for the immunotherapy of immune-cold tumors such as PDAC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00651-y.

Background

Numerous solid tumors, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), evade cytotoxic T cell recognition by downregulating major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules and other components of antigen processing and presentation [1]. This decrease in antigen presentation facilitates immune escape and limits the efficacy of T cell–based immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors [2]. In contrast, natural killer (NK) cells can preferentially target and destroy cells with low or absent MHC-I expression through a mechanism known as “missing-self” recognition. Thus, therapies that redirect NK cells, such as NK cell engagers, represent a rational strategy for treating tumors with deficient MHC-I [3, 4].

PDAC is a prototypical MHC-I–evasive malignancy that generally responds poorly to immunotherapy. It is characterized by a dense, fibrous, and highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, leading to limited T cell infiltration and consistently poor responses to immune checkpoint blockade [5]. Given these challenges, PDAC remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a five-year overall survival rate of approximately 10%, underscoring the urgent need for new therapeutic modalities [6].

For targeted therapy, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is an attractive antigen target in PDAC, as it is overexpressed in up to 95% of cases and is often associated with a poor prognosis. This feature supports the development of EGFR-directed immunotherapy for this disease [7–9].

NK cell function is regulated by integrating inhibitory and activating receptor signals [4, 10]. Inhibitory receptors, including killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) and natural killer group 2 member A (NKG2A), recognize self-MHC-I molecules to limit off-target cytotoxicity. In contrast, activating receptors, including natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) and the natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCRs) NKp30, NKp44, and NKp46, detect stress-induced ligands associated with cellular transformation [10–12]. NKG2D is broadly expressed on NK cells and interacts with a wide range of stress-induced ligands, including MICA, MICB, and six ULBP family members (ULBP1–ULBP6), thereby enabling recognition of tumors that downregulate MHC-I [3, 13].

Despite the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in PDAC, NKG2D expression is largely preserved on circulating NK cells from patients with pancreatic cancer (approximately 80 to 90%), at frequencies higher than those reported for the NCRs [14]. This relative preservation contrasts with reports of functional impairment in certain NK-activating pathways, particularly NCR-mediated signaling, in PDAC [15]. Accordingly, NKG2D represents a compelling activating axis that appears to remain functionally preserved in PDAC. In this context, NKG2D ligand (NKG2DL)-fused proteins or ICEs have been developed to redirect NK cell cytotoxicity toward antigen-defined tumors. For example, fusing the extracellular domain (ECD) of human NKG2DLs, such as MICA, ULBP1, or ULBP2, to tumor-specific antibody fragments (e.g., targeting CD19, CD20, CD138, and HER2) [16–19] or the C-terminus of full-length IgG antibodies (e.g., targeting VEGFR2) [20] has promoted NKG2D-mediated NK cell lysis of targeted tumor cells in preclinical settings. Despite the reported efficacy of individual NKG2DL fusion proteins, prior studies have not provided a direct comparative analysis across diverse ligands, and have specifically neglected the ULBP6 allelic variants ULBP0601 and ULBP0602, whose distinct binding kinetics can modulate NK cell responses [21].

In parallel with NKG2D-based triggering, many antibody-based NK cell engagers leverage Fc-dependent activation through CD16a (FcγRIIIa), a major activating Fc receptor on human NK cells, to elicit antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) [22, 23]. Retaining a wild-type IgG1 Fc domain enables CD16a engagement and can provide an additional activating input alongside receptor–ligand interactions at the tumor cell surface. This dual-signal concept provides a rationale for ICE architectures that combine tumor tethering with coordinated activation via CD16a and NKG2D.

In this study, we designed and profiled a panel of heterodimeric IgG1 Fc-scaffolded αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs incorporating six distinct NKG2DL, including MICA, MICB, ULBP1, ULBP2, and the two ULBP6 allelic variants (ULBP0601 and ULBP0602), to evaluate how NKG2D binding relates to cytotoxic potency within a matched ICE format. Across the affinity range represented in our constructs, increased NKG2D affinity was correlated with enhanced NK-mediated killing. Therefore, we selected ULBP0602 as the top performer ligand for both NKG2D affinity and cytotoxic potency, and subjected it to affinity improvement, yielding a variant (ULBP6#2). The incorporation of affinity-improved ULBP6#2 into an EGFR-targeted ICE produced a molecule with superior cytotoxic activity relative to its wild-type counterpart in multiple pancreatic cancer cell lines. Importantly, these enhanced properties translated into improved antitumor efficacy in vivo, with the affinity-improved construct demonstrating markedly greater tumor growth inhibition in PDAC xenograft models. Together, this study establishes affinity-improved ULBP6 as an effective NKG2D-engaging module within an EGFR-directed ICE framework for NK-mediated control of PDAC.

Materials and methods

Construction, expression, and purification of ICEs

All ICEs were constructed on a human IgG1 EW–RVT heterodimeric Fc (hinge–CH2–CH3) scaffold, with CH3 mutations as follows (EU numbering): chain A contained K360E and K409W, while chain B had Q347R, D399V, and F405T, as previously described [24–26]. To generate αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs, the panitumumab VH–CH1 cassette was fused upstream of Fc chain A, and each human NKG2DL ECD was fused upstream of Fc chain B in pcDNA3.4. The corresponding panitumumab VL–CL light chain was also cloned into pcDNA3.4. NKG2DL ECD sequences were synthesized (LnCBio, Seoul, Republic of Korea) as: MICA (residues 24–307; NP_000238.1), MICB (23–309; NP_001276089.1), ULBP1 (26–216; NP_079494.1), ULBP2 (26–218; NP_079493.1), ULBP0601 (29–218; [21]), and ULBP0602 (29–218; NP_570970.2).

Proteins were transiently expressed in HEK293F cells cultured in FreeStyle 293 expression medium (Gibco) at 37 °C and 8% CO2 with shaking. For each ICE, plasmids encoding the heavy and light chains were co-transfected at equimolar ratios into 100–200 mL cultures, according to the manufacturer’s protocol [27, 28]. After 6 days, supernatants were collected by centrifugation (2,800 × g, 20 min) and filtered through 0.45-µm PES membranes. ICEs were purified by Protein A affinity chromatography (CaptivA PriMAB, Repligen, CA-PRI-0100) and buffer-exchanged with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). For in vivo studies, proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Bio-Rad NGC Quest 10) and formulated in PBS [27]. All protein preparations were sterile-filtered (0.2 μm) before use. Concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay, and absorbance was measured at 280 nm using sequence-derived extinction coefficients. Purity and assembly were evaluated using SDS-PAGE and SEC as previously detailed [27, 28].

Recombinant antigen production and biotinylation

Recombinant human NKG2D (rhNKG2D) ECD (residues 80–216; NP_001186734.1) and human EGFR (rhEGFR) ECD (residues 25–645; NP_005219.2) were synthesized (LnCBio) and cloned into pcDNA3.4. For rhNKG2D ECD, an N-terminal 8×His tag and an Avi tag (MSGLNDIFEAQKIEWHE) were included. For rhEGFR ECD, the same tags were fused to the C-terminus. The proteins were transiently expressed in HEK293F cells in FreeStyle 293 medium and purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [29, 30]. Where required, rhNKG2D ECD was enzymatically biotinylated using BirA ligase (Avidity, BirA500) [29, 31]. Purified proteins were used for ELISA, biolayer interferometry (BLI), and yeast display selection methods, including magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).

Cell lines and culture conditions

Human pancreatic cancer cell lines SNU-213, PANC-1, and AsPC-1, the colon cancer cell line SW620, and the human T lymphoblast line KL-1 were sourced from the Korean Cell Line Bank. BxPC-3 cells were purchased from the ATCC. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) were generated from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using B95-8-derived EBV supernatant as described previously [32]. PANC-1 cells were cultured in DMEM (HyClone, SH30243-01), whereas BxPC-3, SNU-213, AsPC-1, SW620, KL-1, and EBV-LCL cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (HyClone, SH30027-01). The medium was supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) Antibiotic–Antimycotic solution (100×; Welgene, LS 203-01). Cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling (ABION CRO, Seoul, Republic of Korea), used within 20 passages, and routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination during polymerase chain reaction (CellSafe, D-25). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

ELISA

The binding affinity of ICEs to rhNKG2D ECD was quantified using ELISA. Half-area 96-well plates (Corning, 3690) were coated with rhNKG2D ECD (10 µg/mL) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by blocking with PBS (pH 7.4) containing 3% (w/v) skim milk for 1 h. After three washes with PBS-T (PBS, 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20), the ICEs were serially diluted 10-fold and incubated for 45 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS-T, bound ICEs were detected with HRP-conjugated anti-human Fc (1:4,000; Invitrogen, 628420), and the reaction was visualized using TMB substrate (Thermo Scientific, 34028). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan GO plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Off-target polyspecific binding was assessed using ELISA against dsDNA, insulin, hemocyanin, and cardiolipin as previously described [28, 33]. Plates were coated with dsDNA (1 µg/mL; Sigma, D4522), insulin (50 µg/mL; Sigma, I9278), hemocyanin (50 µg/mL; Sigma, H8283), or cardiolipin (150 µg/mL; Sigma, C0563), blocked as described above, and incubated with ICEs. Detection of bound ICEs was performed using HRP-conjugated anti-human Fc (1:4,000; Invitrogen, 628420), and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded, subtracting background values from wells containing only blocking buffer.

BLI

Binding kinetics to rhNKG2D ECD and rhEGFR ECD were assessed using BLI on an Octet QKe instrument (Sartorius) [33, 34]. ICEs (30 µg/mL in PBS-T) were captured on anti-human IgG Fc capture biosensors (AHC; Sartorius, 18-5060), achieving a loading response of approximately 1.2 nm. After a 3-min baseline in PBS-T, sensors were exposed to antigen solutions at specified concentrations for association, then transferred back to PBS-T for dissociation measurements. All experiments were conducted at 25 °C with agitation at 1,000 rpm in 200 µL black 96-well plates (Greiner, 655209). Sensorgrams were analyzed globally using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model in Octet Data Analysis v11.0 to determine the association rate constant (kon), dissociation rate constant (koff), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).

Isolation of PBMCs and ex vivo expansion of NK cells

Peripheral blood was obtained from healthy donors using protocols approved by the Ajou University IRB (AjouIRB-202502-HM-001), following written informed consent [25], and collected using BD Vacutainer tubes (BD Biosciences, 367874). PBMCs were isolated via density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare).

NK cells were subsequently expanded ex vivo as previously described [32]. Briefly, PBMCs were washed in PBS-F (pH 7.4, 2% (v/v) FBS) and co-cultured with gamma-irradiated feeder cells (KL-1 and EBV-LCL, 100 Gy) at a 1:1 PBMC to feeder ratio in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS, 1% (v/v) Antibiotic–Antimycotic solution, and recombinant human IL-2 (rhIL-2, 500 U/mL; Gibco, 200-02-250UG) in 24-well flat-bottom plates (SPL, 30024). Fresh rhIL-2-containing medium was replaced on day 3. On day 6, cultures were transferred to T75 flasks (SPL, 70075) at 2.5 × 105 cells/mL and maintained with subculture every 3–4 days under identical conditions. Expanded cells were used as primary human NK effectors.

For cryopreservation, cells were harvested on day 10, resuspended at 1.5 × 107 cells/mL in FBS containing 10% DMSO, and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Flow cytometry

For ICE binding assays, EGFR-positive BxPC-3 cells, NKG2D-positive primary human NK cells, and EGFR/NKG2D-negative SW620 cells were incubated with ICEs at the indicated concentrations for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were washed twice with PBS-F and stained with FITC-conjugated anti-human IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, F9512) for 30 min at 4 °C [27]. To quantify the proportion of CD45⁺CD3⁻CD56⁺ NK cells, expanded primary human NK cells were thawed and stimulated overnight with rhIL-2 (250 U/mL) prior to flow cytometric analysis. Cells were pre-incubated with Human BD Fc Block (BD Biosciences, 564219) for 10 min at room temperature and then stained for 30 min at 4 °C with APC conjugated anti-CD45 (BioLegend, 304012), APC-eFluor 780 conjugated anti-CD3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 47-0037-42), and PE conjugated anti-CD56 (BioLegend, 318306) [25]. After washing with PBS-F, the samples were acquired on a NovoCyte 2060R flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies) and analyzed using FlowJo v10 (Tree Star).

In vitro NK cell cytotoxicity and cytokine release assays

Cytotoxicity was quantified using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay with ex vivo-expanded primary human NK cells as effector cells. Pancreatic cancer target cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well in 96-well flat-bottom plates (SPL, 30096). ICEs were added at the indicated concentrations and incubated with the target cells for 30 min at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The ex vivo-expanded NK cells were stimulated overnight with rhIL-2 (250 U/mL), washed, and 2 × 105 cells/well were seeded (E: T = 10:1). The co-cultures were then incubated for 5 h at 37 °C. Supernatants were collected, and LDH release was measured using the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega, G1780). Assay reagent (50 µL) was mixed with supernatant (50 µL) and incubated for 10–20 min at room temperature in the dark, then stopped with 50 µL of Stop Solution (Promega, G183A). The absorbance was measured at 490 nm (Cytation 3; BioTek) after subtracting the background absorbance of the medium. Spontaneous release was determined from wells containing only target cells or target cells plus effector cells without ICE. Maximum release was defined using target cells lysed with a 10× lysis solution (Promega, G182A). Percent lysis was calculated using the formula: 100 × (A − B) / (C − D), where A is experimental LDH release, B is spontaneous release from target plus effector cells, C is maximum LDH release, and D is spontaneous release from target cells alone.

EC50 values were derived through nonlinear regression of normalized dose-response curves in GraphPad Prism 5 [25]. For NK cell activation studies, IFN-γ and TNF-α in co-culture supernatants were quantified using human IFN-γ and TNF-α-uncoated ELISA kits (Invitrogen, 88-7316-86 and 88-7346-86), following the manufacturer’s instructions [25, 35].

Yeast surface-display affinity improvement of ULBP0602

ULBP0602 was engineered for improved affinity by targeted randomization of the NKG2D interface residues defined from the ULBP0602–NKG2D co-crystal structure (PDB 4S0U). Spiked oligonucleotides were hand-mixed to introduce substitutions at each selected position, while retaining the parental residue at approximately 50% frequency [29, 36]. The amplified ULBP0602 library (12 µg) and linearized pYDS display vector (4 µg) were co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae AWY101 via homologous recombination using Gene Pulser II (Bio-Rad) [29, 33]. Libraries were enriched by one round of MACS using 1 µM biotinylated NKG2D ECD, followed by three rounds of FACS (FACSAria III, BD).

For FACS, biotinylated NKG2D was used at 100 (round 1), 20, 50 (round 2), or 5 nM (round 3). Rounds 1 and 3 were performed under equilibrium conditions, whereas round 2 included a kinetic off-rate selection step using a 10-fold excess of non-biotinylated NKG2D as a soluble competitor [37]. Surface display was quantified by staining the C-terminal c-Myc tag (mouse anti-c-Myc clone 9E10, 1:200; Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse IgG, 1:600; Invitrogen, A11001), and antigen binding was detected using streptavidin R phycoerythrin (Invitrogen, S866). The top 0.1–0.5% binders for each sort were collected, and the final populations were plated on selective media to isolate individual clones for downstream characterization [38].

Mice

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal and Ethics Review Committee of Ajou University (approval ID: 2023–0096) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Immunodeficient NSG (NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) mice were originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory, bred, and maintained at Ajou University under an approved protocol (approval ID: 2023-0094).

NK-humanized PDAC xenograft studies

Female and male NSG mice (6–13 weeks old) were subcutaneously implanted with either BxPC-3 or PANC-1 cells (6 × 106 cells per mouse) in 100 µL of a PBS–Matrigel mixture (1:1; Corning, 354234). When the mean tumor volumes reached approximately 100–150 mm3, the mice were randomized and intravenously infused with ex vivo-expanded primary human NK cells (1 × 107 cells per mouse). NK cells were obtained from three independent healthy donors and distributed across treatment groups within each xenograft model to reduce donor-related variability. rhIL-2 was administered intraperitoneally three times per week (20,000 or 50,000 units per dose). ICEs or vehicle (PBS) were administered intraperitoneally two times per week for a total of three doses (500 pmol per dose). All NK-humanized xenograft studies were conducted as a single cohort per model. Tumor volume was calculated as V = L × W2 / 2 [39]. Mice were euthanized when tumor volumes reached 1,500 mm³, and survival was defined as time to euthanasia. Due to faster tumor growth in the BxPC-3 model, the observation period was shortened to remain within the predefined tumor volume limit. Tumor growth inhibition (TGI) was calculated on the final day as: TGI (%) = [100 − (VfICEs − ViICEs) / (VfPBS − ViPBS) × 100], where Vi and Vf denote initial and final mean tumor volumes for the indicated groups [39].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5. Data are presented as mean ± SEM for pooled datasets and mean ± SD or SEM for representative experiments (based on two to three independent experiments), unless otherwise specified. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Design and characterization of heterodimeric Fc-scaffolded αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs

To evaluate the impact of NKG2DL identity and binding strength on NK cell activation within a consistent engager framework, we constructed a matched panel of heterodimeric Fc-scaffolded αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs. These ICEs incorporated the ECD of six human NKG2DLs spanning both ligand families (MIC and ULBP), covering a wide range of NKG2D affinities (Fig. 1A). Construction of each ICE utilized an EW–RVT heterodimeric IgG1 Fc scaffold, facilitating IgG-like developability and extended serum half-life [24–26]. The EGFR-binding VH and VL pairs were derived from clinically approved anti-EGFR panitumumab, selected for its high-affinity binding to human EGFR and relatively slow internalization compared with other EGFR-targeting antibodies [40, 41]. To maintain Fc-dependent engagement of CD16a—the principal activating Fc receptor on NK cells—a wild-type IgG1 Fc was employed [24–26].

For the NKG2D-engaging arm, MICA, MICB, ULBP1, ULBP2, and the allelic ULBP6 variants ULBP0601 (Arg106) and ULBP0602 (Leu106) were selected based on their established roles as stress-induced NKG2DLs and demonstrated feasibility for deployment in antibody fusion formats. MICB was included as a lower-affinity MIC ligand relative to MICA [42], and both ULBP0601 and ULBP0602 were incorporated due to distinct NKG2D affinities conferred by a single amino acid difference at position 106 [21], enabling targeted assessment of interaction strength effects within the same ligand scaffold. All six ICEs were successfully expressed and purified as correctly assembled heterodimers (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B), with yields ranging from 17 to 22 mg/L, comparable to an αEGFR×dummy control without NKG2DL fusion (Supplementary Table 1).

Each ICE demonstrated concentration-dependent binding to rhNKG2D ECD, as assessed by ELISA (Fig. 1B). BLI using immobilized rhNKG2D ECD yielded quantifiable KD values for αEGFR×MICA (KD ≈ 229 nM) and αEGFR×ULBP0602 (KD ≈ 28.1 nM). For the remaining constructs, the signals were insufficient for reliable kinetic fitting and therefore could not be quantified under the tested conditions (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 1). On the basis of the ELISA and BLI results, the relative binding hierarchy to rhNKG2D ECD across the panel was ULBP0602 > MICA > ULBP0601 > MICB, ULBP1, and ULBP2 (Supplementary Table 1). In parallel, BLI analysis confirmed that all ICEs bound rhEGFR ECD with high affinity (KD ≈ 0.5 nM) (Supplementary Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 1), approximately ten-fold weaker than that of the parental panitumumab IgG engaging EGFR bivalently (KD ≈ 0.05 nM). This difference is consistent with monovalent EGFR engagement in the heterodimeric ICE configuration [26].

Flow cytometric analysis supported dual-target specificity at the cellular level. All six ICEs bound to EGFR-positive BxPC-3 tumor cells and NKG2D-positive primary human NK cells, whereas the negative control Fc fragment did not (Fig. 1D). No binding was detected in EGFR- or NKG2D-deficient SW620 cells [31]. Taken together, these findings confirm the generation of a structurally matched αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE panel with preserved EGFR targeting and NKG2D engagement, thereby providing a platform for functional comparisons focused specifically on NKG2DL identity and binding properties.

NKG2D-binding affinity of αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs correlates with NK cell cytotoxicity against EGFR-expressing pancreatic cancer cells

To assess whether varying levels of NKG2D engagement within our matched αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE panel translate into graded NK cell cytotoxicity toward EGFR-positive PDAC tumor cells, effector populations were generated via expansion of PBMCs from healthy donors in the presence of irradiated KL-1 and EBV-LCL feeder cells supplemented with rhIL-2 [32]. These expansion conditions yielded NK-enriched effector populations (approximately 50% to 70% CD45⁺CD3⁻CD56⁺ cells), which were used without further purification in subsequent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 2). To model biologically relevant variations in target antigen density, a panel of PDAC cell lines with distinct EGFR surface densities was selected, specifically utilizing moderately expressing BxPC-3 and SNU-213 cells, and highly expressing PANC-1 and AsPC-1 cells (Fig. 2A).

All αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs demonstrated robust, dose-dependent lysis of EGFR-positive PDAC cells following 5 h co-culture with primary NK cells at an E: T ratio of 10:1 (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, no cytotoxicity was observed for EGFR-negative SW620 cells, confirming strict target dependence. Furthermore, the Fc-silenced control αEGFR×dummy-FEA, which contains the L234F/L235E/D265A (FEA) mutations designed to ablate FcγR-mediated effector function [43], showed negligible cytolytic activity across all cell lines. Compared with the non-NKG2DL-fused αEGFR×dummy control, each NKG2DL-fused ICE achieved lower EC50 values (indicating higher potency) and higher maximal lysis (Fig. 2B; Table 1), demonstrating that the NKG2DL module provides an additional activating input beyond Fc–CD16a-mediated signaling. Optimal cytolytic activity was attributed to cooperative targeting of EGFR, CD16a, and NKG2D.

Antigen density further influenced potency. For every ICE evaluated, high-EGFR cell lines PANC-1 and AsPC-1 consistently exhibited lower EC50 values (higher potency) than the moderately expressing BxPC-3 and SNU-213 cells, reflecting established dependencies on target antigen abundance (Fig. 2B; Table 1) [44]. Notably, αEGFR×ULBP0602 exhibited superior intrinsic potency against AsPC-1 (EC50 ≈ 0.067 nM) and PANC-1 (EC50 ≈ 0.20 nM), compared with BxPC-3 (EC50 ≈ 0.37 nM) and SNU-213 (EC50 ≈ 0.64 nM; Table 1). Comparative functional analysis revealed that αEGFR×ULBP0602 induced 3–16% higher maximal lysis and 1.1–3.1-fold lower EC50 values than the next ranked high-affinity ligand-fused constructs, namely αEGFR×MICA and αEGFR×ULBP0601. The order of cytotoxic potency determined by maximal lysis and EC50 was ULBP0602 > MICA > ULBP0601 > MICB > ULBP2 > ULBP1. Quantitative correlation analysis, plotting mean apparent KD values for rhNKG2D ECD (Supplementary Table 1) versus log-transformed mean EC50 values for NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity (Table 1), yielded a strong positive correlation (r = 0.91–0.99, P < 0.05; Fig. 2C) across all four cell lines.

Cumulatively, we evaluated all six NKG2D ligands within the same αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE architecture across assays of rhNKG2D binding, cellular binding, and NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity. Within this controlled screening framework, cytotoxic potency was associated with both NKG2D-binding strength and EGFR antigen density. Based on its consistently higher cytotoxic activity across EGFR-expressing PDAC cell lines, we selected αEGFR×ULBP0602 as the baseline construct for subsequent affinity improvement and in vivo evaluation.

Affinity improvement of ULBP0602 yields an enhanced NKG2D binding module

To further increase NKG2D engagement within the αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE framework, we performed affinity improvement of ULBP0602 using a yeast surface-display system. A focused mutational library was generated by introducing randomized substitutions at key interface residues implicated in NKG2D binding, as defined by the ULBP0602–NKG2D co-crystal structure (PDB ID: 4S0U) [21]. To balance preservation of parental binding and local optimization, the library employed hand-mixed spiked oligonucleotides designed to retain parental amino acids at approximately 50% frequency (Fig. 3A) [29]. This approach yielded a diverse ULBP6 variant library displayed on yeast, with an estimated diversity of approximately 2.5 × 108 as determined by serial dilution plating. Sequencing of 30 randomly selected clones confirmed the intended mutational patterns and library integrity.

The library underwent high-throughput screening: one round of MACS, followed by three rounds of FACS against biotinylated rhNKG2D ECD. To isolate variants with superior binding kinetics, equilibrium screening was combined with stringent kinetic selection, including the addition of a 10-fold molar excess of non-biotinylated target antigen as a soluble competitor to prioritize clones with slower dissociation rates [29, 37]. This strategy led to the isolation of four unique high-affinity ULBP0602 variants for NKG2D. These affinity-improved ligands were subsequently reformatted into the heterodimeric Fc-based αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE architecture. All affinity-improved ULBP6-fused ICEs were expressed and purified as correctly assembled heterodimers, achieving ≥ 90% purity and yields comparable to the parental wild-type construct (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B, Supplementary Table 2).

BLI analysis revealed that αEGFR×ULBP6#2 exhibited the largest improvement in rhNKG2D ECD binding, corresponding to an approximately 13-fold reduction in KD relative to αEGFR×ULBP0602 (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 2). To address potential polyspecificity or off-target reactivity commonly associated with affinity improvement, all isolated ULBP6 variants were evaluated via a multi-antigen non-specificity ELISA [28]. The affinity-improved ULBP6-fused ICEs exhibited negligible binding to structurally unrelated antigens (dsDNA, insulin, hemocyanin, and cardiolipin), consistent with wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Based on its improved affinity and retained binding specificity, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 was prioritized for comprehensive functional analyses.

To verify translation of these improved kinetic properties in cellular systems, flow cytometry-based binding assays were performed. αEGFR×ULBP6#2 maintained robust engagement of cell-surface EGFR on BxPC-3 cells at levels indistinguishable from the wild-type. This confirmed that ULBP6 domain maturation did not compromise the geometry or affinity of the tumor-targeting arm. Conversely, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 demonstrated markedly enhanced binding to primary human NK cells expressing endogenous NKG2D, paralleling the affinity gains observed via BLI (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these results establish αEGFR×ULBP6#2 as a high-affinity, specific ICE with significantly improved potency for NKG2D engagement.

Affinity improvement of the ULBP6 potentiates ICE-mediated NK cell cytotoxicity

To evaluate whether enhanced NKG2D engagement improves NK cell effector function, we compared cytotoxicity and cytokine release mediated by αEGFR×ULBP6#2 and its parental counterpart, αEGFR×ULBP0602. The αEGFR×ULBP6#2 construct induced robust, dose-dependent lysis across various EGFR-positive PDAC cell lines (BxPC-3, SNU-213, PANC-1, and AsPC-1), whereas cytotoxicity toward EGFR-negative SW620 cells remained unchanged, confirming strict antigen-dependent redirection (Fig. 4A, B, Supplementary Fig. 5). For all EGFR-positive targets, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 exhibited superior NK cell cytotoxicity, reflected in an approximate 1.3- to 11.3-fold improvement in EC50 values and an increase in maximal cell lysis ranging from 5.1% to 16.8% compared with that by αEGFR×ULBP0602 (Table 2).

Notably, the magnitude of the functional gain was closely associated with target antigen density. Among high-EGFR-expressing cell lines (PANC-1 and AsPC-1), the affinity-improved construct conferred only moderate improvements over the parental molecule (2.2- and 1.3-fold, respectively). In contrast, cell lines with moderate EGFR expression (BxPC-3 and SNU-213) showed a more pronounced difference, with 3.7- and 11.3-fold increases, respectively (Table 2). These results demonstrate that high EGFR surface expression may mitigate functional differences between ICE constructs with different NKG2D-binding affinities, whereas lower antigen availability accentuates the importance of stronger NKG2D engagement for productive NK cell activation [45, 46]. Thus, the NKG2D affinity-improved αEGFR×ULBP6#2 maintains potent redirection even against tumor cells with lower antigen density, thereby widening the therapeutic applicability of the engager.

Subsequently, we examined whether increased affinity further augmented NK cell activation beyond target cell lysis. Under identical co-culture conditions, treatment with 1 nM ICEs led to significantly higher secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α by primary NK cells in the presence of EGFR-positive targets (Fig. 4C). Compared with αEGFR×ULBP0602, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 increased IFN-γ secretion by 242–380 pg/mL and TNF-α secretion by 418–618 pg/mL across two EGFR-positive cell lines. Absolute cytokine production was consistently greater in PANC-1 cells than in BxPC-3 cells, corresponding with higher EGFR density in PANC-1 cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that affinity engineering of ULBP6 to enhance NKG2D engagement increases both cytolytic potency and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion by NK cells.

Affinity-improved αEGFR×ULBP6#2 ICE demonstrates superior in vivo antitumor efficacy in NK-reconstituted pancreatic cancer models

To determine whether engineering ULBP6 for higher affinity enhances antitumor activity, we evaluated our ICE constructs in two NK cell-reconstituted PDAC xenograft models: PANC-1 and BxPC-3. These models were selected to represent a range of EGFR antigen densities. NSG mice bearing established subcutaneous tumors were randomized into treatment groups once the mean tumor volume reached approximately 100–150 mm3 (Fig. 5A, D). Each mouse received weekly intravenous infusions of ex vivo-expanded primary human NK cells (1 × 107 cells per mouse), with their survival and metabolic fitness supported by intraperitoneal rhIL-2 (20,000–50,000 units per dose) three times per week. Therapeutic intervention included intraperitoneal injections of either 500 pmol αEGFR×ULBP6#2 or its parental αEGFR×ULBP0602, administered two times per week for a total of 3–4 doses. Control groups received PBS (vehicle) or PBS plus NK cells (vehicle + NK) to establish baseline tumor growth kinetics, with the TGI calculated relative to the PBS (vehicle) group (Fig. 5A, D).

In the PANC-1 xenograft model (high EGFR), both ICEs elicited significant tumor suppression relative to vehicle controls, with αEGFR×ULBP6#2 demonstrating greater tumor growth inhibition (Fig. 5B). By day 14, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 achieved an 88.5% TGI, compared with 56.9% for the parental αEGFR×ULBP0602 construct. The divergence was more pronounced in the BxPC-3 model (moderate EGFR), where αEGFR×ULBP6#2 achieved 60.4% TGI, whereas the parental ICE reached 25.0% inhibition on day 10 (Fig. 5E). Across both models, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 was well tolerated without measurable body-weight loss or survival decrement, suggesting a lack of systemic toxicity under these dosing regimens (Fig. 5C, F).

Importantly, despite its measurable effects in vitro, the parental αEGFR×ULBP0602 did not outperform the vehicle + NK control group in either xenograft model (Fig. 5B, E). Although αEGFR×ULBP6#2 consistently achieved numerically greater tumor growth inhibition than the parental construct, this difference did not reach statistical significance relative to vehicle + NK. In this NK-reconstituted setting, the unmodified ULBP0602 domain provides limited NKG2D engagement, which may be insufficient to sustain productive effector–target interactions. Collectively, these results are consistent with a trend toward improved tumor control with enhanced NKG2D-binding in this NK-reconstituted xenograft setting.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a potent heterodimeric Fc-based αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE platform to investigate how the selection of a human NKG2DL governs NK cell effector output. Through a systematic evaluation of six human NKG2DLs within a matched molecular architecture, we demonstrated that stronger NKG2D engagement correlated with enhanced NK cell cytotoxic activity against EGFR-positive PDAC targets. Building on this relationship, affinity engineering of the NKG2DL arm increased NKG2D binding by approximately 13-fold, enhancing in vitro cytotoxicity, cytokine release, and tumor suppression in NK-reconstituted PDAC xenograft models.

We prioritized the NKG2D axis because NK cell function can be impaired in solid tumors, including PDAC, with reduced activity reported for dominant activating receptors such as CD16a and NCRs, NKp30, NKp44, and NKp46 [4, 47, 48]. In contrast, NKG2D expression is reported to be relatively preserved in patient NK cells, supporting its potential as an activating receptor that remains amenable to therapeutic redirection within an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [14, 15, 47].

Notably, PDAC and other solid tumors frequently upregulate stress-inducible NKG2D ligands in response to cellular stress [4, 49]. Although canonical ligands, such as MICA and MICB, are present in PDAC tissues, endogenous immunosurveillance is often suppressed by mechanisms that limit receptor availability and disrupt synaptic stability [49–51]. A key immune escape mechanism involves the proteolytic shedding of MICA and MICB by metalloproteinases, generating soluble ligands that act as decoys and reduce NKG2D expression on NK and CD8+ T cells [50, 52]. This dissociation between preserved receptor competence and shedding-mediated ligand dysfunction creates a therapeutically actionable vulnerability within the NKG2D–NKG2DL axis. To address shedding-mediated attenuation and competition from soluble ligand, we designed an ICE that combines high-affinity NKG2D engagement with robust tumor tethering, thereby sustaining productive effector–target synapses even in environments rich in soluble ligands.

Using the EW–RVT heterodimeric IgG1 Fc architecture, previously optimized for robust bispecific assembly [24–26], we conducted a systematic evaluation of αEGFR×NKG2DL ICEs incorporating six natural ligands that spanned a range of binding affinities. A critical design element was the retention of the wild-type IgG1 Fc region to maintain CD16a engagement, while varying the intrinsic affinity of the NKG2D-binding domains across the MIC and ULBP families. Functional analysis of EGFR-positive PDAC cell lines revealed that the αEGFR×ULBP0602, which possessed the highest affinity for NKG2D, consistently elicited the most potent NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Quantitative analysis established a strong linear correlation between NKG2D-binding affinity and cytolytic EC50 values, identifying interaction strength as a key determinant of ICE potency, consistent with previous observations for MICA, MICB, and ULBP3 [42]. While others have explored the therapeutic potential of native NKG2DLs [16–19] or anti-NKG2D antibodies [53, 54], our work provides a systematic, head-to-head comparison across multiple ligand families and allelic variants, indicating that ligand-intrinsic binding kinetics dictate functional outcomes.

Our data clarify the functional paradox observed for ULBP6 allotypes in their native, untethered forms, where the higher-affinity ULBP0602 (KD ≈ 16 nM) has been associated with weaker NKG2D-dependent killing than the lower-affinity ULBP0601 (KD ≈ 165 nM), an effect attributed to overly stable ligand–receptor interactions that limit serial triggering [21, 55]. However, in our αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE format, the high-affinity αEGFR×ULBP0602 led to more potent NK cell cytotoxicity than αEGFR×ULBP0601. This divergence likely reflects the signaling configuration imposed by the ICE architecture, in which EGFR-directed tethering enforces effector–target proximity and enables coordinated NKG2D and Fc–CD16a signaling. Comparisons with matched controls (αEGFR×dummy and Fc-silenced αEGFR×dummy-FEA) support a contribution from Fc–CD16a engagement, while incorporation of NKG2D ligands confers the additional activation underlying the observed potency gains. Because the construct retains a conventional human IgG1 Fc without intentional Fc engineering, and Fc glycosylation was not explicitly profiled, FcγRIIIa engagement is expected to reflect baseline IgG1 activity and may vary with donor FcγRIIIa allotype and Fc glycosylation. In the absence of an Fc-silenced αEGFR×ULBP6#2 comparator, we cannot quantitatively partition the relative contributions of Fc receptor engagement and NKG2D-mediated activation for the affinity-improved construct. Under these tethered conditions, higher NKG2D affinity is expected to stabilize the immune synapse and increase the probability of productive signaling at the tumor interface, rather than to hinder receptor turnover. Thus, optimal NKG2D binding is format-dependent and is best defined within the structural and signaling context of the engager architecture, rather than inferred solely from the behavior of soluble ligands.

To leverage the format-specific requirement for synaptic stability, we sought to maximize residence time through kinetic reinforcement. Affinity improvement of ULBP0602 yielded αEGFR×ULBP6#2, which displayed an approximately 13-fold improvement in NKG2D affinity, primarily driven by a reduced dissociation rate (koff). Consistent with the kinetic proofreading model [56], this longer residence time translated into up to an 11-fold reduction in EC50 for target cell lysis and increased IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion relative to αEGFR×ULBP0602. Although the reduced koff provides a mechanistically plausible basis for these gains, the enhanced cytotoxic potency may also reflect contributions from synaptic avidity and coordinated Fc–CD16a signaling within the ICE format.

Notably, antigen density modulated the functional impact of NKG2D ligand affinity within this ICE format. High EGFR surface expression reduced functional separation among ICE constructs with distinct NKG2D-binding affinities, whereas lower antigen availability increased the relative advantage of optimized binding kinetics. Consistent with this, αEGFR×ULBP6#2 showed a larger potency gain over the parental construct in moderate EGFR-expressing cell lines than in high EGFR lines, while EGFR-negative cells remained non-responsive. Collectively, these data support an affinity-associated potency trend whose magnitude depends on target antigen abundance, but they do not define a strict minimal EGFR expression threshold in a manner generalizable beyond the tested models. Such observations are consistent with prior reports demonstrating that tuning the affinity and residence time of activating interactions and increasing avidity through valency or CD16a engagement can improve the killing efficiency of NKp30- and CD16a-based formats [44–46, 57, 58]. Extending this principle to the NKG2D axis via direct engineering of ULBP6 variants, our findings suggest that NKG2DL-binding kinetics represent a tunable parameter that can increase potency in a target density-dependent manner while maintaining antigen-dependent specificity.

The enhanced antitumor activity of αEGFR×ULBP6#2 in NK-humanized PDAC xenografts supports the functional relevance of NKG2D-binding kinetics for in vivo efficacy within this model. Notably, the parental αEGFR×ULBP0602 failed to elicit substantial tumor suppression despite measurable in vitro cytotoxicity, suggesting a kinetic threshold imposed by the physiology of solid tumors. In PDAC, productive immune engagement is generally considered to require sustained effector target interactions that can overcome stromal barriers and soluble NKG2DL decoys that may limit receptor availability [59, 60]. In this Fc-competent ICE context, the reduced dissociation rate (koff) achieved through ULBP6 optimization may prolong the effective lifetime of productive engagement. However, avidity effects and Fc receptor-mediated contributions may also influence the apparent potency, and these factors were not independently dissected in the current study.

This study has several limitations. In the NK-humanized NSG xenograft model, ICE activity was evaluated under human NK cell reconstitution, and efficacy was assessed within an observation window defined by the treatment period. Although this system enables assessment of NK cell-dependent antitumor activity, it does not incorporate adaptive immunity and does not fully recapitulate the immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristic of PDAC [61–63]. In addition, the in vivo design did not include intratumoral mechanistic analyses, such as immunohistochemistry. Accordingly, we did not directly assess immune cell infiltration, target expression in residual tumors, or markers of tumor cell death. This design also does not formally isolate the relative contributions of EGFR-mediated tethering, NKG2D engagement, and Fc receptor-mediated activation. Body weight monitoring provides an initial assessment of tolerability but does not establish on-target, off-tumor safety. Moreover, limited murine cross-reactivity of the EGFR arm constrains definitive toxicity evaluation in this model, and translational safety will require dedicated studies in settings that better reflect human EGFR expression in normal tissues. Accordingly, our in vivo findings support improved activity within an NK-focused human xenograft context rather than establishing efficacy under the full PDAC microenvironment. Future studies in more complex models will be required to evaluate performance under more physiological conditions. Nevertheless, comparison of otherwise identical ICE constructs that differ only in NKG2D-binding affinity indicates that enhanced affinity improves antitumor efficacy within this controlled in vivo context.

Beyond its primary effects on NK cells, the constitutive expression of NKG2D as a co-stimulatory receptor on activated CD8⁺ T cells suggests that our αEGFR×NKG2DL ICE platform may also facilitate broader immune responses or synergize with T cell–directed immunotherapies [49, 64]. Recent bispecific strategies targeting the NKG2D axis have shown potential for coordinating both NK and CD8+ T cell responses, provided that activation is spatially constrained through tumor tethering to minimize off-tumor immune activation and systemic cytokine release [35, 65]. Therefore, by establishing interaction kinetics as a quantitatively tunable, format-dependent determinant of NKG2D-engager potency, our work provides a generalizable design framework for extending NKG2D-engaging ICEs to immune-cold solid tumors resistant to conventional immunotherapies due to defective antigen presentation and T cell exclusion [1].

In summary, our study supports NKG2D as a key effector receptor for NK cell redirection in PDAC and defines a quantitative affinity–activity relationship for NKG2D-engaging ICEs within a controlled molecular framework. We show that affinity improvement of ULBP6, exemplified by ULBP6#2, converts the naturally transient NKG2D interaction into a more stable synaptic engagement by reducing the dissociation rate (koff). This engineered engagement translated into augmented in vitro NK cell cytotoxicity and in vivo tumor suppression within the EGFR-targeted, Fc-competent ICE format and the PDAC models evaluated here. Collectively, these findings identify affinity tuning of NKG2D-directed modalities as a strategy that warrants further investigation in immune-cold tumors such as PDAC.
