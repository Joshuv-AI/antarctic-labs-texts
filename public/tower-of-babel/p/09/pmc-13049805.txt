Feeding tube dependence and postoperative complications in older adults after head and neck cancer surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Adam Galazka, Karolina Bienkowska-Pluta, Monika Paszkowska, Magdalena Misiak-Galazka, Katarzyna Stawarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElderly patients undergoing head and neck cancer (HNC) surgery represent a high-risk population due to age-related decline and multimorbidity, with increased vulnerability to complications. Dysphagia and nutritional compromise are common, adversely affecting recovery and outcomes. This study evaluated the incidence of postoperative dysphagia, enteral feeding requirements, and related complications in this population.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 46 patients aged ≥ 70 years who underwent major head and neck cancer surgery between 2019 and 2024. Clinical characteristics, tumor stage, nutritional status, and postoperative outcomes were collected. Enteral feeding strategies, including nasogastric tube (NGT) and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), were evaluated. Dysphagia was assessed clinically and operationalized by the requirement for enteral feeding. Outcomes included feeding route at hospital discharge and at 3- and 6-month follow-up, duration of enteral feeding dependence, and postoperative medical complications. Statistical analyses comprised descriptive statistics and chi-square testing, with logistic regression used to identify predictors of PEG dependence.ResultsFourteen patients (30.4%) required PEG and 19 (41.3%) NGT, with conversion from NGT to PEG in 17.4% (n = 8). Of the 19 patients initially managed with a NGT, 8 (42.1%) subsequently required conversion to PEG, representing 17.4% of the total cohort. At discharge, 65.2% (n = 30) achieved full oral intake; PEG dependence persisted in 23.9% (n = 11) at 3 months and 17.4% (n = 8) at 6 months. Advanced T stage was the only significant oncologic predictor, associated with PEG placement (p < 0.001) and dependence at 3 (p = 0.001) and 6 months (p = 0.049). Regression confirmed advanced T stage increased odds of PEG placement (OR = 5.4) and long-term dependence (OR = 11.4), while reducing the likelihood of oral intake at discharge (OR = 0.15). Laboratory analyses showed significant postoperative changes: serum albumin declined (3.93 ± 0.50 to 3.59 ± 0.63 g/dL, p = 0.0002), hemoglobin decreased (12.64 ± 1.88 to 10.17 ± 1.86 g/dL, p < 0.0001), WBC rose (7.29 ± 2.82 to 9.07 ± 3.92 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.008), INR increased slightly (1.03 ± 0.09 to 1.08 ± 0.12, p = 0.018), and potassium decreased modestly (4.39 ± 0.49 to 4.20 ± 0.45 mmol/L, p = 0.016). Complications were frequent: electrolyte imbalances (22%), aspiration pneumonia (8.9%), delirium (8.7%), and wound complications (8.7%). Less common but clinically relevant were acute kidney injury (6.5%), venous thromboembolism (2.2%), and refeeding syndrome (2.2%).ConclusionsAdvanced T stage was the strongest predictor of PEG dependence in elderly head and neck cancer patients. Early nutritional screening and proactive multidisciplinary care, including geriatric assessment, are critical to reduce dysphagia-related morbidity and improving recovery.

Background

Head and neck cancer (HNC) surgery remains a complex clinical challenge, and this complexity is further amplified in the geriatric population. With advances in modern medicine—including precision oncology and supportive care—life expectancy continues to increase, leading to a growing number of patients over the age of 70 who require surgical intervention [1]. In this age group, any surgical procedure carries an inherently higher risk due to comorbidities, physiological decline, and frailty. Among these, HNC surgeries—particularly those involving complex microsurgical reconstruction with free flap transfer—are among the most demanding and high-risk interventions in elderly patients [2]. Epidemiological data demonstrate a growing cancer burden globally over the past decades, including a significant rise in the incidence of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) among individuals aged 50 and older [3]. While evidence suggests that elderly patients with HNC generally have poorer overall survival compared to their younger counterparts [4], an increasing amount of literature indicates that, in appropriately selected individuals over the age of 70, surgical intervention can achieve complication rates and survival outcomes comparable to those observed in younger patients [5].

Although advances in surgical technique have led to high flap survival rates, functional recovery in this population is frequently limited by sarcopenia, polypharmacy and multiple chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or cognitive impairment [6]. Nowadays, multimorbidity is increasingly recognized as a standard clinical reality, rather than an exception, especially among older adults [7]. While appropriate patient selection for HNC reconstructive surgery is essential to ensure the most effective treatment approach, the risk of serious postoperative complications remains substantial [8]. Therefore, it is essential not only to consider all coexisting comorbidities but also to anticipate potential postoperative complications, including pneumonia, impaired wound healing, malnutrition, electrolyte disturbances, and delirium [9].

Therefore, ensuring adequate postoperative nutrition and hydration is a key component of care in this population, as it plays a crucial role in preventing a wide range of complications. Dysphagia—resulting in weight loss or malnutrition, increased risk of aspiration, and prolonged inability to resume oral feeding—is common in elderly patients due to both surgical disruption of anatomical structures and pre-existing or age-related deterioration of swallowing function [10]. According to the current data dysphagia typically increases from disease presentation through treatment to long-term posttreatment surveillance, ultimately affecting up to 45% to 75% of HNC patients [11]. These challenges often necessitate the use of temporary or long-term enteral feeding methods, most commonly nasogastric tubes (NGTs) or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) [12]. However, the optimal timing, indications, and clinical outcomes associated with both NGT and PEG in elderly patients remain subjects of ongoing debate. Feeding difficulties are closely associated with common postoperative complications in geriatric patients, including delirium, pneumonia, electrolyte imbalances, and overall functional decline [13]. Inadequate oral intake or suboptimal fluid management can exacerbate dehydration and delirium, impair wound healing, prolong hospitalization, increase the likelihood of institutionalization, and negatively impact long-term quality of life [14, 15].

The objective of this study was to evaluate postoperative feeding management strategies in elderly patients undergoing HNC surgery, with particular emphasis on the role of PEG. We present our single-institution experience, focusing on clinical decision-making, complication rates, and functional outcomes related to enteral nutrition.

Materials and methods

Study setting and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included elderly patients (≥ 70 years) diagnosed with HNC who underwent surgical resection followed by microsurgical reconstruction at the Maria Sklodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology in Warsaw, Poland, between 2019 and 2024. Sociodemographic data were extracted from electronic medical records using unique patient identifiers and hospital numbers. Pre-existing factors such as age, sex, smoking history, alcohol consumption, diabetes mellitus (DM), and atherosclerosis were documented. Clinical and pathological tumor characteristics—including primary tumor location, histological subtype, TNM (AJCC/UICC, 8th edition) and type of reconstructive flap—were also evaluated. Dietitians and speech-language therapists were routinely involved in the perioperative assessment and management of nutritional status and swallowing function in patients undergoing major head and neck cancer surgery. Their evaluations contributed to decisions regarding enteral feeding strategies, including NGT and PEG placement.

Biochemical analysis

Several blood parameters relevant to nutritional status and wound healing were assessed preoperatively and re-evaluated within 7 days after surgery. Serum albumin and hemoglobin were included as markers of baseline nutritional reserve and oxygen-carrying capacity, both of which are known to influence wound healing and postoperative recovery in older patients. White blood cell (WBC) count was assessed as an indicator of inflammatory or infectious complications. Electrolytes (sodium (Na) and potassium (K)) were monitored because dysnatremia and hypokalemia are frequent in frail, malnourished patients and contribute to postoperative delirium, arrhythmias, and poor functional outcomes. Coagulation parameters (INR, aPTT) were measured to evaluate bleeding risk and guide perioperative anticoagulation management. Renal function was assessed using creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and the BUN/creatinine ratio, as acute kidney injury (AKI) is a recognized complication in this population and is often precipitated by dehydration or sepsis. Finally, glucose levels were measured to identify perioperative hyperglycemia, which is associated with impaired wound healing, infection risk, and worse overall outcomes in surgical oncology. Blood samples were collected preoperatively from a peripheral venous access using standard procedures, and postoperatively from a central venous line. No complications were reported during the sample collection process.

Post-surgical complications assessment

Postoperative medical complications were systematically recorded during the hospital stay, which typically ranged from 7 to 14 days. They were retrospectively classified according to the Clavien–Dindo classification system based on the level of therapeutic intervention required, using information available in the medical records. Aspiration pneumonia was diagnosed based on clinical symptoms (fever, productive cough, dyspnea), elevated inflammatory markers, and radiographic evidence of pulmonary infiltrates. Delirium was identified using standardized clinical assessment and documentation in patient charts, typically based on acute changes in cognition, inattention, or fluctuating levels of consciousness. AKI was defined according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, using changes in serum creatinine and urine output. Wound healing complications were documented by the surgical team and included dehiscence, infection, or delayed healing requiring intervention. Refeeding syndrome was identified through biochemical monitoring of electrolytes in the context of reinitiation of nutrition. Re-surgery and postoperative bleeding were defined as unplanned events requiring surgical or procedural intervention during the postoperative hospital stay. All complications were verified through multidisciplinary chart review to ensure consistency and accuracy of reporting.

Feeding route evaluation

Preoperative nutritional status was assessed using serum albumin concentration and BMI, with serum albumin also measured postoperatively. Dysphagia was evaluated clinically based on postoperative swallowing ability and the requirement for enteral feeding support. Postoperative feeding strategies were categorized according to the route of administration, namely NGT or PEG (Fig. 1). PEG placement was not protocolized and was determined on a case-by-case basis as part of routine clinical practice. Indications included anticipated prolonged dysphagia, extent of surgical resection, aspiration risk, and an expected need for enteral feeding exceeding 3–4 weeks. NGT placement was typically performed immediately following surgery or within the first 7 postoperative days. PEG placement was indicated in patients with anticipated prolonged feeding difficulties related to the extent of surgical intervention, defined by persistent dysphagia preventing adequate oral intake, a high risk of aspiration, or an expected need for enteral nutrition exceeding 3–4 weeks. For patients requiring PEG, data were collected on the timing of tube placement and the total duration of enteral feeding to distinguish between early and prolonged dependence. Swallowing function was evaluated at hospital discharge and reassessed at 3- and 6-month outpatient follow-up based on clinical assessment, including fiberoptic examination, allowing longitudinal evaluation of functional recovery and ongoing nutritional support requirements.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.8.). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared between groups using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) when distributional assumptions were not met. Pre- and post-surgical values were compared using paired statistical tests. Normality of paired differences (post–pre) was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For parameters with normally distributed differences, the paired Student’s t-test was applied. When normality was violated, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as the preferred non-parametric alternative. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Patients characteristics

A total of 75 patients were assessed for eligibility during the study period. Twenty-nine patients were excluded, most commonly due to recurrent or metastatic disease (n = 11), loss to follow-up or missing follow-up feeding assessments (n = 6), incomplete perioperative nutritional or laboratory data (n = 5), prior PEG or long-term enteral feeding (n = 5), or synchronous malignancy influencing feeding or survival (n = 2). The final study cohort comprised 46 patients, all of whom had feeding outcomes assessed at hospital discharge as well as at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, (Fig. 2). The study cohort comprised 46 patients, including 26 men (56.5%) and 20 women (43.5%). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 19.3 ± 3.3 kg/m², with a median of 19.0 [18.0–21.0] kg/m², indicating a predominantly normal-to-underweight population. DM2 was present in 7 patients (15.2%). A history of smoking was documented in 30 patients (65.2%), with a mean cumulative exposure of (26.1 ± 9.3 pack-years ian 30 [16–31]). Alcohol use was reported by 26 patients (56.5%), with a mean duration of 9.1 ± 5.5 years (median 7.5 [5.0–10.0]). Peripheral atherosclerosis was identified in 10 patients (21.7%). The most common primary tumor site was the tongue (36.9%), followed by the floor of the mouth (28.3%), cheek mucosa (15.2%), and lower gum (10.9%). Less frequent sites included the lower lip (4.3%), upper lip (2.2%), and hard palate (2.2%). Most tumors were diagnosed at an advanced stage, with T4 (41.6%) and T3 (32.6%) predominating, followed by T2 (21.7%) and T1 (4.3%). In contrast, nodal involvement was less advanced, with N0 being the most common (47.8%), followed by N1 (28.3%), N2 (21.7%), and N3 (2.2%). All tumors were histologically confirmed as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Regarding reconstructive strategies, the radial forearm free flap (RFFF) was the most frequently used (71.7%), followed by the anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap (19.5%) and fibula free flap (FFF) (8.8%). Biochemical parameters assessment

In the postoperative period (7 days), several laboratory parameters showed significant changes compared with baseline. Both mean ± SD and median with interquartile range (IQR) were calculated pre- and postoperatively; the results below are presented as mean ± SD. Serum albumin decreased from 3.93 ± 0.50 g/dL to 3.59 ± 0.63 g/dL (p = 0.0002). Hemoglobin declined from 12.74 ± 2.61 g/dL to 10.93 ± 1.91 g/dL (p < 0.001). In contrast, WBC increased from 8.85 ± 2.88 × 10⁹/L to 10.92 ± 4.66 × 10⁹/L (p = 0.0083), and INR rose slightly from 1.05 ± 0.11 to 1.10 ± 0.11 (p = 0.0177). Serum potassium also demonstrated a modest but significant reduction from 4.45 ± 0.61 mmol/L to 4.30 ± 0.55 mmol/L (p = 0.0162). No significant differences were observed for sodium, aPTT, creatinine, BUN, BUN/Cr ratio, or glucose (all p > 0.05). Complications evaluation

Postoperative complications were observed in a notable proportion of patients. Table 3 presents postoperative complications classified according to the Clavien–Dindo classification system. Aspiration pneumonia occurred in 4/46 patients (8.9%), followed by re-surgery in 6 (13.0%), delirium in 5 (10.9%), and wound-healing complications in 5 (10.9%). Bleeding and AKI each affected 4 patients (8.7%). Refeeding syndrome occurred in 3 (6.5%), and fistula, flap necrosis, and VTE each in 2 patients (4.3%). In univariate logistic regression, none of the examined predictors of postoperative complications reached statistical significance. Cumulative smoking exposure (pack-years) demonstrated a trend toward higher odds of complications (OR 1.07, 95% CI 0.98–1.17, p = 0.128), while DM2 was also associated with increased, though imprecisely estimated, risk (OR 2.50, 95% CI 0.20–31.0, p = 0.476). Alcohol use showed a non-significant trend toward reduced complication risk (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.08–1.52, p = 0.159). In the multivariable model, the direction of these associations was preserved: pack-years remained positively associated with complications (OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.98–1.20, p = 0.122), while alcohol use retained a possible protective effect (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.03–1.27, p = 0.088). DM2 again suggested increased odds (OR 4.70, 95% CI 0.28–77.9, p = 0.280), although the confidence intervals were wide. None of these predictors reached the threshold for statistical significance (Table 4). Moreover, when tumor characteristics and reconstructive strategies were analyzed as potential predictors of postoperative complications, no statistically significant associations were observed. Neither primary tumor location (p = 0.471), T stage (p = 0.681), N stage (p = 0.270), nor histological subtype (p = 1.000) showed a meaningful relationship with complication rates. Similarly, the type of reconstructive flap employed did not significantly influence the occurrence of complications (p = 0.424) (Table 5). Nevertheless, significant associations were observed between postoperative laboratory values and complications. Patients with flap necrosis had lower potassium (3.90 vs. 4.31 mmol/L, p < 0.0001) and higher INR (1.20 vs. 1.09, p < 0.000001). AKI was associated with reduced potassium (3.94 vs. 4.33 mmol/L, p = 0.0004). Aspiration pneumonia occurred in patients with higher hemoglobin (12.16 vs. 10.55 g/dL, p = 0.0047) and lower WBC counts (7.40 vs. 12.02 × 10⁹/L, p < 0.0001). Delirium was linked to elevated WBC (13.69 vs. 10.66 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.0022) and lower glucose (103.8 vs. 113.8 mg/dL, p = 0.0296). Venous thromboembolism was associated with lower glucose (89.5 vs. 113.3 mg/dL, p < 0.00001). In refeeding syndrome, INR was reduced (1.00 vs. 1.10, p < 0.000001), while aPTT (35.4 vs. 31.2 s, p = 0.018) and creatinine (0.68 vs. 0.91 mg/dL, p = 0.013) were altered (Table 6).

Discussion

With the global rise in cancer incidence and steadily increasing life expectancy, the proportion of older adults among oncological patients is expanding [32]. This demographic shift presents unique challenges, as older patients frequently experience age-related multimorbidity, physiological decline, frailty, and, notably, unintentional weight loss [33], which reflects declining nutritional reserves and significantly complicates clinical decision-making and the selection of optimal treatment strategies in this vulnerable population.

The results of our study confirmed that the majority of patients in our cohort were underweight. While weight status is frequently examined in the context of cancer, advancing age itself has a profound impact on body composition and nutritional reserve [34]. Age-related processes such as sarcopenia, altered metabolism, and frailty are common in older adults and may reduce physiological reserve, potentially affecting tolerance to treatment and postoperative recovery [16, 35].

Assessment of laboratory parameters revealed significant differences between preoperative and postoperative values. The postoperative decline in serum albumin is consistent with the catabolic response to major surgery and hemodilution following perioperative fluid administration [17]. Surgical stress increases capillary permeability and promotes albumin redistribution and protein catabolism, while fluid resuscitation further contributes to dilutional hypoalbuminemia [19]. Low postoperative albumin may therefore reflect impaired nutritional reserve and has been associated with delayed wound healing, increased complications, and prolonged enteral feeding dependence [20]. Postoperative reductions in hemoglobin were expected following major oncologic surgery due to blood loss, hemodilution, and inflammation, with anemia impairing tissue oxygenation and recovery [21, 22]. Postoperative leukocytosis aligned with the physiological stress response, although marked elevations may indicate infectious complications such as pneumonia or wound infection [23]. Perioperative hyperglycemia, driven by catecholamine and cortisol release, was also observed and is known to impair immune function and wound healing [24, 25]. Finally, changes in postoperative aPTT values indicate a shift toward hypercoagulability after surgery, increasing the risk of venous thromboembolism in older head and neck cancer patients [26, 27].

Furthermore, our study revealed significant associations between postoperative laboratory changes and specific complications. Hypokalemia was strongly linked to both flap necrosis and AKI, which is consistent with the critical role of potassium in vascular tone, wound healing, and renal function [28]. Elevated INR in flap necrosis and altered coagulation parameters in refeeding syndrome (reduced INR, prolonged aPTT) highlight the interplay between coagulation imbalance and tissue viability [29]. Aspiration pneumonia occurred in patients with higher hemoglobin but paradoxically lower WBC counts, suggesting impaired inflammatory response in vulnerable individuals [30, 31]. In contrast, delirium was associated with leukocytosis and reduced glucose levels, reflecting the combined impact of systemic inflammation and metabolic instability [36]. Venous thromboembolism was significantly correlated with lower glucose, which may reflect stress-related metabolic changes in this subgroup [37]. Collectively, these findings underscore that laboratory monitoring provides not only markers of metabolic stress but also potential early warning indicators of major postoperative complications.

The feeding route assessment revealed that PEG placement was required in approximately one-third of patients, with the majority of dependence occurring among those with advanced T stage.The PEG placement rate of 30.4% observed in this elderly cohort appears comparable to, or modestly higher than, rates reported in mixed-age or younger head and neck cancer populations, where PEG placement has been reported in approximately 15–30% of patients, depending on tumor site, stage, and treatment modality. Although direct comparisons are limited by differences in study design and feeding protocols, older patients may be at increased risk of requiring PEG due to reduced physiological reserve, pre-existing swallowing impairment, and greater vulnerability to surgical stress. Our findings therefore support the concept that advanced age, particularly when combined with extensive disease, contributes to a higher likelihood of prolonged enteral feeding dependence and underscores the need for proactive nutritional planning in this population.These findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating that tumor burden is a major determinant of long-term feeding tube dependence in head and neck cancer [38, 39]. Our analysis confirmed that advanced T stage was strongly associated with PEG use, early dependence at three months, and persistent dependence at six months, while also reducing the likelihood of regaining oral intake at discharge. By contrast, neither tumor location nor nodal status were predictive, underscoring the predominant role of primary tumor extent in driving postoperative nutritional support needs. NGT use showed no significant associations with feeding outcomes or tumor characteristics, although there was a nonsignificant trend toward lower oral intake at discharge among NGT patients. These findings suggest that while PEG remains a reliable option for patients with advanced disease, NGT use may primarily reflect short-term supportive needs rather than long-term dependence. However, the association between PEG placement and reduced oral intake at discharge is clinically expected, as PEG was selectively used in patients with an anticipated need for enteral feeding beyond 3–4 weeks. This finding therefore reflects appropriate patient selection and disease severity rather than an adverse effect of PEG itself. In contrast, NGT use was not associated with long-term feeding outcomes, supporting its role as a short-term supportive measure, whereas PEG appropriately identifies patients with expected prolonged dysphagia, particularly those with advanced disease. Nevertheless, the use of standardized and validated nutritional screening tools—such as the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score or similar indices—may allow for a more objective and reproducible assessment of nutritional risk and better stratification of elderly head and neck cancer patients.

Nevertheless, this study is limited by its retrospective, single-institution design and modest sample size, which constrain statistical power and external validity. The exclusive inclusion of patients with oral cavity cancer further limits generalizability to other head and neck subsites, where swallowing outcomes and nutritional requirements may differ substantially. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts including diverse head and neck subsites. Nutritional status and dysphagia were not assessed using validated tools; preoperative nutritional evaluation was limited, and data on weight change, body composition, sarcopenia, and quality of life were not available. Dysphagia was assessed indirectly using NGT and PEG placement as surrogate measures, although enteral feeding decisions may also reflect factors such as surgical extent or aspiration risk rather than swallowing impairment alone. PEG placement was determined on a case-by-case basis rather than by a standardized protocol, introducing potential indication bias. Patients with advanced T stage disease were more likely to undergo extensive resections and therefore be selected for PEG, which may partly explain the observed association between tumor stage and PEG dependence. In addition, although advanced T stage emerged as the only statistically significant predictor, other clinically relevant factors—including reconstructive complexity, baseline swallowing function, and adjuvant therapy—may have been underpowered or incompletely captured. Finally, analyses examining associations between postoperative laboratory parameters and specific complications should be interpreted as exploratory. Multiple comparisons increase the risk of type I error, and these findings are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized nutritional, geriatric, and swallowing assessments and systematic complication grading are needed to better define predictors of postoperative feeding dependence and complications in elderly head and neck cancer patients.

The clinical implications of our findings are twofold. First, aggressive tumor resection and reconstruction can be performed in selected elderly patients, but the risks of nutritional dependence and complications remain substantial. Second, perioperative strategies should prioritize early nutritional optimization, metabolic monitoring, and geriatric co-management to mitigate risks. The integration of geriatric principles—including frailty assessment, individualized nutrition planning, and multidisciplinary perioperative care—appears essential to improving outcomes in this high-risk group.

Conclusion

Older adults undergoing major head and neck cancer surgery with microsurgical reconstruction experience a substantial burden of postoperative nutritional dependence and medical complications. Advanced T stage emerged as the strongest oncologic predictor of PEG requirement and prolonged enteral feeding dependence. This study also identified clinically relevant associations between postoperative laboratory parameters and complications: hypokalemia was linked to flap necrosis and acute kidney injury, coagulation disturbances to flap compromise and refeeding syndrome, aspiration pneumonia to higher hemoglobin but lower white blood cell counts, and delirium to leukocytosis and hypoglycemia. These findings underscore the multifactorial vulnerability of this population, in which surgical stress interacts with age-related physiological decline and metabolic instability. Proactive perioperative strategies—including early nutritional evaluation, frailty-informed care, and close metabolic monitoring—may help reduce complications, limit long-term feeding tube dependence, and improve postoperative recovery in this high-risk group.
