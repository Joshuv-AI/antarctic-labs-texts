Tracking structural variants in early breast cancer using a high-sensitivity ctDNA assay predicts relapse in the post-neoadjuvant setting: the multicenter ALIENOR trial
Authors: C. Blaye, F.-C. Bidard, L. Hue, K. Howarth, J.-Y. Pierga, F. Dalenc, W. Jacot, L. Djerroudi, W. Potonnier, V. Dumas, C. Lalet, M. Maréchal, M. Pulido, M. Brunet, L. Salabert, L. Venat, A. Maran-Gonzalez, L.H. Saal, C. Franchet, S. Birkeälv, G. MacGrogan, H. Bonnefoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentification of breast cancer patients at high risk of relapse after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is of high unmet clinical need. The multicenter ALIENOR study aimed to identify patients with molecular residual disease (MRD) using an ultrasensitive ctDNA assay during follow-up after NAC.Patients and methodsPatients whose tumor did not achieve pathologically documented complete response after NAC were enrolled into a 5-year liquid biopsy surveillance program. The primary objective was to assess relapse-free interval (RFI) in patients with or without circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) detection during adjuvant follow-up. Whole genome sequencing was performed on primary tumor samples, and personalized multiplex digital PCR assays were used to track structural variants (SVs) in ctDNA samples.ResultsEighty-three patients underwent successful SV-personalized fingerprint design (473 plasma samples). Median follow-up time from the date of surgery was 60.6 months (range 7.2-66.3 months), and 27 clinical recurrences (32.5%) were observed. ctDNA was detected in 34 patients. In 44.1% (15 of 34) of these patients, ctDNA was in the ultrasensitive range (<100 parts per million) at the earliest positive sample. Patients with ctDNA detection had a shorter RFI [hazard ratio (HR) 29.2, 95% confidence interval (CI) 6.9-124.0, P < 0.0001]. In multivariate analysis, ctDNA was the most significant prognostic factor for clinical recurrence (HR 69.7, 95% CI 13.3-365.1, P < 0.0001). The median lead times from ctDNA detection to clinical relapse were 13.9 months (range 0.3-60.6) in the entire cohort, 20.9 months (range 5.5-60.6) in estrogen receptor-positive/human epidermal growth receptor 2 (HER2)-negative subtype, 14.2 months (range 3.4-34.4) in HER2-positive subtype, and 4.3 months (range 0.3-19.0) in triple-negative subtype. Of 27 patients who experienced locoregional or distant relapse, 25 had detectable ctDNA (sensitivity 92.6%). Among 34 ctDNA-positive patients, 9 had not relapsed at last follow-up: 6 were positive only at the initial time point before adjuvant therapy, 1 had multiple positive samples without relapse at last follow-up, and 2 were positive at the last visit. The 5-year RFI of patients with a negative postsurgical plasma sample was 72.6% (95% CI 60.1% to 81.6%).ConclusionThis study supports the clinical validity of an ultrasensitive ctDNA assay for longitudinal surveillance of high-risk early breast cancer. ctDNA detection in the ultrasensitive range underscores the importance of high-sensitivity assays for MRD monitoring.

Introduction

Systemic adjuvant therapies, including endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, and trastuzumab, have significantly reduced the risk of relapse and death in clinical trials, and their adoption into routine practice has contributed to the decline of breast cancer mortality in many countries.1,2 Nevertheless, breast cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death among women in 112 countries around the world, including within the European Union.1,3

Recently, several clinical trials have reported reduced risk of relapse and/or death with novel systemic targeted therapies across the three major immunohistochemistry (IHC)-defined breast cancer subtypes: in estrogen receptor (ER)-positive and human epidermal growth receptor 2 (HER2)-negative tumors treated with abemaciclib and ribociclib4, 5, 6, 7; in HER2-positive tumors treated with trastuzumab emtansine8; and in triple-negative (TN) tumors treated with pembrolizumab.9 However, two important caveats must be considered. First, these therapies are associated with additional clinical and financial toxicities that should not be underestimated. Second, only a subset of patients derive benefit, while many undergo unnecessary treatment. This is because the probabilistic strategies that are used in these studies select eligible patients based on broad population-level risk evaluation criteria such as nodal positivity at diagnosis6 or incomplete pathologically documented response (pathological response) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC).8

Another strategy based on a patient-level risk assessment that takes into account the highly individualized response to treatment and can provide a longitudinal assessment of relapse risk during follow-up would be highly preferable. A good example is the detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) during follow-up to identify patients with molecular residual disease (MRD) who have a high risk of relapse. Several studies evaluating the clinical validity of serial ctDNA monitoring using first-generation assays based on whole-exome sequencing (WES) of the primary tumor10, 11, 12, 13 demonstrated high-sensitivity in detecting molecular relapse. The median time between ctDNA detection and clinical recurrence was ∼10.5 months in these studies. A second generation of assays with higher sensitivity has since been developed to detect lower burdens of MRD. These assays commonly use whole genome sequencing (WGS) of the primary tumor to identify tumor-specific single nucleotide variants (SNVs)14 or structural variants (SVs),15 which are subsequently tracked in plasma using ultrasensitive assays to detect MRD. Notably, in two clinical studies evaluating this second generation of assays, the median lead times before relapse were 15.6 and 13.9 months respectively,14,16 leaving time to plan and initiate new effective treatments.

In this manuscript, we report the results of ALIENOR, a multicenter clinical trial designed to evaluate an ultrasensitive ctDNA assay for MRD detection. Patients whose tumor did not achieve pathological complete response (pCR) after NAC were enrolled, and serial plasma samples were collected every 6 months for 5 years during follow-up. WGS of the primary tumor and a patient-specific ultrasensitive tumor-informed SV-based digital PCR (dPCR) assay was used to detect ctDNA. Our aims were to assess the clinical validity of the assay, lead time, and prognostic value for relapse-free interval (RFI).

Methods

Patients and samples

ALIENOR is a multicenter, prospective observational cohort study conducted at six French hospitals from 27 November 2017 to 1 April 2020. During the post-operative visit, patients were invited to participate in this study (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). Eligibility criteria included: aged ≥18 years; histologically confirmed breast cancer of any IHC subtype; large operable (cT2cN1, cT3cN0-1) or locally advanced (cT4cN0-3 or cT1-4cN2-3) tumors; no evidence of distant metastasis after positron emission tomography scan staging; patients should have received from six to eight cycles of NAC; and tumor did not achieve pCR after NAC (defined as ypT0/is ypN0). A total of 30 ml of blood was collected in five EDTA tubes, with 3.0-5.5 ml spun plasma (from one blood tube) used for ctDNA analysis at each time point; 10-63 days after surgery, then every 6 months for up to 5 years along with concomitant clinical examination. Samples were centrifuged twice, and the plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80°C. In a second step, samples were centrally collected in Bordeaux and then sent to SAGA Diagnostics (Lund, Sweden). In addition, a single buffy coat sample from any time point was taken and sent to SAGA Diagnostics. ctDNA results were not shared with the treating physicians or patients. Adjuvant treatment was prescribed according to each center’s policy. All patients provided written informed consent to participate in the study. This study was approved by the French national ethics committee (CPP Est II, Study Registration Number 2017-A00939-44). All data were de-identified. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03357120).

Histopathological assessment and primary tumor sample collection

ER and progesterone receptor were considered negative if <10% of the cells expressed the receptors. HER2 status was locally assessed on diagnostic biopsies using American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists guidelines. The residual cancer burden (RCB) score was locally assessed as previously described.17 Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor tissue blocks from surgical specimens were centrally assessed by one investigator (GMG). Tissue with ≥20% invasive tumor cells and at least 100 ng of DNA were evaluable. In case of <20% invasive tumor cells in the surgical specimens, material from the diagnostic biopsies samples were used. Either 5-μm tissue sections on slides or 1- 3-mm2 tissue cores were sent to SAGA Diagnostics.

Tumor WGS and SV-based ctDNA dPCR assay

Genomic DNA extraction and WGS from tumor samples were performed by SAGA Diagnostics as previously described.16 A development research use only approach was used to design tumor-informed personalized multiplex dPCR assays based on the WGS to track a patient-specific fingerprint comprising 4-8 SVs in plasma samples. All fingerprint SVs identified by WGS were orthogonally validated using dPCR against the tumor, and germline variants were filtered out using matched buffy coat samples, as previously described.16 Cell-free DNA was extracted from plasma, and each personalized fingerprint used for ctDNA analysis (SAGA Diagnostics). Details of the methodology and workflow are provided in Supplementary Data S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766. All ctDNA analysis were conducted blinded to clinical and pathological information. Due to patient privacy, the WGS data, which may contain personally identifiable genetic variation and disease-associated alleles, are not publicly available.

Objectives

The primary objective was to assess RFI in patients with ctDNA detected at any time during follow-up and in patients with no ctDNA detected. RFI was defined as the time from surgery to the first recurrence (locoregional invasive relapse and/or distant relapse) or breast cancer-related death.18 Samples were defined as ctDNA-positive if at least one SV was detected. Secondary objectives were (i) to assess RFI in patients with ctDNA detected in the first postsurgical sample and in patients with no ctDNA detected in this first sample; (ii) to assess overall survival (OS) in patients with ctDNA detected at any time during follow-up and in patients with no ctDNA detected; (iii) to determine the lead time, defined as the interval from first ctDNA detection to recurrence or breast cancer-related death; and (iv) to quantify the level of ctDNA in patients with a positive result at time of the first detection in the entire population and in the three IHC subtypes.

Statistical analysis

The study aimed to include 110 patients based on a 20% recurrence rate at 3 years, as reported in the literature.19,20 This sample size allowed the assessment of an 80% 3-year RFI with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 72% to 89%. An additional 10% sample size was considered to account for non-assessable patients. The sample size was determined based on precision and feasibility, with no formal power calculation. The assessable population comprised patients with the first postsurgical plasma sample available (taken between 10 days and 63 days after surgery), ≥20% invasive tumor cells, and at least 100 ng of DNA from the surgical specimen or from the biopsy taken at diagnosis and a buffy coat sample from any time point. The evaluable population for the primary endpoint included only patients with ctDNA successfully analyzed. Baseline sociodemographic, clinical, and tumor characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means, medians, and standard deviations for continuous variables.

Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival curves for RFI and OS, with differences between ctDNA-positive and ctDNA-negative groups assessed using the log-rank test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for RFI and OS. Multivariate Cox regression models were conducted to assess the prognostic impact of ctDNA on RFI and OS, while adjusting for clinically relevant factors, including clinical stage at diagnosis, molecular subtype, RCB class, and age at diagnosis. Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of patients with ctDNA detection before clinical relapse over the total number of patients with clinical relapse. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) software and the survminer package (version 0.4.9) in R (version 4.4.1) for data visualization.

Results

Patient cohort and assay design

A total of 211 patients with primary breast cancer were screened for eligibility after NAC and before breast cancer surgery. The study flow diagram is reported in Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766. After surgery, 120 patients whose tumor did not achieve a complete pathological response on the surgical specimen were included. One patient was not eligible due to past history of invasive cancer. Further patients were excluded due to <20% invasive tumor cells (n = 4), the first postsurgical plasma sample was missing (n = 6), a delay between surgery and the first plasma sample >63 days (n = 3), failure to collect the buffy coat (n = 9), or less than the minimum tumor DNA input according to predefined quality standards requirements (n = 6). From the remaining 91 patients, 1 failed library construction, 6 had fewer than 4 SVs validated during fingerprint generation, and 1 failed at ctDNA analysis. Thus, 83 patients of 91 with sufficient material (91% success rate) underwent successful SV-personalized panel design (median 8, range 4-8 SVs per patient) and ctDNA analysis and were assessable for the primary endpoint. When considering the entire trial population, including samples not available for analysis, ctDNA results were available for 83 of 120 patients (69%). They were followed with biannual blood sampling for ctDNA analysis, and 473 plasma samples underwent ctDNA testing.

The median age at diagnosis was 52 years (range 26-79), and all assessable patients were female. The three molecular IHC subtypes were represented: 43 with ER-positive and HER2-negative tumors (ER-positive/HER2-negative subtype), 21 with HER2-positive tumors (HER2-positive subtype), and 19 with TN tumors (TN subtype). All patients received 6-8 cycles of NAC including anthracyclines combined with cyclophosphamide followed by taxanes (Supplementary Data S2 and Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). Detailed information on adjuvant systemic treatments is provided in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766. All patients received postsurgical locoregional radiation therapy. As required by French law, data on race and ethnicity were not collected.

The median follow-up time from date of surgery was 60.6 months (range 7.2-66.3). At the time of data cut-off, 27 clinical recurrences (32.5%; distant only: 22 patients, locoregional only: 3 patients, distant and locoregional: 2 patients) were observed. Eleven deaths occurred (all these patients had previously relapsed).

We quantified ctDNA to determine the proportion of cases with ultrasensitive detection (defined as <100 ppm) at the first positive time point (34 positive samples), analyzed across the entire cohort and within the three subtypes, irrespective of whether or not patients later relapsed (Supplementary Figure S3 and Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). In 44.1% (15 of 34) of patients, the earliest positive postsurgical sample was in the ultrasensitive range, with levels <100 ppm (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). Patients with TN subtype had a higher level of ctDNA [median 666.5 ppm, interquartile range (IQR) 304.0-1188.0] than patients with HER2-positive and ER-positive/HER2-negative subtypes (median 94.8 ppm, IQR 15.0-386.0 and median 60.5 ppm, IQR 35.0-407.0, respectively).

We also quantified the level of ctDNA in patients with a positive result at any time during follow-up (65 positive samples) in the entire cohort and within the three subtypes (Supplementary Figure S4 and Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). Patients with TN subtype had a higher level of ctDNA (median 632.0 ppm, IQR 155.0-1188.0) than patients with HER2-positive and ER-positive/HER2-negative subtypes (median 268.0 ppm, IQR 32.0-971.0 and median 229.8 ppm, IQR 42.0-2382.0), but the IQRs were large.

Association between ctDNA detection and survival outcomes

Patients with a positive ctDNA result in any follow-up plasma sample had a shorter RFI (HR 29.2, 95% CI 6.9-124.0, P < 0.0001; Figure 2A and Table 2). We performed a univariate analysis with the following factors: clinical stage at diagnosis, molecular subtype, RCB class, age, ctDNA status in any follow-up sample, and ctDNA status in the first postsurgical sample. In univariate analysis, ctDNA was the only significant prognostic factor. Kaplan–Meier analysis of RFI and OS by RCB class is reported in Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766. We then performed a multivariate analysis including ctDNA status adjusted for well-established prognostic factors (clinical stage at diagnosis, molecular subtype, RCB class, and age) to further explore its prognostic value. In this model, ctDNA status was the most significant prognostic factor for clinical recurrence, independently of the other variables (HR 69.7, 95% CI 13.3-365.1, P < 0.0001; Table 2). RCB class was the only other significant prognostic factor, with RCB class II having a higher risk than class III (HR 2.5, 95% CI 0.4-14.7). In addition, RCB class III had a lower risk than class I (HR 0.5, 95% CI 0.1-3.0). We address this unusual finding in the discussion. As far as OS is concerned, 11 deaths were observed, all from the ctDNA-positive group (Supplementary Table S5 and Figure S6A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). Lead time to relapse

The median lead time from ctDNA detection to clinical relapse across the entire cohort was 13.9 months (range 0.3-60.6) (Table 1). ER-positive/HER2-negative patients had the longest median lead time of 20.9 months (range 5.5-60.6). In the HER2-positive subtype, the median lead time was 14.2 months (range 3.4-34.4), and six of nine patients with ctDNA detection presented with a lead time of >12 months (Figure 1B and Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). In the TN subtype, the median lead time was 4.3 months (range 0.3-19.0), and only one patient of six with ctDNA detection had a lead time of >12 months (Figure 1C and Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107766). As an exploratory analysis, we calculated the median lead times from first ctDNA detection ≥100 ppm compared with ctDNA detection inclusive of <100 ppm. The lead times were extended from 11.0 (range 0.3-32.3) to 13.9 (range 0.3-60.6) months when including detection in the ultrasensitive range. Importantly, in the entire cohort, seven recurrences were only detected <100 ppm, which would have been missed with less sensitive approaches. In addition, with ultrasensitive detection, the upper range was extended from 32.3 months to 60.6 months. Three patients initially had ctDNA detected at a level <100 ppm, which increased to >100 ppm at later timepoints. ctDNA sensitivity and specificity

ctDNA was detected in any follow-up sample in 25 of 27 patients who later developed a locoregional invasive or distant clinical relapse. Thus, using ctDNA detection as a prognostic factor for clinical relapse yields a sensitivity of 92.6% (25 of 27) for locoregional and distant relapses and 96% (24 of 25) for distant-only relapse. Two patients relapsed with no ctDNA detection (Table 1 and Figure 1A and C); one with an ER-positive/HER2-negative tumor presented with local relapse only (subject 74), and one with a TN tumor presented with brain-only metastases (subject 67). Three other patients with brain-only relapse were ctDNA-positive (subjects 27, 57, and 72).

ctDNA was not detected in any follow-up sample in 47 of 56 patients who did not relapse during follow-up (83.9% specificity). Of these 56 patients, nine had a ctDNA detection in one sample or more. Six out of these nine patients cleared on adjuvant therapy with all remaining time points ctDNA-negative over a follow-up period of 58.5-60.7 months (Table 3). The remaining three cases were positive at multiple or the last time point only; all were ER-positive/HER2-negative. Their respective times from the last positive sample to the last clinical visit were 12.0, 12.4, and 17.4 months (Table 3), which is less than the median lead time from ctDNA detection to clinical relapse in the ER-positive/HER2-negative subtype (20.9 months). Patients with ctDNA detected and no relapse

Of 34 patients with ctDNA detected in any follow-up sample, nine were still relapse-free at last follow-up (Table 3). Of these, six patients who were positive at the first postsurgical sample were ctDNA-negative in all subsequent time points. All six patients received adjuvant therapy: capecitabine in one patient with TN tumor, trastuzumab emtansine in one patient with HER2-positive tumor, and endocrine therapy in four patients with ER-positive and HER2-negative tumors (including three patients treated with ribociclib in the context of NATALEE study). One patient had multiple rising positive ctDNA at the last time points, and two patients were positive at the last visit time point only and are pending longer follow-up. These latter two patients were ER-positive/HER2-negative, and the intervals from the ctDNA-positive result to the last visit were 12 and 17 months, respectively. These intervals are shorter than the median lead time observed in our series in patients with ER-positive/HER2-negative subtype; hence, a longer follow-up would be needed to draw further conclusions.

Discussion

In this multicenter trial, we assessed the clinical validity of a tumor-informed SV-based dPCR assay to detect MRD during follow-up. First, we observed that ctDNA detection in any follow-up plasma sample is a strong prognostic factor of subsequent overt clinical relapse and survival. Second, at the time of first ctDNA detection, the level of ctDNA was very low (<100 ppm) in 44% of patients, requiring tools capable of ultrasensitive detection. Third, we observed a median lead time from the first ctDNA detection to clinical relapse of 13.9 months across the entire cohort, with observed lead times extending up to 60.6 months. Fourth, a negative postsurgical landmark ctDNA result was still associated with >25% risk of recurrence at 5 years, even though many patients received additional adjuvant systemic therapy. This finding emphasizes the importance of surveillance monitoring in breast cancer and suggests caution in using a single postsurgical ctDNA assessment to guide treatment de-escalation strategies. Of note, in this study, only patients whose tumor did not achieve a pCR were included.

The first-generation of tumor-informed assays used WES of the primary tumor to identify SNVs in patients with early breast cancer. Several clinical studies demonstrated the prognostic value of ctDNA detection using these assays during follow-up.10, 11, 12, 13 In these studies, the sensitivity of the assays in predicting locoregional and distant relapses was 88%, but the observed median lead times were generally <11 months.10,13 Thus, in an attempt to increase the median lead time, a second generation of tumor-informed assays, called ‘ultrasensitive’, were developed, often using WGS of the primary tumor to identify tumor-specific genomic alterations. The first study was performed using the ultrasensitive MAESTRO ctDNA assay in the TBCRC 030 trial. Samples were taken during NAC but not during follow-up to detect MRD,21 thus precluding the evaluation of the test for recurrence prediction in the adjuvant setting. Two other recently reported studies assessed the performance of ultrasensitive assays both during neoadjuvant treatment and during follow-up to detect MRD.14,16 We focus below on MRD detection results in the adjuvant setting. In the ChemoNEAR multicenter study and the TRACER study, 74 patients and 79 patients respectively with postsurgical serial ctDNA sampling were included14,16; for our study, 83 patients were evaluated.

In the ChemoNEAR and TRACER studies, ctDNA detection in any follow-up sample was a strong prognostic factor.14,16 In our study, ctDNA detection was associated with recurrence with HRs of 29.2 and 69.7 in univariate and multivariate analyses, respectively. RCB class was the only other independent prognostic factor; patients with RCB class II had a worse prognosis than patients with RCB class III. This result must be interpreted with caution because the number of patients in each RCB class is small. Moreover, this finding should be put in perspective with trials with very large number of patients and long follow-up, which repeatedly demonstrated that RCB class III patients have the worst prognosis.22,23

The sensitivity of assays used in the ChemoNEAR and TRACER trials14,16 and in our trial ranged from 89% to 100% across all breast cancer subtypes. In the ChemoNEAR study, the median follow-up was 30.8 months after surgery and 11 recurrences were observed; ctDNA was detected in all recurrences.14 In the TRACER study, the median follow-up was 39.6 months from day 1 of neoadjuvant treatment and 19 recurrences were observed; ctDNA was detected in all recurrences with the exception of two cases with a local relapse.16 In our study, the median follow-up from the date of surgery was 60.6 months and 27 recurrences were observed; ctDNA was detected in all recurrences with the exception of two cases, one with local relapse and one with brain-only metastases. The weakness of ctDNA assays to detect local relapse was reported in the TRACER study.16 The authors hypothesized that the disruption of draining lymph nodes after surgery or the development of new primary breast cancers with different genomic alterations may explain the absence of ctDNA detection. ctDNA negativity in patients with brain-only metastases was previously reported.13 This result is akin to previous observations that brain metastases are associated with a low ctDNA detection rate.24,25

In the ALIENOR study, the median lead time from ctDNA detection to clinical relapse across the entire cohort was 13.9 months, which is very similar to the median lead times observed in the ChemoNEAR and TRACER studies (15.6 months and 13.7 months, respectively).14,16 As an exploratory analysis, we assessed the median lead time in the three subtypes. In ER-positive/HER2-negative and HER2-positive subtypes, the median lead times were 20.9 months and 14.2 months, respectively. However, in TN subtype, the median lead time was only 4.3 months, which is a short period of time if one is considering development of an interventional trial. This result should be put in perspective with the C-TRACK TN trial, which assessed the clinical utility of ctDNA detection using a first-generation tumor-informed dPCR assay.26 A total of 45 patients with ctDNA detection entered the therapeutic phase and were randomly assigned 2:1 to pembrolizumab or observation. However, 72% (23 of 32) of patients randomly assigned to pembrolizumab already had metastases on radiological staging, meaning that the lead time was extremely short in this large proportion of patients when using this ctDNA assay. Thus, the same research group evaluated a more sensitive ctDNA assay using samples from 141 patients included in C-TRACK TN.27 They compared a tumor-informed personalized multimutation sequencing assay to a standard tumor-informed dPCR assay. The median lead time from ctDNA detection to relapse was short with both assays: 6.1 months and 3.9 months, respectively. If confirmed, the observation of short lead times in patients with TN subtype, whether using WES-based or WGS-based ultrasensitive assays, could have an impact on the development of future trials assessing MRD in TN subtype patients.

The specificity of this SV-based ultrasensitive assay is 83.9% (47 of 56), and nine patients had not relapsed at the last clinical follow-up. This observation requires two comments. First, among these patients, one had multiple positive samples, and two were positive at the last visit only. In this study, the lead time between a first ctDNA detection and a recurrence can be up to 60.6 months, and longer clinical follow-up is needed to accurately quantify specificity. Second, among these nine patients, the remaining six were positive at the first time point only and all received adjuvant therapy. One patient with TN tumor received capecitabine for 6 months, which is known to improve OS when given in patients whose tumor did not achieve a pCR after NAC.28 One patient with HER2-positive tumor was treated with trastuzumab emtansine in the KATHERINE study.8 The remaining four patients with ER-positive and HER2-negative tumors received adjuvant endocrine therapy, including three patients treated with ribociclib in the context of NATALEE study. Thus, we hypothesize that ctDNA had cleared in subsequent samples on adjuvant treatment in these six patients. A similar observation was made in the ChemoNEAR study: three patients had a single postsurgical ctDNA detection, and in two of these patients, ctDNA was cleared on adjuvant treatment.14

The potential strength of these two trials, which used second-generation ctDNA assays, is ctDNA relapse was detected roughly 3 months earlier than with the first-generation assays.14,16 The ALIENOR study, with a median lead time of 13.9 months, confirms the clinical validity of second-generation assays. However, the clinical validity of a tumor biomarker test is not synonymous with clinical utility. Although the definition of clinical utility may differ among clinicians, guidelines bodies, and payers, many experts will agree with the following definition: “the demonstration that the patient’s outcome is likely to be better because the tumor biomarker test result was used to select the clinical strategy compared with that of a patient whose care is managed without knowledge of that result.”29 In patients with stage II colon cancer, a trial has demonstrated the clinical utility of a ctDNA-based strategy as compared with standard of care management,30 but this is not yet the case in patients with early breast cancer. Prospective trials are ongoing with first-generation ctDNA assays, and our results should motivate the development of trials that use second-generation, ultrasensitive ctDNA assays.

This study has several limitations. First, the follow-up time was relatively short; thus, it is difficult to accurately quantify specificity. A second analysis with a longer follow-up is planned. Second, the results of the univariate and multivariate analyses assessing the prognostic value of ctDNA and other factors should be interpreted with caution because only 27 recurrences were observed, whereas many factors were included in the analyses (six in the univariate and five in the multivariate analyses). Thus, the results could be unstable. In the univariate analysis, this may explain that well-established factors such as clinical stage, RCB, and age were not prognostic. Similarly, in the multivariate analysis, the extremely unusual finding that RCB class II patients had a higher risk of relapse than RCB class III patients is very likely to be a chance finding due to the small number of events in each RCB class. Third, although 83 patients with evaluable ctDNA detection were analyzed overall, the number of patients by subtype is small. Studies focusing on specific subtypes with a greater number of patients should be performed. Fourth, technical limitations were not addressed in this retrospective analysis; time from tissue receipt to first result typically requires 2-3 weeks, which could have an impact on the conduct of a clinical prospective trial, although subsequent ctDNA results take only 1-2 days. Fifth, ctDNA results were not available in 37 of 120 patients (31%). However, in 64% of these cases, this was due to organizational issues (first postsurgical plasma sample missing in six patients, a delay between surgery and the first plasma sample >63 days in three patients, failure to collect the buffy coat in nine patients; total 18 patients) and could be avoided in the context of a prospective trial assessing the clinical utility of this test.

Conclusion

The results of this multicenter study support the clinical validity of ctDNA assessment with an ultrasensitive assay during follow-up in patients at high risk of relapse. The low level of ctDNA at time of the first detection highlights the importance of high-sensitivity assays for MRD detection. Studies assessing the clinical utility of second-generation ctDNA assays should be performed.
