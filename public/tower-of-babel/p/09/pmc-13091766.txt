Associations between disability and tobacco use in 31 low-income and middle-income countries: a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys
Authors: Zhengdong Zhong, Shengyue Qiu, Jiazhou Yu, Sara Rotenberg, Li Xiang, Hannah Kuper, Shanquan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundGlobally, individuals with disabilities may experience disproportionate tobacco use burden, though evidence comes predominantly from high-income settings. A substantial knowledge gap persists regarding tobacco consumption patterns among populations with disability in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This study aimed to examine associations between disability and tobacco use across diverse LMIC contexts.MethodsWe conducted secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from the sixth round of UNICEF-supported Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), spanning 2017–2023 across 31 LMICs. Participants were adults aged 18–49 years in the MICS Adult Functioning module with valid survey weights (weight >0); we excluded respondents with non-positive weights. For pooled meta-analyses, we applied a prespecified minimum sample-size criterion (<25 smokers with disability), which did not exclude any country. Disability status was measured using the validated Washington Group Short Set criteria (WG-SS). Self-reported tobacco use behaviours constituted primary outcomes. Modified Poisson regression models estimated risk ratios (RR) adjusted for age, marital status, residence, education level, number of children, and wealth status with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), stratified by country. Main analyses were stratified by sex to account for differential sampling frames between male and female respondents. Meta-analysis pooled country-specific estimates for an overall estimate.FindingsThe analytical sample comprised 637,123 adults aged 18–49 living in 31 LMICs, of whom 29,368 (4.61%) reported disability. Pooled meta-analysis revealed a significant positive association between smoking and disability in both sexes. The association was stronger among females (RR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.25–1.68) compared to males (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.14). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across countries, with the most pronounced disparities observed in Yemen (RR = 4.70, 95% CI: 2.6–8.51), Gambia (RR = 4.46, 95% CI: 1.0–19.9), and Chad (RR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.43–4.78) for women with disabilities. Analysis of tobacco product types revealed sex-specific patterns: females with disabilities showed significantly elevated risks for tobacco products (RR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.29–2.50), smokeless tobacco (RR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.25–2.17), mixed smoking products (RR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.09–2.10), and cigarettes (RR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.10–1.77), while males demonstrated more modest but significant associations for smokeless tobacco (RR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.05–1.91), mixed smoking products (RR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.12–1.43), tobacco products (RR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.38), and cigarettes (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12). In contrast, smoking frequency among current smokers showed no significant association with disability status in female (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.96–1.04) and male (RR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00).InterpretationThis multi-country analysis reveals heterogeneous associations between disability and tobacco use across LMICs, with particularly pronounced sex disparities demonstrating increased smoking risk among women with disabilities in some settings. These findings highlight marked sex and geographic variations in smoking disparities among people with disabilities in LMICs. They underscore the imperative for disability-inclusive, sex-responsive tobacco control strategies that acknowledge regional contextual variations. Such evidence-informed approaches are essential to achieve equitable tobacco control outcomes and prevent the perpetuation of health inequities among vulnerable populations. These conclusions should be interpreted in light of key limitations, including the cross-sectional design (precluding causal inference), self-reported tobacco use, and the WG-SS/MICS measurement scope (higher-severity cut-off, adults aged 18–49 years, household-based sampling), which may underestimate disability prevalence and limit generalisability.FundingThis study itself received no specific external grant from any funding agency.Keywords: Disability, Tobacco use, Low- and middle-income countries

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We systematically searched PubMed for studies published up to December 31, 2025, on tobacco use among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Our search strategy incorporated three conceptual domains: (1) disability and specific impairments, using terms including “Disabled Persons” [Mesh] OR “disability” OR “disabled” OR “functional limitation” OR “Washington Group” OR “Mobility Limitation” [Mesh] OR “Hearing Loss” [Mesh] OR “Blindness” [Mesh] OR “Vision Disorders” [Mesh] OR “Self-Help Devices” [Mesh] OR “Amputees” [Mesh] OR “Hearing Impaired Persons” [Mesh] OR “Visually Impaired Persons” [Mesh] OR “paraplegia” OR “quadriplegia”; (2) tobacco use, encompassing “Tobacco Use” [Mesh] OR “tobacco” OR “smoking” OR “cigarette” OR “smokeless tobacco” OR “nicotine”; and (3) LMICs context. While evidence from high-income countries consistently shows higher tobacco use among people with disabilities, research in LMICs remains limited and fragmented. Existing studies have primarily focused on individual countries such as Indonesia, Nigeria, and Rwanda, or specific regions like sub-Saharan Africa. The largest multi-country analysis to date was limited to nine LMICs mainly within sub-Saharan Africa. No previous study has provided a comprehensive, standardised analysis of this relationship across 31 LMICs, nor systematically examined sex-specific patterns and geographic heterogeneity in these associations.

Added value of this study

This study represents the largest analysis of tobacco use among people with disabilities across 31 low- and middle-income countries. Our analysis reveals two crucial findings: first, compared with people without disability, we observed substantial heterogeneity in the disability-tobacco use association by sex. The association was strong and significant among women, while it was attenuated among men. Second, we provide novel evidence that women and men with disabilities show elevated risks across all tobacco product types. The use of standardised Washington Group disability measures across all countries ensures unprecedented comparability.

Implications of all the available evidence

The available evidence, strengthened by our findings, indicates that compared to people without disability, people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries face a higher likelihood of tobacco use. This disparity is not uniform but varies by sex and geographic context, being more pronounced among women with disabilities in relative terms. This underscores the limitation of one-size-fits-all approaches to tobacco control in populations with disability. Policymakers and public health practitioners should develop context-specific, disability-inclusive tobacco control strategies that account for local socioeconomic factors and cultural norms, with particular attention to sex-specific vulnerabilities. Future research should investigate the underlying drivers of the pronounced disparities observed among women with disabilities in certain settings to inform targeted interventions. Interpretation of these implications should consider that the study is cross-sectional and relies on self-reported tobacco use, and that disability was defined using the Washington Group Short Set criteria within the Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys Adult Functioning module (adults aged 18–49 years in household samples), which may attenuate effect estimates and restrict generalisability to other disability definitions or older/non-household populations.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a significant global public health challenge, responsible for over eight million preventable deaths annually and 13 percent of deaths worldwide.1 The burden of tobacco use disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where over 80% of the world's smokers reside.2 These countries experience higher rates of tobacco-related morbidity and mortality due to weaker healthcare infrastructures and limited access to cessation programmes. Furthermore, tobacco consumption in LMICs exacerbates economic inequality and perpetuates cycles of poverty,3,4 due to the high costs of consumption.5 Patterns of tobacco use vary considerably by country, sex, and socioeconomic status.6

Disability, as conceptualized in the WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), is an umbrella term for impairments, activity limitations, and participation restrictions, reflecting the interaction between health conditions and contextual factors.7,8 People with disabilities make up 16% of the global population, but are frequently overlooked in health research,5,9,10 including in relation to tobacco consumption in LMICs. Globally, individuals with disabilities face significant health inequities, including limited healthcare access, higher poverty rates, and increased exposure to social determinants that may influence tobacco use behaviours. Existing research suggests that individuals with disabilities are more susceptible to smoking,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 potentially due to a combination of socio-economic challenges.17,18 Multi-country evidence from Europe shows this disparity is pervasive: smoking prevalence is 2%–15% higher among people with disabilities compared to the general population.19 However, most studies on the association between disability and smoking have primarily focused on high-income countries, for example, Canada,20 South Korea,21 the United States,15,22,23 the United Kingdom,12,24 Australia,25 European countries,19 etc. In contrast, there is limited research on the association between disability and tobacco use in LMICs, where tobacco use patterns may differ from those in high-income countries.26 According to prior research, financial constraints in LMICs may deter tobacco use among people with disabilities, suggesting that economic factors could influence smoking behaviours differently than in high-income settings. Moreover, studies on LMICs primarily focus on individual countries or limited regions, such as Indonesia,27 Nigeria,28 Rwanda,29 nine LMICs mainly in sub-Saharan Africa,6 making broader generalisations difficult. However, comprehensive cross-country studies on how disability relates to diverse forms of tobacco use in LMICs remain limited.

Existing research on the association between smoking and disability mainly relies on data from different databases or single-country datasets for comparison, and combined with inconsistent definitions of disability and tobacco use, making it challenging to compare research outcomes across countries and time periods. Previous studies have reported heterogenous results on the association between disability and smoking, with some suggesting similar smoking prevalence between individuals with and without disabilities,20 while others indicate either lower or higher smoking prevalence among people with disabilities across different domains.15,21 These discrepancies may stem from variations in study populations, methodologies, and socioeconomic contexts.

To address these gaps, this study analyzes data from 31 LMICs across five continents using the data from the UNICEF-supported Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), examining the associations between disability and various forms of tobacco use (including cigarettes, smoked tobacco products, smokeless tobacco products, and mixed tobacco products) across sexes, accounting for the differential sampling of men and women. The findings aim to expand current knowledge of tobacco use patterns among people with disabilities in LMICs, providing evidence to inform future public health interventions and disability-inclusive tobacco control policies.

Methods

Data source and study population

The datasets we used in this study are derived from the UNICEF-supported MICS. The MICS are cross-sectional, population-based surveys conducted by national statistical authorities in some LMICs. The MICS use a multi-stage probability sampling approach to sample clusters of households selected from a previous national sample frame (i.e. census, national survey) to generate nationally representative data on indicators for monitoring the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), health and development.30 All datasets were publicly available for permitted researchers on the MICS data repository of UNICEF website as of March 2023.31

Regarding sampling and respondents within each selected household, the women's questionnaire is administered to all eligible women; the men's questionnaire is administered in a randomly selected subsample of households, and the selection of male respondents depends on the country implementation (e.g., in some MICS surveys, one eligible man is randomly selected).30,32 Consequently, the male analytic sample is substantially smaller than the female sample. As a household survey, MICS is designed to represent the non-institutionalised population living in fixed households and does not cover individuals living in institutions or other group quarters.33

In this study, we used individual participant data from the sixth round of the MICS conducted from 2017 to 2023. The Washington Group Short Set on Functioning (WG-SS) was incorporated in this round of the survey for individual men and women through the adult functioning module, enabling the disaggregation of data according to disability status. In MICS6, the adult functioning module (WG-SS) is administered to adults aged 18–49 years30; therefore, our analytic sample was restricted to respondents in this age range. The surveys used standardised questionnaires and trained data collectors conducted interviews. We selected countries that had anonymised individual data containing information on the variables of interest.30 All analyses were stratified by sex and incorporated the corresponding individuals’ sampling weights.

This analysis covers 637,123 adults aged 18–49 years and focuses on the 31 countries where smoking and disability data were available. The countries included in this study were geographically diverse, with 6 in the East Asia Pacific, 5 in the Latin America and Caribbean, 3 in the Middle East and North Africa, 3 in the South Asia, and 14 in the sub-Saharan Africa, reflecting regions where UNICEF-supported surveys were conducted.

Outcome variables

There are six outcome variables among individuals, including smoking status (yes or no), smoking frequency (frequent, non-frequent), use of cigarettes (yes or no), use of tobacco products (yes or no), use of smokeless tobacco products (yes or no), and use of mixed products (yes or no).

Specifically, smoking status was defined as any use of cigarettes, tobacco products, or smokeless tobacco products during the last one month. Use of cigarettes referred to any use of smoked cigarettes during the last one month. Use of tobacco products referred to any use of smoked tobacco products during the last one month other than cigarettes, such as cigars, water pipe, cigarillos or pipe. Use of smokeless tobacco products referred to any use of smokeless tobacco products during the last one month, such as chewing tobacco, snuff, or dip. Use of mixed products was defined as use of ≥2 product categories (cigarettes, tobacco products, and smokeless tobacco products) during the last one month. Smoking frequency was measured as the number of days of use in the last one month (as collected in MICS); for interpretability, we categorized frequency as <10 days vs ≥10 days in the past month.

Exposure variables

Disability was assessed using the WG-SS through six domains in the adult functioning modules for adults aged 18−49 years.34 Individual participants respond to questions about their level of different functional difficulties associated with disability across six domains (mobility, hearing, vision, self-care, communication, cognition). Individual participants respond with the level of difficulty they experience from four response options (“no difficulty”, “some difficulty”, “a lot of difficulty”, “cannot do at all”; there is no “cannot do at all” option for the communication domain). Individual participants who reported “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in at least one domain were considered to have disability, in line with WG-SS guidance. The full WG-SS item wording and response options are provided in the Supplementary Appendix (Questionnaire items).

Covariables

Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics and household socioeconomic conditions: age (years), sex (male or female), marital status (currently married, formerly married, or never married), residence place (urban or rural), education level (pre-primary or none, primary, secondary, or higher secondary), number of children, and wealth status (top 40% vs bottom 60%). Sex was based on self-reported respondent sex recorded in MICS (female/male). Wealth status was constructed by UNICEF according to data on household characteristics (including ownership of goods, household, living situation, water and sanitation, and other personal assets) via principal components analysis.35,36 We used the harmonized wealth variable available in our analytic dataset and categorized wealth status as top 40% vs bottom 60%, corresponding to the top two wealth quintiles (Q4-Q5) vs the bottom three quintiles (Q1-Q3). This recategorization was adopted to improve the stability of estimates in analyses (particularly where outcome events were sparse within individual quintiles), and has been used in UNICEF/MICS-related analyses when quintile-specific sample sizes are small.37

Statistical analysis

Overall, our analytical strategy comprised three components: (i) country-specific survey-weighted regression analyses, (ii) stratified and pooled analysis of country estimates via meta-analysis, and (iii) robustness checks including sensitivity and stability analyses.

For all analytical components, variables were handled as follows: Outcomes, exposures and covariates were first described by country using summary statistics. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) and categorical variables were shown as frequencies (percentage).

First, to assess relative inequalities in each outcome between participants with and without disabilities, we fitted modified Poisson regression models to estimate risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) separately for each country. Analyses were stratified by sex to account for design differences in the sampling of men and women. We present two adjusted models: Model 1 (minimally adjusted) adjusted for age and place of residence, and Model 2 (fully adjusted) additionally adjusted for marital status, education level, number of children, and wealth status. All analyses incorporated the complex survey design and sampling weights.

Second, meta-analyses were conducted to pool country-specific estimates using random-effects models as the primary approach for both sexes, given the diversity of countries and the implausibility of a single common effect across settings.

In the main analysis, missing values for the study variables ranged from 0.05% (for marital status) to 2.9% (for education level). As the models were fitted in a progressive way, we implemented multiple imputation by chained equations, using all variables included in the models, to avoid biases due to missing data. In addition, as shown in Fig. 1, to reduce bias from small sample sizes, countries with fewer than 25 respondents with disabilities and smokers were removed from pooled estimates38; however, this criterion did not exclude any country in our dataset. All analyses incorporated the provided sampling weights and we excluded only records with non-positive weights (weight ≤0), which do not contribute to weighted population estimates and typically indicate observations outside the target interview population or otherwise not assigned a valid sampling weight. After applying these exclusions, the final analytical sample included 637,123 individuals, accounting for 93.6% of the total dataset (680,456 individuals). This approach helps improve the robustness of the analysis by reducing the impact of observations not contributing to weighted estimates.

Flowchart of sample inclusion.

To further assess robustness, we conducted two sensitivity analyses using survey-weighted estimates. First, we re-pooled country-specific associations using a fixed-effects meta-analysis (two-step approach). Second, as an alternative to the two-step framework, we performed a one-step pooled analysis by combining all countries (except for Nepal, which was excluded by Model 2 due to the lack of educational data.) and fitting survey-weighted Poisson regression models to estimate risk ratios for smoking outcomes.

Finally, to assess stability across different functional difficulties related to disability, we re-estimated the associations between disability and smoking across the six specific functional difficulty domains (cognition, vision, hearing, mobility, self-care, and communication) using one-step pooled survey-weighted Poisson regression models.

Results

Overall sample

The disability prevalence differed by sex: it was 5.08% (n = 24,668) among females and 3.10% (n = 4700) among males. The average age of individuals with disabilities was 35.1 (±9.4) years. Among individuals with disabilities, 31.2% were in the top 40% wealth quintiles, and 35.8% reside in urban areas (see Table 1 for details).

Smoking

A total of 31 countries reported data for females and 26 for males. We pooled survey-weighted country-specific estimates using random-effects meta-analysis. The pooled meta-analysis revealed significant positive associations between smoking and disability in both sexes. For females, disability was associated with a 45% increased risk of smoking (Model 2: RR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.25–1.68). All subsequent risk estimates are derived from this fully adjusted model (Model 2). For males, the association was more modest but still significant, showing a 8% increased risk (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.14). (see Table 3 for details).

Smoking frequency

Pooled meta-analysis revealed no significant association between smoking frequency and disability status in either sex. For females, the adjusted risk ratio was 1.00 (95% CI: 0.96–1.04, P = 0.87), indicating virtually identical smoking frequencies between women with and without disabilities. Similarly, for males, the adjusted risk ratio was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98–1.00, P = 0.19). The consistent lack of association across both sexes, coupled with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0% for females and males), suggests that disability status does not influence the frequency of smoking among current smokers.

Use of different types of products

Pooled meta-analyses revealed significant associations between disability and the use of various tobacco products, with consistent sex-specific patterns. (1) Among females, significant associations were identified across all tobacco product types. The strongest association was observed for tobacco products (RR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.29–2.50), followed by smokeless tobacco products (RR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.25–2.17) and mixed smoking products (RR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.09–2.10). Cigarette use showed the most modest association (RR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.10–1.77), though still statistically significant. (2) Among males, significant but generally more modest associations were identified across tobacco product types. The strongest association was observed for smokeless tobacco products (RR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.05–1.91), followed by mixed smoking products (RR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.12–1.43), and tobacco products (RR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.38). Cigarette use showed the most modest association (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12), though still statistically significant. The consistently stronger effect sizes observed among females across all product types highlight important sex disparities in tobacco use patterns among people with disabilities (see Table 3 for details).

Sensitivity and stability analyses

To assess the robustness, we conducted two sensitivity analyses, with results detailed in the Appendix (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). First, a two-step fixed-effects meta-analysis re-pooled country-specific estimates. This confirmed a significant positive association between disability and overall smoking in both sexes (females: RR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.23–1.37; males: RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10). Second, a one-step pooled analysis combining all countries (excluding Nepal in Model 2 for missing education data) in a single regression model also affirmed this association for overall smoking (females: RR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.12–1.28; males: RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12). Both approaches consistently support the core conclusion of a significant disability-smoking association.

We further examined the stability of the disability-smoking association across specific functional domains using one-step pooled Poisson models (Supplementary Table S8). Associations varied by domain. Among females, the strongest association was for cognition (RR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.33–1.64); hearing (RR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.13–1.62) and vision (RR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.21–1.50) were also significant. Among males, significant associations were confined to hearing (RR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.20–1.60) and vision (RR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.31). These results demonstrate that the positive association between disability and smoking is robust and consistently observed across key functional domains, thereby supporting our main findings.

Discussion

Our pooled meta-analyses provide robust evidence of significant associations between smoking and disabilities across multiple LMICs, revealing important public health implications for disability-inclusive tobacco control.

Consistent with previous studies,12,14,19,59 our findings across multiple LMICs indicated a higher likelihood of tobacco use among individuals with disabilities compared to those without disabilities. Our analysis extends this evidence by demonstrating that this pattern holds in pooled analyses consistently for both women and men with disabilities, with significant pooled associations observed for each sex. This broad sex consistency aligns with and is reinforced by multi-country evidence from Europe,19 further substantiating disability as a key social determinant of tobacco use. Importantly, we observed substantial geographic heterogeneity in the magnitude of disparities. This variation challenges the notion of a uniform global pattern. For instance, while pronounced and consistent disparities are reported in high-income countries such as the United States,13 evidence from other LMIC contexts can differ markedly. For example, a study of nine LMICs in sub-Saharan Africa reported limited difference in tobacco use between men with and without disabilities,6 which contrasts with the pooled significant associations we observed for males in our broader sample. The discrepancy may stem from differences in the countries studied or distinct socioeconomic and policy contexts. These substantial variations, shaped by unique socioeconomic and cultural settings, highlight the imperative for tailored tobacco control and disability inclusion policies.

We also observed notable sex differences in the association between disability and smoking across LMICs. Women with disabilities showed significant associations in smoking behaviours compared to women without disabilities in more countries than their male counterparts. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously because the women's analytic sample is generally larger than the men's in MICS, which may provide greater statistical power to detect associations. However, this pattern is particularly notable given evidence that smoking prevalence among the poorest women is twice that of the wealthiest women.60 The amplified disparities observed among women with disabilities likely reflect compounded disadvantages, including education.61,62 Moreover, tobacco control strategies in many LMICs have historically focused on men, and often fail to reach or address the specific needs of women,62 particularly those with disabilities. As a result, women with disabilities may be both under-protected by public health efforts and underserved by cessation programmes. These findings highlight the urgent need to adopt an intersectional approach to tobacco control, one that recognizes how sex and disability interact to produce unequal health risks. Notably, the overall disability–smoking association remained significant for both sexes under alternative modelling strategies, reinforcing the robustness of this sexed disparity.

In addition, our analysis revealed product-specific variations in smoking disparities. Across tobacco use outcomes, the disability-related disparities were consistently larger among females than males, and tended to be more pronounced for tobacco products, smokeless tobacco products, mixed smoking products than cigarette product. In addition, the elevated likelihood of using mixed products in some settings also warrants attention, as combined use may pose compounded health risks.63,64 These product-specific patterns highlight that tobacco control strategies need to move beyond cigarettes alone and address locally prevalent products and modes of use.

Beyond these main patterns, supplementary stability analyses revealed that disability–smoking association varied across functional difficulty domains and by sex. In the pooled one-step analyses, hearing difficulties and vision difficulties were consistently associated with higher smoking likelihood in both sexes, whereas cognitive difficulty showed a pronounced association among females but not males; mobility, self-care, and communication difficulties showed no clear associations. Similar findings have also been reported in related studies. This pattern aligns with international evidence indicating that tobacco use patterns differ across functional difficulty domains.15,21 These findings indicate possible variation in smoking patterns across functional domains. From a public health perspective, cessation interventions may need to be adapted not only for accessibility, but also to respond to barriers that differ across functional difficulty domains.

This analysis is the largest examining tobacco use by disability status in LMICs globally. This study standardises the definition of disability and smoking indicators, allowing for data comparability across 31 countries. The data are derived from nationally representative surveys, giving our research greater global relevance compared to previous studies.

However, this study has several limitations. (1) First, our data are cross-sectional, so we can identify associations between tobacco use and disability, not causal relationships and mechanistic pathways. (2) Second, information on tobacco use was self-reported, which may lead to recall or social desirability bias. Model 2 may underestimate the association if education, wealth, and marital status lie on the pathway from disability to tobacco use. (3) Third, this analysis was limited by the definition of disability used in the MICS (WG-SS), which covers six core domains and does not explicitly assess psychosocial functioning; and our analysis focused only on individuals who reported “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in at least one of the six functional domains. Therefore, disability prevalence may be underestimated. (4) Fourth, because the Adult Functioning module in MICS6 covers adults aged 18–49 years and MICS sampling frames exclude institutionalised or other group-quarter populations, our findings may not generalise to older adults or to non-household populations. Because the Adult Functioning module is designed for self-response (no proxy), some adults with severe communication/cognition difficulties may have been less likely to complete interviews, which may underestimate disability prevalence and bias estimates of tobacco use disparities. Therefore, the disability prevalence in our analytic sample (4.6%) is lower than the commonly cited global estimate (∼16%), which likely reflects the WG-SS higher-severity cut-off and the MICS design (adults aged 18–49 years and a household-based sampling frame). (5) Fifth, the men's dataset not only covers fewer countries but also exhibits a lower response rate (70–80%) compared to the women's dataset (90–95%), and the smaller male sample sizes may reduce statistical power; thus, cross-country differences in statistical significance by sex should be interpreted with caution. (6) Finally, surveys were pooled across 2017–2023; while only a minority of included surveys were conducted in 2020 (6/31), potential COVID-19 impacts on data collection and smoking behaviours cannot be fully ruled out.

This study provides multi-country evidence from LMICs revealing that adults with disabilities have a higher likelihood of smoking than those without disabilities, with significant variations by sex, functional difficulty types, and geographic setting. Our findings underscore that without targeted and inclusive approaches, global tobacco control efforts will fail to reach this vulnerable population, thereby exacerbating existing health disparities. We recommend action in three key areas: (1) developing accessible cessation materials tailored to different disability needs; (2) training healthcare professionals in disability-competent care; and (3) implementing sex-sensitive programmes alongside supportive policies that prioritize community-based reach. Integrating disability inclusion into routine tobacco control (particularly for non-cigarette products and for different functional difficulty domains at elevated risk) will be essential to translating research evidence into meaningful gains in health equity.
