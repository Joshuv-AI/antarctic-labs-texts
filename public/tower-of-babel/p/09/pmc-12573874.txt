Symptomatic anomalous right coronary artery in mother and daughter, a case report
Authors: Chadi Nahal, Nicholas Borden, Eric Johnson, Cristiano Faber
Published: 2025

Introduction

Anomalous origin of the right coronary artery (AORCA) is a rare congenital cardiac aberrancy. It is most frequently diagnosed incidentally during cardiac evaluation, and if symptomatic, can present with chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope [1].

Because there are few reported of cases of familial AORCA, there is limited knowledge of the inheritability of AORCA. We discuss two cases of a mother and daughter who were both diagnosed with symptomatic AORCA and treated surgically.

Case

A 43 year old female with symptomatic asthma at baseline and previous history of pulmonary embolism 10 years before arrival presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea and chest pain. She attributed her worsening dyspnea over 6 months to her asthma. Her pulmonary embolism was treated with rivaroxaban for 1 year and inferior vena cava filter placement, and she was no longer taking anticoagulation but the inferior vena cava filter remained in place. Echocardiogram showed adequate cardiac contractile function (ejection fraction 65%) without any valvular pathology or regional wall abnormalities, nor any evidence of pulmonary hypertension. Troponin levels were within normal limits. Electrocardiogram did not indicate any ischemic changes. Computed tomographic (CT) angiography evaluation revealed an anomalous right coronary artery with a slit-like ostium and intramural course, originating from the left coronary sinus and traveling between the aorta and pulmonary artery without evidence of pulmonary embolism (Fig. 1A and B). Bilateral lower extremity venous ultrasound was negative for deep venous thrombosis.

Pre-operative CT angiography shows the right (A) and left main (B) coronary arteries originating from the left coronary sinus, with the right coronary artery (red arrow) having a slit-like ostium and intramural course. At 3 months post-operative follow up, cardiac CT showed elimination of the intramural segment with adequate contrast perfusion in the right coronary artery in the axial (C) and curve planar reconstructed (D) views

Coronary angiography similarly revealed a right coronary artery originating from the left cusp as the dominant vessel (Fig. 2). There was no evidence of significant coronary artery disease. Given the patient’s history of asthma, pulmonary function tests were performed and revealed adequate respiratory function. Arterial blood analysis supported these findings. She underwent de-roofing of her aberrant right coronary artery (RCA) without complication. At 3 month follow-up, patient noted improvement in her dyspnea to baseline levels. Post-operative cardiac CT at 3 months imaging showed the arterial contrast through the de-roofed ostium of the right coronary artery with no intramural segment or obstruction (Fig. 1C and D).

Pre-operative left heart catheterization reveals a right coronary artery originating alongside the left main coronary artery from the same origin in the left cusp

Previously, the patients daughter at the age of 14 years presented with similar symptoms of progressive chest pain and shortness of breath over 3 months. On CT angiography, she had an AORCA originating on the anterolateral left aspect of the aorta 1 cm distal to the sinotubular junction and 3 cm distal to the aortic annulus (Fig. 3). She was treated surgically with neo-ostium creation and had improvement in symptoms.

Discussion

We present the case of a 43 year-old female who had symptomatic AORCA and a daughter with a similar diagnosis and presentation. To our knowledge, there are only 17 other published cases of anomalous coronary artery occurring among at least two relatives [2–4]. Our patient posed a unique diagnostic challenge given her known symptomatic asthma and history of pulmonary embolism. Given limited knowledge of inheritability of AORCA, common etiologies of dyspnea and chest pain were evaluated prior to treating for AORCA. Nevertheless, AORCA should be considered in the evaluation of patients with anginal symptoms and further research is warranted regarding the inheritability of AORCA.

Familial cases of AORCA are rare, and the reported occurrence of AORCA in patients’ families suggests a possible inheritability of this condition. There is limited knowledge regarding the genetic origin of AORCA and no screening guidelines are available. One study on albino rats revealed a polygenetic association with AORCA [4]. This genetic variability is supported in a pediatric case series that found a different genetic mutation in nearly each patient with AORCA [5]. Agrawal et al. described a polygenetic association with AORCA in families who underwent genetic testing. They also found a higher occurrence of cardiovascular history in family members of patients with AORCA at 21% compared with the general population at 6.5% [6]. However, two separate reports presented patients with AORCA whose identical twin siblings did not have AORCA, suggesting the pathogenesis of AORCA is related to an embryological insult rather than genetic anomaly [7, 8].

For AORCA, initial diagnostic testing includes transthoracic echocardiogram followed by coronary CT angiography or cardiac MRI for diagnostic confirmation [1]. Cardiac catheterization can be used for pre-operative evaluation and assessment of coronary artery disease, especially if non-invasive imaging is inconclusive. In asymptomatic patients, stress testing and/or nuclear perfusion scanning can evaluate for ischemia secondary to the coronary anomaly. Although our patient did not undergo stress testing, pulmonary function testing did not indicate that bronchoconstriction was contributing to her symptoms. The unremarkable pulmonary function testing and progressive exertional dyspnea indicated that AORCA was contributing to our patient’s symptoms, and stress testing was not pursued. Cardiac stress testing may have been a useful adjunct test if performed, and we recommend this diagnostic modality in patients with AORCA and vague clinical presentation. An anomalous RCA can course anterior to the pulmonary artery, posterior to the aorta, or between the great vessels. The origin can be from the left coronary sinus or above the commissure, with either a separate or shared ostium with the left main coronary artery. Ischemic insults for patients with AORCA have been attributed to ostial stenosis and/or compression between the great vessels [1]. Other variants coursing anterior the pulmonary artery or posterior to the aorta are postulated to be more likely benign with less risk of ischemic insult. In our patient, the slit-like origin and intramural course may have contributed to reduced blood flow and further justifies the de-roofing surgical approach. Theoretically, pulmonary hypertension may increase the diameter of the main pulmonary artery and compress the aberrant coronary artery, but our patient had no pulmonary hypertension on echocardiography. Given the inter-arterial course of the RCA in our patient but improvement in symptoms after surgical de-roofing, ostial stenosis most likely contributed to her exertional dyspnea.

Alternative management options for AORCA include pulmonary artery translocation, osteoplasty, ostial translocation, and bypass grafting [1]. Pulmonary artery translocation can be used for anomalous RCA with an interarterial course to relieve compression between the great vessels. In our patient, the slit-like narrowed ostium more likely contributed to her ischemia and pulmonary artery translocation would not have likely contributed to her post-operative improvement. Ostial translocation is applicable to AORCA with a short intramural course and sufficient margin between arterial ostia, unlike our patient. Osteoplasty has been successfully described in cases of AORCA with a short intramural course and even sharing a common ostium with the left coronary artery [9]. It requires transection of the aorta and can be combined with pulmonary arterial translocation, relieving compression while also reconstructing the origin to the appropriate anatomic location. Bypass grafting is applicable for patients with atherosclerotic disease contributing to ischemic symptoms.

Although there is no confirmatory evidence to suggest that AORCA is inherited, further reporting and genetic testing of patients with AORCA is warranted to evaluate whether family members are at increased risk of similar conditions. This would allow for an expedited and accurate diagnosis of symptomatic AORCA when patients present with symptoms and co-morbidities that obscure the classic anginal presentation. AORCA should be considered in the evaluation of patients with anginal symptoms, especially in individuals with a family history of AORCA.
