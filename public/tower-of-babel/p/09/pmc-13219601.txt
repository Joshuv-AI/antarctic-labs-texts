Parametric study of a new pressure relief borehole layout for high-stress roadway rockburst prevention
Authors: Fangzhou Lu, Linming Dou, Yanjiang Chai, Xiaotao Ma, Jinrong Cao, Haobing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the challenge of coordinating “pressure relief” and “support” in the control of rock bursts in high-stress roadways, this study proposes a novel method of pressure relief using long-distance large-diameter boreholes arranged parallel to the roadway (LDBP), elucidates its underlying rockburst control mechanism. Theoretical analysis is conducted to propose the borehole arrangement and principles for determining the optimal parameters of LDBP. Through numerical simulations, the advantages of LDBP in achieving “pressure relief–support balance” are validated. The influence of borehole parameters on the roadway stress and energy evolution is studied. The results show that, compared with conventional borehole schemes, LDBP reduces stress and energy concentration in the roadway shoulder and sidewall regions while exerting only a minor effect on the overall roadway strength. As boreholes are positioned farther from the roadway, the peak abutment stress shifts deeper into the coal mass and decreases accordingly. The optimal location for LDBP is the interface between the plastic and elastic zones, which in practice in situ can be identified reliably using the drilling-cuttings method. Coordinated design of borehole spacing and diameter is essential for effective destressing; forming a continuous plastic zone is ideal, whereas improperly selected parameters may induce a “double-peak” stress distribution between boreholes and increase peak stress.

Introduction

Rockburst, a dynamic failure of coal and rock masses triggered by the combined effect of static and dynamic loads1–4. With the continuous increase in mining depth, the high stress and intense disturbances caused by mining exacerbate the difficulty and severity of disaster prevention and control5, which significantly hinders the safety and efficient production of coal mines.

Over 90% of rock burst disasters occur in roadway tunnels. The implementation of pressure relief measures to reduce the surrounding stress in these tunnels is an effective way to prevent and control rock bursts and ensure the stability of tunnels6–8. Among these techniques, large-diameter borehole destressing is widely adopted in most mines as a localized mitigation method due to its operational convenience and strong practicality9. Furthermore, this technique also proves effective in controlling large deformations of roadways induced by high stress. The concept of large-diameter borehole destressing was first proposed by Soviet scholars in the 1970s, where mechanically drilled boreholes were used to create destressed zones, thereby transferring high stress and maintaining roadway stability10. Subsequent advancements in numerical simulation technology led to breakthroughs in theoretical research. For instance, Heal et al., utilizing FLAC3D simulations, validated the positive correlation between borehole diameter and the extent of the plastic zone. They emphasized that effective destressing requires a borehole spacing smaller than twice the plastic zone radius to ensure the connectivity of plastic zones around adjacent boreholes11. Driven by practical field requirements, research has entered a stage of parameter refinement and collaborative design of multiple boreholes, with numerous studies focusing on factors such as borehole diameter, borehole spacing, borehole length, borehole arrangement methods, and the development of fractures and plastic zones around boreholes12,13. Zhang et al. characterized the expansion of fractures around boreholes and compared the impact of different numbers and geometries of boreholes on pressure relief effectiveness, both in uniaxial compression and numerical simulations14. The core of borehole destressing effectiveness lies in the transfer of lateral abutment pressure peaks to deeper coal seams15–17 and the dissipation of strain energy around the tunnel, which has been thoroughly studied by Chen et al. They found that reducing borehole spacing, decreasing borehole diameter, and increasing borehole length can effectively enhance strain energy dissipation and improve pressure relief effectiveness18.

To ensure the safety of tunnels throughout their service life, destressing boreholes are typically drilled in sequence: advanced destressing boreholes at the heading face, regional destressing boreholes perpendicular to the rib executed promptly after roadway formation, and supplementary destressing conducted when the roadway enters a high-stress state. According to the “strong–weak–strong” structural control theory for roadways, a robust supporting structure must be formed in the shallow surrounding rock to provide stable support, while outside this structure, the coal/rock mass should be weakened through specific measures to create an energy-absorbing weak zone19,20. Although traditional large-diameter borehole destressing helps build a weak energy-absorbing structure in deep regions and transfers to deeper layers, it simultaneously leads to excessive fracture development in the shallow coal mass and broken surrounding rock. This compromises the ultimate pull-out capacity of rock bolts, thereby undermining the effectiveness of the bolt-cable support system21 and weakening the essential shallow “strong structure”. To address this synergy issue between destressing and support, a variable-diameter borehole destressing technique (e.g., small diameter in the shallow part, large diameter in the deep part) has been developed17,22,23. This method alleviates the damage to the tunnel surface integrity and support strength caused by high-density drilling while ensuring pressure relief effectiveness. However, although variable-diameter borehole pressure relief has partially alleviated the conflict between pressure relief and support, the small-diameter (approximately 75 mm) boreholes drilled in the shallow roadway surface within this scheme offer no benefit to roadway stability or rockburst prevention. Furthermore, drilling large-diameter boreholes in high-risk rock burst areas exposes field personnel to hazardous conditions12,24,25. By employing long-distance directional drilling techniques outside of these hazardous areas, large-diameter boreholes can be drilled to release tunnel stress, greatly reducing the safety risk to workers.

To create an effective plastic zone in stress concentration areas and promote stress transfer deeper into the coal mass, while simultaneously avoiding damage to the integrity of the shallow surrounding rock and the stability of the support system, this study proposes a new method for pressure relief using long-distance, large-diameter boreholes parallel to the tunnel wall based on directional drilling technology. Because constructing stable long boreholes in soft or severely fractured coal seams is extremely difficult, LDBP is mainly applicable to moderately hard to hard coal seams with high coal-mass integrity and good borehole wall stability26,27.

Based on the dynamic and static combined load inducing rock burst theory and the coordinated mechanism of the “strong–weak–strong” structural system, the underlying principle and feasibility of the proposed method are theoretically analyzed. A full-scale roadway model is established to numerically simulate and compare the differences in energy and stress distribution between boreholes drilled parallel and perpendicular to the roadway. A quantitative investigation is conducted into the influence of factors such as borehole spacing and layout position on the destressing effectiveness.

Rock burst prevention mechanism of LDBP based on dynamic and static combined load inducing rock burst theory

According to the dynamic and static combined load inducing rock burst theory, the instantaneous instability and failure of coal and rock masses, i.e., a rockburst, occurs when the combined static load (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{j}$$\end{document}) within the coal/rock mass surrounding the excavation and the dynamic load (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{d}$$\end{document}) generated by sources such as mining tremors exceeds the critical strength (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{bmin}$$\end{document}) of the coal/rock mass for bursting. This criterion can be expressed as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{j}+{\sigma }_{d}\ge {\sigma }_{bmin}$$\end{document}

Here, the static load constitutes the stress and energy foundation for coal/rock instability, while the dynamic load primarily acts as the trigger for the violent failure.

The static load on the coal/rock mass originates mainly from the overburden weight, residual stresses from geological structures, additional stresses induced by mining activities (e.g., abutment pressure and stress concentration caused by specific overlying strata structures), as well as other internal stresses such as gas pressure and pore water pressure. The dynamic load that triggers rockburst failure primarily refers to the mining dynamic response, transmitted in the form of elastic waves, which impinges on the coal/rock mass surrounding the excavation. These elastic waves exhibit significant attenuation during propagation through the coal/rock medium, with the attenuation index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document}) being positively correlated with the development of discontinuities such as joints and fractures within the mass28,29. The strength limit of the coal/rock mass for bursting depends on its mechanical properties and bursting propensity. High-strength pre-tensioned rock bolt support can enhance the integrity of the roadway surrounding rock by applying high radial stress, forming a bolaved structure with higher load-bearing capacity. This effectively increases the self-supporting capacity of the surrounding rock, thereby raising the strength limit for bursting. A support integrity coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta$$\end{document}) for the bursting strength limit is introduced to reflect the improvement in the critical bursting strength due to the pre-stress level and structural integrity of the bolaved rock mass. Based on the coordinated “strong–weak–strong” structural mechanism for pressure relief and rockburst prevention, after implementing pressure relief measures in roadways prone to rockburst, the surrounding rock forms a “strong–weak–strong” structural characteristic from the inside out. This consists of: a shallow “strong structure” maintained by high-strength support; a fractured and softened “weak structure” formed after destressing and fragmentation; and the deep, undisturbed virgin rock “strong structure” unaffected by mining.

Principles for designing parameters of pressure relief boreholes

According to the principle of LDBP control rock burst, the key to effective pressure relief is to use long-distance directional boreholes to construct an energy-absorbing weak structure in the lateral abutment pressure concentration zone while transferring high stress to deeper coal layers without damaging the shallow support structure. If the boreholes are placed too close to the roadway, the advantage of LDBP in preserving the shallow support structure is lost. Conversely, if the boreholes are located beyond the peak point of the abutment pressure, their effectiveness in stress transfer is significantly diminished. Moreover, improper coordination between borehole spacing and diameter may prevent the plastic zone around the borehole from being effectively connected, even increasing the peak stress. Therefore, rational layout parameters are crucial for ensuring the effectiveness of LDBP.

Regarding the energy transfer effect of borehole pressure relief on the tunnel’s shoulder and sidewalls, when the borehole diameter is 0.2 m, the energy concentration zones at the shoulder and sidewalls (with elastic energy density greater than 4100 kJ/m3) shift deeper into the coal body. After the shift, the range of the deep energy concentration zone accounts for approximately 87.20% of the remaining energy concentration zone at the shoulder, meaning nearly half of the energy is transferred to the deeper coal layers. When the borehole diameter is 0.1 m, the energy transfer effect of the borehole pressure relief is not fully realized, and the energy remains concentrated at the shoulder and sidewalls, with the range of the remaining energy concentration zone increasing by about 68.35% compared to the 0.2 m diameter borehole. When the borehole diameter increases to 0.4 m, the energy transfer effect of the borehole pressure relief significantly increases. For the shallow low-energy zone in the tunnel (with a threshold of 500 kJ/m3, corresponding to the anchor support zone), after pressure relief using boreholes with diameters of 0.1 m, 0.2 m, and 0.3 m, the range of the low-energy zone increases by approximately 0.67%, 3.81%, and 7.98%, respectively, compared to before pressure relief. However, the range of the shallow low-energy zone is less than 10% in all cases, significantly smaller than the 39.54% increase seen with traditional large-diameter borehole pressure relief, indicating that the impact on the integrity of the shallow coal wall and the support structure is minimal. Furthermore, considering that the pressure relief range increases with borehole diameter, it is possible to place the boreholes deeper into the coal wall, further reducing the impact on the shallow coal layers. Overall, the effect of increasing the borehole diameter on the integrity of the tunnel’s shallow coal wall and support structure can be considered negligible.

Discussion

This study systematically explores the rock burst prevention mechanism and effectiveness of LDBP through a combination of theoretical analysis, numerical simulations, and field verification. To fully utilize the engineering advantages of the parallel borehole arrangement in protecting the integrity of the shallow “strong structural support” and constructing the deep “energy-absorbing weak structure”, as well as transferring high stress, the borehole construction position must be reasonably designed. The simulation results show that directional long boreholes should be arranged at the boundary between the tunnel’s plastic and elastic zones. If the boreholes are placed within the plastic zone, they will further release the stress and capacity of the tunnel’s support pressure relief zone, potentially affecting the strength of the support structure. Additionally, Eq. (5) theoretically determines the borehole placement range. However, in field applications, the stress field distribution around the tunnel is influenced by various factors, including geological conditions, working face arrangement, and overlying rock structure, making it difficult to determine the optimal borehole location.

The drilling cuttings method is a reliable technique for assessing the impact hazard zone in the field. This method assesses the rock burst risk based on the coal powder and dynamic effects discharged from the borehole during drilling. The amount of coal powder discharged during the drilling process is positively correlated with the stress in the coal and rock body, so the amount of coal powder can reflect the distribution of lateral abutment pressure in the tunnel. By combining the principles for determining borehole placement, this method can guide the optimal design of borehole locations. Vertical boreholes with a diameter of 42 mm are drilled, and the amount of coal powder per meter drilled is recorded at depths of 20–30 m. Pressure relief through LDBP effectively reduces the energy peak in high-stress areas, such as the tunnel shoulder, from above 5000 kJ/m3 to below 4700 kJ/m3. It also shifts the stress peak from 8.9 to 13.1 m deep into the coal body, demonstrating its strong ability for stress transfer and energy dissipation. However, an increase in stress peak occurs during the transfer process (when the borehole is located 6.5 m from the coal wall, the peak stress increases to 33.82 MPa, a 4.48% increase compared to the pre-relief condition), which is contrary to the goal of reducing stress peaks. The peak stress after transfer results from the superposition of the pre-relief stress at that location and the transferred stress, which causes the peak stress to decrease with an increase in the borehole distance from the tunnel (from 35.40 MPa at 4.5 m to 33.82 MPa at 6.5 m). Therefore, the increase in peak stress after the transfer is fundamentally due to the insufficient number of boreholes, leading to a limited distance of stress peak displacement. By increasing the number of boreholes, the pressure relief range can be expanded, allowing for significant reduction in the peak stress while still transferring the stress peak. As seen in the data curve from the newly added scheme in Fig. 6, when the borehole’s influence range reaches 20 m, the peak stress position shifts from 7.0 to 18.0 m from the tunnel, while the peak stress decreases from 32.37 to 29.94 MPa, a reduction of approximately 8.12%. The results from this supplementary scheme resolve the previous contradiction where the peak stress increased after LDBP implementation, and further validate the effectiveness of the pressure relief method.

A new method for pressure relief based on directional drilling technology using long-distance large-diameter boreholes parallel to the roadway is proposed.
