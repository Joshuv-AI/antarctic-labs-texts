Performance of disk diffusion and MIC gradient tests in tigecycline susceptibility testing of enterococci: a Nordic multicentre study
Authors: Ingeborg Mathiesen, Bjørg Christina Haldorsen, Lars Småbrekke, Erika Matuschek, Frode Skjold, Barbara Juliane Holzknecht, Kristján Orri Helgason, Heikki Ilmavirta, Gunnar Kahlmeter, Arnfinn Sundsfjord, Kristin Hegstad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEvaluating the performance of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) disk diffusion (DD) and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) gradient methods for tigecycline susceptibility testing of enterococci using broth microdilution (BMD) as the reference method.MethodsAll 83 Nordic clinical microbiology laboratories were invited to test a blinded collection of diverse whole genome sequenced Enterococcus faecalis (n = 10) and Enterococcus faecium (n = 10) strains using the EUCAST DD and gradient MIC methods (Etest or MTS). Results were compared to reference BMD MICs and genotype (presence/absence of known tigecycline resistance determinants). Categorical agreement (CA), essential agreement (EA), and bias were calculated according to international standards.ResultsForty-four laboratories contributed to 880 EUCAST DD and 800 MIC gradient test observations. The EUCAST DD method demonstrated a CA of 97.3% when compared to reference MIC and 93.0% when compared to genotype. Gradient tests achieved an overall EA of 95.9% (Etest: 96.3%; MTS: 95.5%) and a CA of 96.3% when compared to reference MICs and 96.5% when compared to genotype. Both Etest and MTS exhibited acceptable bias (Etest: +21.8%; MTS: -19.1%).ConclusionThe EUCAST DD method and gradient tests demonstrated acceptable performance in accordance with international standards. Errors were mainly related to borderline strains near the tigecycline breakpoint. Further research is required on tigecycline-resistant enterococci and the development of improved AST methods for categorisation of borderline strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10096-026-05557-7.Keywords: Enterococcus, Tigecycline, EUCAST, Disk diffusion, Gradient test, Broth microdilution

Introduction

Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium are important human pathogens causing urinary tract, intra-abdominal, and bloodstream infections, including endocarditis [1]. They pose a challenge to antimicrobial treatment due to their intrinsic antimicrobial resistance (AMR), remarkable ability to acquire resistance genes, and genetic adaptability, especially in healthcare settings [2]. While clinical isolates of E. faecalis typically demonstrate susceptibility to ampicillin, hospital-acquired E. faecium is associated with a high prevalence of resistance to ampicillin and fluoroquinolones, as well as increasing rates of vancomycin resistance [3]. The increasing multidrug resistance, including vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), drive the use of antibiotics such as daptomycin, linezolid, and tigecycline. However, acquired resistance to these drugs is increasingly reported in enterococci [4].

Tigecycline is a broad-spectrum, bacteriostatic, long-acting third-generation tetracycline (glycylcycline), approved for the treatment of intra-abdominal and skin/soft tissue infections caused by Gram-positive bacteria where other alternative antibiotics are unsuitable [5–7]. It inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30 S ribosomal subunit [6]. Globally, tigecycline resistance occurs in approximately 1% of clinical E. faecium and 0.3% of E. faecalis strains, with a higher prevalence in Europe at 3.9% and 0.4%, respectively [8]. Tigecycline as part of a combination treatment is considered a potential salvage therapy for VRE bacteraemia refractory to daptomycin and/or linezolid therapy, but clinical data are limited [7]. However, a recent retrospective study on refractory VRE infections in immunosuppressed patients showed clearance of bacteraemia on tigecycline therapy of 16/19 cases [9].

Acquired tigecycline resistance involves various mechanisms, including mutations in intrinsic genes selected during tigecycline exposure [10]. Reduced susceptibility to tigecycline in Enterococcus spp. primarily involves alterations in the ribosomal S10 protein, encoded by rpsJ, and overexpression or mutations in tet genes encoding ribosomal protection proteins such as Tet(M) or the efflux pump protein Tet(L) [11, 12]. The genetic mechanisms underlying tigecycline resistance have significant knowledge gaps, making it important not to rely solely on genotypic methods for antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) [13].

Recent findings from the Norwegian Centre for Detection of Antimicrobial Resistance (K-res) highlight the challenges of detecting tigecycline resistance in enterococci. In the Norwegian Surveillance System for Antibiotic Resistance in Microbes (NORM) 2019 and 2020 reports, a high proportion of clinical E. faecium and E. faecalis strains were reported as tigecycline-resistant by disk diffusion (DD) AST. However, upon reanalysis at K-res, all tested strains were found to be susceptible to tigecycline by broth microdilution (BMD). The discrepancies were attributed to challenges in reading inhibition zones for enterococci. Uneven bacterial growth frequently resulted in zones appearing smaller than their actual size [14]. These observations highlight the critical importance of standardised and precise susceptibility testing for tigecycline.

This multicentre study aimed to evaluate the performance of phenotypic methods for tigecycline susceptibility testing in enterococci using a blinded, well-characterised, genetically diverse collection of tigecycline-resistant and susceptible enterococci. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) DD method and gradient tests were evaluated using the broth microdilution (BMD) method as the reference.

Materials and methods

Study design

All Nordic clinical microbiology laboratories (Denmark, n = 11; Finland, n = 24; Iceland, n = 1; Norway, n = 21; and Sweden, n = 26) were invited to participate in the study. Each participating laboratory received a blinded strain collection (n = 20) to (i) identify the bacterial species and document the identification method used. Furthermore, they were asked to perform AST of each strain using (ii) the DD method as recommended by EUCAST and (iii) tigecycline MIC determination either by BMD or a gradient test as well as (iv) using a semiautomated AST method, if routinely used in the laboratory. E. faecalis ATCC 29212 was the quality control (QC)-strain in all AST methods. Laboratories were instructed to report their results, along with the manufacturers and lot numbers of the AST material used. All analyses were to be performed as part of routine diagnostic workflow and genotypic confirmation was not permitted. Upon submission of data, laboratories received the corresponding reference analysis results.

Results were interpreted according to EUCAST Breakpoint Tables (version 16.0), with the following breakpoints: MIC S ≤ 0.5; R > 0.5 mg/L and zone diameter S ≥ 20; R < 20 mm for DD (15 µg tigecycline) [15]. The DD and MIC gradient tests were compared to the BMD. Performance criteria were assessed in accordance with ISO 20776-2:2021 and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing breakpoints [16, 17]. Focusing on reproducibility, accuracy, and precision, the following metrics were calculated: essential agreement (EA), categorical agreements (CA), and bias. As per ISO-20776-2, acceptable performance requires EA ≥ 90%, and bias within the interval − 30% to + 30% [16]. Similarly, Cumitech 31 A, defines acceptable AST performance as EA and CA ≥ 90% [18]. Very major discrepancies (VMD: resistant strains reported as susceptible) and major discrepancies (MD: susceptible strains reported as resistant) were also observed. However, these discrepancies were not commented further, as they are context-dependent metrics that are heavily influenced by the composition of the strain collection, particularly the inclusion of strains with borderline MIC values.

Strain collection

The strain collection consisted of E. faecalis (n = 10) and E. faecium (n = 10), including three strains with genetically defined resistance mechanisms against tigecycline (Table 1). All strains were from human screening or clinical samples, collected at K-res since 2012. They were identified by MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, Massachusetts, United States) and characterised by whole genome sequencing performed by an Illumina MiSeq platform using Nextera library construction on 250-bp paired-end runs according to standard protocols (Illumina, San Diego, California, United States). Reads were trimmed based on their quality scores, and adapters were removed with Trimmomatic-0.39 in paired-end mode [19]. Reads were error corrected and assembled with Shovill 1.1.0 (with a minimum contig length of 200 bp) [20]. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was determined using the pubMLST database [21]. Core genome MLST (cgMLST) and cluster type (CT) analysis were performed using SeqSphere software v8.5.1 (Ridom GmbH, Münster, Germany), E. faecium cgMLST or E. faecalis cgMLST [22–25]. Tigecycline resistance mechanisms were confirmed using BLAST [26].

Determining tigecycline reference MICs

Tigecycline reference MICs were determined by K-res and the EUCAST Development Laboratory (EDL) using BMD EUENCF, EUVENC, and custom-made plates (Sensititre, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). For tigecycline-susceptible strains and strain 20, which showed high-level resistance, reference MICs were set as the median of three observations. However, determining the reference MICs for strains 6 and 19, with known tigecycline resistance mechanisms and borderline MICs, posed a challenge. Ten to fifteen independent BMD MIC measurements were performed for these isolates. For strain 6, MIC values were evenly distributed around the breakpoint (median 0.5–1 mg/L; range 0.25–1 mg/L). The reference MIC for this strain was set to 0.5-1 mg/L and was classified as resistant (MIC > 0.5) based on the most conservative interpretation. For strain 19 ten of fifteen measurements showed an MIC of 0.5 mg/L. Thus, strain 19 reference MIC was set to 0.5 mg/L (median 0.5 mg/L; range 0.5–1 mg/L) which classify as susceptible although the strain was genotypically resistant. These decisions, while necessary for standardisation, are acknowledged as a limitation due to the borderline nature of the MIC-values.

Since strains 6 and 19 showed discrepancies between their phenotypic susceptibility and the presence of known resistance mechanisms, two definitions of tigecycline resistance were used: (1) phenotypic resistance based on reference MICs (S/R), and (2) genotypic resistance based on confirmed tigecycline resistance mechanisms. For consistency in calculating EA, CA, and bias, all strains assigned an MIC ≥ 2 mg/L by the laboratories were categorised as > 2 mg/L.

Quality control

The tigecycline-susceptible QC strain (E. faecalis ATCC 29212) results were evaluated according to the EUCAST QC Table [27].

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel was used to create pivot tables and graphs for the organisation and visualisation of data. The figures were converted to eps format using Inkscape. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, United States). Due to the small sample sizes and distribution of data, differences between categorical variables were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Laboratory participation and data submission

Forty-six laboratories participated in the study. One laboratory failed to provide the mandatory QC data, and another submitted QC data only for gradient test. Data from these two laboratories were excluded, leaving 44 laboratories (52% of 83 invited) included in the final analysis: Denmark (n = 5), Finland (n = 7), Iceland (n = 1), Norway (n = 16), and Sweden (n = 15). The laboratories provided data on bacterial identification (n = 44), EUCAST DD (n = 44), MIC gradient test (n = 40), BMD (n = 5), and semi-automated AST (n = 4). Data for BMD and semi-automated AST were excluded due to the limited sample size. Manufacturers for DD were Oxoid (n = 37; Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK), MAST (n = 3; MAST Group, Bootle, UK), ROSCO (n = 2; ROSCO Diagnostica, Taastrup, Denmark), and BD (n = 2; Becton Dickinson, NY, United States). Tigecycline MIC determinations were performed using gradient tests; Etest (n = 19; bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) and MIC Test strip (MTS) (n = 21; Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy).

Quality control

All laboratories (n = 44) reported tigecycline zone diameters in agreement within the accepted range for the QC strain using the EUCAST DD method (20–26 mm). Fifteen laboratories (34%) reported inhibition zones on target (23 mm) (Fig. S1). Forty laboratories provided QC data using MIC gradient tests, all within the accepted range for the QC strain (0.03–0.125 mg/L). Twenty-five laboratories (63%) reported MIC values on target (0.06 mg/L) (Fig. S2).

EUCAST DD method performance

Forty-four laboratories reported 880 observations. Stack column and heat map illustrations of DD zone diameter distributions compared with the reference BMD MIC are presented in Figs. 1 and 2, respectively. For strains with a tigecycline MIC of ≤ 0.25 mg/L and no known tigecycline resistance mechanism, all laboratories reported inhibition zone diameters above the susceptibility breakpoint, except for two observations by different laboratories. When outliers were excluded, the individual inhibition zone diameter ranges for each strain varied between 5 and 10 mm (Fig. 2), which is deemed acceptable. For strain 20 with a tigecycline MIC of > 2 mg/L and known tigecycline resistance mechanisms, all laboratories reported tigecycline zone diameters below the susceptibility breakpoint. The two E. faecium strains (6 and 19), with known tigecycline resistance mechanisms and borderline MIC values (MICs of 0.5–1 mg/L and 0.5 mg/L, respectively), were frequently misclassified. Yet, when compared to categorisation by reference BMD MICs, the CA was 97.3%, whereas compared to categorisation based on genotypic resistance showed a CA of 93.0% (Table 2). VMDs and MDs were solely related to the borderline MIC strains 6 and 19 except for two outlier observations for strains 5 and 18. A VMD rate of 6.8% was reached when using genotypic definition of resistance based on confirmed mechanisms.

Forty laboratories reported 800 observations comprising Etest (n = 380) and MTS (n = 420). For strains with tigecycline MIC ≤ 0.25 mg/L and no known tigecycline resistance mechanism, all laboratories reported gradient MICs below the susceptibility breakpoint. For strain 20, all laboratories reported gradient MICs above the EUCAST breakpoint (> 0.5 mg/L). Nine laboratories reported the tigecycline-resistant strain 6 as susceptible (MIC = 0.5 mg/L); Etest (n = 2) and MTS (n = 7). Twenty-one laboratories reported the tigecycline-susceptible strain 19 as resistant, with most of the observations reporting a MIC of 1 mg/L (n = 17).

The EA and CA for the MIC gradient test compared to reference BMD MICs are shown in Table 2. There was no statistically significant difference between Etest and MTS in the distribution of phenotypic (p = 0.1086) or genotypic errors (p > 0.9999). The EA of the two different gradient tests compared to the BMD reference MIC was 96.3% for Etest and 95.5% for MTS. However, the bias differed between Etest (+21.8%) and MTS (-19.1%).

Discussion

Forty-four laboratories successfully participated. The disk diffusion and MIC gradient tests demonstrated reliable performance in detecting tigecycline-susceptible enterococci. However, two strains, 6 and 19, with known tigecycline resistance mechanisms and borderline susceptibility values posed challenges. All laboratories (n = 44) reported results on target or within the acceptable QC ranges for E. faecalis ATCC 29212 for both the DD and gradient test methods, confirming reliability of the testing procedures.

Notably, in the initial planning phase of the study, strains (6, 19, and 20) with known tigecycline resistance determinants were classified as resistant both genetically and phenotypically. During the study, EUCAST revised the clinical breakpoints for tigecycline-resistant enterococci. The MIC breakpoint was raised one dilution step from 0.25 mg/L to 0.5 mg/L, and the zone diameter breakpoint was harmonized to 20 mm for all Enterococcus spp. (previously 22 mm for E. faecium and 20 mm for E. faecalis). The breakpoint revision (effective for version 15.0, 01-01-2025) was based on reassessing wild type MIC and zone diameter distributions for enterococcal species and weak clinical data indicating a favourable response of wild type enterococci to tigecycline. EUCAST decided to base the clinical breakpoint on the ECOFF since data on infections with non-wild type isolates was lacking. Whether or not non-wild type enterococci will respond to tigecycline therapy is unknown.

The DD method and MIC gradient tests demonstrated reliable performance in detecting tigecycline-susceptible enterococci and the tigecycline-resistant enterococcal strain (strain 20), which exhibited a tigecycline MIC > 2 mg/L and known resistance mechanisms. Overall, the EUCAST DD achieved an acceptable CA ≥ 93% when compared to the reference MIC and genotype. The gradient tests demonstrated acceptable EAs when compared to the reference MIC (> 95% for both Etest and MTS). No significant statistical differences were observed between the Etest and the MTS in terms of distribution of phenotypic and genotypic errors. While both methods demonstrated an acceptable bias (Etest + 21.8%; MTS − 19.1%), the MTS results tended to be lower than the reference MIC, while the Etest values tended to be higher (Table 2). These differences may reflect variations in the calibration of the two tests. Improved alignment of both methods with the gold standard, BMD, would improve their accuracy and reliability, particularly for borderline strains.

Strains 6 and 19 with known tigecycline resistance mechanisms and phenotypic borderline susceptibility values posed challenges across laboratories. Thus, K-res and EDL repeated the reference MIC testing for these strains. Upon retesting, strain 6, which was initially considered susceptible (MIC 0.5 mg/L), was assigned a reference MIC of 0.5–1 mg/L (range 0.25-1 mg/L). In contrast, after retesting, strain 19, which was initially considered resistant (MIC 1 mg/L), was assigned a reference MIC of 0.5 mg/L (range 0.5-1 mg/L), placing it below the EUCAST breakpoint and thus classified as susceptible. It is important to acknowledge, that the reference values for these two strains included the clinical breakpoint, thus making reliable categorisation (S/R) difficult, even with the reference method. As a national reference laboratory, K-res has identified several clinical strains of E. faecium with identical mutations in S10 as are found in strain 6, with various tigecycline MICs (ranging from 0.5 to > 2 mg/L, data not shown) and identical mutations as those found in strain 19, which were reported in German isolates with a much higher MIC (> 2 mg/L) [12, 28–30]. These observations strengthen the relevance of selecting strains with borderline phenotypes, and the need for more knowledge about mechanisms of resistance, and their clinical relevance.

Several studies have shown an association between mutations or deletions in the S10 protein and elevated tigecycline MICs [12, 28–30]. It is therefore highly likely that mutations in S10, along with Tet(M) and Tet(L) modifications, play a role in tigecycline resistance development. Continuous tigecycline exposure can select for additional mutations in tigecycline-resistant associated genes, as well as an increased copy number of mobile genetic elements harbouring tetM/L and higher MICs [12, 30]. Tigecycline exposure has been shown to be associated with the development of resistance in E. faecium [10, 28]. Our case report of strain 19 showed the development of an increased tigecycline MIC from 0.125 to 0.5 mg/L through a deletion of K57Y58 in ribosomal protein S10 along with a tetM leader peptide deletion [10]. Continuous exposure to tigecycline is likely to select further elevated MICs in strains that already show a resistant genotype, but studies are needed to confirm the potential clinical significance of such strains.

Beabout et al. suggested that position 57 in S10 plays a critical role in the development of tigecycline resistance [12]. Considering the difference in tigecycline susceptibility, the K57R substation (strain 6) may contribute more effectively to the resistant profile than the K57Y58 deletion (strain 19). However, we cannot rule out that strain 6 harbours additional resistance mechanisms. Strain 20 (MIC > 2 mg/L) has a larger deletion in S10 (R53-H56) along with an additional K57Q mutation. It has been shown that a similar deletion in S10 increases resistance without compromising bacterial fitness, while the additional K57Q mutation further enhances tigecycline resistance [12]. Together, these mechanisms likely play a significant role in explaining the higher tigecycline MIC observed in strain 20.

In this study, genotypic resistance refers to the presence of known resistance mechanisms and was used to evaluate the sensitivity of tests in detecting such mechanisms rather than to define clinical resistance. Discordance between genotype and phenotype highlights the complexity of tigecycline resistance and emphasises the importance of complementing genotypic approaches with phenotypic testing. This aligns with previous findings by Coll et al., which demonstrated limited sensitivity of genotypic prediction for tigecycline resistance in E. faecium [13]. It should be noted that in the study by Coll et al. many of the isolates with genotypical tigecycline resistance mechanisms showed an MIC of 0.5 mg/L which with the current breakpoint would result in an even poorer correlation of genotype versus phenotype, but the material used was partly based on non-reference MICs [13].

The strengths of this study include its multicentre design across Nordic laboratories, the use of standardised protocols, the inclusion of well-characterised clinical strains with known resistance mechanisms and the inclusion of two borderline resistant isolate (MIC 0.5–1 mg/L), which complicated categorisation and increased the risk of categorical errors. These factors enhance the study’s validity, comparability, and clinical relevance. However, the study has some limitations, including the small number of resistant isolates and the absence of resistant E. faecalis.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates strong concordance between the reference BMD MIC and both the EUCAST DD method and the MIC gradient tests for tigecycline susceptibility testing in enterococci. The results align with ISO standards and are supported by acceptable QC data. Both phenotypic methods produced comparable results, with neither being superior to the other. However, challenges remain in detecting resistance in strains with borderline MIC values. The study is limited by the small number of resistant isolates and several borderline strains, which may affect the generalisability of the findings. As tigecycline resistance is increasing, further improvement of phenotypic AST performance and interpretation are needed, alongside a deeper understanding of the underlying resistance mechanisms in borderline strains and their clinical relevance. Although this study is based on a limited number of isolates, it suggests that the increased EUCAST susceptibility breakpoint introduced in 2025 has resulted in a weaker correlation between phenotype and genotype in E. faecium. However, the clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain and should be reassessed as additional data on tigecycline treatment for refractory VRE infections become available.
