Hippocampal subfield-specific alterations in post-stroke dementia with subcortical lesion
Authors: Guojun Xu, Bin Lin, Yun Jiang, Jie Ming, Linhui Ni, Guocan Han, Zhiyong Zhao, Huaying Cai, Lulu Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The structural and functional characteristics of hippocampal subfields have been extensively studied in dementia, with findings indicating stronger associations with cognitive performance than those observed in the whole hippocampus (HP). However, the impact of post-stroke dementia (PSD) on the structural and functional connectivity between hippocampal subfields and cortical regions remains unclear. The objective of this study is to examine alterations in the functional and structural connectivity between hippocampal subfields and cortical regions in PSD. We collected resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data from 24 PSD patients, 36 post-stroke non-demented (PSND) patients, and 21 normal controls (NC). These data were used to estimate fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), fractional anisotropy (FA), and diffusivity maps in the hippocampal subfields. Additionally, we constructed functional and structural connectivity matrices between hippocampal subfields and cortical regions for each participant, highlighting group-specific connectivity alterations. Statistical analyses were conducted using a linear mixed model to compare group differences and assess the relationship between MRI measures and clinical evaluations. Our results revealed distinct PSD-related changes in functional connectivity, particularly with the temporal-occipital cortex, within hippocampal subfields compared to the whole HP. Notably, different subfields contributed differently to connectivity changes within the entire HP. Furthermore, we identified positive correlations between diffusivity in the bilateral hippocampal tails and illness duration in PSND patients, which were not observed in PSD. These findings highlight the significant impact of PSD on hippocampal subfields, with subfield analysis offering new insights into the underlying mechanisms of PSD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11682-025-01034-5.

Introduction

Stroke is the third leading cause of death and disability worldwide (Feigin et al., 2022), with neurological damage contributing significantly to post-stroke fatalities (Chen et al., 2017). Post-stroke dementia (PSD) refers to any form of dementia that develops after a stroke, including post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI), Alzheimer's disease, and vascular dementia (Mok et al., 2017). Dementia is a common neurological complication following stroke, affecting up to one-third of stroke survivors (Mijajlović et al., 2017). The risk of developing dementia after a stroke is influenced by factors such as age, stroke location, severity, and coexisting health conditions (Pendlebury & Rothwell, 2009). A recent prospective study found that 33% of stroke survivors develop dementia within five years, with incidence rates correlating with stroke severity (Pendlebury & Rothwell, 2019). Therefore, understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PSD is crucial for disease prevention, predicting progression, developing effective treatments, and improving patient quality of life. The current study aims to investigate the functional and structural connectivity between hippocampal subfields and cortical regions to better understand the effects of PSD.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a valuable neuroimaging tool for assessing post-stroke cognitive impairment and PSD (Mijajlović et al., 2017; Weaver et al., 2021), offering high sensitivity and specificity for detecting pathological changes (Mehrabian et al., 2015). Advanced MRI techniques provide insights into the mechanisms of PSCI and dementia. For example, structural MRI (sMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) have revealed cortical thinning in regions connected to the sites of subcortical infarcts six months after stroke (Duering et al., 2015; Kuchcinski et al., 2017). DTI metrics, including fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), and mean diffusivity (MD), are considered reliable indicators of white matter microstructure due to their sensitivity to cellular density, axonal size, water content, myelin content, and other tissue properties (Beaulieu, 2002; Fjell et al., 2008). Specifically, FA reflects the degree of non-isotropic diffusion, while AD varies across brain regions and white matter pathologies. RD is typically elevated with demyelination or reduced axon density (Alexander et al., 2007), and MD, the weighted mean of AD and RD, measures the overall magnitude of water diffusion within a given voxel or brain region (Assaf & Pasternak, 2008; Basser et al., 1994; Sullivan & Pfefferbaum, 2006). Therefore, combined DTI metrics may provide a more comprehensive understanding of white matter microstructure in PSD.

The hippocampus (HP), a key component of the limbic system, plays an essential role in cognitive processes and is particularly susceptible to stroke-induced damage (Blum et al., 2012; Debette et al., 2011). Extensive research has explored structural and functional changes in the HP among stroke patients with cognitive dysfunction (Gemmell et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2014). Clinical studies have shown hippocampal atrophy in patients with post-stroke cognitive impairment or dementia (Khlif et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021a). For example, Werden et al. reported hippocampal volume loss in ischemic stroke patients compared to healthy controls (Werden et al., 2017). Another study demonstrated reduced functional connectivity between the HP and inferior parietal lobule in stroke patients (Jung et al., 2021). Recent literature also highlights altered functional connectivity and disrupted neural networks in the HP of patients with cognitive dysfunction (Jung et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2020). Cross-sectional studies of PSCI and PSD emphasize the HP, posterior cingulate cortex, and corpus callosum as critical regions of interest, analyzed using both structural MRI (Khlif et al., 2022; Vos et al., 2018) and functional MRI (fMRI) (Zhao et al., 2021b). Therefore, combining sMRI and fMRI may provide a more comprehensive assessment of hippocampal structure and function in PSD.

The entire HP is crucial for various cognitive functions, including declarative memory, spatial navigation, emotions, and resilience (Moscovitch et al., 2016; Vos et al., 2018). However, hippocampal subfields may be more sensitive to structural changes in disease states (Jack et al., 2000; van de Pol, 2006). For example, research has shown that the volume of the cornu ammonis (CA) 1 subfield is particularly sensitive to structural changes at the predementia stage in conditions like Alzheimer's disease, mild cognitive impairment, and semantic dementia, outperforming whole hippocampal volume assessments (La Joie et al., 2013). Another study found dementia-specific atrophy in the left granule cell layer of the dentate gyrus and CA4 in PSD patients compared to normal controls and post-stroke non-dementia (PSND) patients (Zhao et al., 2021a). Additionally, each hippocampal subfield exhibits unique functional connectivity with the neocortex, supporting specific cognitive functions (Genon et al., 2021). Directed functional connectivity studies have identified altered information processing within individual hippocampal subfields in PSD patients (Zhao et al., 2021b). However, the effects of PSD on functional and structural connectivity between hippocampal subfields and cortical regions remain unclear.

In this study, we aim to investigate dementia-related changes in hippocampal subfields in stroke patients using both structural and functional imaging techniques. By comparing different patient groups and examining the relationship between MRI metrics and clinical evaluations, we hypothesize that PSD exerts subfield-specific effects on hippocampal microstructure, leading to abnormal functional connectivity with cortical regions. Building on prior findings of reduced hippocampal volume and altered functional connectivity in hippocampal subfields among PSD patients, our analysis aims to provide new insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying dementia following stroke.

Methods

Participants

This study recruited 24 patients with PSD, 36 patients with PSND, and 21 normal controls (NC) from Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital between September 2017 and October 2019. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the hospital (approval number: 20200115–33). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion and exclusion criteria followed those described in our previous studies (Zhao et al., 2021a, 2021b). Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged between 45 and 85 years, have a history of ischemic stroke with lesions located in the basal ganglia, complete neuropsychological tests during the acute phase and at three months post-stroke, undergo imaging between 1 to 6 months after stroke onset, experience a first-ever stroke, be right-handed (due to known effects of handedness on brain function lateralization after stroke) (Cuellar-Partida et al., 2021), and have confirmed acute ischemic stroke based on head MRI or CT. Exclusion criteria included any neuropsychiatric comorbidity (e.g., depression, epilepsy, brain tumor, brain trauma, drug or alcohol abuse), clinically significant or unstable medical conditions, contraindications for MRI, prestroke dementia (Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) score > 3.31), aphasia before or after stroke, and lesions in the HP.

Cognitive status was assessed using the IQCODE to evaluate pre-stroke cognitive function, with both the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and miniCog assessments used to evaluate post-stroke cognitive performance at the acute stage and at three months post-stroke. Vascular risk factors, including blood pressure, lipids, blood glucose, and smoking, were managed with appropriate medications. All patients received standardized treatment in accordance with the"Guideline for Early Management of Adults with Ischemic Stroke"(Rezaei et al., 2016) and were followed up in an outpatient clinic by a single clinician (H.Y.C., MD). Normal controls underwent cognitive assessments (MMSE and miniCog) prior to MRI scanning. Clinical data were recorded by two neurologists, who were blinded to MRI data, and cognitive assessments were conducted by the same clinicians.

Post-stroke patients were classified into PSD and PSND groups according to criteria outlined in our prior studies (Zhao et al., 2021a, 2021b). Briefly, PSD was diagnosed by two neurologists with 15 (H.Y.C.) and 4 (L.H.N.) years of experience, based on the 2019 Chinese Vascular Cognitive Impairment Guideline, which defines PSD as cognitive impairment lasting for at least three months post-stroke (Association, 2019). Diagnosis was made three months post-stroke, aligning with international consensus (Skrobot et al., 2017) (within 6 months). A patient was classified as PSD if they met one of the following criteria: (1) MMSE score < 24 (for patients with education above junior middle school), < 19 (for primary school education), or < 17 (for illiterate patients), or (2) adjusted miniCog score < 3. Otherwise, the patient was classified as PSND. MMSE and miniCog scores for patients are provided in Table S3 (Association, 2019).

MRI data acquisition

MRI scans were performed approximately three months after stroke using a Siemens 3 T MAGNETOM Skyra MRI scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a 20-channel head coil. The duration of illness was defined as the time from stroke onset to the MRI scan. We obtained 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE), resting-state functional echo planar imaging (fMRI), and diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI) sequences. The parameters for these sequences were consistent with those used in our previous studies (Zhao et al., 2021a, 2021b).

Lesion analysis

Lesion regions were obtained on the nondiffusion-weighted image (b0) slice by slice using a manual tool named MRIcro software (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricro). One neurologist with 4 years of experience (L.H.N.) manually delineated the lesion masks, which were subsequently verified by a second neurologist with 15 years of experience (H.Y.C.). The final lesion locations and volumes were determined after consensus between both neurologists. The lesion volume for each patient was calculated by counting the number of voxels within the lesion masks.

The MRI image processing pipeline consisted of the following steps: (A) acquisition of raw images including rs-fMRI, T1-weighted (T1WI), and diffusion-weighted (DWI) images; (B) preprocessing of all MRI images; (C) registration among the three MRI modalities; and (D) reconstruction of functional and structural indices and connectivities

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) data were denoised using a Marchenko-Pastur-PCA-based algorithm, with corrections for eddy currents, head motion, and distortions via outlier replacement and slice-to-volume registration. Bias field inhomogeneity was also corrected. The corrected volumes were voxel-fitted to derive FA, MD, AD, and RD using MRtrix3.

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data processing utilized the Advanced Data Processing Assistant for rs-fMRI (http://www.restfmri.net) and Statistical Parametric Mapping (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) toolkits in MATLAB. The first 5 functional volumes were discarded, followed by slice-timing and head-motion corrections. The remaining volumes were normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space with an isotropic voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3 and smoothed using a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. Time series were detrended linearly and subjected to temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) to remove low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise. Nuisance covariates, including head-motion parameters, cerebrospinal fluid signal, white matter signal, and global mean signal, were regressed out (Yin et al., 2021). The time series of each voxel were transformed into the frequency domain using a fast Fourier transform to compute the power spectrum, from which the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) was derived (Zang et al., 2007). Fractional ALFF (fALFF) was calculated as ALFF (0.01–0.1 Hz) divided by the ALFF (0.01–0.25 Hz), and all fALFF values were standardized by division by the mean (Zou et al., 2008).

T1-weighted MPRAGE images were processed using SPM12 for bias correction and segmentation into six tissue classes: gray matter, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and three non-brain tissue classes. A deformation field was used to normalize the images to MNI space, with intracranial volume masked using thresholding. Hippocampal substructures were segmented using FreeSurfer version 7.4.1 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) based on its subfield segmentation protocol (Iglesias et al., 2015). The quality of segmentations was manually assessed using the Enhancing Neuroimaging through Meta-Analysis Stroke Recovery Quality Control protocol (Liew et al., 2022), with results reviewed by two neurologists (H.Y.C. and L.H.N.) until agreement was reached. The following regions of interest (ROIs) were extracted for each hemisphere: the entire HP, subiculum (Sub), CA1, CA2/3, CA4, dentate gyrus (DG), and hippocampal tail (Tail).

T1-weighted MPRAGE images were co-registered to the mean b0 EPI volume and the mean fMRI volume using boundary-based registration in FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT) (Jenkinson et al., 2002). For rs-fMRI, time series for each ROI were extracted to construct a functional connectome matrix. For diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), an initial tractogram was generated, and Spherical-deconvolution Informed Filtering of Tractograms (SIFT2) (He et al., 2024) was applied to obtain streamline connectivity and construct a structural connectome matrix (Tournier et al., 2019). Additionally, the AAL template was registered from MNI to native space to construct functional and structural connectivity matrices between AAL-based regions and the HP and its subfields for each participant.

Statistical analysis

A linear mixed model with age, gender, education, head motion and lesion information as the covariates and with subject factor as a random variable was used to compare the difference between the groups and evaluate the relationship between MRI measurements and clinical assessments. We also performed the comparisons between groups with the duration of illness as an additional covariate. Then, for showing a significant group level main effect, post hoc t-tests were performed to detect the pairwise differences in MRI based parameters. To control for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, with a voxel-level p < 0.01 and a cluster-level p < 0.05.

Results

No significant differences were observed in age (p = 0.07), handedness, or head motion (p = 0.72) across the three groups (Table S1). Clinical assessments and duration of illness did not differ significantly between PSD and PSND groups (p = 0.88). However, significant differences were found between NC and patient groups for gender (p < 0.01), education (p < 0.01), MMSE (p < 0.001), and MiniCog scores, but not between PSD and PSND (gender: p = 0.053; education: p = 0.60). MMSE and MiniCog scores were significantly lower in PSD patients compared to PSND patients.

Lesion analysis (Table S2) revealed no significant differences between the two patient subgroups in lesion volume (p = 0.93), lesion location (left, right, or both hemispheres) (p = 0.33), or the number of lesions (p = 0.70). No patients exhibited hemorrhagic transformation after stroke.

More PSD-related changes in functional connectivity were observed in the hippocampal subfields (Fig. 2B, Table S3, FDR corrected, all p < 0.05). Discussion

The present study employed both structural and functional MRI to investigate microstructural and functional connectivity alterations in hippocampal subfields of PSD patients. Our findings revealed the following key results: 1) functional connectivity changes associated with PSD were more pronounced in the subfields compared to the entire HP, with distinct contributions from each subfield to overall connectivity changes, but these results were not observed in structural connectivity; 2) PSD patients showed abnormal diffusion metrics in several hippocampal subfields but not in the entire HP; and 3) progressive disease in PSND patients exhibited increased diffusivity in the hippocampal tails. These results highlight the significant impact of PSD on hippocampal subfields and provide insights into disease mechanisms beyond whole hippocampal analysis.

Compared to NC, PSD patients showed significant increase in all diffusivity indices (MD, AD, and RD) in the left CA1 and left hippocampal tail. Klawiter et al. reported increased RD as a marker of worsening demyelination in multiple sclerosis (Klawiter et al., 2011), while Song et al. demonstrated significant increases in AD and RD in white matter impairment mouse models (Song et al., 2002, 2003, 2005). Given that MD reflects a linear combination of RD and AD (Alexander et al., 2011), it is linked to similar biophysical properties, which aligns with the observed increases in PSD. These findings suggest that microstructural damage in the left CA1 and left hippocampal tail may be associated with PSD. Despite the stroke lesions being distant from the HP, microstructural damage in other regions, such as hippocampal subfields and the corpus callosum, could result from excessive glutamate release, activation of adenosine triphosphate receptors, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis after stroke (Mifsud et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2016).

Additionally, PSD patients exhibited significantly lower fractional anisotropy in the right CA3 and higher RD in the right hippocampal tail. Baijot et al. found that MD and RD, but not FA or AD, correlated negatively with cognitive test scores in multiple sclerosis (Baijot et al., 2022). A decrease in FA, an unaffected AD, and an increase in RD all suggest demyelination and/or neuronal loss (Feldman et al., 2010), which leads to atrophy, as reported in previous studies (Zhao et al., 2021a, 2021b). Cagol et al. found that inflammatory activity in multiple sclerosis was associated with increased brain atrophy, particularly in deep gray matter regions (Cagol et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that the volumes of hippocampal subfields, especially the CA1 and hippocampal tail, are reduced in both stroke patients (Gemmell et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2021a) and Alzheimer's disease (La Joie et al., 2013; Šimić et al., 2015). Specifically, atrophy in the CA1 and hippocampal tail may provide a more accurate link to cognitive impairment than whole hippocampal atrophy (Khlif et al., 2022). Therefore, the microstructure of hippocampal subfields may provide an important insight into understanding the mechanisms of PSD.

HP tail plays an important role in memory processing (Liang et al., 2020), of which the decreased volume is one of the most validated and widely used biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease (de Flores et al., 2015). Previous studies have shown significant gray matter volume reductions and decreased resting-state functional connectivity between the hippocampal tail and the right medial prefrontal cortex in individuals with subjective cognitive decline (Liang et al., 2020). Additionally, studies have reported demyelination in the left hippocampal tail in cognitively normal Parkinson's disease patients (Uribe et al., 2018), and increased atrophy in the hippocampal tail following stroke (Khlif et al., 2022). In our study, we found increased diffusivity in the left hippocampal tail and significant positive correlations between the diffusivities of bilateral hippocampal tails and the duration of illness in PSND patients, consistent with previous research. These findings suggest that changes in hippocampal tail diffusivity may serve as a novel biomarker for stroke. Additionally, previous studies comparing hippocampal microvessel characteristics in postmortem tissue from post-stroke survivors, Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, and normal aging controls found that PSND patients had significantly lower hippocampal microvessel length density and larger vessel diameters compared to PSD patients (Burke et al., 2014; Gemmell et al., 2012). Therefore, we speculate that the observed correlations between illness duration and hippocampal tail diffusivity might be related to changes in hippocampal microvasculature following stroke, though further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

A previous study reported dementia-specific alterations in information flow within the entire HP and its subfields in PSD patients. Similarly, we found that PSD-related changes in FC were more pronounced in hippocampal subfields than in the entire HP. The abnormal FC pattern associated with the hippocampal subfields of PSD patients not only partly replicated those of the entire HP but also revealed new alterations. Furthermore, our analysis showed that different subfields contributed differently to the connectivity changes of the entire HP. These results are consistent with prior studies showing that the entire HP is part of the default mode network (Aggleton, 2012; Ezama et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2021b), while the subfields also participate in both the somatomotor network (CA1, CA3, CA4, DG) and the default mode network (Sub, CA4, DG) (Yassa & Stark, 2011). Although we did not examine the coupling between structural and functional MRI data in this study, we speculate that microstructural damage to the hippocampal subfields may disrupt the functional connectivity between the HP and cortical regions.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, while the PSD and PSND groups were matched for age and gender, there were significant differences between these groups and the NC group. However, our primary comparisons focused on the differences between the PSD and PSND groups, and we controlled for age and gender as covariates in comparisons between the NC and patient groups. Second, the sample size was relatively small. Although the current analysis is reasonable, larger studies would provide more robust conclusions. Third, the MRI scanning time spanned a broad range, which may not align precisely with the timing of clinical assessments. Previous studies suggest that the prevalence of PSD peaks 3–6 months after stroke and then declines, reaching approximately 50% of initial rates after one year (Liu et al., 2023; Zavoreo et al., 2009). Although our scanning time aligns with the diagnosis of PSD and PSND, the brain's structural and functional states may not perfectly correspond with clinical data (Lansberg et al., 2001). Therefore, future studies should focus on a more controlled illness duration. Fourth, the reference criteria used to categorize patients into PSD and PSND subgroups were not the most recent version. However, the data were consistent with previous studies (Zhao et al., 2021a, 2021b), and we opted not to update the segmentation scheme to maintain consistency with prior work. In future studies, we will update the segmentation scheme to allow for comparisons with previous research. Finally, the HP and subfields were segmented using FreeSurfer in stroke patients. Despite manual quality control, segmentation errors may have occurred (Zavaliangos et al., 2022). We will adopt new segmentation methods as they become available.

Conclusion

This study investigated microstructural and functional connectivity changes in hippocampal subfields in PSD patients. Our results demonstrate that PSD patients exhibit altered microstructure and disrupted functional connectivity involving cortical regions within hippocampal subfields. These findings highlight the significant impact of PSD on hippocampal subfields and underscore the potential of subfield analysis to uncover the underlying mechanisms of PSD.
