Nanomedicine targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in autoimmune diseases: breaking conventional barriers to restore immune tolerance
Authors: Gang Xiang, Yuanxu Cui, Pan Wang, Yuantao Feng, Chengyuan Zhang, Jie Lou, Xing Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune diseases (ADs) arise from the breakdown of self-tolerance, leading to pathogenic immune responses against healthy tissues. The PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint is pivotal for maintaining peripheral tolerance by suppressing autoreactive T cells, and its dysfunction drives the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), multiple sclerosis (MS), and other ADs. Conventional approaches—including monoclonal antibodies, soluble PD-L1 fusion proteins, small-molecule or RNA regulators, and cell- or gene-based therapies—have shown promise. However, their broader application is constrained by systemic immunosuppression, off-target effects, infection and malignancy risks, and manufacturing complexities. Nanomedicine offers transformative solutions by leveraging polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes or lipid nanoparticles, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and biomimetic cell-membrane coatings to deliver PD-1/PD-L1 agonists with high spatial and temporal precision. These nanocarrier platforms enable localized checkpoint activation, co-delivery of tolerogenic agents, and improved biodistribution with reduced systemic toxicity. Here, we systematically review the structural and signaling features of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, outlines the limitations of conventional therapeutic modalities, and highlights how nanocarrier-based approaches overcome these barriers to restore immune homeostasis in ADs. By integrating mechanistic insights with advanced delivery technologies, this review outlines a roadmap for next-generation, precision-engineered interventions aimed at reestablishing immune tolerance and improving clinical outcomes in ADs. Introduction

Autoimmune diseases (ADs) are chronic conditions characterized by impaired tolerance to autoantigens, categorized into organ-specific and systemic diseases [1]. The etiology of ADs involves a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors [2–4]. The genetic predisposition to ADs is intricately associated with the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), notably with the MHC class II molecules [5–8]. Various environmental factors, such as pathogens, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, dietary patterns, and medications, significantly contribute to the development of ADs [9, 10]. Despite extensive research, curative therapies for ADs remain elusive. Current treatment options are primarily pharmacological, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), glucocorticoids, and conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) (e.g., methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide). These interventions aim to modulate immune responses and alleviate inflammation in various ADs [11]. Emerging therapeutics, including JAK inhibitors (e.g., Tofacitinib) and targeted monoclonal antibodies like anti-TNF biologics (e.g., infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept), have significantly impacted the prognosis of diseases, offering improved management outcomes for ADs [12, 13]. However, a universal therapeutic strategy may not be applicable to all ADs or subsets within specific diagnostic categories [14].

In 1992, Ishida et al. first identified programmed death 1 (PD-1) along with its ligand PD-L1, originally isolated from apoptotic T-cell hybridomas [15]. PD-1, an immune checkpoint protein, is expressed on activated T cells, B cells, and macrophages [16]. Its ligand, PD-L1, selectively binds to PD-1, inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation and promoting programmed cell death of activated lymphocytes [17]. The PD-1/PD-L1 pathway plays a crucial role in regulating immune responses to protect healthy cells and tissues from immune-mediated attacks. It contributes to maintaining immune homeostasis by keeping the immune system in a deactivated state, thus preventing any inappropriate attacks or harmful reactions [18]. Dysregulations in the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have emerged as significant findings in ADs, supported by studies utilizing experimental models and genetic polymorphism analyses, linking alterations in PD-1 and its ligands to various human ADs, including SLE [19, 20], RA [21, 22], MS [23, 24], ulcerative colitis (UC) [25], and Crohn’s disease [26, 27]. Conversely, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies have been developed to treat solid tumors [28, 29] and hematological malignancies [30–32]. By blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway, these agents enhance T cell-mediated antitumor activity and reinforce immune surveillance against malignant cells [33, 34]. However, immunotherapies can induce excessive immune system stimulation, leading to T-cell activation and loss of self-tolerance, resulting in immune-related adverse effects (irAEs), which are likewise prevalent in ADs [35, 36]. These irAEs can manifest as organ-specific or non-specific, affecting various organs including the skin, intestine, liver, kidney, lung, eye, endocrine gland, heart, muscle, central or peripheral nervous system, with presentations such as dermatitis, enterocolitis, and pneumonitis [37, 38].

It is of considerable interest that, beyond PD-L1, cells markedly upregulate another ligand, PD-L2. This molecule, akin to PD-L1, engages PD-1 to exert an inhibitory effect on T cell activation [39]. Concurrently, it also has the capability of fostering T cell activation. This confers upon PD-L2 a paradoxical capacity to both dampen and enhance T cell activation, suggesting that mere inhibition of PD-L2 may not suffice to augment T cell activation [40, 41]. In the clinical realm, PD-L1 expression levels serve as a biomarker to gauge the therapeutic outcomes of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 interventions, whereas the implications and significance of PD-L2 remain under scientific scrutiny and has not yet been employed as extensively in clinical prognostication and treatment as PD-L1 [42]. Hence, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is widely regarded as instrumental in sustaining peripheral T lymphocyte tolerance and modulating inflammatory responses [43, 44]. These complexities emphasize that precise control of the PD-1/PD-L1/PD-L2 axis is important for immune homeostasis, and dysregulation of this checkpoint contributes to AD pathogenesis.

Given the critical role of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in ADs, developing targeted therapies has become a major focus. Although PD-1/PD-L1–targeted therapies aiming to restore immune tolerance in ADs can be effective, excessive immune dampening raises concerns about increased susceptibility to infections and possibly elevated malignancy risk due to prolonged immunosuppression [45]. This has led to the exploration of novel therapeutic strategies, particularly those based on nanotechnology. Recent nanotechnology advances have produced nanoparticle-based platforms targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint. Such nanocarriers can deliver PD-1 or PD-L1 modulators with high precision to immune cells, improving efficacy and reducing off-target effects. For example, hybrid nanoparticles decorated with recombinant PD-L1 and loaded with methotrexate effectively engaged PD-1-expressing T cells, expanded regulatory T cells (Tregs), and protected mice from systemic autoimmune inflammation [46]. Similarly, EVs derived from regulatory immune cells inherently express PD-L1 and other inhibitory signals, leveraging their immunosuppressive properties to help re-establish immune tolerance [47]. These nanomedicine strategies promise to transform AD therapy by enabling precise, context-specific checkpoint modulation.

This review aims to comprehensively elucidate the significance of the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis in the progression of diverse ADs, starting from its structural and molecular characteristics, exploring its role in different ADs, and then delving into the emerging nanomedicine-based therapeutic strategies. By providing in-depth insights, we hope to contribute to the development of more rational and effective therapeutic approaches for both ADs and related oncological diseases.

Structure of PD-1/PD-L1 and their signaling pathway

PD-1, also known as CD279, is a type I transmembrane receptor with a molecular weight ranging from 50 to 55 kDa [48, 49]. It belongs to the CD28 family and shares approximately 30% sequence homology with other molecules of the family, such as inducible co-stimulator (ICOS), CD28 and CTLA-4. The cytoplasmic tail of PD-1 comprises two tyrosine motifs: the immune receptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) and the immune receptor inhibitory tyrosine-based switch motif (ITSM). Experimental evidence has confirmed the essential role of ITSM in mediating the immunosuppressive function of PD-1 [50, 51]. PD-1 has traditionally been considered monomeric; however, a recent study by Philips et al. now demonstrates that it can form cis dimers through interactions within the transmembrane domain. This finding aligns with the behavior of some other immune receptors, such as CTLA-4, known to function as dimers, showing that PD-1 and its ligands also dimerize due to transmembrane domain interactions, with this dimerization correlating with PD-1’s capacity to inhibit immune responses [52]. Additionally, PD-1 possesses a proline-enriched motif crucial for binding to CD80 and CD86, which is absent in other CD28 family members. These structural differences contribute to the unique ligand recognition and signaling mechanism of PD-1 [53].

PD-L1, also known as B7-H1, is a member of the B7 family and serves as a ligand for PD-1. The PD-L1 protein comprises immunoglobulin variable (IgV) and immunoglobulin constant (IgC) domains, a transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic tail domain [54]. PD-L1 expression is upregulated on tumor cells, antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and related lymphocytes, while PD-1 is highly expressed on activated T cells. The interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 triggers the phosphorylation of tyrosine residues within the cytoplasmic domains of PD-1 via ITIM and ITSM motifs. This phosphorylation facilitates the binding of PD-1 to the Src homology 2 (SH2) domains of SH2-containing phosphatase 2 (SHP2). Consequently, this interaction leads to the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway by dephosphorylating molecules upstream of PI3K, thereby attenuating AKT activation. This interaction negatively regulates T cell activation, promoting immune tolerance and preventing excessive immune responses (Fig. 1). Contrary to conventional understanding, Giorgia et al. demonstrated that SHP2 is not indispensable for the global induction of T cell exhaustion mediated by PD-1 signaling in vivo [55]. This challenges the previously accepted notion that PD-1’s ability to inhibit T cell activation is predominantly facilitated through SHP2 recruitment. Nevertheless, these signaling cascades ultimately dampen various T cell functions, such as proliferation, cytokine secretion, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte differentiation [51, 55–57]. Overall, this interaction contributes to the inactivation and exhaustion of immune T lymphocytes, underscoring the potential of targeting this pathway for therapeutic intervention in ADs.

PD-1/PD-L1 in Tregs induced immune tolerance

Studies revealed that the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 suppresses local T cell activation and promotes peripheral immune tolerance [58, 59]. Immune tolerance refers to the state of unresponsiveness of the immune system, lacking adaptive or innate immunity against specific antigens. In tumors, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis-mediated immune tolerance may attenuate the host immune response, leading to cancer immune escape and uncontrolled tumor cell growth. However, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis also shows promise in alleviating disease symptoms in patients with ADs, prompting the consideration of targeting the immune tolerance pathway as a therapeutic strategy for combating ADs [60, 61]. Previous studies have shown that PD-1 knockout mice are predisposed to developing ADs, such as dilated cardiomyopathy [62], at an early age, typically around 5–6 weeks old. Furthermore, the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway has been implicated in establishing maternal-fetal tolerance, as evidenced by PD-L1 expression in the placenta [63–65].

Tregs, a specialized subset of T cells, are essential for maintaining self-tolerance and restraining excessive immune activation, thereby playing a pivotal role in the regulation of ADs [66–68]. Tregs stably express the IL-2 receptor α chain (CD25) and forkhead box protein 3 (Foxp3), inducing immune tolerance and inhibiting tumor development by suppressing the cytolytic activity of CD8+ T cells and the secretion of IFN-γ, partially regulated by PD-1 and PD-L1 antibodies. This suggests that the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis is critical for Foxp3+ Tregs to suppress immune responses and promote immune tolerance [69]. PD-1 molecules are prominently present on the surfaces of Tregs and significantly influence their growth and maturation [67]. They modulate peripheral tolerance by inhibiting effector T cells (Teffs) and protecting against immune-mediated tissue damage [59] (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical analysis of 501 cases of breast cancer demonstrated a high correlation between PD-L1 expression and Foxp3+ Treg infiltration [70]. Therefore, the significant role of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in maintaining Treg-mediated immune tolerance is evident. Targeting this pathway may activate the immunosuppressive function of Foxp3+ Tregs, offering a promising approach in the treatment of ADs..

Role of PD-1/PD-L1 in autoimmune diseases

PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 constitute a fundamental immune checkpoint that enforces peripheral tolerance by transmitting inhibitory signals to antigen-activated T cells, thereby preventing autoreactive immune responses [71]. Upon engagement, PD-1 recruits the phosphatase SHP-2 to its cytoplasmic ITSM motif, attenuating T-cell receptor (TCR)–mediated activation, cytokine secretion, and proliferation [45] (Fig. 3A). Dysregulation of this pathway has been implicated across diverse ADs, where it exerts disease-specific regulatory effects. In RA, PD-1 expression is significantly elevated on synovial T cells, while the expression of its ligand, PD-L1 is insufficient within the synovial microenvironment. This imbalance disrupts the inhibitory signaling cascade mediated by the PD-1 pathway (Fig. 3B). As a direct consequence, the chronic activation of autoreactive T cells, a hallmark of RA pathogenesis, is not effectively restrained. Unchecked T-cell activation, in turn, sustains persistent synovial inflammation and drives disease progression [72] (Fig. 3B). Consistent with these findings, animal studies have demonstrated that PD-1 deficiency leads to increased incidence and severity of arthritis [73]. These observations suggest that dysfunction of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis contributes to the pathogenesis of RA by failing to maintain immune tolerance and limiting pathogenic T-cell responses. In MS, upregulation of PD-L1 on astrocytes and microglia limits CNS inflammation, yet many infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells lack PD-1, thereby escaping PD-L1–mediated inhibition. The failure to constrain the activation and effector function of these PD-1-negative CD8⁺ T cells perpetuates chronic CNS inflammation and promotes disease progression [74–76] (Fig. 3C). In SLE, aberrant PD-1 and PD-L1 expression—including defective cytokine-regulated PD-L1 on monocytes—impairs the inhibitory effect on T cells, thereby disrupting immune tolerance and contributing to disease pathogenesis [77] (Fig. 3D). In inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), PD-L1 deficiency in colonic epithelial cells or dendritic cells (DCs) fails to restrain excessive effector T cell activation, leading to uncontrolled expansion of pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 subsets and increased production of cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-17, and TNF-α (Fig. 3E). Concurrently, the impaired induction and stability of Foxp3⁺ Tregs further disrupt immune homeostasis at the gut mucosal interface, thereby promoting the breakdown of tolerance and exacerbating intestinal inflammation [43, 78, 79]. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in preventing autoimmunity and provide a strong rationale for therapeutic approaches aimed at enhancing checkpoint signaling in ADs management.

Rheumatoid arthritis

RA is a chronic systemic AD characterized by inflammatory joint involvement, notably destructive synovitis, which entails inflammation of the synovial lining accompanied by proliferation of synovial villi and infiltration of inflammatory cells, including cytotoxic CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells [80, 81].

Recent studies have identified PD-1 expression in plasma and EVs extracted from synovial fluid, as well as in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the synovium of RA patients [73, 82]. Additionally, PD-L1 expression has been observed in synovial lining cells [80]. However, the pre-existing PD-1/PD-L1 pathways appear insufficient to inhibit T-cell activation and reduce inflammation in synovitis patients. This inadequacy may stem from the low concentration of PD-L1 in the synovium, rendering it ineffective in suppressing T-cell activity, or from the inability of PD-1/PD-L1-mediated immune tolerance to counteract robust immune stimuli [73, 80]. Elevated levels of soluble PD-1 (sPD-1) detected in serum and synovial fluid of RA patients suggest a potential mechanism of resistance to PD-1-expressing T-cell-induced immune tolerance [21, 73, 83]. Notably, administration of recombinant PD-L1-Fc or PD-L1-Ig has shown promise in ameliorating symptoms of collagen-induced arthritis (CIA), implicating sPD-1 as a potential biomarker or therapeutic target for immunomodulatory therapies in RA [73, 84–87].

Furthermore, regulatory B cells (Bregs) have emerged as key players in immune response suppression through interleukin-10 (IL-10) production [88]. Flow cytometry analysis revealed increased frequencies of PD-L1-expressing Bregs in treated RA patients compared with untreated individuals. This finding suggests that cytokine production in Bregs can be mediated via the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, highlighting the potential therapeutic role of PD-L1-expressing Bregs in RA treatment [89].

In summary, the involvement of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in regulating immunity in RA pathogenesis offers insights into novel therapeutic strategies for RA management.

Multiple sclerosis

MS is a chronic disease characterized by inflammation and demyelination within the central nervous system (CNS), typically presenting as relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) [90]. A defining characteristic of MS is the repeated presence of inflammatory lesions within the CNS. Although the precise pathogenesis of MS remains elusive, numerous studies have suggested the involvement of immune cells in its development.

In MS lesions, the expression of PD-L1 in astrocytes is significantly elevated compared to healthy controls, providing a mechanism to attenuate the response of CD8+ T cells. However, many infiltrating CD8+ T lymphocytes do not express the corresponding receptor PD-1, rendering them insensitive to cells expressing PD-L1 [75, 76]. Therefore, increasing PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for MS. Investigations into the administration of high doses of autoantigens via intravenous injection for AD therapy are underway. This approach stimulates CD4+ T cells to release interferon-γ (IFN-γ), initiating the synthesis of IL-27 by conventional DCs type 1 (CDC1). IL-27, in turn, upregulates PD-L1 expression in monocytic-derived DCs (MODC) within the CNS of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mice. Subsequently, IL-27 directly interacts with PD-1 expressed on pathogenic CD4+ T cells, inducing apoptosis and thereby ameliorating the disease, ultimately leading to immune tolerance [91, 92].

Forkhead box protein A1 (FoxA1)+ Tregs primarily develop within the CNS and induce the expression of PD-L1 by binding to the PD-L1 promoter. Studies have demonstrated that FoxA1+ Tregs can inhibit CNS inflammation in EAE [93]. Bregs play a role in inhibiting autoimmunity; however, they are deficient in patients with MS. Treatment with alemtuzumab, a highly effective therapy for relapsing MS, promotes the proliferation of Bregs. Following alemtuzumab administration, Bregs with high PD-L1 expression interact with PD-1 on T cells, inhibiting T cell proliferation and suppressing MS disease activity [94].

Systemic lupus erythematosus

SLE is a condition characterized by the dysregulation of the immune system, resulting in multi-organ damage. The pathogenesis of SLE involves inherited immune response deficiencies, aberrant activation of autoreactive T and B cells, and the presence of diverse pathogenic autoantibodies [95–98]. The role of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in the development of SLE has been identified as crucial [19].

Studies have reported that serum levels of sPD-1 and sPD-L1 are higher in SLE patients than in healthy individuals, whereas sPD-L2 expression is significantly lower [95, 99]. Abnormal expressions of sPD-1, sPD-L1, and sPD-L2 may be associated with immune dysregulation in SLE. Flow cytometry analyses have revealed elevated levels of CD19+ PD-L1+ B cells in SLE patients, suggesting that abnormal activation of autoantibody-producing B cells contributes to SLE pathogenesis [100]. Furthermore, increased expression of PD-L1 on neutrophils in SLE patients may correlate with disease severity, potentially serving as a negative feedback mechanism to prevent excessive autoimmune responses [101].

Conversely, deficits in PD-1/PD-L1 expression have been implicated in SLE development. Specifically, reduced PD-L1 expression on monocytes has been shown to enhance T cell activity. The expression of PD-L1 on monocytes has been associated with TNF-α and TGF-β, with TNF-α restoring PD-L1 expression in monocytes affected by lupus, while TGF-β inhibits PD-L1 expression in normal monocytes [77].

Overexpression of PD-L1 in CD3+ T cells via plasmid transfection has been demonstrated to ameliorate lupus glomerulonephritis [102, 103]. Additionally, PD-1-deficient mice have shown symptoms of lupus glomerulitis and lupus autoimmune proliferative arthritis [99, 104]. While PD-L1 deficiency alone may not induce SLE, it can accelerate and exacerbate systemic autoimmunity in lupus-susceptible mice [18, 105].

Although the exact role of PD-1/PD-L1 disruptions in SLE development requires further investigation, they still may be considered as intriguing therapeutic targets and potential biomarkers for disease severity.

Vasculitis

Vasculitis is an AD characterized by inflammation of blood vessels, leading to organ and tissue damage due to vessel inflammation, necrosis, and impaired blood flow [106]. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial for managing these diseases effectively.

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is the predominant form of autoimmune vasculitis, characterized by intense inflammation of arterial walls primarily affecting medium to large-sized vessels, resulting in significant vascular occlusion and remodeling. Immunopathological studies have revealed defective PD-L1 expression and dysfunction of CD8+ T regulatory cells in GCA [107]. Although PD-1+ T cells are abundant in GCA, the level of PD-L1 in vascular DCs in GCA patients is lower compared to healthy controls, indicating that PD-L1 loss is a primary mechanism underlying impaired arterial immune function in GCA [108]. Given that PD-L1 serves as a critical immunosuppressive checkpoint to maintain peripheral immune tolerance, its deficiency in GCA renders vascular endothelial cells susceptible to CD8+ T cell-mediated immune attack, leading to disruption of blood vessel integrity and vessel leakage. Therefore, overexpression of PD-L1 on vascular endothelial cells may prevent immune-induced damage to blood vessels [109].

Type 1 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an organ-specific, chronic autoimmune condition characterized by significant immune infiltration, necessitating lifelong exogenous insulin therapy. The hallmark of T1D is the progressive and selective destruction of pancreatic β cells orchestrated by a complex autoimmune network [110]. Central to this process is the T-cell-mediated attack, in which autoreactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells infiltrate pancreatic islets (insulitis) and execute β-cell destruction via multiple mechanisms, including the release of cytotoxic cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β), perforin-granzyme exocytosis, and Fas–FasL interactions [111–113]. Autoreactive CD8+ T cells are the predominant effectors, directly recognizing β-cell autoantigens presented on MHC class I molecules and inducing apoptosis [114]. Autoreactive CD4+ Th1 cells, recognizing autoantigenic peptides on MHC class II molecules presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), amplify the immune response by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines that enhance CD8+ T-cell activation and recruit additional immune cells [115]. Beyond T cells, other immune cells contribute significantly: B cells not only produce autoantibodies against β-cell antigens (e.g., insulin, GAD65, IA-2, ZnT8) but also function as APCs to activate autoreactive T cells [116, 117]; DCs and macrophages release inflammatory mediators and present antigens to naïve T cells, perpetuating autoimmunity [118]; natural killer (NK) cells further exacerbate β-cell damage through direct cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion [119]. Notably, research has highlighted a correlation between the absence of co-inhibitory immune checkpoint ligands (such as PD-L1 and CD86) in islet β cells and the progression of T1D [120, 121]. In rare cases, autoimmune diabetes has been reported as an adverse event in cancer patients treated with PD-L1 antibodies. One potential mechanism involves the infiltration of activated T cells into the pancreas, leading to an attack on pancreatic β cells [122–128]. Further investigation revealed that the absence of PD-1/PD-L1 in NOD mice accelerates diabetes development [129]. Consequently, inhibiting T cell infiltration into the pancreas through the PD-1/PD-L1 immunosuppressive pathway may represent a prospective approach in managing T1D.

In vitro experiments with islet β cells from non-diabetic individuals have shown that stimulation with IFN-γ, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, can upregulate PD-L1 expression in β cells [130, 131]. Mechanistically, IFN-γ not only activates JAK-STAT1/IRF1-dependent transcription of CD274 (PD-L1) in β-cells, but also triggers a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis in both immune and target cells. Such reprogramming, well established in immune and tumor cells and likely relevant in β-cells, is mediated by STAT3-dependent induction of c-Myc and HIF-1α, which enhances glucose uptake, lactate production, and histone acetylation at the PD-L1 promoter, thereby stabilizing PD-L1 mRNA and protein expression [132]. Moreover, hypoxia or lactate accumulation can further amplify HIF-1α/NF-κB signaling, generating a positive feedback loop that sustains PD-L1 expression in β-cells even after the initial IFN-γ stimulus declines [133]. Thus, metabolic reprogramming—whether imposed by infiltrating immune cells or by the islet microenvironment—may function as an “immunometabolic rheostat” that modulates the strength of the PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint. Marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), a promising treatment for immune-related diseases, protect pancreatic islets from invasion and delay hyperglycemia onset in NOD mice by inducing PD-L1 expression in response to autoreactive T cell-secreted IFN-γ, suppressing autoreactive T cell proliferation, and promoting immune tolerance [134, 135].

Studies have demonstrated that gene transfer of islet-defining factor Neurogenin-3 and islet growth factor Betacellulin into the livers of diabetic NOD mice induces new islet production. However, only PD-L1-overexpressing new islets respond to glucose stimulation, secrete insulin, and reverse hyperglycemia in NOD mice. PD-L1 overexpression in newly generated islets may induce apoptosis of infiltrated CD8+ T cells, preventing autoimmunity [136]. Similarly, PD-L1-overexpressing platelets aggregate in the inflamed pancreas, inhibiting CD8+ T cell activity and preventing pancreatic β cell destruction, effectively maintaining average blood glucose levels, and reversing diabetes in NOD mice [137]. Utilizing hematopoietic stem and precursor cells (HSPCs) engineered to overexpress PD-L1 has shown promise in managing T1D by inhibiting autoimmunity [138]. These findings underscore the central role of PD-L1/PD-1-induced immune tolerance in T1D.

Conventional PD-1/PD-L1-targeted strategies for ADs therapy

The programmed death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1 form a pivotal immune checkpoint axis responsible for maintaining peripheral tolerance and preventing autoimmune responses [43]. While blockade of PD-1/PD-L1 interactions has achieved remarkable success in oncology by reactivating exhausted T cells [59], the same pathway, when engaged, plays a protective role in autoimmunity by dampening hyperactive immune responses [139]. Dysregulation or insufficient activation of this pathway has been implicated in the pathogenesis of a range of ADs, including RA, SLE, MS, and IBD [72]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies that enhance or mimic PD-1/PD-L1 signaling have emerged as promising avenues for restoring immune balance in autoimmune settings [140].

Over the past decade, multiple approaches have been developed to therapeutically target the PD-1/PD-L1 axis—ranging from agonistic monoclonal antibodies and sPD-L1 fusion proteins to RNA-based regulators, small molecules, and cell or gene therapies [139]. These strategies share the central goal of promoting immune tolerance by suppressing autoreactive T cell activity, yet they differ in specificity, durability, and safety profiles. Some agents (e.g., rosnilimab in RA [NCT06041269] and UC [NCT06127043]) have advanced into early-phase clinical trials, while others remain in preclinical proof-of-concept stages.

In the following sections, we comprehensively review the current landscape of conventional PD-1/PD-L1-targeted therapies in the context of ADs (Table 1). We discuss the mechanistic rationale, preclinical and clinical evidence, representative agents, and translational challenges for each strategy. We also highlight the limitations and risks associated with these conventional approaches—including systemic immunosuppression, infection and malignancy risks, off-target effects, and high manufacturing costs—which underscore the need for more precise and safer delivery systems. These limitations serve as the foundation for the emergence of nanomedicine-based strategies, which will be discussed in detail in the subsequent section.

Current landscape of conventional PD-1/PD-L1-targeted therapies in ADs

Monoclonal antibodies

Among the conventional approaches to modulate the PD-1/PD-L1 axis for ADs therapy, monoclonal antibodies represent the most clinically advanced class (Table 2). Unlike in oncology—where PD-1/PD-L1 antagonists are used to reactivate T cells—therapeutic strategies for autoimmunity instead aim to enhance PD-1 signaling to suppress autoreactive T cells and restore immune tolerance [141–143]. Agonistic anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies are engineered to mimic or enhance the physiological inhibitory function of PD-L1. By binding to PD-1 receptors on activated T cells, they promote receptor clustering and recruit the phosphatase SHP-2, thereby attenuating downstream TCR signaling cascades, including ZAP70, PI3K/Akt, and RAS/MEK/ERK pathways. This ultimately suppresses cytokine production, cell proliferation, and cytotoxic activity [141] (Fig. 4A). To further enhance efficacy, many of these monoclonal antibodies incorporate Fc domains optimized for binding FcγRIIB, promoting receptor crosslinking and functional agonism [144].

A lead molecule in this class is rosnilimab (ANB030), a humanized IgG1 that targets PD-1high T cells—commonly found in sites of autoimmune inflammation—and acts both as a signaling agonist and selective depleter of pathogenic T-cell populations [144–146]. Preclinical studies have shown that rosnilimab suppresses inflammatory cytokines and limits T-cell infiltration in the joints and gut [144]. Currently, it is in Phase 2 clinical trials for moderate-to-severe RA (NCT06041269) and UC (NCT06127043). Preliminary top-line data indicate significant improvements in disease activity scores with a favorable safety profile, including reduced IL-17 levels and minimal immunosuppression-related adverse effects [144].

In addition to monospecific antibodies, bispecific PD-1 agonists are being developed to increase tissue-specificity and limit systemic immunosuppression. These molecules combine a PD-1–binding domain with another targeting moiety—such as anti-HLA-A2 or CD4—to direct PD-1 engagement specifically to autoreactive T-cell subsets. A notable example is the “ImmTAAI” bispecific fusion reported by Suzuki et al., which connects a PD-1 agonist to a TCR fragment recognizing HLA-A2–presented islet antigens. In a preclinical MS model (EAE), this construct achieved localized PD-1 activation and inhibited autoreactive T cells without affecting systemic immunity [147].

Thus, agonistic PD-1 monoclonal antibodies and bispecific formats provide a highly specific approach to immune tolerance induction, targeting PD-1high T cells to quench pathogenic inflammation while maintaining regulatory and protective immune functions. Rosnilimab’s progression into Phase 2 clinical trials demonstrates proof-of-concept for this strategy, and bispecific agonists offer even more precision. Nonetheless, systemic exposure and incomplete tissue targeting pose ongoing challenges, underscoring the need for advanced delivery platforms—such as nanotechnology—to maximize safety and efficacy.

PD-L1 fusion proteins

sPD-L1 fusion proteins represent another class of conventional strategies aimed at reactivating the PD-1 pathway for therapeutic immune suppression in ADs [148]. These biologics are typically constructed by fusing the extracellular domain of human PD-L1 to an immunoglobulin Fc region (PD-L1-Fc), which allows systemic administration and prolonged half-life. Mechanistically, these fusion proteins function as exogenous ligands that bind PD-1 on activated T cells, delivering inhibitory signals that suppress proliferation, cytokine production, and effector function, thereby restoring peripheral tolerance [59]. Unlike monoclonal antibodies that engage the receptor itself, PD-L1-Fc therapies emulate the physiological ligand-mediated regulation of PD-1 signaling, and in doing so, offer a more biomimetic and potentially less disruptive approach to checkpoint activation [149] (Fig. 4B).

Preclinical studies have provided substantial evidence supporting the immunoregulatory potential of PD-L1-Fc. In a murine model of CIA, systemic administration of PD-L1-Fc significantly attenuated joint inflammation, reduced IL-17 and TNF-α production, and improved histopathological scores. These effects were accompanied by a decrease in pathogenic Th17 cells and an increase in Treg populations, indicating successful rebalancing of the immune environment [73]. In models of experimental EAE, PD-L1-Fc similarly suppressed CNS infiltration of autoreactive T cells and ameliorated clinical symptoms, further validating its cross-disease applicability [150]. For example, Liu et al. (2022) demonstrated that adding recombinant PD-L1 protein to co-cultures of TAO patient T cells and orbital fibroblasts significantly dampened T-cell activation and fibroblast inflammatory responses [151]. This effect was accompanied by reduced expression of CD40 and downstream MAPK/NF-κB signaling in the fibroblasts, indicating that PD-L1 engagement interrupts the CD40–CD40L inflammatory pathway between T cells and orbital fibroblasts [151]. Such findings suggest that a PD-L1-based therapy could ameliorate orbital inflammation by restoring the missing checkpoint signal in TAO tissues [151].

One of the key limitations of PD-L1-Fc therapy, however, is the systemic exposure associated with intravenous delivery, which can suppress protective immune responses and increase infection risk. To address this, recent work has focused on the targeted delivery of PD-L1-Fc using nanocarriers [152]. For instance, Tang et al. (2024) developed reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive nanoparticles encapsulating PD-L1-Fc for the treatment of colitis. These nanoparticles accumulated in inflamed colonic tissue and released PD-L1-Fc in response to high ROS levels. This localized checkpoint activation led to a marked reduction in Teffs infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, along with restoration of epithelial integrity and disease remission in a DSS-induced colitis model [152].

Despite promising efficacy in preclinical models, PD-L1-Fc therapies have not yet entered clinical trials for ADs. Challenges such as short half-life in vivo, potential immunogenicity of fusion constructs, and risks associated with prolonged systemic checkpoint activation have limited their translational progress. However, with improvements in delivery systems—such as nanoparticles or tissue-specific expression vectors—the therapeutic window for PD-L1-based ligands may be expanded. These approaches retain the conceptual appeal of re-engaging a native inhibitory axis in a controlled and reversible manner, setting the stage for refined tolerance-inducing interventions in autoimmunity.

Small molecule modulators and SiRNA

Compared to biologics, small molecule drugs and RNA-based modulators targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis are at a much earlier stage of development for AD therapy [153]. While checkpoint blockade via small molecules is being actively explored in oncology, strategies aimed at activating the PD-1 pathway using small molecules or genetic regulation are conceptually more challenging [154]. This difficulty stems from the structural complexity of PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, which involve large, flat protein–protein interfaces not easily disrupted or stabilized by conventional low molecular weight compounds [155]. Nevertheless, efforts are underway to indirectly modulate the PD-1 pathway by influencing the expression or clustering of PD-1 and PD-L1 through epigenetic regulation, cytokine signaling, or microRNA (miRNA) manipulation.

One indirect approach involves upregulating endogenous PD-L1 expression in APCs, such as DCs, to increase their tolerogenic capacity. For instance, vitamin D3 analogs, TGF-β, and IL-10 have been shown to enhance PD-L1 expression in DCs, leading to increased induction of Tregs and reduced activation of autoreactive Teffs [156]. These effects are often exploited in ex vivo generation of tolerogenic DCs for adoptive cell therapy, but could also be targeted in vivo using small-molecule delivery systems. In this context, liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles have been proposed as carriers for small molecules or siRNAs that silence PD-L1-suppressive miRNAs in DCs—such as miR-155 or miR-34a—thereby boosting PD-L1 expression on the cell surface and promoting immune tolerance [157] (Fig. 4C).

Although the delivery of such gene-regulatory cargoes remains technically complex, proof-of-concept has been established in related fields. For example, in cancer immunotherapy, lipid nanoparticles have been used to deliver siRNAs that silence PD-L1 in tumors to restore T cell activity [158]. By contrast, in autoimmunity, the inverse strategy—enhancing PD-L1 expression using RNA modulators—has been proposed but remains largely preclinical [159]. One speculative approach involves using miRNA inhibitors or CRISPR activation systems to selectively enhance PD-L1 transcription in tissue-resident DCs or endothelial cells in inflamed organs [160, 161]. These strategies may offer high specificity and reversibility, particularly when combined with targeting ligands or responsive delivery platforms.

Another emerging concept is the design of PD-1 agonist peptides or mini-proteins. Using structural modeling and protein engineering, researchers have created trimeric or multivalent PD-L1 mimetics that bind and cluster PD-1 receptors on T cells, mimicking the natural inhibitory signal [162]. A study by Bryan et al. (2021) described a synthetic 50-amino-acid trimer peptide that could bind PD-1, inhibit TCR signaling, and suppress cytokine secretion in activated T cells in vitro [162]. While promising, these peptide agonists have not yet been validated in AD models or advanced to in vivo testing due to concerns over stability, bioavailability, and potential immunogenicity.

At present, there are no approved small-molecule PD-1 agonists or siRNA-based PD-1/PD-L1 enhancers for autoimmune indications. Nevertheless, the development of RNA delivery technologies (e.g., lipid nanoparticles as used in mRNA vaccines) and progress in tissue-specific gene modulation provide a strong rationale for further exploration. These approaches offer a modular, non-protein-based alternative to antibodies and fusion proteins, with the potential for lower cost and improved pharmacokinetics. However, delivery specificity, off-target effects, and long-term immunological consequences remain significant hurdles. For these reasons, small molecule and siRNA strategies targeting PD-1/PD-L1 in autoimmunity remain largely exploratory, but they represent a promising adjunct to more mature biologic approaches.

Cell-based and gene therapies

Cell-based and gene therapies offer an advanced and increasingly precise means to re-establish immune tolerance through modulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Unlike systemic biologics, these approaches allow localized or cell-specific delivery of immunoregulatory signals, either by engineering cells to express PD-L1 or by using gene transfer methods to induce PD-L1 expression in target tissues. Their strength lies in their capacity to mimic or even restore the natural mechanisms of peripheral tolerance through controlled engagement of PD-1 on autoreactive lymphocytes.

One prominent direction is the use of gene therapy vectors—such as adeno-associated virus (AAV) or lentivirus—to locally induce PD-L1 expression in diseased tissues [163]. In a study by Li et al. (2023), intra-articular injection of AAV5 encoding PD-L1 into inflamed joints in a CIA mouse model resulted in localized overexpression of PD-L1 on synoviocytes [163]. This therapy led to marked reductions in joint swelling, synovial hyperplasia, and T cell/macrophage infiltration, with preservation of systemic immunity. Histological scores and cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) were significantly improved compared to controls, underscoring the effectiveness of tissue-specific PD-L1 expression in restoring immune homeostasis (Fig. 4D).

In parallel, transgenic expression of PD-L1 in target tissues has been explored in autoimmune diabetes [164]. For instance, in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice, PD-L1 overexpression specifically in pancreatic β cells delayed or prevented the onset of diabetes, with significantly reduced insulitis and a higher proportion of Tregs in pancreatic lymph nodes. These models demonstrate that enhancing PD-L1 signaling in a site-restricted manner is sufficient to prevent autoimmunity in genetically susceptible hosts.

Beyond gene delivery, cellular therapies leveraging PD-1/PD-L1 signaling have been extensively investigated. One strategy involves the adoptive transfer of tolerogenic DCs (tolDCs) that are genetically or pharmacologically programmed to express high levels of PD-L1 and other co-inhibitory molecules [165]. TolDCs loaded with disease-relevant autoantigens can induce antigen-specific T cell anergy or promote Tregs differentiation (Fig. 4D). For example, in preclinical models of MS, T1D, and RA, PD-L1high tolDCs reduced disease severity and inflammatory cytokine production [165]. Clinical-grade tolDCs have been tested in early-phase trials: autologous tolDCs pulsed with citrullinated peptides showed good tolerability and immunological efficacy in patients with RA (e.g., NCT02618902).

Another promising platform is the development of Tregs engineered to express T cell receptors (TCRs) specific for self-antigens. These CAR-Tregs often retain or upregulate PD-1 expression, enabling enhanced checkpoint sensitivity [166]. For instance, in a Phase I trial for T1D (NCT01210664), autologous polyclonal Tregs are expanded ex vivo and re-infused into patients to suppress islet-specific immune responses. These cells act, in part, by engaging PD-1/PD-L1 interactions in the local microenvironment, thereby reinforcing immune quiescence (Fig. 4D).

Extending beyond immune cell therapies, bioengineering approaches have also harnessed PD-L1 signaling to protect transplanted tissues. A representative example is the artificial pancreas constructed from PD-L1-overexpressing β-cell microspheres (PD-L1-β-MCSs). In this system, exosomal PD-L1 derived from engineered β cells engages the PD-1/PD-L1 axis to induce T-cell exhaustion, suppress CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ effector responses, and promote M2 macrophage polarization. Collectively, these effects mitigate inflammation, immune infiltration, and fibrosis. Notably, PD-L1-β-MCSs not only preserve glucose-responsive insulin secretion but also protect transplanted β cells from autoimmune destruction, maintaining normoglycemia in T1D models. When combined with oxygen-producing Chlorella to alleviate hypoxia, PD-L1-β-MCSs achieved prolonged graft survival and metabolic stability. Thus, PD-L1 overexpression endows β-cell implants with intrinsic immunoregulatory capacity, presenting an innovative strategy to overcome immune rejection and enhance the long-term functionality of bioengineered β-cell replacement therapies [167]. In parallel, platelet engineering has emerged as an innovative bioengineering approach to exploit PD-1/PD-L1 signaling for the induction of immune tolerance. A recent preclinical study demonstrated that IFN-γ–primed megakaryocytes produced bioengineered platelets (IFN-γ platelets) with markedly increased PD-L1 expression, together with other inhibitory ligands including PD-L2, BTLA, and Galectin-9. Functionally, PD-L1high platelets engaged PD-1 on autoreactive T cells, driving their exhaustion and apoptosis. This intervention curtailed effector T cell infiltration into pancreatic islets, preserved β-cell viability, and simultaneously promoted local Treg expansion and M2 macrophage polarization, thereby creating a tolerogenic microenvironment. Notably, these immunomodulatory effects were accompanied by enhanced pancreatic angiogenesis and β-cell proliferation, culminating in the reversal of newly onset T1D in murine models. Collectively, platelet-based bioengineering offers a distinctive means to reprogram the immune landscape and safeguard β-cell function through sustained PD-1/PD-L1–mediated tolerance [168].

Taken together, cell- and gene-based strategies represent a frontier in PD-1/PD-L1-targeted autoimmunity therapy, with the potential for antigen specificity, tissue selectivity, and long-term disease modulation. Their major advantages lie in the ability to establish tolerogenic niches at disease sites and avoid systemic immune suppression. However, they also face substantial translational hurdles, including high manufacturing complexity, variability in patient-derived products, regulatory challenges, and cost. As of now, most approaches remain in early clinical stages or advanced preclinical development, but they offer a conceptual blueprint for next-generation immune tolerance therapies based on the PD-1/PD-L1 axis.

Limitations and risks

Conventional PD-1/PD-L1–targeted therapies carry several caveats. Prolonged systemic PD-1/PD-L1 agonist therapy imposes sustained immunosuppressive pressure, rendering patients vulnerable to opportunistic and recurrent infections. Mechanistically, PD-1 signaling attenuates T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, impairing host defense against viral and bacterial pathogens [169]. For example, Clinical evidence demonstrated that treatment with the PD-1 agonist peresolimab (300 mg) resulted in infections and infestations in 24% of patients with RA, which was notably higher than in the placebo group (12%). Among them, one patient in the peresolimab (300 mg) group discontinued treatment due to a bacterial skin infection on the foot [170]. Additionally, chronic PD-1 activation has been shown to facilitate viral infections such as hepatitis B and HIV, impair vaccine responses, and may promote immune evasion by latent pathogens that reactivate when immune surveillance is diminished [171]. Another major concern is the potential of PD-1/PD-L1 agonists to undermine oncologic immune surveillance. By reinforcing inhibitory signaling, these agents can inactivate tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and promote PD-L1 expression on malignant cells, facilitating immune evasion. These effects parallel observations with JAK inhibitors, which, when used chronically, increase the risk of malignancy in rheumatic populations [172]. Although current data on PD-1 agonists are limited, the biological plausibility of tumor promotion through attenuated immune surveillance warrants careful consideration. Beyond infection and malignancy, PD-1 agonists may induce paradoxical inflammation. Clinical trials of the PD-1 agonist peresolimab have reported adverse events such as nasopharyngitis, gastroenteritis, mastitis, and rhinitis [170]. Although these events are less well characterized than those seen with checkpoint inhibitors in oncology, they reflect the inherent instability of immune regulation under artificial systemic checkpoint manipulation. Moreover, the effect of PD-1 agonists appears to be context-dependent and may vary with disease stage or patient genetics, raising concerns about unpredictable off-target effects [173]. Systemic biologics and gene therapies also face practical issues: lack of tissue specificity can cause unwanted systemic effects, and manufacturing complex proteins/cells is expensive [44]. High costs and the need for personalized cellular products (Tregs/DCs) limit accessibility. In summary, while PD-1/PD-L1 agonists show promise for dampening autoimmunity, careful design (e.g., targeted delivery, combination therapy) is required to mitigate infection or tumor risk and ensure safe, durable tolerance.

Recent advances in nanomedicine-based strategies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway for ADs

In recent decades, considerable efforts have been directed towards developing treatment strategies aimed at selectively dampening autoimmune reactions in specific tissues or organs while preserving overall immune function. The ultimate goals of these efforts are to restore immune balance and promote tissue healing. Despite the complexity of these objectives, recent innovations, such as Peresolimab, a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds and activates PD-1, have shown efficacy in patients with RA in a Phase 2 clinical trial published in NEJM, suggesting a potential novel treatment for ADs [170]. In addition to Peresolimab, several other ongoing clinical trials of PD-1 agonist antibodies are being conducted for ADs (Table 2). Moreover, nanomedicines offer promising approaches to enhance immune targeting, representing a transition from idealistic aspirations to practical solutions.

In this section, we will provide a brief overview of recent research advancements in reconstructing immune tolerance and restoring immune homeostasis at localized sites of ADs using nanomedicine-based strategies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway (Table 3).

Overview of recent advances in nanomedicine-based strategies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway for ADs

Polymeric nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles have gained significant attention in the immunotherapy of ADs due to their tunable size, biodegradability, and capacity for multi-functionalization [174]. Compared with free antibodies or checkpoint ligands, polymeric nanoparticles allow controlled and localized delivery of immune-modulatory agents, reducing systemic toxicity while enhancing therapeutic efficacy. In the context of PD-1/PD-L1-based interventions, polymeric NPs serve as platforms for either presenting PD-L1 ligands on their surface or delivering PD-1/PD-L1-activating molecules into inflamed tissues.

One of the most representative studies utilized a ROS-responsive polymeric nanocarrier system to deliver PD-L1-Fc fusion protein in a murine model of DSS-induced colitis. Tang et al. developed a ROS-sensitive α-cyclodextrin–based polymer (Oxi-αCD), which was conjugated with PD-L1-Fc and coated with a PEGylated lecithin shell [152] (Fig. 5A). This system leveraged the high ROS levels in inflamed intestinal tissue (Fig. 5B) to trigger targeted release of PD-L1-Fc, thereby locally activating PD-1 signaling on Teffs. The nanoparticles efficiently accumulated in the colon and significantly reduced the infiltration of proinflammatory cells and the expression of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6. Importantly, there was also a marked increase in Foxp3+ Tregs, indicating immune rebalancing toward tolerance (Fig. 5C). Compared with free PD-L1-Fc protein, the nanocarrier system showed superior protection against intestinal epithelial damage, highlighting the advantage of spatially controlled checkpoint activation.

Another compelling approach involves hybrid nanoparticles co-loaded with methotrexate (MTX) and surface-modified with recombinant PD-L1. Zhang et al. constructed PLGA-based particles (PLGA-PEG-PD-L1/MTX) that combine checkpoint activation with chemotherapeutic immunosuppression [46]. These hybrid nanoparticles selectively bind to PD-1+ CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, blocking their proliferation while promoting Treg generation. MTX further aids in reducing systemic inflammation and lymphocyte activation. In a T cell transfer model of multi-organ autoimmune inflammation, mice treated with the PD-L1/MTX hybrid NPs showed significant reductions in hepatic and splenic inflammation and cytokine storm. Compared to either free drug or unmodified NPs, this system achieved both potent immune suppression and immune tolerance reconstruction.

A further refinement in polymeric nanomedicine involves dual-loading of immunomodulatory agents. Researchers have investigated polymeric nanoparticles co-delivering small molecules such as TGF-β mimetics or vitamin D3 analogs together with PD-L1 constructs, aiming to create a synergistic tolerogenic microenvironment [175, 176]. Although these systems are still in early preclinical stages, the combinatorial potential of Polymeric nanoparticles for checkpoint and cytokine modulation offers a versatile strategy for complex autoimmune phenotypes like SLE or MS.

In summary, polymeric nanoparticles allow precise control over cargo release and surface ligand display, which is especially important in autoimmune therapy where over-suppression or systemic exposure could result in infection or malignancy risk. These systems demonstrate that PD-1/PD-L1 modulation, when spatially restricted and combined with traditional immunosuppressants, can reverse immune dysregulation in ADs while reducing systemic side effects.

Liposomes and lipid nanoparticles

Lipid-based nanoparticles, including traditional liposomes and modern lipid nanoparticles, are widely used in clinical drug delivery due to their high biocompatibility, ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic agents, and scalable manufacturing [177]. Their application in AD therapy, particularly for targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, has provided promising avenues to suppress autoreactive immune responses while minimizing systemic immunosuppression.

One elegant strategy was reported by Singh et al., who designed liposomes co-encapsulating an autoantigenic peptide (such as collagen II peptide) and the active form of vitamin D3 (calcitriol) to induce antigen-specific immune tolerance in experimental ADs [178]. These liposomes are constructed from phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol, with additional PEGylation to prolong circulation time (Fig. 6 A). Once administered subcutaneously, they drain to skin-associated lymph nodes, where they are preferentially taken up by PD-L1high DCs. Calcitriol modulates DC phenotype to a more tolerogenic state, reducing MHC II and CD40 expression, while the autoantigen presentation occurs in a non-inflammatory context. Most importantly, the induction of PD-L1 on DCs is a key mechanism, as it drives the expansion of antigen-specific Foxp3+ IL-10+ Tregs (Fig. 6B). In CIA and Goodpasture’s vasculitis models, this approach led to durable immune tolerance and disease suppression, with minimal off-target effects [178].

Another innovative use of lipid-based systems is seen in the delivery of nucleoside-modified mRNA encoding sPD-L1 [179]. Xu et al. developed DOTAP-based cationic lipid nanoparticles for targeted pulmonary delivery [179]. These lipid nanoparticles were used in a model of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) to locally express sPD-L1 in the lung epithelium. Unlike systemic PD-L1-Fc protein injections, which often have limited bioavailability and systemic toxicity, the mRNA-LNP system ensures local checkpoint protein expression where inflammation is highest. The PD-L1 expressed in situ engaged PD-1 on infiltrating T cells, leading to reduced leukocyte recruitment, cytokine production, and pulmonary edema. Mice treated with sPD-L1 mRNA lipid nanoparticles showed significantly improved lung histology and survival rates compared to controls. While this work was in the context of ARDS, the strategy is directly translatable to autoimmune pulmonary diseases such as lupus pneumonitis or autoimmune alveolitis.

Additionally, researchers are exploring liposomal systems that co-deliver PD-1 pathway activators with other immune-modulatory RNAs or peptides [180]. One example is liposomes containing siRNAs that silence PD-L1-suppressive miRNAs in DCs, thereby boosting endogenous PD-L1 expression on DCs in target tissues. Although such systems are still at the exploratory stage, they represent a hybrid strategy of gene regulation and checkpoint modulation using lipid platforms [181].

In essence, liposomal nanocarriers provide a safe and effective method for modulating the PD-1/PD-L1 axis through either direct ligand presentation (via mRNA or fusion protein) or by inducing PD-L1 expression in APCs. Their ability to drain to lymphoid tissues, protect labile cargo, and be engineered for specific tissue accumulation makes them strong candidates for tolerance restoration in autoimmune settings.

Exosomes

Exosomes, a subtype of EVs typically ranging from 30 to 150 nm in diameter, are secreted by virtually all cell types and play critical roles in intercellular communication [182]. Derived from endosomal pathways, exosomes retain the surface and cargo features of their parent cells, including membrane-bound proteins such as PD-L1, MHC molecules, and tetraspanins (CD9, CD63). Their natural targeting capability, low immunogenicity, and ability to traverse biological barriers make them highly attractive candidates for immune modulation in ADs [47].

In the context of PD-1/PD-L1-based strategies, exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem/stromal cells or regulatory immune cells can inherit and present immunosuppressive ligands like PD-L1 on their surface [183]. This allows them to directly engage PD-1 on autoreactive T cells or modulate APCs, restoring immune homeostasis without the need for artificial modification.

A representative example comes from studies using silk fibroin hydrogel encapsulated with olfactory ecto-mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (OE-MSC-EV) in CIA models [184]. These exosomes were found to express high levels of PD-L1 and could be isolated from OE-MSCs pre-treated with inflammatory stimuli (e.g., IFN-γ). Upon intravenous injection, PD-L1high OE-MSC-EV preferentially accumulated in secondary lymphoid organs and inflamed joints. Mechanistically, they inhibited the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in CXCR5+ T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, reducing their support for germinal center B cells and consequently dampening autoantibody production. This led to significant improvement in joint pathology, reduced inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α), and preservation of cartilage (Fig. 7).

In a separate study focusing on Sjögren’s syndrome, the same type of OE-MSC exosomes was shown to restore the suppressive function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), another immunoregulatory cell population implicated in autoimmune control. MDSCs in Sjögren’s models often lose their immunosuppressive phenotype due to chronic inflammation [185]. PD-L1+ exosomes restored MDSC functionality and suppressed Tfh cell activation, thus inhibiting downstream B cell overactivation and pathogenic autoantibody production. This led to decreased lymphocytic infiltration in the salivary glands and improved salivary flow rate—key therapeutic endpoints in Sjögren’s syndrome.

Other groups have begun exploring engineered exosomes, where PD-L1 is overexpressed on donor cells prior to EV isolation, or loaded with PD-L1 mRNA for in situ translation. For example, Fang Xu et al.. utilized lentivirus-mediated gene transfection technology to engineer PD-L1-expressing MSC-derived EVs (MSCs-EVs-PD-L1) for immune microenvironment modulation in ADs. MSCs-EVs-PD-L1 regulated different activated immune cells and induced immunosuppression in vitro. Notably, MSCs-EVs-PD-L1 accumulated significantly in inflamed tissues of UC and psoriasis mouse models, surpassing PD-L1+ MSC accumulation. In both UC and psoriasis models, MSCs-EVs-PD-L1 reshaped the inflammatory ecosystem within the local immune environment, demonstrating therapeutic efficacy [186]. This highlights the potential of using MSC-sEVs-PD-L1 as a natural delivery platform for the treatment of ADs, offering promising clinical applications. Additionally, Giacomo Casella et al.. investigated the induction of PD-L1 expression on target cells using MSC-derived EVs containing myelin antigen (Ol-EVs) for restoring immune tolerance in CNS autoimmunity. Intravenous administration of Ol-EVs effectively prevented and suppressed clinical disease in EAE models, dependent on myelin antigen presence. The therapeutic effects were mediated through monocytes, which upregulated PD-L1 expression in an interleukin 10 (IL-10)-dependent manner, resulting in encephalitogenic CD4+ T cell apoptosis. These findings highlight the potential of Ol-EVs to restore immune tolerance in CNS autoimmunity and underscore the role of induced PD-L1 expression in therapeutic effects [187]. In another study, Yang et al.. genetically engineered Raw264.7 macrophages to generate artificial EVs simultaneously overexpressing PD-L1 and Gal-9 (PD-L1–Gal-9 aEVs), which not only promoted effector T-cell apoptosis and enhanced Treg generation in vitro, but also significantly reversed new-onset hyperglycemia and suppressed pancreatic T-cell infiltration in NOD mice, thereby preserving β-cell function and delaying type 1 diabetes progression [188]. These approaches aim to boost the immunomodulatory efficacy and stability of exosomes, while maintaining their native tolerogenic properties.

Overall, exosome-based therapies represent a biomimetic and minimally invasive strategy to restore immune tolerance in ADs. Their ability to naturally express PD-L1 and interact with immune cells without provoking a strong immune response positions them as powerful tools for treating T cell–mediated autoimmunity.

Biomimetic and cell membrane-coated nanoparticles

Biomimetic nanomedicine—particularly nanoparticles coated with cellular membranes—has opened new frontiers in AD therapy by enabling immune evasion, tissue targeting, and immune modulation simultaneously. Unlike synthetic carriers, these “top-down” systems inherit membrane proteins from their source cells, including immunoregulatory ligands such as PD-L1, which can be directly used to engage PD-1 on pathogenic T cells [189]. When combined with controlled drug release systems, such nanoparticles can mimic natural immunosuppressive mechanisms while delivering anti-inflammatory or tolerogenic agents to inflamed sites [189].

One compelling example is from Guo et al., who developed immunosuppressive membrane-coated nanoparticles (IM-MNPs) by fusing IFN-γ–primed tumor cell membranes (lacking MHC-I to reduce immunogenicity) with dexamethasone (DXM)-loaded polymeric cores [189]. The surface of the IM-MNPs expressed PD-L1 and TIGIT ligands, allowing for co-inhibitory signaling to autoreactive T cells [189] (Fig. 8 A). In an MRL/lpr lupus mouse model, these IM-MNPs preferentially targeted CD4+ T cells in the spleen and kidneys, reducing their activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-17 A). Simultaneously, Foxp3+ Tregs were expanded, and lupus nephritis symptoms were markedly alleviated—evidenced by reduced glomerular damage and proteinuria. The inclusion of dexamethasone provided synergistic anti-inflammatory support while the membrane coating enhanced specificity and immune modulation (Fig. 8B).

Another notable strategy was employed in RA, where nanoparticles were coated with membranes from regulatory synoviocytes (FLSreg) [190]. These synoviocytes were pretreated with IFN-γ and rapamycin to upregulate PD-L1 and downregulate co-stimulatory markers. The resulting FIRN (FLSreg immune-regulatory nanoparticle) exhibited strong binding to inflamed synovial tissue and inhibited CD4+ T cell proliferation in a PD-L1–dependent manner. In CIA mice, FIRN treatment significantly reduced joint swelling, bone erosion, and inflammatory infiltration, while preserving systemic immune function. The ability to induce joint-localized immune suppression without systemic toxicity was a key advantage over systemic immunosuppressants.

A third example involves coating nanoparticles with IFN-γ–activated macrophage membranes to generate macrophage membrane-coated nanoemulsions (MP@NEs) [191]. Zhong et al. incorporated citrullinated antigen peptides (to enhance antigen specificity) and triptolide (a tolerogenic phytochemical) into the core. The PD-L1-rich macrophage membrane allowed the NPs to accumulate in arthritic joints and bind PD-1 on infiltrating T cells. The result was a significant increase in Tregs and Bregs, with reductions in pro-inflammatory Th17 cells, collagen-specific IgG, and inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α. Importantly, this system demonstrated potent antigen-specific tolerance induction, a feature critical for long-term disease remission without broad immune suppression.

These biomimetic systems reflect a highly translational direction in autoimmune nanotherapy. By customizing the membrane source—tumor cells, regulatory fibroblasts, macrophages—researchers can fine-tune surface expression of PD-L1 and other checkpoints, while pairing them with disease-relevant antigens or drugs. Their ability to target specific immune cell subsets or inflamed tissues, mimic immune-tolerant microenvironments, and minimize systemic exposure makes them uniquely suited for restoring immune balance in ADs.

Outlook of nanomedicine-based PD-1/PD-L1 strategies

The diverse applications of nanomedicine in modulating the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have opened new avenues for restoring immune tolerance in ADs. Across multiple nanocarrier systems—including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, exosomes, and biomimetic/cell membrane-coated nanoparticles—researchers have developed sophisticated delivery platforms. These platforms not only enable precise spatiotemporal control of PD-L1 or PD-1 signaling but also co-deliver immunomodulatory agents or autoantigens to inflamed tissues or immune-regulatory organs.

Each nanomedicine type brings unique advantages: (1) Polymeric nanoparticles offer tunable release profiles and surface functionalization for dual drug/checkpoint delivery (Fig. 9A). (2) Lipid-based systems, especially liposomes and lipid nanoparticles, provide high biocompatibility and lymph node targeting, and are well suited for peptide, vitamin D3, or mRNA delivery (Fig. 9B). (3) Exosomes, as natural vesicles, inherently carry PD-L1 and demonstrate potent immunomodulatory functions with minimal immunogenicity (Fig. 9C). (4) Biomimetic nanoparticles, coated with cell membranes from PD-L1–rich or tolerogenic cells, recapitulate the immunosuppressive microenvironment while enhancing targeting and immune cell-specific interactions (Fig. 9D).

Preclinical studies have repeatedly demonstrated that nanomedicines targeting PD-1/PD-L1 can reduce disease severity in models of RA, SLE, inflammatory bowel disease, and Sjögren’s syndrome by suppressing Teffs responses, expanding Tregs and Bregs, and mitigating cytokine storms. Importantly, many of these effects are achieved with lower systemic toxicity compared to traditional therapies.

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of nanomedicine-based strategies, their clinical translation still faces several interconnected challenges. The primary challenges lie in manufacturing and scalability. Many laboratory-scale formulations, particularly biomimetic and exosome-based systems, rely on batch processes such as cell-culture membrane harvesting or ultracentrifugation, which are difficult to scale under GMP while maintaining key quality attributes (e.g., size distribution, surface protein composition, payload loading, sterility) [192]. For lipid-based systems, particularly lipid nanoparticles that have enabled siRNA and mRNA therapeutics, key variables such as internal payload distribution, the proportion of empty particles, and the sensitivity of particle assembly to process parameters (e.g., mixing kinetics, lipid ratios) significantly influence pharmacokinetics and therapeutic potency [193]. This complicates the lot release criteria and bioequivalence for follow-on products. Standardized characterization methods and robust quality control assays are therefore urgently required to satisfy regulatory expectations. Another challenge relates to the immunogenicity of nanocarriers. While lipid nanoparticles have demonstrated clinical feasibility in mRNA vaccines, their potential to activate innate immune sensors, induce complement activation, or elicit anti-PEG antibodies remains a significant concern, particularly in patients with immune dysregulation [194]. Similarly, biomimetic coatings or exosomal proteins may introduce unintended antigens, thereby altering immune tolerance or provoking off-target responses. These risks underscore the importance of immunotoxicity profiling early in development. Long-term safety also warrants careful consideration. Unlike cancer immunotherapy, ADs may require chronic dosing, raising concerns regarding nanoparticle accumulation, durability of immune modulation, and risks of opportunistic infection or malignancy associated with sustained PD-1/PD-L1 engagement [195]. Comprehensive studies on biodistribution, clearance, and immunotoxicity are therefore indispensable. Finally, regulatory and clinical translation pathways remain complex. Nanomedicines that combine biological components (e.g., exosomal proteins, membrane coatings) with synthetic carriers often fall at the interface of biologics, medical devices, and advanced therapy medicinal products. This complicates regulatory classification, release assays, and trial design. The recently proposed DELIVER framework highlights the importance of integrating manufacturability, validated analytical characterization, bio–nano interaction studies, and regulatory strategy into the early stages of nanomedicine development, rather than treating these aspects as downstream considerations [196]. Early dialogue with agencies, adoption of scalable platforms (such as microfluidic-based LNP manufacture), and harmonized guidelines for characterization and safety assessment may help accelerate clinical progress.

In conclusion, nanomedicine-based PD-1/PD-L1 agonists hold substantial promise for the treatment of ADs, yet their successful translation will depend on parallel progress in scalable manufacturing, immunogenicity de-risking, and regulatory science. Addressing these challenges systematically will be critical to ensure that these innovative therapeutics can be advanced from preclinical proof-of-concept toward clinical benefit.

Conclusion

ADs arise from dysregulated T cell responses and impaired immune tolerance, with the PD-1/PD-L1 axis playing a pivotal role in maintaining immune homeostasis (Fig. 3). Enhancing PD-1/PD-L1 signaling has demonstrated therapeutic potential in re-establishing T cell regulation, reducing tissue inflammation, and promoting Treg function. Nanomedicine approaches—such as ROS-responsive nanoparticles delivering PD-L1-Fc—offer a new frontier in precision immunomodulation, enabling targeted delivery of checkpoint agonists directly to inflamed tissues and minimizing systemic exposure. In DSS-induced colitis models, such PD-L1-Fc–loaded nanoparticles significantly alleviated colonic inflammation and reshaped immune cell cytokine profiles, illustrating superior efficacy and safety compared to systemic PD-L1 administration (Fig. 5) [152].

Despite these advances, the clinical translation of PD-1/PD-L1–targeted nanotherapeutics remains constrained by challenges in scalable GMP manufacturing, safety, and regulatory complexity. Many laboratory-scale formulations rely on batch processes that are difficult to scale under GMP conditions. For lipid-based delivery systems, numerous formulation variables influence therapeutic efficacy, thereby complicating quality control. Immunogenicity and long-term safety concerns, including risks of accumulation, infection, and malignancy, further impede clinical advancement. Regulatory approval is complicated also by the composite nature of these systems. To address these barriers, early and integrated development strategies—such as scalable manufacturing platforms, standardized analytical assays, and proactive engagement with regulatory agencies—are crucial to facilitate the translation of these nanotherapeutics from bench to bedside.

Looking ahead, translation of these novel therapeutics into clinical settings requires meticulous optimization of delivery platforms, dosing strategies, and administration regimens. Future studies should investigate long-term safety—including infection rates, oncogenic risks, and biodistribution—in relevant animal models. Elucidating the interactions between nanocarrier-mediated PD-1/PD-L1 activation and immune cell subsets will additionally guide rational design. With rigorous mechanistic studies and robust preclinical validation, PD-1/PD-L1–targeted nanomedicines could establish a new paradigm for durable, precise, and personalized treatment of ADs.
