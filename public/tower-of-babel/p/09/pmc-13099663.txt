P2Y2 Purinergic Receptor Promotes Trafficking of Hematopoietic Stem/Progenitor Cells (HSPCs)
Authors: Mateusz Adamiak, Katarzyna Brzezniakiewicz-Janus, Magda Molenda, Janina Ratajczak, Yong Tang, Magdalena Kucia, Mariusz Z. Ratajczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The family of P2Y purinergic receptors, which are G protein-coupled seven-transmembrane receptors, is activated by various purine and pyrimidine nucleotides, including adenosine triphosphate (ATP), adenosine diphosphate (ADP), uridine triphosphate (UTP), uridine diphosphate (UDP), and UDP-glucose. To date, eight P2Y receptors have been identified in humans: P2Y1, P2Y2, P2Y4, P2Y6, P2Y11, P2Y12, P2Y13, and P2Y14, whereas in mice, seven have been identified because P2Y11 is not present. Among these receptors, only P2Y2 and P2Y11 in human cells respond to extracellular ATP (eATP); since P2Y11 is absent in murine cells, the P2Y2 receptor alone responds to the eATP gradient. In our previous work, we established the role of several P2X receptors in the trafficking of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) and detected considerable redundancy in responsiveness to eATP. Because the P2Y2 receptor is also activated by eATP and is expressed on murine HSPCs, we investigated its role in eATP-mediated trafficking of these cells. We focused on chemotaxis and pharmacological mobilization of bone marrow (BM) cells into peripheral blood (PB). In this report, we ruled out a role for other P2Y receptors in HSPC trafficking, as ligands such as ADP, UTP, UDP, UDP-glucose, and GTP did not chemoattract clonogenic HSPCs, although they attracted other non-clonogenic BMMNCs. To examine the specific role of P2Y2 in HSPC trafficking, we used the small-molecule inhibitor AR-C118925XX of this receptor. We observed that the migration of human and murine HSPCs in Transwell chambers toward eATP was inhibited after exposure to AR-C118925XX. Furthermore, administration of this inhibitor during pharmacological mobilization of HSPCs in mice challenged with G-CSF or AMD3100 resulted in reduced mobilization compared to control animals. We also observed impaired mobilization of other types of BM-residing stem/progenitor cells, including mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), endothelial progenitors (EPCs), and very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs). These findings indicate that, along with ionotropic P2X receptors, the P2Y2 receptor contributes to optimal mobilization of bone marrow-residing stem cells, and that eATP is the only promigratory nucleotide among all purinergic signaling ligands for HSPCs.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) reside in stem cell niches within the bone marrow (BM), with some circulating at low levels in peripheral blood (PB). The number of circulating HSPCs follows a circadian rhythm, peaking in the morning and reaching a low point late at night [1]. These cells are also released into PB in response to tissue or organ injuries and fever, and their numbers can increase more than 100-fold during pharmacological mobilization after administration of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) or the CXCR4 receptor antagonist AMD3100 (Plerixafor) [2]. In addition to HSPCs, other types of stem/progenitor cells residing in the BM, including mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), endothelial progenitors (EPCs), and very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs), are also mobilized into PB [3].

In our previous research, we demonstrated the significant roles of complement cascade cleavage fragments (C3a and C5a) and purinergic signaling mediators (ATP) in the trafficking of HSPCs, both during mobilization into PB and in their homing to the BM after transplantation [4, 5]. Specifically, extracellular ATP (eATP) is a vital component of alarmins released from cells in response to stress, and pharmacological mobilization exemplifies stress related to sterile inflammation of the BM caused by G-CSF or AMD3100 administration or by myeloablative conditioning for transplantation [6].

We have already investigated the positive role of P2X purinergic receptors, which are specifically activated by eATP, in mobilization and demonstrated an essential role for P2X1, P2X4, and P2X7 receptors in the optimal release of HSPCs from BM and their navigation after transplantation from PB to BM, followed by homing and engraftment [7, 8]. As also demonstrated, P1 receptors activated by adenosine have an opposite role [9].

It is important to note that purinergic signaling involves not only purines (ATP, ADP, GTP) but also pyrimidines (UTP, UDP, UDP-glucose). All of these mediators activate eight G-protein coupled receptors from the P2Y receptor family []. Among these, P2Y2 and P2Y11 respond only to eATP. Since mice do not express P2Y11, P2Y2 is the only potential candidate for eATP stimulation [10]. To investigate the role of this receptor in the trafficking of murine HSPCs, we used a specific inhibitor, AR-C118925XX [11]. We observed that bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMNCs) respond in Transwell chambers to ATP, ADP, UTP, and UDP by chemotaxis, but only clonogenic HSPCs respond to eATP, and their migration was affected by exposure to the P2Y2 inhibitor. Furthermore, administering AR-C118925XX during pharmacological mobilization of HSPCs in mice after G-CSF or AMD3100 administration resulted in reduced mobilization compared to control animals. We also observed impaired mobilization of other types of BM-residing stem/progenitor cells, including mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), endothelial progenitors (EPCs), and very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs).

Based on this, our work provides new insights into the role of all eATP-stimulated receptors in HSPC trafficking. Additionally, we present evidence that, unlike eATP, other purinergic signaling mediators—such as ADP, UTP, UDP, UDP-glucose, and GTP—are not significantly involved in the chemoattraction of HSPCs.

Material and Methods

Animals

Pathogen-free, 6–8-week-old female C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME; USA) or the Central Laboratory for Experimental Animals at the Medical University of Warsaw. Before bone marrow collection, the mice were housed in the animal facility under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 AM to 7:00 PM) with constant access to water and standard rodent food. All studies were conducted in accordance with the European act on the protection of animals used for scientific or educational purposes and with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Warsaw Medical University (Warsaw, Poland).

Migration Assay

The RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used for the Transwell migration assay. A 650-μl volume of medium with or without stromal-derived factor 1 (SDF-1, 5 ng/ml, PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), ATP (10µm), ADP (10µm), GTP (10µm), UTP (10µm), UDP (10µm), UDP-glucose (1 µM), and GTP (10µm), all from SigmaAldrich, was prepared. An aliquot (1 × 106 cells per 100 μl) of murine BMMNCs, exposed (or not, control) to AR-C118925XX (10µM for 1 h), was loaded onto the upper chamber, separated by a 5-μm pore-size insert. The plate was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Following incubation, an aliquot of cells from the lower chamber was harvested, split, and scored by FACS analysis. Briefly, the cells were gated according to their forward-scatter (FSC) and side-scatter (SSC) parameters and counted during a 30-s acquisition at a high flow rate. The remaining BMMNCs from the lower chamber were resuspended in a human methylcellulose base medium provided by the manufacturer (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), supplemented with murine granulocyte/monocyte stimulating factor (mGM-CSF, 25 ng/ml) and murine interleukin 3 (mIL-3, 10 ng/ml) to determine the number of colony-forming unit-granulocyte/monocyte (CFU-GM) colonies. Next, the cultures were incubated for 7 days at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The CFU-GM colonies were then counted under an inverted microscope.

Drug Administration in Animals

In mobilization studies, mice were injected subcutaneously with Granulocyte-Colony Stimulating Factor (G-CSF, 150 μg/kg daily; Amgen, Thousand Oaks, CA, USA) for 3 days or with a single dose of AMD3100 (SigmaAldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) (5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally (i.p.)). Experimental group mice also received injections of the P2Y2 inhibitor AR-C118925XX (Tocris, Minneapolis, MN, USA) (10 mg/kg/dose, i.p.), administered prior to and during mobilization for a total of 4 days.

Isolation of Peripheral Blood and Bone Marrow

Peripheral blood (PB) and bone marrow (BM) samples were collected from all groups of mice. Briefly, 50 μl of PB was withdrawn from the retro-orbital plexus or tail vein into EDTA-coated microvette tubes (Sarstedt Inc., Newton, NC, USA) for hematologic analysis. The blood samples were run on an Exigo hematology analyzer (Boule Diagnostics, Spanga, Sweden) within 2 h of collection. PB was also collected after opening the abdomen under inhalation anesthesia (isoflurane 3% for induction; 1.5% for maintenance) from the posterior vena cava (with a 25-gauge needle and a 1-ml syringe containing 250 U heparin). Red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed with hypotonic lysis buffer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and PB mononuclear cells (PBMNCs) were obtained by centrifugation. BM mononuclear cells (BMMNCs) were flushed from the tibias and femurs with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). RBCs in the BM were lysed and washed, and BMMNCs were resuspended in PBS or RPMI-1640 medium (Corning, New York, NY, USA) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning, New York, NY, USA) as necessary.

Mobilization Analysis

Peripheral blood was withdrawn from the retro-orbital plexus and used for white blood cell (WBC) counts. The blood samples were analyzed on an Exigo hematology analyzer (Boule Diagnostics). PB blood isolated from the vena cava was lysed, and PBMNCs were isolated as described above. PBMNCs were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% FBS. The PBMNCs were cultured in a human methylcellulose-based medium (R&D Systems) supplemented with 25 ng/ml recombinant murine granulocyte/macrophage colony-stimulating factor (mGM-CSF, PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NY, USA) and 10 ng/ml recombinant murine interleukin 3 (mIL-3, PeproTech). Cells were incubated for 7 days (37 °C, 95% humidity, and 5% CO2). After 7 days of incubation, the CFU-GM colonies were scored using a simple inverted microscope (Olympus, Center Valley, PA, USA).

For staining of Lin − /Sca-1 + /c-Kit + (SKL cells), Lin − /Sca-1 + /CD45 + (HSCs), Sca-1 + /Lin − /CD45 − (VSELs), Lin − /CD45 − /CD31 + (EPCs), and Lin − /CD45 − /CD31 − /CD90 + (MSCs), the following monoclonal antibodies were used: FITC–anti-CD117 (c-Kit, clone 2B8; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and PE–Cy5–anti-mouse Ly-6 A/E (Sca-1, clone D7; eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). All anti-mouse lineage marker antibodies, including anti-CD45R (B220, clone RA3-6B2), anti-Ter-119 (clone TER-119), anti-CD11b (clone M1/70), anti-T cell receptor β (clone H57–597), anti-Gr-1 (clone RB6-8C5), anti-TCRγδ (clone GL3), and anti-CD45 (clone 30-F11), were conjugated with PE; anti-CD31 (clone MEC 13.3) was conjugated with APC; and anti-CD90.2 (clone 30-H12) was conjugated with BV510. These antibodies were purchased from BD Biosciences. Staining was performed in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% FBS. All monoclonal antibodies were added at saturating concentrations, and cells were incubated for 30 min on ice, washed twice, and analyzed on the BD FACSVerse instrument (BD Biosciences).

Statistical Analysis

All results are reported as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were evaluated using multiple unpaired t-tests, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ns – not significant; * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001.

P2Y Family Member P2Y2 Receptors are also Involved in HSPC Bigration

Our previous publications revealed that P2X receptors play an important role in HSPC responsiveness to the eATP gradient. Blockade or depletion of P2X1, P2X4, and P2X7 leads to decreased chemotactic responsiveness and impaired egress of these cells from BM into PB, as observed during pharmacological mobilization or homing after transplantation to BM [7, 8].

Since one of the P2Y receptors in mice, namely P2Y2, also responds to eATP stimulation, we tested whether it is involved in HSPC migration. Our preliminary Transwell data showed that HSPC migration along an eATP gradient was inhibited by ~ 20% after blocking the P2Y2 receptor with the small-molecule inhibitor AR-C118925XX. Based on these encouraging results, we used this inhibitor in our HSPC mobilization studies (Fig. 2). Discussion

The key findings of this brief report are that among the purinergic signaling ligands that activate the P2Y receptor family, only P2Y2, which responds to eATP, is involved in promoting HSPC trafficking. To support this, among all the purinergic signaling ligands, eATP alone is a significant chemoattractant for HSPCs. Other purinergic signaling ligands, by activating other P2Y receptors, chemoattract only non-clonogenic BMMN cells.

HSPCs are retained in BM niches, and, as we postulated, their egress from BM into PB is mediated by the induction of sterile inflammation in the BM microenvironment [6]. Potent pro-mobilizing agents such as G-CSF and AMD3100 activate a proteolytic microenvironment in BM and the complement cascade (ComC) in PB [3–5]. Recent data indicate that the involvement of innate immune cells in mobilization (e.g., leukocytes, monocytes, and dendritic cells) [14, 15] and the activation of liver-derived ComC [6] and complement components within HSPCs (complosome) [16, 17] are crucial for this process. In addition to ComC, purinergic signaling also plays an important role in the induction of sterile inflammation in BM [6]. The major signaling mediator of this pathway is eATP. This purinergic mediator is also a major danger molecular pattern molecule (DAMP) or alarmin released from stressed cells. Since alarmins, in addition to ComC, are part of the soluble arm of innate immunity, this suggests the challenging concept that purinergic signaling could be a “lost tribe” of innate immunity. In fact, as we demonstrated, both ComC and purinergic signaling are mutually activated during sterile inflammation [6].

In addition to eATP, several other minor mediators of purinergic signaling, including other purines (ADP, AMP, GTP, GDP) and pyrimidines (UTP, UDP), have been reported to be involved in hematopoietic cell migration [12, 13]. Our data, however, indicate that these ligands activate a family of P2Y G protein-coupled receptors and chemoattract BM mononuclear cells; eATP alone is a significant chemoattractant for clonogenic HSPCs.

Mobilization of HSPCs remains a clinical challenge, as a significant number of patients are classified as poor mobilizers in response to G-CSF or AMD3100 [18, 19]. Therefore, it is important to identify this defect at the molecular level. Our work in murine models of mobilization demonstrated that eATP is an important promoter of optimal mobilization. This signaling molecule acts through activation of P2X1, P2X4, and P2X7 receptors. Blockade of these receptors or defects in their expression on cells results in impaired mobilization [7, 8]. Here, we identified another receptor, P2Y2, from the P2Y family, that is also involved in the migration and mobilization of HSPCs in response to eATP. Together, these results indicate redundancy among purinergic signaling receptors in orchestrating optimal mobilization.

In summary, the P2Y2 receptor is a promising new target for optimizing HSPC mobilization with small-molecule inhibitors. More research is needed to evaluate its role in HSPC homing and engraftment after transplantation. Additionally, it would be important to conduct similar studies in P2Y2-KO mice. However, considering that AR–C 118925XX is a potent, selective, competitive, and reversible P2Y2 receptor antagonist, we expect to see similar results.
