Predictors and nomogram for upstaging to invasive breast carcinoma in ductal carcinoma in situ diagnosed by ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy
Authors: Xiaoli Zhang, Yanning Zhang, Junfeng Zhao, Wanwan Wen, Jianmin Zhao, Lanyan Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuctal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) cases diagnosed by ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy (US-CNB) carry a risk of postoperative upstaging to invasive breast carcinoma, complicating clinical management. This study aimed to investigate clinicopathological and ultrasound (US) predictors for postoperative upstaging and to develop a nomogram for individualized risk prediction.MethodsA total of 240 patients with 246 DCIS lesions diagnosed by US-CNB were enrolled in this retrospective study from May 2013 to January 2025. Clinicopathological and US features were compared using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression identified predictors of upstaging to DCIS with invasive components (DCIS-IC). A nomogram was developed and internally validated. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Calibration was evaluated through calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test, and clinical utility was examined using decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsAmong all the lesions, 161 (65.4%) were diagnosed as pure DCIS, while 85 (34.6%) were upstaged to DCIS-IC, including 37 (15.0% of total) with microinvasive carcinoma. Age [per 1-year increase, odds ratio (OR) =1.04; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–1.06; P=0.01], Ki-67 >20% (OR =2.56; 95% CI: 1.35–4.86; P=0.004), and suspicious axillary lymph node (ALN) on US (OR =3.00; 95% CI: 1.07–8.45; P=0.04) were independent predictors of postoperative upstaging to DCIS-IC. The nomogram showed moderate discrimination with an apparent area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.65–0.78), which was internally validated as 0.70 (95% CI: 0.66–0.72) using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. It demonstrated good calibration (H-L test, P=0.86). The DCA showed that the nomogram provided net benefit across a threshold probability range of 20% to 88% compared to default strategies. Although larger tumor size (>1 cm; P=0.02) and non-circumscribed mass margins (P=0.03) were associated with upstaging in univariate analysis, they were not retained as independent predictors in the multivariate model.ConclusionsThe nomogram incorporating age, Ki-67, and suspicious ALN on US effectively predicts DCIS upstaging risk in cases diagnosed by US-CNB and may assist in clinical decision-making. US characteristics (size >1 cm, non-circumscribed mass margins) may provide supplementary information but require further validation.Keywords: Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), invasive carcinoma, upstaging, ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy (US-CNB)

Key findings

• The nomogram incorporating independent predictors [age per 1 year increase, Ki-67 >20%, and suspicious axillary lymph node (ALN) on ultrasound (US)] demonstrated clinically meaningful predictive value, showing moderate discrimination [apparent area under the curve (AUC) =0.72, bootstrap-corrected AUC =0.70] with good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, P=0.86).

• Lesion size >1 cm and non-circumscribed mass margins on US may provide supplementary information for assessing ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) upstaging risk.

What is known and what is new?

• A proportion of DCIS cases diagnosed by ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy (US-CNB) are upstaged to invasive carcinoma upon surgical excision.

• The study developed a multivariate prediction model and developed a nomogram to guide clinical decision-making for DCIS patients initially diagnosed by US-CNB.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians may utilize these predictors and the nomogram to preoperatively stratify high-risk DCIS cases, optimizing surgical planning.

• Multidisciplinary workflows should now incorporate these validated independent risk factors to guide DCIS decision-making.

Introduction

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is characterized by the proliferation of neoplastic epithelial cells confined to the mammary ducts without invading the basement membrane, accounting for 20–25% of all breast cancers (1). Clinically, ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy (US-CNB) has become a routine method for sampling breast lesions. However, due to the limited tissue volume obtained by US-CNB, discrepancies between preoperative and postoperative pathological diagnoses are common, posing a significant clinical challenge. Notably, studies report that up to 53.3% of cases initially diagnosed as DCIS are upstaged to invasive carcinoma upon surgical excision (2). This phenomenon of postoperative pathological upstaging substantially affects the clinical management.

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines do not recommend routine sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) in patients undergoing breast conserving therapy for DCIS (3). Furthermore, evidence indicates that even in mastectomy for DCIS, SLNB may represent overtreatment unless there are high-risk features with potential upstaging to invasive carcinoma (4). However, due to concerns about missed invasive components in DCIS diagnosed by US-CNB, SLNB is frequently performed, resulting in unnecessary complications and overtreatment in cases of pure DCIS (5). Therefore, accurate preoperative differentiation between pure DCIS and DCIS with invasive components (hereafter referred to as DCIS-IC) is critical to guide personalized treatment strategies. Although previous studies have explored risk stratification for postoperative upstaging to DCIS-IC, with some focusing on clinical parameters, others on imaging features, and still others combining both approaches, their clinical utility remains limited. Key limitations include the absence of clinically implementable nomograms in most studies and insufficient internal validation of proposed models, collectively limiting their translational potential (6-8).

This retrospective study aimed to investigate clinicopathological and ultrasound (US) risk predictors for postoperative upstaging to DCIS-IC in DCIS initially diagnosed by US-CNB, to develop a nomogram for individualized risk prediction and to validate the model through internal validation. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-169/rc).

Methods

Data collection from patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2022-P2-030-01). Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study. We retrospectively identified 330 patients with US-CNB diagnosed DCIS lesions treated at Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University between May 2013 and January 2025 from our database. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who had not undergone surgery in our institution (n=36), missing US images or detailed clinical data (n=39), and coexistence with other types of breast tumors (n=15). Finally, 240 patients with 246 breast lesions who met the inclusion criteria were included in this study cohort; of these, 6 patients had bilateral DCIS. All eligible cases during the study period were consecutively enrolled. Based on surgical pathology, patients were divided into two groups: pure DCIS (no invasive component) and DCIS-IC, including microinvasive carcinoma, defined as tumor clusters ≤1 mm beyond the basement membrane according to American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition (9) (Figure 1). This cohort had a mean age of 55.5±10.7 (range, 27–78) years. For model development, all cases were included with internal validation performed by bootstrap resampling. The clinicopathological and US imaging data were reviewed for each lesion.

Tumor pathological analysis

Two pathologists (Y.Z. and Jianmin Zhao) independently extracted and reviewed all pathological data from original biopsy and surgical reports, blinded to clinical and imaging data. The following parameters were systematically recorded from pathological reports: DCIS grade (Van Nuys criteria: low/intermediate, or high) (10). Estrogen receptor (ER)/progesterone receptor (PR) (positive: ≥1% tumor nuclei with positive staining) (11). Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) (0 and 1+ were considered negative, 3+ was considered positive, and 2+ was indeterminate) (12). Ki-67 (cutoff: 20%, based on St. Gallen 2015) (13).

US imaging and assessment

All patients underwent real-time grayscale and color Doppler ultrasound examinations within 2 weeks prior to surgery, using high-frequency linear transducers (10–15 MHz, Philips IU22/IU Elite/EPIQ 5 or Mindray Resona R7). Two fellowship-trained radiologists (X.Z. with 16 and L.Q. with 13 years of experience) independently assessed preoperative US images on a dedicated workstation, blinded to final pathology. Any discrepant interpretations were resolved through consensus discussion.

Lesions were classified as either mass lesions or non-mass lesions (NMLs) according to the Japan Association of Breast and Thyroid Sonology (JABTS) guidelines (14). Mass lesions were defined as three-dimensional space-occupying lesions with convex margins, distinct from surrounding tissue, characterized according to the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) lexicon for shape, orientation, margin, echo pattern, and posterior echo features (15). NMLs were defined as lesions lacking three-dimensional mass effects in at least two scanning planes and were subdivided into five types: abnormalities of the ducts, hypoechoic areas, architectural distortion, multiple small cysts, and echogenic foci without a hypoechoic area (14). For all lesions, the following parameters were recorded: US lesion size (maximal diameter), microcalcification (defined as small calcium deposits, size 0.1–1.0 mm), blood flow (graded according to the Adler scale, assessed with 0, I, II, and III) (16) and suspicious axillary lymph node (ALN; defined by an asymmetric hypoechoic cortex ≥3 mm, displacement or absence of the hilar structure) (17,18).

US-CNB procedure

After obtaining informed consent, two experienced breast radiologists (L.Q. and Junfeng Zhao) performed US-CNB with a 16-gauge automated biopsy gun (Sterile; TSK Laboratory, Tochigi, Japan). Two or three core samples per lesion were obtained for each case.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., NY, USA), R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation, Austria). Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with 5 imputed datasets.

Comparative analyses between pure DCIS and DCIS-IC groups were conducted using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Variables with P<0.1 in the univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors of upstaging to DCIS-IC (with retention criteria of P<0.05).

A nomogram was developed to predict upstaging risk by summing points based on patient characteristics and mapping the total to the probability scale via the nomogram’s reference line. Model performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Discrimination was assessed using both the apparent area under the curve (AUC) and bootstrap-corrected AUC (derived from 1,000 bootstrap replicates for internal validation), while calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the net benefit of the model across a range of threshold probabilities. Statistical significance was defined as a P<0.05.

Results

Histological upstaging

Among the 246 lesions, 161 (65.4%) showed postoperative histopathological evidence of pure DCIS, while 85 (34.6%) were upstaged to DCIS-IC, including 37 (15.0% of total) with microinvasive carcinoma.

Clinicopathological features

Clinically, patients with DCIS-IC were significantly older than those with pure DCIS (mean age: 57.3±11.0 vs. 54.5±10.4 years, P=0.049), with a higher proportion aged >50 years (76.5% vs. 63.4%, P=0.04). Although nipple discharge (11.8% vs. 6.8%) and palpable mass (27.1% vs. 17.4%) were more frequently observed in DCIS-IC compared with pure DCIS, these differences did not reach statistical significance (P=0.056).

Pathologically, the Ki-67 index was significantly higher in DCIS-IC than in pure DCIS (42.4% vs. 20.5%, P<0.001). The proportion of high-grade nuclei was higher in DCIS-IC compared with pure DCIS, though the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.08), for multivariable regression, missing data (6.1%) were handled through multiple imputation. No significant differences were observed in ER, PR, or HER2 expression between the two groups (Table 1).

US features

Three ultrasonographic features showed significant differences between pure DCIS and DCIS-IC: suspicious ALN on US were more frequent in DCIS-IC than in pure DCIS (15.3% vs. 4.3%, P=0.003), lesions >1 cm on US were more frequent in DCIS-IC than in pure DCIS (83.5% vs. 70.2%, P=0.02), and non-circumscribed mass margins were more frequent in DCIS-IC than in pure DCIS (98.0% vs. 86.6%, P=0.03).

DCIS-IC showed a non-significantly higher prevalence of complex cystic and solid components than pure DCIS (11.8% vs. 3.7%, P=0.09). The two groups showed similar ultrasound characteristics in both mass/NML distribution and NMLs subclassification. Hypoechoic areas were the dominant NMLs morphology (>80% prevalence in both cohorts), followed by abnormalities of the duct, while architectural distortion, multiple small cysts, and echogenic foci without a hypoechoic area were less frequently observed (all P>0.05). No significant differences existed in other mass characteristics (shape, orientation, posterior features), BI-RADS classifications, microcalcifications and blood flow distribution (all P>0.05) (Table 2).

Development of personalized prediction models

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified age [per 1-year increase, odds ratio (OR) =1.04; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–1.06; P=0.01], Ki-67 >20% (OR =2.56; 95% CI: 1.35–4.86; P=0.004), and suspicious ALN on US (OR =3.00; 95% CI: 1.07–8.45; P=0.04) as independent predictors of DCIS upstaging to DCIS-IC (Table 3). Of note, mass margin (P=0.03) and mass echo pattern (P=0.09) were not included in the multivariate model because their evaluation was limited to the mass lesion subgroup (n=133), whereas the nomogram aimed to provide a universally applicable tool for all DCIS lesions. Based on these independent predictors, we developed a nomogram that quantifies upstaging risk through point summation of patient characteristics, with total points converted to predicted probabilities via the reference line (Figure 6).

Performance and validation of the nomogram

The nomogram showed moderate discrimination with an apparent AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.65–0.78) and a bootstrap-corrected AUC of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.66–0.72) derived from 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The model showed good calibration by H-L test (P=0.86). Clinical validation through DCA confirmed the nomogram’s net benefit across threshold probabilities of 20% to 88% (Figure 7).

Discussion

DCIS represents a biologically and clinically heterogeneous disease, with a variable risk of progression to invasive carcinoma. Postoperative upstaging to DCIS-IC remains a significant clinical challenge due to the potential underestimation of invasive components. In this study, we identified key preoperative predictors of upstaging, including age (per 1-year increase), Ki-67 >20%, and suspicious ALN on US, and developed a clinically applicable nomogram.

The overall postoperative upstaging rate to DCIS-IC in this study was 34.6%, including 15.0% microinvasive carcinoma, which was categorized as DCIS-IC due to its more aggressive biological and clinical behavior compared with pure DCIS (19-21). Our findings are consistent with the previously reported range of 10–53.3% (2,22).

Our nomogram identified age (per 1-year increase) as an independent predictor of upstaging, which contrasts with the younger age distribution reported in prior DCIS-IC cohorts (23). This discrepancy may primarily stem from methodological differences, particularly due to their magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based assessment and inclusion of both vacuum-assisted biopsy and core needle biopsy (CNB). Age showed significant associations both as continuous and categorical (≤50 vs. >50 years) variables, with the continuous form selected for precise individualized risk stratification when combined with other predictors such as Ki-67 and ALN status.

Ki-67 has been widely recognized as a valuable biomarker for assessing cellular proliferation in breast cancer. In this study, the Ki-67 index was significantly higher in DCIS-IC than in pure DCIS (P<0.001), consistent with previous studies (24-26), and multivariate analysis further identified Ki-67 as an independent predictor, reinforcing its role in risk stratification. Notably, 79.5% of pure DCIS in this study showed low Ki-67, suggesting that proliferative quiescence may maintain ductal confinement. However, this contrasts with a previous report which found comparable Ki-67 between pure DCIS and lesions subsequently upstaged to DCIS-IC, but significantly higher Ki-67 in invasive components compared with their paired DCIS elements within individual lesions (27). This observation raises an important question: does increased proliferation drive invasion, or is it merely a consequence of invasive progression? A “growth or go” hypothesis has been proposed, suggesting that nutrient limitations within mammary ducts may force tumor cells to either proliferate slowly or acquire invasive potential to seek alternative resources (28). Our finding of predominantly low Ki-67 in pure DCIS aligns with the “growth” phenotype, while the elevated proliferation in DCIS-IC supports the transition to the “go” state. It should be noted that the interpretation of Ki-67 remains challenging due to inconsistent international cutoffs (13) and interobserver variability (29).

Furthermore, our study confirms the independent predictive value of US suspicious ALN for DCIS upstaging. Unlike previous studies incorporating mammographic/MRI or hematologic parameters (8,30), our ultrasound-only approach maintained this association, demonstrating its practical utility. Although preoperative biopsy remains the diagnostic standard, this ultrasound feature may aid risk stratification, especially if biopsy is not feasible. The wide CI likely reflects the limited number of ALN-positive cases in this cohort, but the coexistence of suspicious ALN with other high-risk features (e.g., Ki-67 >20%), should prompt heightened clinical vigilance.

Nuclear grade is one of the most important discriminating features of DCIS. While the meta-analysis of 7,350 cases (6) reported higher upstaging rates in high-grade versus non-high-grade lesions, our results showed a non-significant trends toward higher upstaging in high-grade lesions (P=0.08). This apparent discrepancy is more likely attributable to interobserver variability (31) rather than the multiple imputation performed for 6.1% of cases with missing data, given the small proportion of imputed values. Notably, a previous study has suggested that necrosis status may serve as another valuable predictor for upstaging risk (32). However, we did not include it in the analysis due to incomplete data in this retrospective cohort. Future prospective studies with standardized necrosis assessment protocols are warranted to validate its predictive value.

US imaging characteristics showed significant associations with upstaging risk. While DCIS-IC lesions were more frequently >1 cm in size than pure DCIS, this association was not retained in multivariate analysis, likely due to confounding by other factors such as proliferative activity (e.g., high Ki-67 tumors tending to be larger). This finding contrasts with prior studies identifying larger thresholds of >2 cm (33,34) or 3.2 cm (35). Notably, recognizing that NMLs represent up to 60% of DCIS cases (36,37), we applied unified NMLs evaluation criteria from both the JABTS guidelines and forthcoming BI-RADS 6th edition (38), provided new insights. This advanced classification revealed that non-circumscribed margins in mass lesions were significantly more common in DCIS-IC than pure DCIS, a finding obscured in previous studies using conventional BI-RADS descriptors (39,40). This morphological feature may reflect invasive tumor-stromal interactions, while the high NMLs proportion explains why traditional BI-RADS features showed limited predictive value for DCIS upstaging risk assessment.

The developed nomogram demonstrated clinically useful performance for predicting postoperative upstaging in CNB-diagnosed DCIS, showing moderate discrimination (AUC =0.72, bootstrap-corrected AUC =0.70) with minimal optimism (ΔAUC =0.02) and good calibration (H-L test, P=0.86), indicating reliable internal validity. DCA revealed meaningful clinical utility across probability thresholds >20%, suggesting particular value for preoperative decision-making regarding SLNB consideration in high-risk patients while potentially sparing low-risk cases from overtreatment, thereby optimizing individualized surgical management for DCIS patients.

While these results are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged, including the retrospective, single-center design and relatively small DCIS-IC cohort, which may affect generalizability and statistical power. The operator-dependent nature of ultrasound also necessitates external validation of this nomogram. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts should validate these results, while incorporating other imaging modalities (e.g., mammography, MRI), molecular profiling, and artificial intelligence to enhance predictive accuracy for personalized surgical planning.

Conclusions

In summary, the nomogram incorporating age, Ki-67, and suspicious ALN on US effectively predicts DCIS upstaging risk in cases diagnosed by US-CNB and may assist in clinical decision-making. US characteristics (size >1 cm, non-circumscribed mass margins) may provide supplementary information but require further validation.
