Behavioral and circuit principles of temperature gradient navigation
Authors: Kaarthik Abhinav Balakrishnan, Martin Haesemeyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYBehavioral thermoregulation is critical for survival across animals, including endothermic mammals. However, we do not understand how neural circuits control navigation toward preferred temperatures. Zebrafish exclusively regulate body temperature via behavior, making them ideal for studying thermal navigation. Here, we combine behavioral analysis, machine learning, and calcium imaging to understand how larval zebrafish seek out preferred temperatures within thermal gradients. Using a stimulus-controlled Markov model of thermal navigation, we find that hot avoidance largely relies on the modulation of individual swim decisions. The avoidance of cold temperatures, a particular challenge in ectotherms, however, relies on a deliberate strategy combining gradient alignment and directed reversals. Calcium imaging across the medulla identified seven classes of temperature-responsive neurons. These response types represent position and direction within temperature gradients relative to the preferred temperature. Medullary response types furthermore predict transitions within the Markov model. Medullary encoding thereby enables navigation. Our findings establish a key link between neural activity and thermoregulatory behavior, elucidating the neural basis of how animals seek out preferred temperatures.

INTRODUCTION

The physiology of organisms is optimized for specific temperatures. Even small deviations from these temperatures can have severe consequences.1–4 Organisms therefore evolved abilities to seek out preferred temperatures through thermoregulatory behaviors like thermal gradient navigation or sunbathing.5–8 Endotherms like mammals and birds additionally regulate body temperature using autonomous mechanisms.9,10 Despite this ability, thermoregulatory behaviors are conserved in endotherms as they conserve energy.11,12 Therefore, mammals will seek out preferred temperatures to reduce fluctuations in body temperature.13–19 Ectotherms lack internal mechanisms for temperature regulation and exclusively rely on behavior to find optimal temperatures.9,20 Despite the universal importance and high conservation, thermoregulatory behavior and its neural basis remain poorly understood in vertebrates.21,22

Here, we combined behavioral recording, functional calcium imaging, and modeling in larval zebrafish to understand how vertebrates seek out preferred temperatures through the interaction of two competing drives: the avoidance of hot and cold temperatures. As ectotherms, larval zebrafish must seek out preferred temperatures to thermoregulate, and due to their small size, they are largely at equilibrium with the temperature in their environment. This simplifies the interpretation of results since the position within a thermal environment can be used to infer body temperature. Previous work in larval zebrafish uncovered behavioral and neural features of heat avoidance,23–25 how swim kinematics and turn sequencing change with temperature,26,27 and demonstrated that structures implicated in mammalian thermoregulation have conserved roles in zebrafish.28 However, these studies did not address how larval zebrafish seek out a preferred temperature, which requires a balance of hot and cold avoidance. Namely, thermoregulation is a goal-directed behavior that requires a change in valence attached to thermal stimuli. While heating stimuli signal worsening conditions above the preference (“worsening context28”), they signal improving conditions below (“improving context”). We therefore focused on comparing behavioral mechanisms of hot and cold avoidance, and how neurons within the medulla of larval zebrafish encode features of temperature gradients to enable this switch in stimulus valence.

We characterized zebrafish behavior across different temperature profiles to determine thermal navigation strategies. This led to the identification of behavioral modes such as reversals and persistent swimming, which were critical for cold avoidance but less important for hot avoidance. This suggested an asymmetry in behavioral navigation strategies between hot and cold avoidance, which we captured in a stimulus-driven Markov model. Temperature encoding within the zebrafish medulla reflected the behavioral asymmetry. Here, different response types were split into encoding temperature and temperature change either above or below the preference. This suggested a simple solution to the problem of assigning different valences to heating or cooling stimuli above vs. below the preference. Importantly, medullary response types predicted key features of the behavioral model suggesting that these neurons can drive the seeking of the thermal preference.

RESULTS

Larval zebrafish efficiently avoid hot and cold temperatures

To investigate how larval zebrafish navigate temperature gradients to thermoregulate, we designed a gradient chamber similar to Le Goc et al.,26 see STAR Methods and Figure 1A. Individual larvae swam in two 200-mm-long and 50-mm-wide lanes within an aluminum chamber. We established linear temperature gradients or uniform temperature profiles within the chamber, and the position and heading of zebrafish were tracked under white-light illumination at 100 Hz while they explored the chamber (Figure 1B). This allowed us to extract swim kinematics (Figure 1B, bottom) such as changes in heading angles (“turn angle”), the waiting time between consecutive swim bouts (“interbout interval” [IBI]) as well as the total displacement for each swim (“displacement”).

As observed previously, when exploring uniform temperature profiles, swim vigor was highly temperature dependent.26 As temperatures increased, the displacement per bout increased (Figure 1C) while IBIs decreased (Figure 1D) as evidenced by rightward and leftward shifts in the respective distributions. At the same time, the number of straight swims was reduced at higher temperatures while the prevalence and angles of turns increased (Figure 1E). In isolation, this modulation of swim vigor by absolute temperature would lead to diffusional trapping of larval zebrafish at cold temperatures,26 illustrating the challenge larval zebrafish face in escaping the cold.

To characterize behavior in thermal gradients, we performed 72 experiments within an 18°C–26°C gradient and 34 experiments within a 24°C–32°C gradient. We chose to conduct the gradient experiments between 18°C and 32°C to avoid noxious temperatures. While the influence of absolute temperature on behavior frustrates cold avoidance, larval zebrafish efficiently avoided not only hot (Figure 1F) but also cold temperatures (Figure 1G). Specifically, larval zebrafish spent most of their time in a region from 24°C to 26°C and avoided temperatures below 22°C or above 28°C where their occupancy was less than a fifth of the peak (Figures 1F and 1G). Based on these results, we assume that the preferred temperature of larval zebrafish is 25°C, which is in agreement with previous measurements of the preferred temperature.28 The exaggerated time larval zebrafish spent near the edge of the arena (26°C in the cold regime and 24°C in the hot regime) is due to thigmotaxis,29 which was also observable in uniform temperature profiles (Figure S1A).

Comparing swim kinematics at matched temperatures revealed a smaller reduction in swim vigor at lower temperatures during gradient than uniform temperature conditions. IBIs were significantly shortened below 22°C, and displacements significantly lengthened when zebrafish were exploring a gradient (Figures S1B and S1C) indicating that part of the reduction in swim vigor at colder temperatures was compensated if swims served the behavioral goal of thermoregulation.30 More critical for navigation, however, is the ability of animals to reorient on trajectories that lead away from the preference (worsening context28). Within our gradients, larval zebrafish experienced temperature changes of up to 0.2°C in a given swim bout corresponding to 1.0°C/s (Figures S1D and S1E). As expected, when larval zebrafish were above their preferred temperature and experienced a temperature increase during a swim bout, they significantly increased the turn magnitude of the next swim28 (Figure 1H). The same modulation was observed in cold temperatures. Whenever a swim bout led to a decrease in temperature, the next swim had a significantly increased turn magnitude (Figure 1H). Therefore, worsening contexts increased turn magnitude independently of absolute temperature, which should increase reorientation toward the preference.

To test whether modulation of swim kinematics by experienced temperature is sufficient to explain gradient navigation, we performed a nonparametric simulation. We binned the swim data obtained during the gradient experiments according to absolute temperature at the start of each swim, as well as changes in temperature experienced during the previous swim bout (Figure S1F). We then drew swim bouts at random according to temperature and previous temperature change in virtual hot and cold gradients (see STAR Methods) and implemented the selected swims. These simulations demonstrated that the influence of temperature on swim kinematics largely explains how larval zebrafish avoid hot temperatures (Figure S1G) as shown by a small Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the real fish distribution within the hot gradient and the distribution of the simulated particle in the virtual gradient. However, the simulations did not capture cold avoidance well (Figure S1H).

In summary (Figure 1I), larval zebrafish readily avoided both hot and cold temperatures even though absolute temperature appeared to change behavior in a manner that opposed cold avoidance. This detrimental influence was likely offset by a more deliberate strategy of avoiding cold. Part of this strategy is the modulation of turn magnitude according to worsening context, which is present in both hot and cold temperatures.

Cold avoidance behavior is organized into longer-term modes

Simulations indicated that modulation of swim kinematics by the temperature stimulus is not sufficient to explain cold avoidance. However, within these simulations consecutive swim bouts were drawn independently, i.e., they only captured swim-by-swim modulation of behavior according to the stimulus. We therefore speculated that gradient navigation relied on longer-term organization of behavior to overcome problems with reduced movement frequency and vigor in the cold. In line with this, we observed stimulus-dependent autocorrelations between displacements and turn angles of successive swims (Figures 2A and 2B). We also noticed that larval zebrafish often swam along the gradient direction in the cold, terminating these trajectories with sharp reversals when facing away from the preference (Figure 2C).

To quantify these aligned swims and reversals, we defined a gradient “alignment metric” as the absolute cosine of the angle between the swim vector and the gradient direction. This alignment metric is 1 if the swim is perfectly aligned to the gradient direction, and 0 if the swim is perpendicular (Figure 2D). Within the gradient, temperature influenced alignment: at colder temperatures, swims were more aligned to the gradient than chance, while they were less aligned than chance above 30°C (Figure 2E). In uniform temperature experiments alignment was independent of chamber position but greater than chance (Figure S2A). Since temperature does not provide a directional cue in uniform profiles, this suggests that the direction of the alignment was partially independent of temperature and maybe based on visual cues. The modulation of alignment by temperature (Figure 2E) suggested that larval zebrafish modulate directional persistence of swimming according to the temperature stimulus.

To investigate persistent directional swimming and whether changes in persistence are dependent on worsening vs. improving context, we divided swims into “aligned swims” (within a 45° cone around the gradient direction) and “unaligned swims” (outside the cone; Figure 2D). We defined persistence length as the number of successively aligned swims (Figure 2D). The average persistence length was highest (> 6 bouts) in the cold when context was improving (increasing temperatures; Figure 2F). During worsening context in the cold, persistence length was significantly reduced (Figure 2F). Modulation of persistence length was smaller above the preferred temperature. However, the dependence on context was maintained such that decreasing temperatures had a longer persistence length above the preference (Figure 2F). This indicated an efficient strategy centered on maintaining or breaking gradient alignment based on context, especially at temperatures below the preference. While context in the gradient modulated persistence length by up to 4 bouts in the cold and 2 bouts in hot temperatures, modulation by swim direction in uniform experiments was less than half a bout indicating that the observed differences in the gradient were a consequence of the stimulus (Figure S2B).

To understand how context influences changes in direction, we defined reversals as swim sequences that take larval zebrafish from one aligned direction (e.g., facing toward the cold) to the opposite (facing toward warmth; Figure 2D). The average length of reversals was 4–6 swim bouts irrespective of context (Figure S2C). Furthermore, across reversals 85% of bouts turned in the same direction, regardless of temperature or context (Figure S2D). Analyzing reversal initiation by worsening or improving context revealed the opposite dependency compared with persistence. Between 18°C and 30°C the reversal initiation probability after a temperature decrease dropped around 2-fold, while the probability to initiate reversals after a temperature increase increased around 6-fold (Figure 2G). Importantly, context strongly modulated reversal initiation. Namely, below the preference, the initiation probability was higher for temperature decreases while this relationship flipped above the preference (Figure 2G). Directly comparing the initiation of reversals during worsening vs. improving context in the cold and the hot regime indicates that reversals play a larger role during cold than hot avoidance (Figure 2H). Specifically, at comparable distances from the preference, reversals were more favored during worsening context below the preferred temperature than above (Figure 2H). The modulation of reversal initiation by context affected trajectory length before reversals occurred based on position and direction (Figure 2I). In the cold, reversals were often initiated within fewer than ten swim bouts when zebrafish were swimming away from the preference, while larvae often maintained direction for more than 20 bouts when swimming toward the preference (Figure 2I, top). Near the preferred temperature, direction within the gradient did not influence the length of trajectories until reversal (Figure 2I, middle). At hot temperatures, the amount of swim bouts until reversal was again reduced for trajectories away from the preference (Figure 2I, bottom). In uniform temperature experiments, reversal probability was barely modulated by chamber position and swim direction (Figure S2E). In line with this, starting from the same chamber positions as in the gradient experiments, trajectories until reversal were not modulated by direction in uniform temperatures (comparing Figures S2F with Figure 2I).

In summary (Figure 2J), we identified longer-term behavioral modes that allow larval zebrafish to align to the gradient direction and periodically break these alignments using direction reversals. Analysis of these modes in the hot and cold regimes suggests that they are more strongly modulated by context during cold avoidance than hot avoidance.

Temperature neurons in the medulla tile temperature space

After characterizing gradient navigation behavior above and below the preference, we set out to identify the neural substrates mediating the sensorimotor transformations. Larvae expressing the calcium indicator GCaMP6s across all nuclei in the brain31 were exposed to continuous temperature-controlled water flow, and a thermistor was used to monitor the temperature at the preparation (Figure 3A). We provided stimuli within the same temperature range used in the experiments (18°C–32°C) (Figures S3A and S3B). After segmenting the imaging data using Suite2P32 we used model identification of neural encoding33 (MINE) to identify neurons whose activity encoded the temperature stimulus. Previous work implicated neurons within the zebrafish medulla in swim generation during heat avoidance.25 Furthermore, medullary heat responses were partially dependent on sensory trigeminal ganglion inputs.25 We therefore focused our imaging onto the medulla (13 experiments total) and trigeminal ganglion (10 experiments total) to understand temperature encoding across the thermal range, and how thermal processing enables the behavioral algorithm of thermoregulation. Suite2p segmented, on average, 5,298 neurons within the medulla in each experimental fish and 31 neurons within the trigeminal ganglia.

Within the medulla, correlation-based clustering34 identified seven response types (Figures 3B and 3C). As expected, unclustered neurons had overall low pairwise correlations (Figure S3C). Each of the seven response types encoded distinct features of the temperature stimulus, such as only responding during cold temperatures (Figure 3D) or hot temperatures (Figure 3F), responding to temperature increases while cold (Figure 3E), or responding to temperature increases while hot (Figure 3E). Correlations between the different response types were low (Figure S3D). This representation tiled stimulus space, which aids in the identification of improving and worsening contexts. Intriguingly, significantly more neurons responded at temperatures below the preference (711 neurons on average per fish) than above the preference (86 neurons on average per fish, p=0.0004, Wilcoxon rank sum test). Importantly, all neuron types were identified in more than half of the fish (Figure S3E). Further analysis using MINE revealed that most neurons responded nonlinearly to the temperature stimulus. For only 10% of neurons a linear approximation explained at least 50% of the variance of the response (Figure S3F). Among the remaining 90% nonlinear neurons, less than 30% could be explained by a second-order approximation (Figure S3G) suggesting high complexity in the responses.33 This complexity was largely masked in our previous imaging studies focusing on warm temperatures33 and was likely a consequence of the strong rectification across response types, which either only responded above or below the preference.

In zebrafish and other vertebrates, sensory trigeminal neurons project to the zebrafish medulla and spinal cord.10,35 Accordingly, conversion of photo-activatable GFP (PA-GFP) in trigeminal sensory neurons revealed ramifications within the medulla (Figure 3G; Video S1). To understand the relationship between thermosensory responses and the representation of temperature within the medulla, we imaged neural activity within the trigeminal ganglia of larval zebrafish under the same stimulus conditions. Employing the same analysis approach revealed three trigeminal thermosensory neuron types (Figures 3H, 3I, and S3H). The most abundant type encoded adapting cold responses while the other two types responded to temperatures below 23°C (cold) or temperatures above 20°C (hot) (Figure 3I). The “hot” response type was only identified in one individual fish across multiple planes. The trigeminal ganglion is located on the ventral side of the brain, and it might therefore be that this response type could not be reliably identified across individuals. As described previously,25 responses across types were more correlated in the trigeminal ganglion (Figure S3J) than in the medulla (Figure S3D). The decorrelation of neural encoding in the medulla likely serves the purpose of representing important stimulus features involved in behavioral control such as improving and worsening contexts.

In summary, we discovered that larval zebrafish encode temperatures across the entire thermal range. Sensory trigeminal neurons already split encoding across warm and cool temperatures; however, only neurons within the medulla were split according to behaviorally important stimulus features such as context.

Temperature neurons are spatially organized

The seven temperature-response types were not confined to specific nuclei within the medulla (Figure S4A). However, we wondered whether the functional organization of medullary temperature-response types was reflected in an anatomical organization. Specifically, we tested whether response types were organized according to two features: first, the temperature regime, and second whether similar responses were present in the trigeminal ganglion or whether their responses were instead computed de novo in the medulla. We computed a spatial clustering metric that is 1 if neurons are distributed as expected by chance, and greater than 1 if neurons within a group are spatially more compact than expected relative to an outgroup (STAR Methods). Separating neurons by temperature revealed that they were spatially clustered with respect to whether they responded preferentially above or below the preferred temperature (Figure 4A). We found a similar organization with respect to computation. Specifically, neurons with responses that were already encoded in sensory trigeminal neurons were closer to each other and separated from neurons with responses that were likely computed within the medulla (Figure 4B). This suggests that despite a lack of obvious regional organization, temperature neurons within the medulla are spatially organized into processing circuits.

Temperature neurons encode behaviorally relevant features of thermal gradients

To understand the functional role of the temperature-response types, we wanted to know whether it would be possible to decode current temperature, temperature change, and context from their neural activity, since these features influenced both individual swims as well as longer-term behavioral modes (Figures 1 and 2). To this end, we used MINE33 to predict the activity of each response cluster based on the temperature stimulus. To make this prediction more relevant to behavioral timescales, we used a short sensory history of 2 s within the MINE models and predicted the cluster average of deconvolved imaging traces (Figures 3D–3F). On test data consisting of the last 6 min of stimulus presentation, each of these predictions generalized well with correlations of at least 0.8 in all cases (Figure S4B). We subsequently used these models to predict the activity of each response type during the gradient behavior experiments by taking each larva’s experienced temperature as inputs (see Figures S4C–S4E for examples).

Notably, each response type encoded a nonlinear transformation of the stimulus (Figures S3F and S3G), but we reasoned that features important for behavior should be linearly decodable from activity. We therefore fitted linear models predicting temperature and temperature change, as well as context using response type activity as inputs. Models were fitted on 80% of the data and tested on the remaining 20%. Perhaps not surprisingly, temperature could be predicted with high accuracy from neural activity, with a median R2 value greater than 0.9 across experiments (Figure 4C). Temperature change could be predicted with high accuracy as well (median R2 of 0.65; Figure 4C). Medullary activity was especially sensitive to detecting cooling at cold temperatures. Specifically, at temperatures below 22°C temperature decreases of less than 0.1°C/s were predicted to be temperature decreases with at least 95% confidence (Figure S4F, top). For temperatures above 28°C, heating stimuli were not as well predicted as cooling stimuli in the cold, requiring a change of at least 0.3°C/s to be predicted as significantly greater than zero (Figure S4F, bottom). Importantly, the correct context (worsening, improving, and none) could be predicted from medullary activity during gradient navigation in 94% of cases (Figure 4C).

Analyzing the predicted activity along swim trajectories of larvae suggested an efficient encoding of thermal gradient space. Namely, the seven response types tiled the temperature gradient such that the combination of activities marked specific positions and swim directions, such as restricted activity during trajectories in the cold or specific responses on trajectories that started in warm temperatures and moved the fish toward the preference (Figure 4D). This was also apparent when analyzing neural responses according to gradient temperature and movement direction, which showed a clear segregation across response types (Figures S4G–S4M).

In summary, these results revealed that the medulla encodes information relevant for thermal gradient navigation behavior, including position and heading within temperature gradients. Features relevant for the modulation of individual swims, such as temperature and temperature change, could be readily decoded from the activity using linear models. The same was true for context, which modulates both turning and longer-term behavioral modes.

A stimulus-controlled Markov model reveals structure of temperature gradient navigation

To link gradient navigation, swim modes, and neural activity we generated a parametric, stimulus-controlled “Navigation model” (Figure 5A). Based on the split of medullary response types according to temperature (above vs. below the preference) and temperature change (increases vs. decreases) we reasoned that larval zebrafish can extract information about temperature, distance to the preference, temperature change, and absolute temperature change. Linear models relating medullary activity to these stimulus features support this reasoning (Figure S5A). Therefore, we chose these features and their first-order interactions as model inputs. First-order interactions were included since multiplication and coincidence detection are known neural computations.36–40

Based on our observations of gradient navigation behavior, we defined three swim modes and integrated these into a Markov model in which generalized linear models (GLMs) control the mode transitions based on stimulus features: a “reversal mode” capturing reversal maneuvers, a “persistent mode” capturing swims with a persistent direction, and a “general mode” for all other swims (see STAR Methods). For each swim mode, we fit separate emission models using stimulus-driven GLMs that generate IBIs, displacements, and turns. Emission GLMs also included previous behavior as an input to capture observed autocorrelations (Figures 2A and 2B).

We used Markov chain Monte Carlo to fit all transition and output parameters of the Navigation model.41,42 This allowed us to perform all model tests and simulations using draws from the joint posterior distribution rather than relying on a maximum likelihood estimate. For example, in the transition GLM from the general mode into the reversal mode, the parameter for the interaction of the current temperature and the change in temperature during the previous bout was significantly greater than 0 (Figure 5B). This was expected since reversals depend on worsening vs. improving context, which depends on an interaction of where the fish is within the gradient, and the direction in which it is swimming. In this GLM, parameters for temperature T, and the interaction of temperature and absolute temperature change T|ΔT| were anticorrelated (r=-0.69) (Figure 5C) whereas the corresponding inputs were only weakly related (r=0.14) (Figure S5B). This suggested that while the temperature parameter was not significantly different from 0 (Figure 5B; 95% confidence interval includes 0) there was an inflexible direction in parameter space that maintained the ratio of the parameter values for T and T|ΔT| in a small range.

To assess the generalization of our model, we split the experimental data into a training set (80% of all trajectories) and a held-out test set. We subsequently computed the log-likelihood of model predictions of swim modes (transition model), as well as kinematic features (emission models) on the test data, and compared it to a “chance model” that uses the overall data distribution for predictions (see STAR Methods). In all cases, the log-likelihood of fitted emission models surpassed that of the chance model (Figure S5C). For the transition model, we compared two model structures. The first had stimulus-controlled transitions as outlined above. The second was a comparison model with constant transition probabilities dictated by the data. This Constant model still implements a Markov process and has the same emission models as the stimulus-controlled Navigation model. Hence, it allowed us to assess the importance of stimulus-controlled swim modes for hot and cold avoidance. The largest jump in log-likelihood of the test data under the models occurred from a chance model assuming independent mode probabilities to the model assuming constant transitions (Figure 5D). This simply indicated that swim modes indeed followed a Markov process. However, the log-likelihood improved significantly (p<0.001, Wilcoxon rank sum test across 100 draws from the posterior) for the stimulus-controlled model, indicating that swim-mode transitions were indeed controlled by the temperature stimulus (Figure 5D).

We subsequently used the Navigation model to understand how larval zebrafish used swim modes within the gradient by predicting three important quantities that describe the navigational strategy. We quantified the contrast of the reversal initiation probability according to the temperature change experienced over the last swim bout across the temperature gradient. In the cold, reversals were predicted to be enhanced around 2-fold when fish experienced decreases in temperature compared with increases. However, above the preferred temperature, the relative enhancement of reversals during heating vs. cooling stimuli was considerably weaker (Figure 5E). The length of persistent trajectories was predicted to be 1.5-times longer for cooling stimuli below the preference compared with heating stimuli, while the contrast was again lower at temperatures above the preference (Figure 5F). Finally, we predicted the probability of the general swim mode throughout the gradient, since the general mode signals the prevalence of movements that are not organized into longer-term strategies. Above 28°C nearly half of the swims were predicted to be in the general mode, while temperature changes across the previous bout suppressed this mode for lower temperatures. This further underlined that longer-term organization of movement played a smaller role during hot avoidance, while zebrafish instead relied on biased individual movements to escape heat (swim-by-swim modulation).

The emission models reflected the expected behavior within each mode. The persistent mode was dominated by straight swims with smaller angle turns (Figure 5H), while reversals were dominated by large-angle turns. During reversals, the previous turn direction strongly influenced the direction of the current turn (Figure 5I), as evidenced by the asymmetry of the turn direction distributions induced by the model. This was expected since reversals are characterized by larval zebrafish joining turns in the same direction (Figure S2D). Displacements were largely independent of swim mode but strongly influenced by the displacement of the previous swim bout (Figures 5J and 5K).

In summary, the Navigation model allowed us to analyze the dependence of swim modes and individual behaviors on the temperature stimulus and past movement history. The results suggest that larval zebrafish transition from longer-term behavioral strategies at colder temperatures to the modulation of individual swims at warmer temperatures.

Behavioral modes are critical for cold avoidance

After fitting the Navigation model, we used it to ask whether behavioral modes are important for thermoregulation by comparing simulations of the full Navigation model with the model using constant transition probabilities. Specifically, at each swim bout, we used either the constant or the stimulus-specific transition probability to change the swim mode and subsequently drew a swim bout from IBI, displacement, and turn angle distributions that were controlled by the current behavioral mode and the stimulus (Figure 6A; see STAR Methods).

The Constant model led to clear hot avoidance (Figure 6B). Including stimulus-controlled transition probabilities further improved hot avoidance, significantly reducing the KL divergence with the true fish distribution (Figure 6D). For cold avoidance however, the Constant model led to accumulation at cold temperatures and hence a KL divergence that was greater than the KL divergence of a uniform distribution (Figures 6C and 6D). The Navigation model with stimulus-controlled transitions, on the other hand, led to cold avoidance (Figure 6C) with a significant reduction of the KL divergence compared with both the Constant model and a uniform distribution within the gradient (Figure 6D). This strongly suggests that stimulus-controlled behavioral modes were critical for cold avoidance, while they were less important for hot avoidance, which could be largely accomplished by the stimulus-controlled modulation of individual swims. Qualitatively, trajectories generated by the Navigation model mimicked the behavioral trajectories of larval zebrafish (Figure 6E). Furthermore, the modulation of swim kinematics by gradient position and movement direction in the simulations closely matched the corresponding observations of fish navigating the thermal gradient (Figures S6A–S6E vs. Figures 2 and S1) with the exception of autocorrelations of successive turns (Figure S6E vs. Figure 2B). This indicates that the structure of the Navigation model closely captured real fish behavior.

In summary, simulations based on the Navigation model replicated hot avoidance and important aspects of cold avoidance. By changing the stimulus dependence of the transition model, we could show that stimulus-dependent mode transitions are important for thermoregulation. This was especially true for cold avoidance, where the emission models were not enough to drive navigation but instead required stimulus-controlled transitions as well.

Medulla temperature neurons predict Navigation model features

Considering the important role of stimulus-controlled transitions between behavioral modes, we tested whether the medullary response types could explain these transitions. Specifically, we used linear regression to test whether the temperature-response types could predict the probability to enter the reversal mode, to maintain persistent swimming, and to occupy the general swim mode. Since the transition model was parametrized according to the temperature stimulus, we could compute the respective probabilities across time in our imaging experiments and fit the desired linear regression models (Figure 7A, black lines; STAR Methods). The maintenance of persistent aligned swimming was best predicted, with the model explaining 78% of the variance in the probability on held-out test data (Figure 7A, middle). The probability of occupying the general mode was least well predicted with less than 50% of variance explained (Figure 7A, bottom). Importantly, in all cases, a model based on medullary response types performed better than a model based on the stimulus itself (Figure 7B). This suggests that medullary circuits represent temperature in a manner that simplifies the control of swim-mode transitions.

Model coefficients revealed meaningful dependencies of probabilities on neural activity. The probability to enter the reversal mode was most strongly enhanced by activity in “cold and cooling” as well as “hot and heating” neurons, which both signal worsening context (Figure 7C, top). However, “hot and cooling” neurons, which signal improving context above the preference were also predicted to increase this probability (Figure 7C, top). This might account for the reduced contrast in entering the reversal state in hot vs. cold temperatures (Figure 5E). The maintenance of persistently aligned swims was predicted to be most strongly enhanced by activity in “cold and heating” neurons (Figure 7C, middle), which is in line with persistent swims dominating during improving context in the cold (Figure 2F). In line with behavioral observations, the persistent swim mode was predicted to be increased when “cold” neurons were active and reduced when “hot” neurons were active (Figure 7C, middle). The general swim-mode probability was predicted to be enhanced by activity in hot neurons, which mirrors the increase in this swim mode above the preferred temperature, while neurons signaling temperature change were overall predicted to reduce the probability of the general swim mode, which was reflected in the behavior as well (compare Figure 7C, bottom with Figure 5G).

In summary, activity across the seven temperature-response types within the medulla was able to predict key features of the mode transitions. Furthermore, individual neuron types had the expected influences on these long-term behavioral strategies. However, the unexplained variance in the predictions suggests that responses generated in other brain regions are important to refine swim-mode transitions.

DISCUSSION

Behavioral thermoregulation is highly conserved as it is critical for survival across species. Despite this fundamental importance, we know little about the neural and behavioral algorithms that underlie this process in vertebrates. Here, we set out to understand thermoregulation in larval zebrafish by developing a quantitative model of behavior and testing its relationship with neural processing. We used a thermal gradient navigation paradigm to compare the avoidance of temperatures above the preference (hot avoidance) to the avoidance of temperatures below the preference (cold avoidance). In hot temperatures, larval zebrafish increased overall movement vigor. Paired with increased turning during worsening context, this simple swim-by-swim strategy was nearly sufficient to explain hot avoidance. In the cold, on the other hand, larval zebrafish tend to persistently align to the gradient during improving context, while initiating reversal maneuvers during worsening context. This difference in strategies for avoiding hot and cold temperatures is supported by a split encoding of temperatures within the zebrafish medulla. Namely, functional calcium imaging revealed seven distinct temperature responses within the medulla that tile temperature space such that most neurons either respond above or below the preferred temperature. This encoding also provides an elegant solution to the problem of changing stimulus valence around the preference, since different neurons encode temperature increases or decreases above and below the preference. Medullary responses can therefore predict stimulus context (worsening vs. improving conditions) with high fidelity both above and below the preference (Figure 4C). A Navigation model, combining a stimulus-driven Markov model with stimulus-driven emission models for swim kinematics, allowed us to parametrize longer timescale behavioral modes. This revealed that stimulus-driven control of persistent swims along the gradient with intermittent direction reversals was crucial for cold avoidance. The parametric model also allowed us to link neural activity to behavioral modes, making clear predictions about the importance of specific medullary response types in controlling direction reversals and persistent swimming.

Attraction vs. avoidance

The goal of behavioral thermoregulation is to seek out a preferred temperature. In theory, one can distinguish two approaches for reaching a preference: active attraction to the preference or avoidance of any temperatures above and below the preference. Since these are two sides of the same coin, it is difficult to disambiguate the two possibilities. However, parsimony speaks to an avoidance-based mechanism of behavioral thermoregulation. At any moment in time, the fish can, at best, know how much warmer or colder it is than the preference and whether it was moving toward (improving context) or away from the preference (worsening context). The spatial location of the preferred temperature cannot be accurately inferred from the gradient without knowledge about the slope and linearity of the gradient. It is therefore likely that thermoregulatory behavior uses local information about the thermal gradient. This local information may then be combined with visual information about landmarks to effectively avoid both hot and cold temperatures. The temperature encoding in the medulla (Figure 3B) provides the necessary information for local navigation decisions through the encoding of context.

Cold avoidance as gradient descent

Larval zebrafish are ectotherm animals. Their nervous and muscular systems are therefore largely at equilibrium with environmental temperature. This creates specific challenges for avoiding cold temperatures.43–45 As muscle physiology and force generation are impaired, less resources can be mounted in the cold to escape. Larval zebrafish circumvented this problem by using a more deliberate strategy to escape cold compared with hot temperatures. They aligned to the gradient while temperatures were increasing in the cold, thereby picking the most efficient route to their preference akin to a gradient descent algorithm for learning. However, when temperatures were decreasing in the cold, they initiated direction reversals (Figure 2). Reversals appeared to be a fixed behavioral maneuver regardless of temperature in which a small number of swims were used to reverse the heading, correcting course to face toward the preferred temperature. These reversals likely corresponded to “U-maneuvers,” described as continued turning in the same direction during hot avoidance.28 However, the current results suggest that these reversal maneuvers were more critical for cold than hot avoidance. Overall, keeping a persistent direction up the gradient in improving context paired with reversals during worsening context allowed larval zebrafish to avoid cold temperatures despite longer rest times between swims and overall reduced per-swim displacement. Endotherms are less impacted by cold temperatures, as they are able to raise their body temperature above environmental temperatures. However, spinal circuits in mammals preferentially encode cooling stimuli,46 and humans are more sensitive to cooling than heating stimuli.47–49 This parallels our behavioral and neural findings in zebrafish, where cold and cooling responsive neurons outnumbered warm and heating responsive neurons in the medulla. This suggests evolutionary conservation of the importance of cold responses and cold avoidance from ectotherms to endotherms.

This preferential encoding of cold stimuli was already present in the trigeminal ganglia, in which two neuron classes encoded temperatures below 23°C (Figures 3H and 3I). How zebrafish detect these temperatures is unclear. Teleost fish lack the canonical receptor for ambient cold, TrpM8,50,51 and it has been speculated that fish might not actively avoid cold temperatures since most species can tolerate cold.52 Given the presence of cold-activated thermosensory neurons, it would be of great interest to identify the corresponding cold receptor to gain a clearer understanding of the evolution of thermosensation.

Instantaneous modulation and behavioral modes

The most simplistic, yet inefficient, strategy for seeking out a preference is orthokinesis or diffusional trapping. It relies on increasing movement with increasing distance from the preference, while increasing rest at the preference. This strategy exclusively aids hot avoidance in larval zebrafish.26 A more efficient strategy, employed in bacterial chemotaxis and thermotaxis, is a biased random walk.53–55 Here, unfavorable changes (worsening context) lead to increases in reorientation (turn angles in the case of zebrafish). This strategy, together with orthokinesis, is largely sufficient in explaining the effective hot avoidance larval zebrafish display23,28 (Figures S1F and S1G). Larval zebrafish additionally repeat the same turn direction during worsening context, enacting near ballistic reversals or U-maneuvers,28 which enhances the effectiveness of reorientations. The ability to reverse direction across multiple turns enhanced hot avoidance and was critical for cold avoidance, which in addition relied on the fish persistently aligning to the gradient direction (Figures 2 and 6). How these longer-term strategies are controlled by motor circuits is still unclear; however, potential neural substrates have been identified in zebrafish. A putative oscillator within the medulla underlies the maintenance of turn directions during exploratory motion and phototaxis,56–58 while habenular circuits enable persistent turning during hot avoidance.28

Increased turning as temperatures increase (Figures 1E and 1H) reduces the ability to maintain gradient alignment. Hence, it is difficult to assess whether neural control of persistent swimming differs above and below the preference, or whether the differences are a consequence of increases in swim vigor. It is therefore unclear whether larval zebrafish implement two different strategies for hot and cold avoidance. Behavior may instead operate on a continuum of longer-term control and modulation of swim kinematics with one dominating below the preference and the other above.

The neural basis of thermoregulatory decisions

In vertebrates, behavioral thermoregulation relies on a distributed network of temperature-encoding neurons across largely subcortical brain regions.9,22,59 In larval zebrafish, trigeminal thermosensation, as well as computations within medullary circuits, the preoptic area (POA), and the habenula, have been implicated in hot avoidance.25,28,33,60 However, neural responses to temperatures below the preference have previously not been recorded. Here, we performed functional calcium imaging across the same thermal range (18°C–32°C) in which we tested gradient navigation. Neurons within the medulla encoded temperature across seven functional types (Figure 3). All of these were preferentially active at either warm or cold temperatures with a zone of overlap. One cell type each was activated by warm or cold temperatures, while the other five types preferentially responded to temperature change, three in the cold, two in the warmth. We used a convolutional neural network method we recently developed (MINE33) to infer the contribution of these neuron types to hot and cold avoidance during behavioral thermoregulation (Figure 4). Intriguingly, the seven response types formed a place-like code within thermal gradients, marking position relative to the preference and direction of movement, indicating an efficient strategy to represent natural thermal environments.

Outlook

We focused our functional analysis onto the medulla since it is the first integrative center of skin temperature in vertebrates, through structures such as the nucleus of the trigeminal and the lateral parabrachial nucleus (lPB).9,10,61 And in zebrafish, temperature-encoding neurons within the medulla are sufficient to explain critical features of hot avoidance.25 It is interesting that already at this stage an efficient code arises that can be linearly read out to control various aspects of thermoregulatory behaviors. However, we currently do not distinguish ascending structures within the hindbrain such as the lPB from processed descending outputs that may directly interact with pre-motor neurons within the medial and anterior medulla. A careful analysis of inter-regional projections and connectivity will therefore be important in the future.

For example, activity in the medulla is likely influenced by outputs from zebrafish forebrain structures such as the POA and the habenula. The POA is a major integrative center in mammalian thermoregulation,10,62–64 and ablation of the POA in zebrafish reduces the enhancement of turn angles during worsening context in hot avoidance.28 The habenula, on the other hand, has been implicated in maintaining direction across consecutive turns during hot avoidance in zebrafish,28 and in mammals habenular neurons have been implicated in thermoregulation as well.65,66 These forebrain structures might be directly involved in computing navigational motor outputs, or they could have a modulatory role. We previously identified temperature-encoding neurons within the zebrafish POA, which encode temperature fluctuations on longer timescales.25,33 As in mammals, the POA may therefore serve a role of setting or modulating the thermal preference in larval zebrafish rather than directly controlling the behavioral strategy. It would be worthwhile to test whether modulatory inputs from the forebrain, potentially via the raphe, alter the sensitivity of medullary neurons responding below vs. above the preference. This modulation could alter the preferred temperature larval zebrafish seek.

Limitations of the study

Due to technical limitations, we were not able to directly assess the thermal preference of larval zebrafish. This would have required an extended thermal gradient and a larger chamber, as zebrafish started to behave erratically in steep gradients. However, the modulations of kinematics and behavioral modes we observed can be used to estimate the preferred temperature range to be between 23°C and 27°C. Within this range, there is little modulation of turn magnitude and reversal probability by temperature change (Figures 1 and 2). The same is true for the length of persistent trajectories, which are consistently modulated by increasing vs. decreasing temperature only above 27°C and below 23°C (Figure 2F). Using the Navigation model to simulate navigation across the entire thermal range, while maintaining the overall slope of the gradient, suggested a preference between 22°C and 26°C, which might suggest a slightly lower preference than the observations about behavioral modulation (Figure S6F).

The limitations in capturing cold avoidance of the Navigation model are likely a consequence of treating reversals as a consecutive series of individual turns rather than a compound maneuver, as well as the inability to enforce gradient direction alignment within the model. Specifically, by construction, a Markov model will assume that the probability of the next state only depends on the current state and not any previous states. Reversal maneuvers are therefore interrupted within the model, even though fish data suggest that reversals generally run to completion in a fixed number of swim bouts. Furthermore, the lack of an input related to gradient direction meant that the persistent state would lead to less efficient swims within the model compared with the real fish. These choices were made deliberately, until we have evidence of behavioral and sensory mechanisms that could underlie compound reversals and gradient alignment, respectively.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animal handling and experimental procedures were approved by the Ohio State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC Protocol #: 2019A00000137-R1). All behavioral and functional imaging experiments were performed in unpigmented offspring of incrosses between mitfa +/−; Tg(Elavl3-H2B:GCaMP6s)31 zebrafish at 5–7 days post fertilization. Photo-activatable GFP labeling experiments of trigeminal neurons were performed in unpigmented offspring of incrosses between mitfa +/−; Tg(Elavl3-H2B: RFP)67; Tg(alpha-tubulin:C3PA-GFP).68 All fish were reared at 28 °C in a 14:10 hour light dark cycle.

METHOD DETAILS

Temperature setup and experimental protocol

The gradient setup consisted of an aluminium chamber which was machined with two swim lanes and two temperature measurement-lanes in the center. Each swim lane measured 200mm in length and 50mm in width with a depth of 5mm. The measurement lanes had the same length and depth but a width of 5mm. The base of the chamber was fitted with 6 Peltier elements (Custom Thermoelectric, USA), one per swim lane at the front, middle and back of the chamber along the long edge. Each pair of these Peltier elements (front, middle, back) was independently controlled by a custom written software PID controller (C# Microsoft, USA) that compared the current temperature in the measurement lane to the desired set temperature. The Peltier drive current was set by the PID controller through a motor driver (Pololu, USA) via analog outputs from a National Instruments PCIe 6343 DAQ board (National Instruments, USA). The current temperature was measured along the temperature-lanes using thermistors (Littlefuse USP16673, Digikey, USA) that were placed at the centers of the respective Peltier elements. The swim lanes were filled with E3 medium and the temperature-lanes were filled with distilled water to protect the thermistors. The waste heat generated by the Peltier elements was dissipated using a custom-machined copper block, followed by standard CPU cooling fans. The temperature was set using a custom written UI that displayed the current temperature and the set temperature. Temperature measurements were also continuously recorded to disk by the same program. The set temperature was typically reached quickly and gradients and uniform temperature profiles stabilized within 30–60minutes, beyond which there was little fluctuation from the set temperature (<±0.5°C). Experiments were only started after the gradient or uniform temperature profile had stabilized.

The gradient chamber was illuminated from the sides by two white light panels (Nanlite, USA) situated on either side of the chamber. A Blackfly S BFS-U3-28S5M camera (FLIR, USA) was placed above the chamber taking images at a framerate of 100Hz with a resolution of 0.14mm/pixel.

For each experiment, a single larval zebrafish was introduced into each swim lane and images were acquired with custom written software (C# Microsoft, United States) and custom tracking algorithms were used to find the position and heading direction of the fish as previously described24 over the course of a 30-minute experiment. Due to practical limitations in data storage, tracking was performed online and raw camera images were not stored to disk. All further analysis was performed on the stored positions and heading directions of the larvae. Each experiment consisted either of a gradient ranging from 18 °C to 26 °C (“cold regime”), a gradient ranging from 24 °C to 32 °C (“hot regime”) or a uniform temperature profile in which the temperature was set to a fixed value between 16 °C and 34 °C, probing every 2 °C for a total of 10 different uniform temperature conditions. See Table S1 for the number of experiments performed in each condition. We note that each animal was only tested in one condition (either one of the gradient conditions or one of the uniform temperature profiles).

Imaging setup and experimental protocol

Functional calcium imaging was performed on a custom-built two-photon microscope33 at 2.5Hz with a pixel resolution of 0.78μ m/pixel (medulla/hindbrain) or 0.33μ m/pixel (trigeminal ganglion) and at an average power of 12mW at sample at 950nm (measured optical resolution of the system is <1μm lateral and <4μm axial). Imaging was either in the medulla or the trigeminal ganglion. Larval zebrafish were mounted in a custom designed chamber in which water was flowing through a channel. During imaging the head of the larva was fixed in 2.5% medium melt agarose (Fisher Scientific, USA) while the tail was kept free to move. Even though tail-traces were acquired during imaging, due to technical reasons these were not of high enough quality to allow for behavioral analysis. To generate different flow temperatures, an inline flow heater (Warner Instruments, USA) with associated controller was used. Control temperatures were calibrated such that at sample temperatures in the range from 18°C to 32°C were reached. Temperatures at sample were continuously monitored through a thermistor (Warner Instruments, USA) placed behind the larva and were recorded during the experiment. During imaging, scan stabilization along the z-axis was used to avoid artifacts induced with heating and cooling the agarose with the flowing water.

For temperature stimulation, within each imaging plane a 18minutes long stimulus was presented to the larval zebrafish. The stimulus was fixed across all imaging planes and consisted of sine wave fluctuations, ramps, and temperature holds. Imaging planes were spaced 5μm apart and 10–20 planes were imaged in each fish for experiment in the medulla and 4–6 planes for trigeminal imaging experiments.

Photo-activatable GFP experiments

Photo-activatable GFP (PA-GFP) experiments were performed under the same 2-photon microscope. Larval zebrafish were fully embedded in 2.5% low-melting-point agarose (Sigma, USA). The trigeminal ganglia were identified by imaging pan-neuronal nuclear RFP at 1040nm. A region of interest encompassing the entire ganglion across multiple planes was subsequently imaged at 740nm. In each plane, the cells in the region of the trigmeninal ganglion were individually marked and PA-GFP was activated by specifically imaging those neurons at 740nm for 5 repeats. After completing each plane, this procedure was repeated for each plane that contained neurons in the trigeminal ganglion, which was typically 3–5 planes. 24 hours post conversion full-brain anatomy stacks were acquired by imaging the RFP at 1040nm and the converted PA-GFP at 950nm.

Image registration

All medulla and PA-GFP imaging stacks were registered to a previously generated reference brain25 using non-rigid image registration through CMTK.69 The registrations were used to display temperature responsive neurons in a common coordinate system (Figure S4A) and to combine trigeminal PA-GFP conversions (Figure 3G; Video S1). Spatial clustering of neurons (Figures 4A and 4B) however was computed on a per-fish basis on pre-registration data.

Gradient occupancy

For display purposes, occupancy within gradient experiments, uniform temperature profiles and simulations was defined as follows. Histograms were computed counting the number of frames that the fish or particle spent in each temperature or y-coordinate bin. These histograms were subsequently normalized such that all counts were divided by the count in the bin with the largest count. Therefore, occupancy is 1 at within the bin were the fish spent the most time and would be one in each bin for a uniform occupancy. This normalization was chosen for display purposes as it allows for an easy comparison across conditions and experiments even if the size and count of the bins changes, while the former would impact measures of density and the latter measures of proportion.

Swim bout identification and kinematics

Fish position traces in x and y were filtered using a Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of 2 frames (20ms) and the instantaneous speed was subsequently calculated as the per-frame Euclidean distance traveled. The instantaneous speed was used to identify bouts via peak detection. Peaks with a minimum speed of 5mm/s and a minimum width of 50ms were identified. Bout starts and ends were defined as the first frame before/after the peak where the acceleration/deceleration dropped below 0.1mms2. According to these bout calls, kinematics were defined. The interbout-interval (IBI) was defined as the time between the end of the previous bout and start of the current bout. The turn angle was defined as the change in angle between the average heading angle of five frames following the bout and five frames preceding the bout, such that all turns were assumed to be smaller than 180degrees. Similarly, the bout displacement was defined as the Euclidean distance between the position after the bout and prior to the bout.

For turn magnitude and direction correlation analyses, turns were separated from straight swims using a cutoff of ± 5°. This cutoff was chosen as it was the average standard deviation of the straight swim Gaussian across all swim modes.

Exclusion of swims near the edge of the arena

Since the edges of the arena likely influence swim kinematics, e.g., by preventing turning into a wall, all swim bouts that were within 4mm (one fish length) of either of the four sides of the chamber were excluded from all further analysis.

Definition of continuous trajectories

For all correlation based analysis and Markov modeling, only continuous swim trajectories were considered. Continuous trajectories were defined as a collection of successive bouts with inter-bout intervals below 2 s. We note that some trajectories at colder temperatures were likely cut because of this interbout interval rule even though they were continuous. However, this stringent cutoff was chosen to ensure that correlations and Markov transitions were only evaluated across a continuous block of time and not influenced by the tracker intermittently losing the fish. Only trajectories with at least three swim-bouts were used for Navigation model fitting and testing.

Assignment of stimulus features to swim bouts

For stimulus based analysis, model fitting, and simulations, two stimulus variables were assigned to each bout: the temperature at the start of the bout as well as the temperature change experienced over the previous bout. This value, rather than average temperature change over a fixed time interval, was chosen, since swims are the only time where fish will experience changes in temperature within a gradient.

The temperature change across the previous bout was used to define “increasing” and “decreasing” temperatures for single bout analysis (Figure 1). Specifically, the median absolute temperature change (~ 0.052 °C/bout) was used as a cutoff, such that bouts with a previous temperature change above this threshold or a negative temperature change below the negated threshold were considered to have followed an “increasing” or “decreasing” temperature bout, respectively. For trajectory based analysis (Figure 2) “increasing” and “decreasing” were defined based on the movement direction being either towards the hot or the cold end respectively. The different thresholding was chosen for the two analyses since one was concerned with the influence of the stimulus on kinematics which likely depended on the stimulus crossing a threshold while the latter was concerned with how fish change direction within the gradient based on direction of travel.

Defining maneuvers and swim modes

To analyze longer-term behavioral strategies as well as behavior in relation to the gradient direction, additional features of swim bouts were defined based on the displacement direction of swims within the chamber rather than the heading angle of the fish. Specifically, for each swim displacement the “gradient direction” was defined as the cosine of the angle of the displacement relative to the gradient direction, such that swims in the increasing temperature direction had a negative gradient direction while swims in the decreasing temperature direction had a positive gradient direction (since increasing y-coordinates were associated with colder temperatures within the chamber). The absolute value of gradient direction (absolute cosine of the angle) was defined as the “gradient alignment” which is 1 for any swim displacement directly along the gradient direction and 0 for any swim perpendicular to it. Based on these metrics, swim displacements within a ± 45° cone around the gradient direction (absolute cosine > 0.71) were defined as aligned swims as this definition leads to an equal partition of swim directions into aligned and non-aligned types.

Based on this labeling, swim modes were defined in the following manner. A “reversal” trajectory was defined as the set of all bouts that started with the fish aligned in either the positive or negative direction followed by a set of bouts that ended with a displacement in the opposite aligned direction. 90% of such reversals were completed within 10 swim bouts and 10 swim bouts was therefore chosen as the maximum length to call this trajectory a “reversal maneuver.” A “persistent” trajectory was defined as the set of all bouts that were consistently aligned to the same gradient direction with a minimum length of two swim bouts. All remaining swim bouts were assigned as “general bouts.”

By construction, every bout was classified into one of these categories. These categories were therefore used to define the current swim mode as either reversal, persistent or general. Those labels were used to calculate reversal initiation probabilities, the average length of persistent trajectories as well as the length and handedness of reversals. The swim modes also formed the basis of the Navigation model.

General approach to model fitting and testing

All models were fitted using Hamiltonian Markov chain Monte Carlo via STAN.42 For model-fitting, the respective design matrices X were orthogonalized using QR-Decomposition within STAN. Model parameters θ were fit in this space and subsequently put back into the original space using the inverse transform. Linear dependencies were thus removed from the fit while still obtaining parameters β that could be used on raw data during tests and simulations.

Swim bouts were grouped into trajectories and 80% of all trajectories were used to fit models (Markov model transitions, emission models) while the remaining 20% were used for model testing. Trajectories were split into the train/test set by adding 4 consecutive trajectories into the train set, followed by adding one trajectory into the test set and so on. Therefore, each set was made up of trajectories across all fish. Model tests were therefore used to assess generalization of fits w.r.t. an average fish, not across fish. For each model, 4000 draws of parameters were generated from the posterior distribution across four independent chains. For all model tests the individual draws were used (one draw per test across 100 test iterations) rather than testing performance on the parameter average.

Emission models of swim bout kinematics

To capture swim bout generation dependent on swim mode as well as the stimulus and behavioral history the following emission models were fit. For each model, the training data was first split according to swim mode and subsequently separate emission models were fit for each mode. The rationale for this approach was that modes were likely differentiated by eliciting different swim bouts. At the same time the expectation was that within each swim mode, bout kinematics would be influenced by the stimulus as well. Hence separate GLMs relating stimulus features to kinematic distributions were fit for each mode.

Simulations

Based on experimentally identified swim bouts and bout parameters along with their dependence on temperatures, the fish was simulated as a virtual particle moving in a gradient. Since all analysis of the fish in experiments assumed the fish as a point particle with a defined centroid and heading angle, the virtual fish in simulations was modeled as a point particle with an associated vector as a heading direction as well. A virtual chamber was defined according to identical dimensions as the experimental setup, with 1464 pixels along the length and 320 pixels along the width. The spatial resolution was defined as 7pixels/mm, identical to the image resolution during experiments. The particle was started at a random point in this chamber with a random heading direction, and was moved along the chamber by picking “bouts” from either the actual experimental distribution (temperature+temperature change simulations; Figure S1) or generated bouts according to the Navigation model (Figure 6), either with stimulus-controlled or constant transition probabilities. Each simulation was run for a length of 180,000 frames at 100Hz, the same length as the 30-minute experiments. Two kinds of gradients, similar to the experimental setup, were imposed on the chamber, one ranging from 18 °C at the rear end (y=1464) to 26 °C in the front end (y=0), to another set ranging from 24 °C at the rear end and 32 °C at the front end. The slope of both of these gradients was therefore the same as the slope of the experimental gradients 0.4 °C/cm. Additionally, a “full” gradient was simulated, ranging from a temperature of 18 °C to 32 °C. The length of the chamber was increased to 2562 to match the slope of the gradient to the experiments and other simulations.

For the simulations combining temperature and temperature change, bout data from the gradient experiments was binned according to a) the temperature at the start of the bout and b) the temperature change experienced during the preceding bout. The bin width for temperature was chosen to be 0.5 °C and the bin width for temperature change was chosen to be 0.05 °C. The virtual particle was started at a random position in the chamber with a random heading angle and a previous temperature change of 0 °C. Since the bin sizes were small, the bin with the closest matching temperature and temperature change was chosen to instantiate the next bout. From all swim-bouts within this bin one was chosen at random to determine turn angle, bout displacement and the next interbout interval. Using these quantities, the particle was moved to a new position in the chamber, first updating the heading angle, followed by a displacement along the new direction, followed by an interbout interval. The temperature change induced by this bout was subsequently used to find the bin for picking the next bout and so on. Edge conditions were resolved such that whenever a movement would take the particle across the edge of the virtual arena, its direction of movement was aligned with the edge. This approach was chosen to mimic edge tracking behavior in larval zebrafish.

Simulations using the Markov model proceeded in the same manner as the other simulations. The major difference was that swim bout were chosen according to a parametric model instead of from raw data bins. At the start of the simulation the particle was set to be in the General swim mode. Subsequently swim bouts were chosen in the following manner. The Markov model was used to implement a probabilistic state transition with the transition matrix being computed using the current temperature and the temperature change across the previous swim bout. For computation a random parameter sample was drawn from the posterior distribution. After the state transition, a turn angle, displacement, and interbout interval were drawn according to the probability distributions generated by the emission models for the newly selected state. Characteristic parameters of the distributions (see: Emission models) were computed using temperature, temperature change, previous bout angle, and previous displacement by drawing a random parameter sample from the swim modes posterior distributions. Subsequently a random turn angle, displacement, and interbout interval were drawn from the emission distributions. The angle and displacement were used to implement movement of the particle while the interbout interval was used as a waiting time until the next swim mode transition and bout implementation.

Identification of neuron response types

Raw imaging data was processed using Suite2P32 to extract individual neural responses in each plane as well as deconvolve the calcium traces. Suite-2P was fine tuned to properly identify neurons in our imaging setup. Subsequently MINE was used to identify neural responses that could be predicted based on the temperature stimulus.33 A correlation cutoff on test data of r=0.6 was used to decide that a neuron’s activity was related to the stimulus. For hindbrain data all neurons passing threshold were used for subsequent clustering analysis. For trigeminal data, since our imaging planes also contained part of the hypothalamus, an anatomical mask was applied before the MINE fitting step to only select responses of trigeminal neurons.

Temperature stimulus related neurons were subsequently clustered using a greedy correlation based approach,34 with a correlation cutoff of r≤0.5. For hindbrain data a minimal cluster size of 25 was imposed and each cluster had to be present in at least 25 independent imaging planes. For trigeminal data, this criterion was relaxed to a minimal size of five neurons, present across at least three imaging planes. Clustering was performed on the raw fluorescence traces. For hindbrain data cluster average activity was reported using the deconvolved data. This approach was chosen to eliminate noise during clustering while improving timing accuracy in the reported responses to aid activity prediction during behavior (see below). This approach was not possible for the trigeminal data. Since the trigeminal ganglion is located at the ventral extent of the brain, imaging data obtained in the ganglion was too noisy to allow for deconvolution.

Prediction of neural activity during swimming and activity based modeling

MINE yields models with high predictive power allowing to predict how neurons were responding during the behavioral experiments. To exploit this fact, we trained MINE convolutional neural network (CNN) models to predict the deconvolved activity of each response type in the medulla based on the temperature stimulus. To better capture behaviorally relevant response timescales, only two seconds of stimulus history were used in these predictions. The depth of the CNN was increased to four layers which was required to get predictive power on some of the response types. The models were subsequently used to predict the response of each of the seven response types in the medulla at each timepoint in the gradient experiments using the temperature experienced by experimental fish exploring the gradient as inputs.

To test whether gradient position and direction could be decoded from the activity, linear regression models were trained to predict the current temperature as well as temperature change from the activity levels of each of the seven response types. The same train/test split on trajectories as in fitting the Navigation model was used. Models were fit on train data using Ridge regression and the ridge penalty was set using hold-one-out cross validation. Prediction R2 values on test data were subsequently reported. A similar approach was followed for the prediction of context, except that a logistic regression model was used to predict “worsening context”, “no context” and “improving context” on the data. The fraction of correct predictions on test data was subsequently reported. To define context, the following cutoffs were used. If the temperature was within 1 °C of the preference of 25 °C or if the temperature change was below the previously defined increasing/decreasing temperature threshold of 0.052 °C the condition was defined as “no context”. Outside these limits, worsening and improving context were defined as expected (e.g., decreasing temperature and below 24 °C indicated worsening context).

Activity based prediction on imaging data

To identify model-inputs that were plausible given the neural responses, linear regression models were fit to predict temperature T-25°C, absolute temperature |T-25°C|, temperature change ΔT and absolute temperature change |ΔT| in the imaging experiments based on the activity in the seven response types. The temperature change on the imaging experiments was computed as the change between the current temperature and the temperature one second ago to maintain consistency with the temperature change per bout assuming an average swim rate of 1Hz. The linear regression models were fit on 80% of the data, tested on the remaining 20% and R2 values on the test data were reported as goodness of the fit.

To predict model transition features (Figure 7), the Markov model GLM was first used to convert the temperature and temperature change (computed as above on the imaging trace) into model transition probabilities. From these, the probability of entering the reversal state (=p(g)p(g→r)+p(p)p(p→r)), the probability of staying in the persistent state (=p(p→p)) and the probability of being in the general state p(g) were computed for each timepoint during the imaging experiment. After a logit-transform of these quantities, linear regression models were fit to explain the quantities using the activity in the medullary temperature response types. The linear regression models were fit on 80% of the data, tested on the remaining 20% and R2 values on the test data were reported as goodness of the fit.

Analysis of spatial clustering of neuron response types

To test whether neuron types cluster spatially, for each neuron within a given type, the average distance to the five nearest neighbors of the same type NNin and to the five nearest neighbors of neurons outside the type NNout were computed. The expectation is, distances to neurons within the type would be reduced if neurons cluster spatially, while distances to other types would increase. Nearest neighbor distances were chosen over the average pairwise distance, since the bilateral symmetry of the brain would create spatially separated islands on the left and right side of the brain for types even if they were clustered into defined nuclei which would artificially inflate measured distances. All reported errors are non-parametric bootstrap standard errors or confidence intervals as indicated in the figure legends. As indicated in the figure legends, bootstrapping was either performed across fish (Figures 1, 3, and 4), simulations (Figure 6) or trajectories (Figure 2).
