The correlation between loss of cervical lordosis and the severity of ligament ossification in patients with cervical ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Kaiwen Chen, Shenyan Gu, Yaqing Guo, Yu Zhu, Feizhou Lyu, Jianyuan Jiang, Xiaosheng Ma, Xinlei Xia, Chaojun Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth genetic and environmental (e.g., mechanical stress) factors have been considered multifactorial components contributing to the development and progression of ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL). Importantly, loss of cervical lordosis may change the mechanical stress on posterior longitudinal ligament. However, few studies have discussed the relationship between the cervical sagittal alignment and OPLL. The aim of this study is to quantify the cervical sagittal alignment and severity of ligament ossification in patients with cervical OPLL and to analyze their correlation.MethodsCervical sagittal alignment was measured in 142 patients and 90 healthy subjects. All patients underwent assessments of the degree of ligament ossification and the severity of cervical cord injury. Finite element models of cervical lordosis, straightness and kyphosis were established, and the mechanical tension of the posterior longitudinal ligament was compared among the different models.ResultsThere was no difference of all measurements of cervical sagittal alignment between the normal control and patient groups (P > 0.05). Subgroup analysis demonstrated that the cervical OPLL patients in cervical kyphotic group showed greater OPLL ossification index (OP-index) and higher density [Hounsfeld unit (HU)] of ossified ligament compared to cervical lordotic group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, in the cervical kyphotic subgroup, the C2-7 Cobb angle was associated with both the OP-index (r=-0.61, P < 0.05) and the HU value (r=-0.78, P < 0.05), and there was a relationship between the C7 slope and HU value (r=-0.66, P < 0.05) in this subgroup. However, there was no relationship between the cervical sagittal alignment and OPLL severity in both cervical lordotic and straight groups (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the tension of posterior longitudinal ligament was greater in cervical kyphotic model than that in the other two models (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThere was a relationship between the loss of cervical sagittal alignment and both the degree and size of cervical OPLL in the cervical kyphosis subgroups in patients with OPLL. Therefore, it is necessary for clinicians to be aware of cervical sagittal alignment during cervical OPLL treatment, especially in patients with kyphosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09982-9.

Background

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is a disorder characterized by heterotopic bone formation in the posterior longitudinal ligament with a preponderance for the cervical spine [1, 2]. Cervical OPLL may cause neurological deficits, including myelopathy or radiculopathy, due to compression of the cervical cord and nerve roots [1–3]. Therefore, this disorder is receiving increasing attention worldwide.

Despite the clinical significance of this disease, the true pathogenesis of cervical OPLL is unclear. Both genetic and environmental factors have been considered multifactorial components contributing to the development and progression of this disorder [2–5]. In the past, the former was regarded as more important since OPLL most commonly occurs in the Asian population [6, 7]. However, with the increasing number of case reports of this disorder in other geographic regions and ethnicities (the prevalence ranges from 0.1 to 2.5% in North American and European countries) [8, 9], recently published reviews have indicated that local environmental factors (e.g., repeated/continuous mechanical stimulation) are presumed to be more important [10, 11].

It is generally considered that loss of cervical sagittal alignment is common in patients with cervical spinal diseases. Importantly, several previous studies have demonstrated that loss of cervical lordosis will increase anterior wall length of the spinal canal [12, 13], which may increase the mechanical stimulation on the posterior longitudinal ligament; thus, loss of cervical lordosis may be associated with the development and progression of cervical OPLL. However, there are few studies involving the cervical sagittal alignment in patients with cervical OPLL.

As such, the aims of the present study were to quantify both the cervical sagittal alignment and the severity of ligament ossification in patients with cervical OPLL and to investigate their association.

Methods

All methods used in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the ethical committee approval for this study was granted by the Human Ethics Committee (Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, China).

Setting

All subjects in this study underwent cervical-neutral X-ray. The patients with cervical OPLL underwent CT and MRI in the cervical neutral position, testing to determine the central motor conduction time (CMCT) (Yiruide CCY-I, Wuhan, China) detection and completed a cervical Japanese Orthopedic Association questionnaire (JOA; 0 = complete disability, 17 = no disability) [14, 15].

Participants

In this study, all subjects with cervical OPLL were recruited in our institution (spine center or health examination center) between March 2017 and April 2023, and healthy subjects were recruited from the health examination center (the department that regularly tests healthy people or screens certain occupational groups to exclude potential diseases) of author’s institution during the same period. Informed consent was obtained from the participants in this study.

Patients who were included in this study had a confirmed diagnosis of cervical OPLL by imaging studies, including X-ray, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with or without postoperative histopathology records. The exclusion criteria included previous spine surgery, spinal tumor/malformation/infection, ankylosing spondylitis, thoracic/lumbar spinal disorders, and other coexisting diseases that would affect peripheral nerve function.

The subjects in the normal control group exhibited no neck or arm symptoms and normal neurological examinations, including normal muscle strength, sensory function and deep tendon reflexes. Subjects who had undergone a history of diagnosis and treatment related to the whole spine and who exhibited current or past spinal trauma, spinal deformity, spinal tumor, systemic arthritis, diseases of the central nervous system, peripheral neuropathy, muscular dystrophy, diabetes, or peripheral vascular disease were excluded from the normal control group.

Variables

Imaging assessments

On cervical lateral X-ray, the C2-7 Cobb angle, C2-7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and C7 slope were measured. In the Cobb method, the angle between the inferior margin of C2 and the inferior margin of C7 is measured (Fig. 1). According to the C2-7 Cobb angle, the patients with cervical OPLL in this study were divided into cervical lordosis (> 10°), straight (0–10°) or kyphosis groups (< 0°) [16, 17]. Furthermore, the C7 slope was defined as the angle between a horizontal line and the C7 upper end plate (Fig. 1), and the C2-7 SVA was measured as the distance from the posterior superior tip of C7 to the C2 center vertical line (to the ground) (Fig. 1).

On cervical mid-sagittal CT images, the ossification index for OPLL (OP-index) was defined as the sum of the level of ossification at the vertebral bodies and the intervertebral discs according to methods described in previous studies [18, 19]. Furthermore, the spinal canal occupying ratio (COR) of OPLL was measured as the ratio of the maximal ossification thickness to the anteroposterior spinal canal diameter on cross-sectional CT imaging (Fig. 1). In addition, the density of the ossified ligament at the segment with maximal ossification thickness was measured via Hounsfeld units (HUs) from cross-sectional CT according to a previous study (Fig. 1) [20]. All of these measurements were used to reflect the severity of ligament ossification.

On the basis of the cross-sectional T2-weighted MRI, fatty infiltration of the posterior cervical paraspinal muscles was measured on both sides (Image-J; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and the mean values were recorded (Fig. 1). During the assessment of fatty infiltration, the average signal intensity of the posterior cervical muscles at the C4/5 segment were measured, and the average signal intensity of the subcutaneous fat was also measured at the same segment (Fig. 1). The degree of fatty infiltration at the C4/5 segment was subsequently measured by multiplying the posterior cervical muscle-subcutaneous fat ratio by 100 (the posterior cervical muscle-subcutaneous fat ratio was defined as the mean signal intensity of the muscle divided by the mean signal intensity of the subcutaneous fat) [21, 22].

Electrophysiological assessments

In accordance with our previous study [23], transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) was performed via a round 12.5 cm diameter coil. The centre of the coil was held over the vertex when motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were recorded from more-symptomatic abductor digiti minimi by surface recording electrodes. The stimulus intensity was adjusted to 20% above the threshold of the MEPs during maximum voluntary contraction, and the cortical motor threshold was identified. The shortest latency from the MEP out of 3–5 trials was regarded as the latency of the MEP (Fig. 2).

Both M-waves and F-waves were measured from the ipsilateral ADM by surface recording electrodes with stimulation at the wrist, and the shortest latency of both F-waves and M-waves were measured (Fig. 2).

The CMCT is measured by subtracting peripheral motor latency (PML) from corticomotor latency (CML), namely CMCT = CML – (F-latency + M-latency – 1) / 2.

Finite element analysis

The models used in this study were developed according to the CT images of a 52-year-old male from the normal control group. The C2-7 images were imported into Mimics Research 21.0 (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) software to generate three-dimensional models. The model optimization was completed with Geomagic 2017 (3D Systems, Inc., Rock Hill, South Carolina, USA), and the modified models were then imported into SolidWorks 2017 computer-aided design software (Dassault Systèmes SolidWorks Corporation, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The involved elements, including the ligament, cortical bone, cancellous bone, endplate, annulus fibrosus, nucleus pulposus, and articular cartilage were created. Then, according to the mean values of the C2-7 Cobb angle among the three different patient groups in this study, the position of the cervical vertebral body was adjusted to obtain different degrees of cervical curvature (cervical lordosis, straight and kyphosis) (Fig. 3). The biomechanical properties of the different elements were set (Supplementary Table 1), and the elements were meshed according to previous studies using ANSYS 21.0 (ANSYS, Ltd., Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA) [24, 25]. Finally, these cervical spinal models, namely, the cervical lordotic, straight and kyphotic models, were established.

The boundary and loading conditions were set according to a previous study [24]. A vertical force of 73.6 N was applied on the superior surface of C3 when the constraint on the lower surface of C7 was fixed, and then static biomechanical analysis was implemented via ANSYS 21.0 in three different cervical models. According to the equivalent stress results of the posterior longitudinal ligament, the ligament was divided into 11 segments according to the position of the vertebral body, and 20 points were randomly selected from each segment via probes to obtain their equivalent stress values (Fig. 3).

Furthermore, during this vertical force, 1.0 N.m bending moments of flexion, extension, left/right bending, and left/right rotation were also applied on the superior surface of C3 to simulate the physiological movements of the cervical segments, and the range of motion (ROM) of the above models was recorded and compared with those of previous studies for validation of the model.

Statistical methods

All the data were analyzed via SPSS version 21.0 (IBM, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test normally distributed data. The measurements between the patients with cervical OPLL and normal controls were evaluated via an independent t test or the Mann-Whitney test. The differences in the frequencies between the patients with cervical OPLL and normal subjects or among three cervical curvature groups were analyzed via the chi-squared test. One-way ANOVA (Bonferroni correction) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to evaluate the measurements among the different cervical curvature groups. The relationships between the measurements of cervical sagittal alignment and the degree of ligament ossification were evaluated via partial correlation analysis, controlling for variables such as age, BMI, disease duration, the degree of cervical spinal cord injury (SCI) and the degree of fatty infiltration. Similarly, partial correlation analysis was also used to assess the relationship between ligament ossification and fatty infiltration and the relationship between the ligament ossification and cervical SCI. In all instances, a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The current study included 142 patients with cervical OPLL and 90 healthy subjects, and there was no difference of age, sex and BMI between these two groups (Table 1). Furthermore, there was no difference of all measurements of cervical sagittal alignment between the normal control and patient groups (P > 0.05, Table 1), and the incidences of loss of cervical lordosis (e.g., cervical straight or kyphosis) were similar between these two subject groups (P > 0.05, Table 1).

Furthermore, fatty infiltration was weakly associated with OP-index (r = 0.26, P < 0.05); however, there was no correlation between fatty infiltration and cervical sagittal measurements in patients with cervical OPLL (P > 0.05). In addition, there was no relationship between either CMCTs or JOA scores and cervical sagittal measurements in the OPLL patients (P > 0.05), and these two measurements of cervical SCI was also not associated with the OPLL severity (P > 0.05).

According to the mean values of the C2-7 Cobb angle in the three different cervical OPLL subgroups, we established cervical lordotic, straight and kyphotic models (Fig. 3), and the results indicated that the ROMs of the current model were in accordance with the findings published previously [24, 25]. Furthermore, finite element analysis revealed that the length of the posterior longitudinal ligament gradually increased with the loss of cervical lordosis (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, obviously greater tension in the posterior longitudinal ligament was observed in the cervical kyphotic model than in both the cervical lordotic and straight models, and the cervical straight model clearly presented with greater ligament tension than the cervical lordosis model did (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, the tension of the posterior longitudinal ligament at the intervertebral disc level was obviously greater than that at the vertebral level (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 2). Equally important, significantly more patients with cervical OPLL presented with maximal ossification thickness at the intervertebral disc level than at the vertebral level (108/142, 76.1% vs. 34/142, 23.9%; P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3) in this study.

Discussion

The results of the present study demonstrated that there was no significant loss of cervical sagittal alignment in patients with cervical OPLL, and similar condition was also reported in many previous studies [26, 27]. According to the previous study [12], the volume of the spinal canal decreases with increasing spinal extension and increases with increasing spinal flexion; thus, the loss of cervical lordosis may be a compensatory pathological change to increase the volume of the spinal canal to reduce the compression of spinal cord/nerve roots, and this compensatory pathological change was common in patients with degenerative cervical spondylosis [28]. However, unlike degenerative cervical spondylosis, several previous studies demonstrated that cervical flexion may induce greater cervical cord compression in some cervical OPLL patients, especially those with cervical kyphosis or having OPLL with a high occupying rate [29, 30], which may be a possible reason for similar cervical sagittal alignments between healthy subjects and patients with cervical OPLL. Furthermore, the development of ligament ossification is very slow, and many patients with severe spinal stenosis caused by cervical OPLL remained myelopathy-free for a long period of time [1–3], which was supported by no relationship between the cervical SCI severity and OPLL severity in this study. Therefore, the ankylosed area of the cervical spine caused by pre-existing continuous bony connections in these patients may limit the occurrence of this compensatory pathological change when severe cervical cord compression caused by OPLL occurs, and approximately half of the patients in this study having continuous osteophyte formation (continuous-type and mixed-type OPLL) supported this possibility.

Importantly, above-mentioned possibility also provided an explanation for that fatty infiltration was correlated with OPLL severity but not with cervical sagittal alignment, and similar condition was also reported in a previous study [5], and this previous study further indicated a significant relationship between the cervical range of motion (ROM) and pathological changes of cervical paravertebral muscles. According to previous studies [28, 31], patients with cervical OPLL had significantly reduced cervical ROM because of the formation of bony connections. Therefore, compared with mechanical initiating factors, pathological changes of cervical paravertebral muscles may be the result of limited activities (disuse atrophy) caused by continuous bone connections. Fatty infiltration was correlated with OP-index but not with other measurements of OPLL severity, which may further support this explanation, since OP-index represented the range of longitudinal involvement in ossification; thus, greater OP-index was more common in the continuous-type OPLL patients.

Although there was no difference of incidence of loss of cervical lordosis between the OPLL patients and healthy subjects, the OPLL patients with loss of cervical lordosis showed relatively severe ligament ossification compared to those without loss of cervical lordosis, and subgroup analysis demonstrated a close correlation between the OPLL severity and cervical sagittal alignment in the cervical kyphotic subgroup. Finite element analysis revealed that mechanical tension on the posterior longitudinal ligament may gradually increase with the deterioration of cervical lordosis loss. Therefore, mechanical stress may be one of the original trigger for OPLL, which was further supported by many previous clinical and in vitro studies demonstrating that the persistent accumulation of tensile stress to the ligaments may cause and/or worsen ligament ossification [25, 32, 33]. Importantly, these results collectively supported that there was a relationship between the loss of cervical sagittal alignment and the severity of cervical OPLL in patients with kyphosis.

The results of this study should be interpreted with caution. Due to the cross-sectional design, the study cannot determine if kyphosis causes OPLL progression or vice versa. Although 50% patients with continuous/mixed-type OPLL had continuous bony connections, kyphosis may still be a beneficial protective mechanism secondary to spinal cord compression caused by severe OPLL in the other patients; thus, the relationship between these two measurements may be bidirectional. Furthermore, although long-term follow-up may be better for clarifying the causal relationship between the cervical sagittal alignment and ligament ossification, a large number of early OPLL patients were asymptomatic and difficult to diagnose through simple X-rays; thus, most patients included in this study already had varying degrees of clinical symptoms, which may cause selection bias. However, similar incidences of loss of cervical lordosis between healthy subjects and these patients with relatively severe OPLL may provide potential clues for exploring the causal relationship between cervical sagittal alignment and ligament ossification. In addition, although finite element analysis is a non-invasive method with simplicity and accuracy, capable of constructing complex models and calculating relevant biomechanical properties, the models in this study only involves static loading and single patient data and has applied some idealized simulations (e.g., boundary and loading conditions are highly idealized); thus, it cannot validate the stress-ossification hypothesis alone. Additionally, another clinical limitation of this study is low sample size, and more significant results to explore the causal relationship between these two measurements might be achieved in a future prospective (preferably blinded) long-term follow-up study with an increase in the number of patients.

Conclusions

The results of this study indicated that there was a relationship between the loss of cervical sagittal alignment and both the degree and size of cervical OPLL in the cervical kyphosis subgroups in patients with OPLL. Therefore, it is necessary for clinicians to be aware of cervical sagittal alignment during cervical OPLL treatment in patients with kyphosis.
