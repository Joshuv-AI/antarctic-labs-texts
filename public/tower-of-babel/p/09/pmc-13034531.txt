Unusual triple vascular presentation of antiphospholipid syndrome
Authors: Zhong Xhen Khor, Giiulia Ferrannini, Matteo Nicoletto, Carlos Minguito Carazo
Published: 2026

Case description

A man in his 30 s, an active smoker without prior comorbidities, presented with 2 weeks of calf claudication and dry gangrene of the right fifth toe (Figure 1A). Examination revealed absent distal pulses. CT angiography (CTA) demonstrated an eccentric mural thrombus in the infrarenal abdominal aorta extending into the iliac arteries (Figure 1B, 1C), along with extensive left iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (Figure 1D). ECHO was done, noting normal left ventricular ejection fraction 65%, no regional wall motion abnormalities and no intracardiac thrombus was noted.

(A) Picture of the right foot showed dry gangrene of the right fifth toe with dusky discolouration of adjacent toes and plantar surface of foot. (B) Coronal CT angiography (CTA) view showing an eccentric mural thrombus in the infrarenal abdominal aorta (arrow) extending into bilateral common iliac arteries. (C) Coronal CT angiography view showing extensive left iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (arrow). (D) Cross-sectional CTA view of the infrarenal aorta showing eccentric mural thrombus (arrow), with no evidence of atherosclerosis or aneurysm.

Laboratory testing showed prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 82.5 s (normal: 25–35 s) and an INR of 1.37 (normal: 0.8–1.2) and elevated anticardiolipin IgG antibodies [IgG 31.541 U/ml (normal: <15 U/ml)]; repeat testing confirmed persistent elevation of anticardiolipin [IgM 67.9 U/ml (normal <15 U/ml); IgG 56.2 U/ml (normal: <15 U/ml)] together with newly detected and elevated anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies IgM 66.4 U/ml (normal: <20 U/ml), IgG 103.5 U/ml (normal: <20 U/ml)), fulfilling the 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).1 He was started on lifelong warfarin therapy and smoking cessation advised. He was generally compliant to warfarin, with a time in therapeutic range of 70% (INR target 2–3). After 4 months, his claudication improved, and venous thrombosis resolved, as indicated on repeated ultrasound lower limb. CTA was not repeated due to logistic issues.

In antiphospholipid syndrome, thrombosis arises from a combination of endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and hypercoagulability driven by antibodies.1

Venous and arterial thromboses are well-recognized features of APS, but aortic mural thrombus (AMT) is exceedingly uncommon.2 AMT typically occurs with aneurysmal or atherosclerotic disease; in young patients without structural aortic pathology (Figure 1B), APS should be considered. AMT carries significant embolic risk and may be present as limb ischaemia or visceral infarction. While surgical or endovascular management is reported, this patient was managed with anticoagulation alone, with good outcome.

This case emphasizes the rarity of AMT and the importance of sequential antibody testing—from isolated anticardiolipin positivity to subsequent dual positivity with β2-glycoprotein I antibody—confirming the APS diagnosis. Recognition is critical, as timely anticoagulation prevents catastrophic embolic events.
