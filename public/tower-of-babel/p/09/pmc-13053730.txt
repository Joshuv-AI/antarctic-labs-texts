Bacterial cellulose–enhanced MTA versus conventional MTA for indirect pulp capping in primary teeth: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: V. Lalitha Priya, Kavitha Ramar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This randomized controlled trial aimed to compare the clinical and radiographic success rates of bacterial cellulose-reinforced MTA (BC-MTA) and conventional MTA in indirect pulp capping (IPT) treatment of primary teeth. A total of 40 primary molars diagnosed for IPT were randomly allocated into two groups: Group I (BC-MTA, n = 20) and Group II (Conventional MTA, n = 20). IPT procedures were carried out under aseptic conditions following local anesthesia, and teeth were restored with glass ionomer cement (GIC). Follow-up evaluations were performed clinically and radiographically at 3 and 9 months postoperatively. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 20, applying descriptive statistics, Chi-square, and independent t-tests (p < 0.05). At the end of 9 months, 75% of cases reported complete pain relief. Internal root resorption was observed in two teeth in Group I. Overall success rates at 9 months were 88.23% for BC-MTA and 100% for conventional MTA. Both BC-MTA and MTA showed favorable and comparable outcomes in IPT of primary teeth. While BC-MTA demonstrates potential due to its biocompatibility and improved handling characteristics, further studies are needed to validate its long-term clinical efficacy.Trial Registration: Ethical clearance was granted by SRM University IEC (SRMIEC-ST0323-442) and registered retrospectively in CTRI (CTRI/2024/12/077670).

Introduction

The most prevalent chronic oral illness in the world is dental caries, affecting approximately 2 billion adults and 520 million children (Peres et al. 2019). Its prevalence is increasing in developing countries due to lifestyle changes and urbanization. Preserving primary teeth until its natural exfoliation is crucial in pediatric dentistry, often achieved through vital pulp therapy. This includes direct pulp capping or pulpotomy for exposed pulps and indirect pulp treatment (IPT) for deep caries (Fuks 2008).

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) recognized indirect pulp capping (IPT) as a conservative approach to preserve pulp vitality in primary and permanent teeth—a concept introduced by John Tomes in 1859 (Marchi et al. 2007). IPT aims to halt caries progression, promote reparative dentin formation, and maintain pulp health by removing infected dentin while leaving the deepest layer intact to protect the pulp (Fuks 2008; Innes et al. 2016). This technique reduces the risk of pulp exposure and further damage to the dentin–pulp complex (Garrocho-Rangel et al. 2017). The first pulp capping was performed by Philip Pfaff using gold foil (Qureshi and Soujanya 2014), and since then, various materials have been used, including calcium hydroxide, biodentine, chlorhexidine (CHX), mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), theraCal-LC, and resin-modified glass ionomer cements (RMGIC) (Saber et al. 2021).

MTA is a biocompatible material composed of tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, calcium oxide, and bismuth oxide. (4:1 ratio) (Chauhan et al. 2018). The effectiveness is attributed to its fine particle size, sealing ability, alkaline pH, and sustained calcium ion release, which promote pulp cell growth, cytokine release, and dense tissue formation resembling hydroxyapatite (Chauhan et al. 2018). It also induces hydroxyapatite crystal formation upon contact with tissue fluids, aiding stem cell differentiation and pulp cell mineralization (Gurcan and Seymen 2019). Caprioglio et al. (2014) reported that MTA forms a thicker dentin bridge than calcium hydroxide does, with reduced inflammation and necrosis. However, their drawbacks include difficult handling, low compressive strength, long setting time, high cost, and potential tooth discoloration (Qureshi and Soujanya 2014; Ramar and Priya 2024).

Recent advancements in MTA include the development of calcium phosphate/silicate/bismutite cements, New Resin Cement (NRC) with 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), and MTA modified with 4-methacryloxyethyl trimellitate anhydride in methyl methacrylate initiated by tri-n-butyl borane (4-META/MMA-TBB). Innovations have also extended to light-curable materials such as TheraCal LC and the incorporation of chitosan (Pushpalatha et al. 2022; Mariyam et al. 2023). More recently, bacterial cellulose nanocrystals (BCNCs) have been incorporated into MTA to enhance its physiochemical properties for improved clinical performance (Ramar and Priya 2024).

Cellulose, sourced from abundant plant-based materials and certain bacteria, has gained attention for its eco-friendly and versatile applications, particularly bacterial cellulose, a naturally occurring hydrogel (Mohammadi et al. 2023). BCNCs are hygroscopic polymers derived from members of the Acetobacteraceae family and are predominantly produced by Gluconacetobacter xylinum (reclassified under the Komagataeibacter genus) in culture. BCNCs possess high crystallinity, excellent water retention, mechanical strength under wet conditions, and outstanding biocompatibility, making them ideal for integration with MTA (Ramar and Priya 2024; Cañas-Gutiérrez et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2021; De Oliveira et al. 2021). The ability of these materials to disperse effectively in matrices ensures material integrity, whereas their biodegradability and nonhemolytic nature further increase their applicability in biomedical materials (Cañas-Gutiérrez et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2021). Additionally, BCNCs contribute to the mechanical reinforcement of composites, forming robust scaffolds that support the structural stability of MTA-based materials (Singh et al. 2021).

Bacterial cellulose (BC) was considered as Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) by the US FDA for food applications and authorized for use in dentistry under code 42 by the DCGI. BC holds significant promise for dental applications, particularly in combination with bioactive materials such as MTA (Ramar and Priya 2024; Chen et al. 2018; Shi et al. 2014; Azeredo et al. 2019). The null hypothesis stated that there would be no statistically significant difference between BC-MTA and conventional MTA in the IPT procedure for primary teeth. The alternative hypothesis of this study was to evaluate and compare the clinical and radiographic success rates of BC-MTA with those of conventional MTA in the IPT procedure for primary teeth.

Materials and methods

Trial design

This was a randomized, triple-blind clinical trial was conducted at SRM Kattankulathur Dental College and Hospital in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry in Chengalpattu, Tamil Nadu. Trial registration and ethical approval

This research was ethically approved by the institutional ethics commission of SRM University in Kattankulathur, India (SRMIEC-ST0323-442). On December 4, 2024, the trial was registered retrospectively in the Indian clinical trials registry (CTRI/2024/12/077670).

Sample size calculation

The G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2 for Windows) was utilized to perform the power analysis for determining the success rate. A Cohen’s d effect size of 0.49 was estimated to detect clinically important differences between the study groups, assuming a Type I error probability (α) of 0.05 and a study power of 0.8 (80%). The calculated sample size was 17 subjects per group, which was increased by 19% (to 20 subjects per group) to compensate for possible dropouts.

Recruitment and informed consent

Individual participants comprehensive personal, medical, and dental histories were recorded before the start of the trial. The study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, safety protocols, and expected duration were thoroughly explained to all participants. The participants meeting the eligibility criteria provided written informed consent in both English and Tamil, affirming ethical compliance and voluntary participation in the trial.

Group allocation

Participants were randomized into two groups—Bacterial cellulose reinforced MTA (Group I, experimental) and conventional MTA (Group II, control group).

Random sequence generation and allocation concealment

Simple randomization was performed using a random sequence generator from Randomness and Integrity Services Ltd. (https://www.random.org/), assigning numbers 1 to 20 to Group I and 21 to 40 to Group II. A computer-generated allocation sequence was used to achieve randomization.

Blinding

This study employed a triple-blind design in which the participants, outcome assessor, and statistician were blinded to group allocation. Operator blinding was not possible because the materials had distinguishable handling characteristics, making the intervention identifiable during placement. Nevertheless, blinding of the assessor and statistician was maintained to minimize detection and analysis bias, thereby strengthening the validity of the study findings.

The overall process of participant management in the study, including assessment for eligibility, enrollment, randomization, intervention, allocation, follow-up, and analysis of participants, is comprehensively illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Materials

The materials used in this trial were BCNC powder (Vedayukt India Private Limited, Jharkhand, India) and MTA Plus™ (Prevest DenPro Limited, Jammu and Kashmir, India).

Preparation of experimental group

BC-MTA was formulated by combining 33.34% BC with 66.66% MTA (Jinga et al. 2014). The weights of both powders were precisely measured using a digital microbalance. To ensure the homogeneity and distribution of the mixture, the nanocrystals and MTA powder were initially blended using a mortar and pestle, then meticulously stirred for 30 min (Azmy et al. 2022). To maintain its integrity, the formulated sample was stored in an amber glass container, which was placed in a cool, dark setting to protect it from exposure to direct sunlight (Ramar and Priya 2024).

Standardization

A single clinician performed all treatments to minimize variability in material handling and application, strictly adhering to the manufacturer's instructions.

Clinical protocol

The selected participants were administered with 0.5 ml of local anesthesia (epinephrine:1:80,000, 2% lidocaine hydrochloride) and rubber dam isolation was carried out thereafter. Deep carious lesions involving the primary molars were managed in accordance with the revised AAPD guidelines.

According to Jinga et al. (2014), BC-MTA (group I) was manipulated with a 3:1 powder-to-liquid ratio. The slurry was manually and vigorously mixed on a glass slab using a Birtle’s applicator for approximately 30 s at 23 °C (± 2 °C) to obtain a uniform consistency (Ramar and Priya 2024; Jinga et al. 2014). The mixture was then transferred with a ball-ended condenser tip, lightly condensed with a moist cotton pellet, and temporized.

In the control group, MTA (group II) was manipulated with a standard powder and liquid proportion of 3:1 (Torabinejad et al.1993). The mixture was manipulated for approximately 30 s (Chauhan et al. 2018), transported into the oral cavity with a sterile MTA carrier, and placed on the cavity floor using a ball-ended condenser.

In both the experimental and control groups, the treated teeth were permanently restored with glass ionomer cement (GIC).

Clinical assessment

A baseline digital radiograph was taken using a calibrated grid. A metallic grid with 1 mm × 1 mm squares was positioned against the RVG sensor during exposure to guarantee precise image scaling. In this study to maintain consistency across all radiographs, the X-ray tube angulation and the participant’s cephalic position were standardized. The integrity of the restorations was evaluated on the basis of defined clinical and radiographic parameters. The presence of one or more of the following parameters was considered an indication of restoration failure (Airsang et al. 2022).

Restored teeth from both groups that were classified as “failures” were excluded from the study and were retreated accordingly.

Follow-up procedures

Clinical and radiographic evaluations of the restorations were assessed immediately after treatment (baseline) and at follow-up intervals of 3, 6, and 9 months.

Assessor calibration

Two blind, calibrated evaluators (S.P. and K.P.) independently assessed the outcomes, using standardized criteria to maintain consistency and reliability.The study did not involve the calibrators, and they resolved discrepancies between their evaluations through mutual discussion until they reached a consensus.

Inter- and intra-evaluator agreement levels were determined to be 97% and 99%, respectively, using Cohen’s kappa statistic. The weighted kappa (κ) was computed following the Fleiss–Cohen approach and presented along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Based on standard interpretation criteria, reliability was classified as excellent (κ > 0.75), fair to good (κ between 0.4 and 0.75), or poor (κ < 0.4). Only those evaluators who demonstrated “excellent” agreement levels were included in the assessment process.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome—to assess the clinical and radiographic success rate of BC-MTA and MTA as IPT material in primary teeth.

Secondary outcome—to evaluate variations in age and gender distribution between both the groups.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All explanatory and outcome factors were descriptively analyzed using frequency and proportions for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. The clinical and radiological evaluation criteria for groups I and II were compared over a 3-month and 9-month period using a McNemar's chi-squared (χ2) test. The distribution of age and gender between the two groups was compared using an independent t-test. A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was established.

Results

At baseline, 40 teeth (n = 20, each group) were treated, including 10 males (31.25%) and 22 females (68.5%). 35 cases were reported for the 3-month follow-up (group I = 19 and group II = 16), and 32 teeth were reported for follow-up at 9 months (group I = 19 and group II = 16). The mean age of the participants was 6.66 ± 2.54 years.

When evaluating the treatment results (n = 32 teeth), the combined dropout cases at the end of three and nine months were eliminated. The 6-month follow-up was not available due to a natural limitation. Universities in Chennai were forced to close due to flooding and severe rainfall. This natural disaster consequently made it impossible to successfully finish the 6-month follow-up.

In this study, five teeth in total had failed at 3-month intervals (group I: n = 1 and group II: n = 4) and three additional teeth (group I: n = 2 and group II: n = 1) failed at a 9-month interval (Tables 1 and 2). Discussion

Considering the biological lifespan of primary teeth, IPT is considered a conservative approach for managing the dentin-pulp complex (Marchi et al. 2007). When performed with a thorough dental history, controlled caries activity, and appropriate capping materials, IPT has demonstrated successful outcomes in primary teeth (Marchi et al. 2007).

The basis of IPT lies in the potential of mature odontoblasts to increase their synthetic and secretory functions following a decrease in microbial presence. Such activity leads to the formation of tertiary dentin, which increases the distance between carious lesions and the pulp, thereby reducing dentin permeability (Chauhan et al. 2018). While IPT and pulpotomy share similar indications, IPT is often preferred because of concerns associated with pulpotomy, such as internal root resorption, formocresol toxicity, discoloration, and the rare occurrence of dentigerous cysts. Additionally, IPT has been credited with long-term success, lasting 3–4 years, making it a more favorable option than pulpotomy in primary molars (Chauhan et al. 2018).

MTA is widely used in pulp therapy because of its sealing ability, alkaline pH, and calcium ion release, which promote healing and dentin formation (Pushpalatha et al. 2022). However, drawbacks such as long setting time, low compressive strength, handling difficulties, high cost, and tooth discoloration limit its use. To address these issues, modifications such as the addition of chitosan, calcium phosphate, and light-cured cements have been explored (Ramar and Priya 2024; Mohammadi et al. 2023). BC is a biocompatible hydrogel that improves the compressive strength, microhardness, and overall properties of MTA while preserving its biocompatibility. BC-MTA demonstrates superior crystallinity, cell viability, and mechanical properties over conventional MTA, highlighting their potential as a promising alternative (Cañas-Gutiérrez et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2021; De Oliveira et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2018).

Cañas-Gutiérrez et al. (2020) stated that researchers such as Helenius et al. (2006), Mendes et al. (2009), De Souza et al. (2011), Pértile et al. (2013), Li et al. (2015), Lv et al. (2016), and Osorio et al. (2019) have studied in vivo compatibility of BC in animal models including rats, mice, rabbits, and dogs. BC demonstrated excellent biocompatibility and integrated well with host tissues, without triggering inflammation or foreign body reactions (Cañas-Gutiérrez et al. 2020). Also Andrade et al. (2011), Leitão et al. (2013), and Osorio et al. (2019) classified BC as non-haemolytic per ASTM F756-00, 2000, and its hemocompatibility can be enhanced by material modifications. BC has been described by Yoshino et al. (2013), De Oliveira et al. (2021) as a novel material for root canal treatment in animals. A bacterial cellulose/otolith (OTL) nanocomposite was developed by Costa et al. (2012) for pulp capping in dogs. The researchers found that BC promoted the development of a mineralized barrier with an osteoid-like characteristic, suggesting that it might be used as a biocompatible pulp capping material.

This study is the first of its kind to evaluate the clinical and radiographical success rates of conventional MTA and MTA-modified BC. In this present study, 40 primary molars with deep caries management were selected. Complete pain relief was reported in 75% of the cases. Clinical and radiographic evaluations were planned to be carried out at 3, 6, and 9 months postoperatively. Owing to natural calamity, the 6-month follow-up data were omitted.

The overall success rate after 9-month intervals in group I was 88.23% and in group II was 100%. There was no statistically significant difference between both the groups at 3 months and 9 months. Therefore, we failed to reject the null hypothesis. This study's satisfactory outcome can be attributed to the standard protocol that was followed, as stated in the AAPD (2024). Careful case selection and an appropriate treatment plan are essential to VPT's success. The patients enrolled in the study were in the 3–9-year-old age group (mean age: 6.66 ± 2.54 years), as the younger pulp is more cellular and has better potential for healing and regeneration after removing the infected pulp tissue (Airsang et al. 2022).

The concerns regarding the tooth restoration interface include marginal integrity, marginal stain, and mechanical characteristics such as the thermal expansion coefficient, elasticity modulus, and the overall finishing of the enamel margin (Gianordoli-Neto et al. 2016). The marginal integrity criteria showed distinct values; nevertheless, there was no significant statistical difference. BC-MTA biomaterials exhibited slight degradation over time, which was observed through the changes in restoration scores from alpha to charlie during the 3- and 9-month periods. In the first 3 months, group I (BC-MTA) showed 47.36% alpha score and 26.31% in bravo and charlie scores; after 9 months, they showed 76.47%, 17.64%, and 5.88% of alpha, bravo, and charlie scores, respectively. The low clinical performance of BC-MTA could possibly be due to mechanical limitations or an inadequate level of bioactivity required for effective pulp tissue repair (Sulaeva et al. 2015). A study by Mohammadi et al. (2023) involving bacterial cellulose-reinforced GIC reported a higher incidence of cohesive failures within the BCNC-modified GIC group, which could account for the higher incidence of restoration loss observed in the BC-MTA group in the present study. Cohesive failure refers to the internal breakdown of the pulp capping material itself, rather than failure at the material–tooth interface (Dabbagh et al. 2021). According to Dabbagh et al. (2021) and Hilton (2009), such a failure within an IPT material may jeopardise the overall restoration's success and integrity.

The control group (MTA) showed 100% alpha scores in marginal integrity in both 3- and 9-month intervals. Previous studies by Chauhan et al. (2018) and Gurcan and Seymen (2019) support these findings. The sustained performance of MTA may be attributed to its ability to act as a calcium hydroxide reservoir (Gandolfi et al. 2012) and its known bioactive characteristics, including calcium ion release and hydroxyapatite formation, which contribute to pulp healing and dentin regeneration (Parirokh and Torabinejad 2010). Additionally, the quality of the final restoration plays a critical role in treatment success, as bacterial leakage can severely compromise outcomes (George et al.2015). Nandini et al. (2007) reported that placing GIC over MTA after 45 min does not interfere with its setting and that calcium salt deposition at the interface may enhance the seal.

In the present study, no statistically significant difference was found between the materials. However, a numerical variation was noted in Group I, as shown in Tables 1 and 2. This lack of statistical significance may be attributed to the similarities in the chemical composition of the materials. BCNCs, with a particle size ranging from 10 to 40 nm (Vasconcelos et al. 2017), account for approximately 33.34% of the total weight and contain a higher number of smaller particles. In contrast, MTA particles range from less than 1 µm to over 30 µm (Ha et al. 2016), contributing to around 66.67% of the total weight, also with a high proportion of fine particles. It is possible that significant differences may emerge over a longer observation period.

Internal root resorption was identified in two cases in Group I at the 9-month follow-up. The resorption was self-limiting and detected only radiographically, with no associated clinical signs or symptoms. This finding may be related to early material degradation and subsequent restoration loss, which could have allowed microleakage and pulpal irritation, thereby triggering inflammation and internal resorption (Akcay et al. 2016).

The limitations of this study include the short follow-up period and the lack of radiographic evaluation of dentin bridge formation, which is commonly assessed in pulp therapy studies. However, by 9 months, significant pathological changes were observed in the BC-MTA group, both clinically and radiographically. These findings suggest that BC-MTA may not be a reliable long-term substitute for MTA in the indirect pulp capping of primary teeth. While the BC-MTA procedure offered the advantage of a shorter chairside time, its long-term follow-up revealed radiographic failure, further questioning its durability and effectiveness over time.

Despite its limitations, this study highlights the potential of BC-MTA as a cost-effective alternative to MTA, particularly in environments with limited MTA availability. Nevertheless, the small sample size and patient dropout limit the ability to generalize the findings. Further research is needed to optimize its formulation, improve its properties, and validate its performance through larger studies and extended follow-ups. Additionally, comparative studies with other bioactive materials, such as calcium silicate-based cements, could offer deeper insights into the most suitable material for IPT in deciduous dentition.

Conclusion

This study concluded that all indirect pulp capping procedures demonstrated clinically satisfactory outcomes, with no significant differences observed between both groups over the 9-month evaluation period. Also, MTA consistently outperformed BC-MTA, showing better long-term clinical success and radiographic healing.
