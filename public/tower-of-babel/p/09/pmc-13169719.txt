Choroid plexus enlargement contributes to CSVD progression and white matter microstructural deterioration via brain-fluid dysregulation
Authors: Zan Wang, Dandan Liu, Xueli Cai, Yingying Yang, Xinya Zheng, Guanyu Niu, Jing Jing, Yongjun Wang, Yuesong Pan, Yilong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe choroid plexus (ChP) plays a pivotal role in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) production, blood-CSF barrier maintenance, and immune surveillance at the brain-periphery interface. While ChP enlargement has been implicated in several neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory disorders, its role in the pathogenesis of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) and frequently cooccurring white matter (WM) microstructural damage has not been systematically investigated, particularly in large-scale, longitudinal, population-based settings. We aimed to determine whether ChP enlargement contributes to CSVD progression, WM microstructural deterioration, and cognitive decline via glymphatic impairment and interstitial fluid accumulation.MethodsIn a community-based cohort (n = 2,186; mean age 61.3 ± 5.8 years), participants underwent multimodal brain MRI and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) at baseline and a median 2.5-year follow-up. Brain-fluid dysregulation was indexed by elevated extracellular free water (FW) and reduced diffusivity along the perivascular spaces (DTI-ALPS). Total CSVD burden, CSVD imaging markers, WM fibre-tract integrity (tissue-specific fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity), and WM network-level topology were comprehensively assessed. An independent hospital-based cohort (n = 249; mean age 67.2 ± 8.1 years) with comprehensive neuropsychological assessments, was included to serve as an external validation sample and to enable domain-specific analyses of cognitive impairment associated with ChP enlargement.ResultsAt baseline, larger ChP volume and greater brain-fluid dysregulation (i.e., elevated FW and reduced DTI-ALPS) were consistently associated with higher total CSVD burden, increased white-matter hyperintensity volume, higher numbers of lacunes and cerebral microbleeds, and more pronounced disruption of WM microstructural integrity (at both the WM fibre-tract and network levels), as well as poorer cognitive performance—particularly in executive function and information processing speed. Serial mediation analyses delineated hierarchical cascades: ChP enlargement affected WM microstructural integrity and cognition via elevated FW and reduced DTI-ALPS. Furthermore, in the PRECISE cohort, no significant relationship was observed between baseline ChP volume and longitudinal decline in global cognitive function (∆ MoCA scores). However, higher baseline ChP volume was robustly associated with longitudinal CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration over the median 2.5-year follow-up, with increases in FW significantly mediated these associations.ConclusionsThese findings support a framework in which ChP enlargement is associated with brain-fluid dysregulation and downstream CSVD pathology and WM microstructural degeneration. Further longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive neuropsychological batteries are warranted to clarify the role of ChP-related fluid dysregulation in domain-specific cognitive trajectories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-01981-1.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) encompasses a spectrum of age-related pathological changes affecting the brain’s small arteries, arterioles, capillaries, and venules. Radiologically, CSVD is characterized by white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS), and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), frequently accompanied by progressive white-matter (WM) microstructural damage. Overall, CSVD represents one of the major problems facing global society today, causing about a quarter of all ischemic strokes and the vast majority of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhages [1]. Moreover, CSVD is widely recognized as the most common pathology underlying vascular cognitive impairment and vascular dementia. CSVD-related cognitive impairment involves multiple cognitive domains, with deficits often most pronounced in processing speed, attention, and executive function [2]. However, despite its high prevalence and clinical significance, effective prevention or treatment strategies for CSVD remain limited, largely due to its heterogeneous and poorly understood pathophysiology. A deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms driving CSVD development, WM microstructural disruption, and concomitant cognitive impairment is critical for the development of potential intervention targets.

The choroid plexus (ChP), a highly vascularized structure located within the brain’s ventricular system, comprises a monolayer of epithelial cells encasing a stroma enriched with fenestrated capillaries, connective tissue, and resident immune cells [3]. The ChP plays a crucial role in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) production, maintenance of the blood–CSF barrier (BCSFB), and immune surveillance at the brain-periphery interface [3, 4]. With biological ageing and under chronic low-grade inflammation, the ChP undergoes substantial structural and functional remodeling, including epithelial hypertrophy, basement membrane thickening, microvascular rarefaction, and mitochondrial dysfunction [5, 6]. These alterations are accompanied by BCSFB disruption, dysregulated immune cell migration and activation, and the release of proinflammatory factors within the central nervous system [7–10], which together may contribute to WM demyelination and neuronal dysfunction, and ultimately cognitive impairment [5, 8]. Furthermore, given that the ChP produces approximately 65–80% of the total CSF, its dysfunction may alter CSF secretion, affecting brain-fluid dynamics and the function of the glymphatic system, a critical brain-wide waste clearance network [11, 12]. In the glymphatic system, CSF enters the brain parenchyma through the perivascular space around arteries, moves into the interstitial space via an astrocytic aquaporin 4-dependent process to remove interstitial fluid waste products, and then drains through perivenous spaces to the meninges and meningeal lymphatic vessels near major venous sinuses [13]. Impaired glymphatic clearance may promote interstitial fluid stasis and sustained neuroinflammation, thereby potentially exacerbating CSVD lesion accumulation and WM damage.

Emerging evidence indicates that ChP enlargement is associated with reduced ChP microvascular perfusion [14, 15], permeability [16], metabolic activity [17], and a slower rate of CSF secretion [13, 16], as well as sustained neuroinflammation [18, 19], supporting its potential as an in vivo marker of ChP dysfunction for use in large-scale clinical cohort studies. Although ChP enlargement has been implicated in several neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory disorders [20–23], its role in CSVD-related pathology and associated WM microstructural disruption remains underexplored [24, 25], particularly in large-scale population-based settings. Through its integrated roles in fluid regulation and neuroimmune balance, the ChP may serve as a mechanistic nexus linking glymphatic impairment and neuroinflammatory perturbations to CSVD-related brain injury and cognitive impairment.

Among the potential downstream consequences of ChP dysfunction, extracellular free water (FW) accumulation and impaired glymphatic clearance have emerged as sensitive indicators of pathological brain-fluid dynamics [26, 27]. FW, derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), reflects isotropic water diffusion in extracellular spaces and has been associated with blood-brain and blood-CSF barrier disruption, perivascular inflammation, and interstitial fluid stasis [26, 28]. In parallel, the DTI-ALPS (Diffusion Tensor Image Analysis Along the Perivascular Space) index offers a noninvasive in vivo marker of glymphatic function by quantifying perivascular diffusivity along perivascular pathways and CSF-interstitial fluid exchange [26, 27]. Both elevated FW and reduced DTI-ALPS index have been linked to greater CSVD burden, WMH volume, and worse performance in multiple cognitive domains [26, 29]. However, whether these fluid-related alterations mediate the associations between upstream ChP enlargement and downstream CSVD pathology, WM degeneration, and cognitive impairment in ageing populations has not been systematically investigated, particularly in longitudinal contexts.

To address this gap, we propose a novel framework whereby ChP enlargement may contribute to glymphatic impairment and extracellular fluid accumulation, which in turn promote CSVD progression, WM microstructural deterioration, and cognitive decline. To test this hypothesis, we performed cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses in a large community-based cohort (PRECISE), and externally validated the findings in an independent hospital-based cohort (NGCHS). Specifically, we aimed to: (1) examine cross-sectional associations between ChP volume and brain-fluid dysregulation markers (FW fraction and DTI-ALPS index); (2) evaluate their relationships with total CSVD burden, individual CSVD imaging markers, WM microstructural integrity at both the fibre-tract and network levels, and cognitive performance (both global and domain-specific, including episodic memory, visuospatial function, information processing speed, and executive function); (3) assess whether FW fraction and DTI-ALPS index mediate the associations between ChP volume and downstream CSVD pathology, WM microstructural integrity, and cognitive performance; and (4) determine, using longitudinal data, whether baseline ChP volume predicts subsequent progression of brain-fluid dysregulation, CSVD progression, WM microstructural deterioration, and cognitive decline, and whether changes in FW and DTI-ALPS mediate these trajectories. This work aims to elucidate the upstream role of ChP-related fluid dysregulation in the pathological cascade leading to CSVD progression, WM microstructural deterioration, and cognitive decline, providing novel insights into fluid-based mechanisms of brain ageing and highlighting potential targets for early intervention.

Methods

Study population

Cohort 1: Community-based cohort (PRECISE study)

The Poly-vasculaR Evaluation for Cognitive Impairment and vaScular Events (PRECISE) study is a population-based, prospective community cohort study conducted in Lishui City, Zhejiang Province, China [30]. The study aims to investigate the prevalence and progression of multi-territorial atherosclerosis and its associations vascular events and cognitive decline. Eligible participants were community-dwelling adults aged 50–75 years, selected through cluster sample from six villages and four communities. These villages and communities are stable residential areas (not occupational communities) with little population migration. Exclusion criteria included participants with contraindications to MRI (e.g., artificial tooth, implantable automatic defibrillator or any implanted metal device) and to computed tomography angiography (CTA) contraindication (e.g., allergy to iodine contrast, thyroid disease and renal impairment or dysfunction), those with a life expectancy ≤ 4 years due to advanced cancer and other severe disease, and individuals with mental disorders. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of the Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2017-010-01) and Lishui Hospital (2016-42), and all participants provided written informed consent.

At baseline (May 2017 to September 2019), 3,067 participants were enrolled. For the present cross-sectional analysis, those with available multi-modal brain MRI data, including 3D T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR), susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), as well as global cognitive assessment using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), were considered eligible. After excluding participants with missing or poor-quality 3D-T1w/DTI data, or data preprocessing errors, 2,217 participants with both analyzable 3D-T1w and DTI data remained. Following further exclusions due to incomplete CSVD evaluation, missing serum neurofilament light chain (NFL) data (n = 10), or missing MoCA scores (n = 21), 2,186 participants were included in the final cross-sectional analyses.

At follow-up, repeat multimodal brain MRI and MoCA assessments were conducted. Among the 2,186 participants included in the baseline cross-sectional analysis, 179 were excluded due to incomplete CSVD evaluation and 4 due to missing DTI or preprocessing errors at follow-up. After further excluding 159 participants without follow-up cognitive assessments, a total of 1,844 participants included in the longitudinal analysis (median follow-up, 2.5 years; interquartile range [IQR] 2.4–2.8 years; August 2020 to March 2022).

Cohort 2: Hospital-based validation cohort (NGCHS)

The Nanjing Geriatric Cognitive Health Study (NGCHS), conducted at the Department of Neurology, Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University enrolled 254 non-demented adults aged 50–80 years [31]. Participants underwent global (i.e., Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] and Mattis Dementia Rating Scale-2 [MDRS-2] and domain-specific neuropsychological assessments covering four cognitive domains (episodic memory, visuospatial function, information processing speed, and executive function), and multimodal brain MRI (3D-T1w, T2w, T2-FLAIR, DTI). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of ZhongDa Hospital, Southeast University (2019ZDSYLL189-P01). Detained inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods. After excluding 3 participants with missing 3D T1w data and 2 with missing DTI data, 249 were included in the external validation analysis.

Brain MRI acquisition

PRECISE dataset

All participants underwent brain MRI using the same 3.0T scanner (Ingenia 3.0T; Philips) at both baseline and follow-up. The protocol included the following sequences: 3D T1w magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE; echo time [TE] = 3.0 ms, repetition time [TR] = 6.7 ms, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3), T2w imaging, T2-FLAIR, SWI, and DTI. For baseline DTI, acquisition parameters were: TE = 90ms, TR = 3500ms, b-values = 0 and 1000 s/mm2 with 32 diffusion encoding directions, slice thickness = 2.5 mm. For the follow-up DTI, acquisition parameters were: TE = 86ms, TR = 6944ms, b-values = 0 and 1000 s/mm2 with 32 diffusion encoding directions, slice thickness = 2.5 mm.

NGCHS dataset

Brain MRI scans were acquired on a 3.0T scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) using a 12-channel head coil. Protocol included 3D T1w MPRAGE (TE = 2.48 ms, TR = 1900 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3), T2w imaging, T2-FLAIR, and DTI (TE = 90 ms, TR = 10,000 ms, b-values = 0 and 1000 s/mm2 with 30 diffusion encoding directions, slice thickness = 2.0 mm).

Detailed acquisition parameters for each MRI sequence in both the PRECISE and NGCHS datasets are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Measurement of serum NFL level

NFL is expressed in neuronal axons and stabilizes myelinated axons and provides structural support. When neuronal axons are injured, NFL is markedly elevated in the blood and CSF [32]. In the PRECISE cohort, fasting venous serum samples were collected in the morning. Blood samples were subsequently transported to the Beijing Tiantan Hospital biobank for storage via a cold chain system. Serum NFL concentrations, were measured using a Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) assay (Cat. #K1517XR-2) on the MSD SQ120 platform.

Each marker was rated independently by two blinded raters. Discrepancies were resolved by a senior neurologist who also blinded to the initial ratings. Inter-rater reliability was high, with κ-coefficients of 0.80 for presence of lacunes, 0.82 for WMH Fazekas grading, 0.90 for EPVS severity, and 0.80 for presence of CMBs.

Total CSVD burden was quantified using two validated ordinal scales: Wardlaw’s scale [34] (total CSVD burden; scored from 0 to 4): one point each for (1) WMH burden (Fazekas grade 3 for periventricular WMHs or Fazekas grades 2 ~ 3 for deep WMH), (2) presence of lacunes, (3) presence of CMBs, and (4) moderate-to-severe BG-EPVS (n > 10); Rothwell’s scale [35] (modified total CSVD burden; scored from 0 to 6): one point each for (1) presence of lacunes, (2) CMBs burden (n = 1 ~ 4), (3) severe BG-EPVS (n > 20), (4) modified WMH burden (total periventricular + deep WMH grade 3 ~ 4); two points for (5) CMBs burden (n ≥ 5), (6) modified WMH burden (total periventricular + deep WMH grade 5 ~ 6). These scoring systems are widely used in clinical research to comprehensively capture CSVD pathology.

MRI volumetric analysis

WMH volumes were estimated using the fully automated UBO Detector toolbox (https://cheba.unsw.edu.au/group/neuroimaging-pipeline) [36]. Briefly, individual FLAIR images were linearly registered to the corresponding T1w images, followed by tissue segmentation and nonlinear normalization to DARTEL space. Candidate WMH clusters were identified using FSL FAST, and a k-nearest neighbor classifier incorporating intensity, anatomical location, and cluster size was applied to exclude false positives. All WMH masks were visually inspected for quality. Normalized WMH volume was calculated as WMH volume divided by total intracranial volume (TIV) and expressed as WMH/TIV × 102, and log-transformed for normality.

ChP volume was extract from 3D-T1w images using FreeSurfer software (version 7.3.2), which performs tissue segmentation based on probabilistic atlases [24]. The pipeline includes motion correction, skull stripping, intensity normalization, surface reconstruction, and subcortical segmentation. ChP volumes from right and left hemispheres were extracted and combined, then normalized to TIV to generate a composite ratio (ChP/TIV × 103), which was used for subsequent analyses. All segmentations were visually inspected for quality by a neurologist blinded to clinical and imaging data and approved without any manual correction.

DTI processing

DTI data were processed using FMRIB’s Diffusion Toolbox (FSL, www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/) [24]. Briefly, raw DTI data were first corrected for head motion and eddy current-induced distortion using the “eddy_correct” function. The skull was then removing using the FSL’s Brain Extraction Tool (BET). Diffusion tensor maps were computed using the “dtifit” function of FSL.

DTI-ALPS calculation

The DTI-ALPS index was calculated using a standard atlas-based approach [31]. Four 5-mm spherical regions of interest (ROIs) were placed in bilateral projection and association fibre areas at the level of the lateral ventricle, based on the ICBM-DTI-81 WM atlas. ROIs were registered to each participant’s native diffusion space via nonlinear warping (FSL “fnirt”) and verified by visual inspection. FW mapping calculation

FW estimation was performed using a single-shell two-compartment model implemented in DIPY (Diffusion Imaging in Python) [26]. Each voxel’s signal was decomposed into tissue and isotropic (free water) components. The model outputs included the FW fraction (ranging from 0 to 1) and FW-corrected diffusion tensor maps, including tissue-specific fractional anisotropy (FA-t) and mean diffusivity (MD-t).

FW fraction, FA-t, and MD-t maps were first generated in each participant’s native diffusion space. For ROI definition and group-level aggregation, these maps were then nonlinearly registered to a standard FA template. Mean FW, FA-t, and MD-t values were extracted from 20 predefined WM ROIs using the JHU ICBM-DTI-81 WM atlas (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/other/datasets.html). Specifically, we calculated the mean FW, FA-t and MD-t within each ROI, and subsequently derived whole-brain mean FW, FA-t and MD-t by averaging the ROI-wise mean values. Because our primary outcomes were ROI-averaged diffusion metrics (rather than voxel-wise inference), the estimates are generally less sensitive to residual misregistration and interpolation effects [37, 38]. Although such effects cannot be entirely ruled out, their influence on ROI-level mean estimates is likely to be limited.

WM structural network construction and topological analyses

Structural connectivity networks were reconstructed using the Diffusion toolkit with the FACT algorithm [39]. Streamline propagation was terminated when the turning angle exceeded 45° or when it entered a voxel with FA value < 0.2. FA images were co-registered to T1w images, which were then normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space. The Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas was inverse-warped to native space, allowing parcellation into 90 cortical and subcortical nodes. Edges were defined streamlines connecting ≥ 3 fibres between regions, generating a 90 × 90 binary adjacency matrix representing the fibre number (FN)-based network for each participant. For sensitivity analysis, FA-weighted matrices were also constructed.

Global network metrics were calculated to characterize topological organization [39], including (i) small-world properties: clustering coefficient (Cp) and characteristic path length (Lp); (ii) Network efficiency: local efficiency (Eloc) and global efficiency (Eglob). Cp and Eloc quantify local segregation and neighborhood connectivity. Eglob quantifies the efficiency of parallel information transfer across distant regions and reflects integration capacity. Lp reflects the average shortest path length between all pairs of nodes, with lower values indicating more efficient global communication.

Statistical analysis

In the PRECISE cohort, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as global cognitive performance (i.e., MoCA scores), were compared across quartiles of baseline normalized ChP volume (Q1, < 0.609; Q2, ≥ 0.609; Q3, ≥ 0.757; Q4, ≥ 0.938). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with IQRs, and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Kruskal-Wallis test, or analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (n, %) and compared using the chi-square (χ2) test. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS V9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA), with two-tailed P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

NGCHS cohort (external validation)

Cross-sectional analyses assessing associations of normalized ChP volume, FW fraction, and DTI-ALPS index with WMH volume, WM microstructural integrity, and domain-specific cognitive function were replicated in the NGCHS cohort, adjusting for age, sex, and education. Serial multiple mediation models, using the same framework as in the PRECISE cohort, were applied to validate the mechanistic pathways.

Longitudinal analyses in the PRECISE cohort

To assess the longitudinal impact of baseline ChP enlargement, multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate associations between baseline ChP volume and subsequent CSVD progression, WMH growth, WM microstructural deterioration, and cognitive decline (defined as ∆ MoCA scores = follow-up minus baseline). All models were adjusted for baseline age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, prior heart disease, prior stroke, current smoking, and current drinking.

We further evaluated whether baseline ChP volume predicted longitudinal changes in brain fluid dysregulation, including increases in FW fraction and decreases in DTI-ALPS index (∆ FW fraction and ∆ DTI-ALPS index, defined as follow-up minus baseline).

Given the absence of a significant association between baseline ChP volume and longitudinal cognitive decline (∆ MoCA scores; see Results), we focused our mechanistic investigation on the pathway linking baseline ChP enlargement to CSVD progression, WMH growth and WM microstructural deterioration. Mediation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro in SAS (“Model 4” function) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples, adjusting for the same baseline covariates. These analyses tested whether ∆ FW fraction and ∆ DTI-ALPS index mediated the associations between baseline ChP volume and CSVD progression, WMH growth and WM microstructural deterioration. Standardized β and 95% bias-corrected CIs were reported for each direct and indirect path.

Step 2: serial multiple mediation

Serial mediation models (PROCESS “Model 6”) further delineated hierarchical pathways from ChP enlargement through brain-fluid dysregulation to CSVD pathology, WM microstructural disruption, and global cognitive impairment (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. 3).

In Model 1, the associations between ChP volume and WM network-level topological properties (Cp, Lp, Eloc, and Eglob) were significantly mediated by the DTI-ALPS index.

In Model 2, reduced DTI-ALPS index showed significant direct associations with lower MoCA scores, and this effect was partially mediated by disrupted WM network topology, which in turn was significantly associated with poorer global cognitive function.

Importantly, the sequential indirect effects of the full pathway from ChP volume through DTI-ALPS index and WM network-level topological disorganization to MoCA scores were all statistically significant, explaining 3.60% (Cp), 10.40% (Lp), 6.09% (Eloc), and 11.77% (Eglob) of the total effect. These findings support a mechanistic cascade whereby ChP enlargement contributes to cognitive impairment via glymphatic impairment and secondary WM network-level disruption.

Longitudinal associations of baseline ChP volume with cognitive decline, CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration: findings from the PRECISE cohort

However, larger baseline ChP volume was significantly associated with progression of total CSVD burden, WMH growth, and the counts of incident lacunes and CMBs over the median 2.5-year follow-up period. Sensitivity analyses using FA-weighted WM network-level topological properties

To assess the robustness of our findings to different WM network construction strategies, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using FA-weighted WM structural connectivity matrices instead of the FN-based binary matrices used in the primary analyses. Across the PRECISE cohort (at both baseline and follow-up data) and the NGCHS cohort (at baseline), key topological measures (Cp, Lp, Eloc, and Eglob) derived from FA-weighted matrices showed results largely consistent with those observed in the main analyses (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Tables 4–5; Supplementary Figs. 8–11), thereby supporting the robustness and stability of the observed associations across different network modeling approaches.

We additionally adjusted for lateral ventricular volume normalized to TIV (LVV/TIV) to address potential confounding by ventricular enlargement in both baseline cross-sectional and longitudinal associations in the PRECISE cohort (Supplementary Tables 6–7). After additional adjustment for LVV/TIV, effect estimates were generally attenuated but remained directionally consistent for most outcomes. Importantly, baseline ChP enlargement remained significantly associated with WMH growth, the counts of incident CMBs, WM microstructural deterioration, and progression of brain-fluid dysregulation (e.g., longitudinal increases in FW). These findings suggest that the observed associations are unlikely to be solely a secondary correlate of ventricular enlargement and that ChP enlargement may capture additional ChP-related pathology beyond ventricular size.

Discussion

In this community-based neuroimaging study, with external validation in a hospital-based cohort, we provide integrated evidence that ChP enlargement is associated with brain-fluid dysregulation, and with greater CSVD pathology, WM microstructural degeneration, and poorer cognitive performance.

Specifically, larger ChP volume was cross-sectionally associated with elevated extracellular FW and reduced DTI-ALPS, reflecting greater interstitial fluid accumulation and compromised glymphatic function. Serial mediation analyses suggested a sequential association in which ChP enlargement was linked to alterations in FW and DTI-ALPS indices, which in turn were associated with greater CSVD burden, disrupted WM microstructural integrity (at both the fibre-tract and network levels), and poorer cognitive performance, most notably in executive function and information processing speed. Furthermore, in the PRECISE cohort, no significant relationship was observed between baseline ChP volume and longitudinal decline in global cognitive function (∆ MoCA scores). However, higher baseline ChP volume was robustly associated with longitudinal CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration over the median 2.5-year follow-up, with increases in FW significantly mediated these associations.

Comparison with previous studies and novel contributions

ChP enlargement has been increasingly recognized across a range of neurological disorders, and accumulating evidence links it to WM damage at both macrostructural and microstructural levels, as well as cognitive impairment. For example, in Alzheimer’s disease, a large ADNI-based study reported that ChP enlargement was independently associated with WM microstructural damage (indexed by higher PSMD) and worse global cognitive performance (MMSE), and further suggested that WM microstructural damage may partially mediate the association between ChP enlargement and cognitive decline [43]. In multiple sclerosis, a larger normalized ChP volume has been associated with greater T2-hyperintense WM lesion volumes, and glymphatic dysfunction indexed by a lower DTI-ALPS was reported to partially mediate this association, supporting a potential ChP–glymphatic impairment–WM lesion burden pathway [13]. In addition, ChP enlargement has been linked to WM microstructural abnormalities in other conditions such as post-COVID condition (long COVID), further supporting a cross-disorder ChP–WM damage axis [22].

In ageing-related and population-based settings, recent studies have further suggested that ChP enlargement is clinically relevant, showing associations with CSVD pathology [24, 25, 44] and cognitive impairment [45]. Building on these observations, our findings align with and extend prior population-based evidence. However, these prior associations were largely observational, and the underlying mechanisms driving them remained speculative. Moreover, most prior work focused primarily on WMH volume, without systematically evaluating total CSVD burden, other CSVD imaging markers, or WM microstructural integrity, and lacked large-scale longitudinal validation.

By leveraging a large-scale, population-based cohort with both cross-sectional and longitudinal assessments of brain imaging and cognitive function, our study provides several important advancements. First, we introduced brain-fluid dysregulation, captured by FW accumulation and a reduced DTI-ALPS index, as a potential mechanistic bridge between ChP enlargement and downstream CSVD pathology. Second, our serial mediation analyses suggested a sequential association in which ChP enlargement was linked to reduced DTI-ALPS and elevated FW, which in turn were associated with greater CSVD burden, disrupted WM microstructural integrity, and poorer cognitive performance. Third, our longitudinal analyses demonstrated that baseline ChP volume predicted subsequent increases in FW fraction, CSVD progression, and WM microstructural deterioration, thereby strengthening the temporality and pathophysiological relevance of these associations.

Collectively, these findings not only validate and extend previous observations regarding the clinical significance of ChP enlargement but also offer novel mechanistic insights. They highlight ChP-driven brain-fluid dysregulation as a potentially important pathway contributing to CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration, thereby suggesting a potential target for early intervention and disease modification in ageing populations.

Mechanistic pathways linking chp enlargement to CSVD and WM degeneration

The ChP plays a multifaceted role in maintaining CNS homeostasis, and its dysfunction may act as a critical upstream event in the cascade leading to brain-fluid dysfunction, CSVD pathology, WM microstructural disruption, and cognitive impairment.

First, as the primary site of CSF production, the ChP generates the hydrostatic pressure gradient necessary to drive glymphatic circulation. With physiological ageing or disease-related remodeling, ChP epithelial cells exhibit diminished secretory capacity, resulting in reduced CSF production and impaired convective clearance of interstitial solutes [15, 24]. Such attenuation in fluid propulsion compromises glymphatic efficiency, causing interstitial fluid stagnation, extracellular FW accumulation, and reduced clearance of neurotoxic metabolites—processes strongly implicated in CSVD pathogenesis and WM degeneration.

Second, the ChP serves as a crucial immunological interface between periphery circulation and the CNS. During ageing and chronic inflammatory states, ChP epithelial cells develop a senescence-associated phenotype characterized by a cytokine-chemokine imbalance skewed toward Th2-like epithelial inflammation and impaired immune surveillance capacity [8]. This state is marked by compromised tight junction integrity and aberrant upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, including type I interferon (IFN-I)-dependent signaling molecules [7], the Th2-associated chemokine CCL11 [8], tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [46], and cathepsin S [9]. These changes disrupt the blood-CSF barrier integrity and facilitate leukocyte infiltration into the CSF, thereby amplifying neuroinflammatory cascades within the CNS [7, 8, 46]. A persistently dysfunctional ChP may thus promote a pro-inflammatory CSF environment and sustained neuroinflammation, disproportionately affecting WM regions. Moreover, sustained neuroinflammation may impair the expression and polarization of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) channels on astrocytic end-feet [47, 48], further hindering glymphatic clearance and exacerbating FW accumulation. This interplay between fluid dysregulation and neuroinflammation may form a self-perpetuating cycle, contributing to progressive WM damage. In this context, the DTI-ALPS index has been proposed as a noninvasive approach to evaluate glymphatic system function in vivo, with lower values suggesting impaired glymphatic clearance [24]. Elevated WM FW fraction may reflect extracellular fluid accumulation due to neuroinflammatory or neuroimmune perturbations, vasogenic edema, or intramyelinic vacuolization [26, 49, 50]. Elevated FW may also indicate impaired interstitial fluid clearance, potentially reflecting reduced glymphatic outflow [26]. Our findings that both the DTI-ALPS index and FW fraction mediated the associations between ChP enlargement and increased CSVD burden and WM microstructural disruption support the hypothesis that ChP-driven alterations in brain-fluid dynamics and immune-inflammation dysregulation collectively promote to cerebral microvascular injury and WM degeneration.

Third, beyond its roles in CSF secretion and immunological surveillance, the ChP synthesizes and releases a repertoire of neurotrophic and metabolic factors, including 5-Methyltetrahydrofolate, growth factors and axon growth factors, which are essential for maintaining neuronal healthy, promoting myelination, and facilitating WM repair [5]. Disruption of this neurotrophic environment may compromise oligodendrocyte viability, axonal resilience, and remyelination capacity, thereby further exacerbating WM degeneration, network disintegration, and cognitive vulnerability.

Collectively, these mechanisms are likely synergistic: fluid stagnation, neuroinflammatory perturbation, and neurotrophic insufficiency may interactively create a permissive environment for CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration in ageing populations.

ChP enlargement as an in vivo imaging marker of ChP dysfunction

ChP volume, which can be robustly quantified from routine high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MRI, provides a simple structural imaging marker that aligns closely with multiple dimensions of ChP dysfunction in previous work [14–17, 51, 52]. Importantly, ChP enlargement is not a direct functional readout; rather, it may represent a macroscopic signature of ChP structural remodeling that can co-occur with functional alterations at the blood-CSF interface. Histopathological studies suggest that ChP enlargement may reflect morphological remodeling of the ChP, including increased stromal fibrosis, stromal dystrophic calcification, blood vessel thickening, and inflammatory cell infiltration, changes that may eventually lead to failure of CSF production and clearance [16, 53]. In line with this concept, prior work in patients with WMH demonstrated that larger ChP volume was consistently associated with lower FA and higher MD within the ChP [24], indicating microstructural disorganization in the enlarged ChP. Moreover, in a DCE-MRI cohort across the Alzheimer disease clinical spectrum, larger ChP volume was accompanied by lower Ktrans and lower fractional plasma volume (Vp), suggesting microvascular compromise and lower permeability within the enlarged ChP [16]. Considered together with accumulating evidence that ChP enlargement is associated with reduced CSF secretory capacity [15], impaired glymphatic clearance [24], and sustained neuroinflammation [18, 54], these convergent lines of evidence support the interpretation that volumetric ChP enlargement may capture a composite state of CSF secretory dysfunction, glymphatic impairment, microcirculatory compromise, and inflammatory activation. In this context, normalized ChP volume emerges as a biologically grounded and practically accessible surrogate imaging marker of ChP dysfunction, well suited for large-scale research. Nevertheless, direct in vivo quantification of ChP function remains challenging at scale; therefore, normalized ChP volume should be viewed as a pragmatic surrogate phenotype, and future studies incorporating dedicated functional ChP imaging and CSF biomarkers are warranted for more direct validation.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of this study lies in the integration of a large, population-based cohort with both cross-sectional and longitudinal multimodal brain MRI data and cognitive assessments, enabling comprehensive evaluation of ChP volume, brain-fluid dynamics, CSVD pathology, WM microstructural integrity at both the WM fibre-tract and network levels in relation to cognitive function. Importantly, the application of serial mediation models delineated a mechanistic cascade linking ChP enlargement to brain-fluid dysregulation, CSVD progression, WM microstructural deterioration, and ultimately cognitive decline. Furthermore, the largely harmonized MRI acquisition protocols across time points enhanced the validity and robustness of our findings on CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration. Finally, replication and extension of these associations in an independent hospital-based cohort further strengthened the generalizability and external validity of our findings.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, automatic segmentation of the ChP may not fully capture its complex and highly folded anatomy. However, given the large sample size, we employed a widely used automated method that has been previously validated and demonstrated good inter-method reliability with manual segmentation [24, 31, 55]. All segmentations were visually inspected for quality by a neurologist blinded to clinical and imaging data (Supplementary Fig. 12 A). Additionally, we employed a deep learning algorithm [56] to calculate ChP volumes and compared the results with those obtained using FreeSurfer. The deep learning–derived ChP volume showing a strong correlation with FreeSurfer estimates (Spearman r = 0.740, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 12B). This algorithm has been previously validated with manual segmentation and shown to have good consistency across various datasets. Second, while extracellular FW has been increasingly considered a sensitive in vivo marker of neuroinflammation [50, 57, 58], our study did not include direct measures of central neuroinflammation, such as CSF cytokines or translocator protein positron emission tomography (TSPO-PET) imaging. Further studies incorporating such direct assessments are warranted to validate the proposed mechanistic links. Third, in the PRECISE cohort, cognitive function was primarily assessed using a brief global screening tool (i.e., MoCA), which may have limited sensitivity for detecting subtle cognitive changes over a relatively short follow-up period. Further studies incorporating comprehensive neuropsychological batteries at both baseline and follow-up are warranted to better characterize domain-specific trajectories of cognitive decline. Fourth, EPVS severity was assessed using a visual semi-quantitative scale on conventional 2D T2-weighted MRI. Although standardized STRIVE-based criteria with substantial inter-rater reliability were applied, visual grading may still be susceptible to rater variability. Emerging AI-based approaches enable more objective quantification of EPVS burden (e.g., volumetric measures), but these methods typically require high-resolution 3D T2-weighted imaging and validated segmentation pipelines, which were not available in our dataset. Further studies incorporating such advanced imaging and validated automated pipelines are warranted to enable more precise and objective EPVS assessment. Fifth, although we adjusted for a wide range of demographic and vascular risk factors, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded due to the observational nature of our study. Sixth, while our findings were internally replicated across two independent cohorts with different recruitment settings (community-based and hospital-based), both cohorts consisted entirely of individuals from a Chinese population. Therefore, further validation in non-Asian populations is warranted to confirm the broader generalizability of our findings. Finally, this study is hypothesis-generating in nature and does not establish causality; mechanistic inferences should be interpreted with caution and further validated through experimental or interventional studies.

Conclusions and clinical implications

This study supports a framework in which ChP enlargement is associated with brain-fluid dysregulation, thereby contributing to CSVD progression and WM microstructural deterioration in ageing populations. Further longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive neuropsychological batteries are warranted to clarify the role of ChP-related fluid dysregulation in domain-specific cognitive trajectories. Importantly, ChP volume is readily quantifiable from routine brain MRI scans, underscoring its translational potential as an imaging marker for early risk stratification. By incorporating extracellular FW and DTI-ALPS index as intermediate markers of glymphatic dysfunction and interstitial fluid accumulation, we mapped a mechanistic pathway linking ChP enlargement to CSVD pathology and WM microstructural disruption.

These findings highlight the clinical relevance of ChP structure and function in the ageing brain and suggest that ChP-related fluid dysregulation may serve as a promising target for early intervention in CSVD and cognitive disorders associated with impaired brain fluid homeostasis. Further investigations are required to validate these findings and elucidate the involved mechanisms.
