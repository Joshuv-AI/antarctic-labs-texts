Latent biochemical phenotypes delineate divergent health trajectories in older adults
Authors: Raquel González-Martos, Irene Rodríguez-Gómez, Javier Galeano, Ignacio Ara, Luis M Alegre, Leocadio Rodríguez-Mañas, Francisco J Garcia-Garcia, Carmen Ramírez-Castillejo, Amelia Guadalupe-Grau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgeing heterogeneity hampers prevention and care. We used routine biochemical panels and unsupervised learning to identify latent phenotypes in community-dwelling older adults. In 1491 participants from the Toledo Study for Healthy Ageing (TSHA) with ~10–11 years of follow-up, 39 blood biomarkers were dimension-reduced and clustered, yielding three phenotypes: Healthy, Metabolic (subclinical dysmetabolism), and Haematological (low erythroid/renal profile). Phenotypes differed in functional capacity, frailty, and independence at baseline (all p < 0.05 after age/sex adjustment) and predicted long-term mortality (Metabolic women HR = 1.49, p = 0.016). Sex-specific analyses revealed distinct disease-trajectory patterns (e.g., hypertension in Metabolic women HR = 1.30, p = 0.005; thrombosis in Haematological men HR = 7.20, p = 0.018; syncope in Haematological women HR = 1.88, p = 0.009). Findings are partially replicated in a cohort of physically active older adults (EXERNET), supporting the generalizability of the Metabolic phenotype. Standard laboratory data, integrated through machine learning, capture ageing-relevant biology and stratify future risk without specialised assays, enabling low-cost, scalable precision prevention.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Endocrinology, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Ageing is associated with increased disease burden, reduced physiological resilience, and higher mortality risk1. Routine blood tests provide early insight into these processes, as biochemical parameters—often referred to as indicators of “biological age”—reflect multi-organ function and may expose subclinical dysregulation before clinical disease appears2. Even within reference ranges, their combined patterns may help identify individuals at elevated risk and inform preventive strategies.

Multiple routine analytes have been associated with adverse outcomes in old age3–5. However, they are often studied individually, overlooking the body’s integrated nature6. In this context, previous work developed composite biological age metrics based on several blood biomarkers (e.g., Phenotypic Age), demonstrating improved prediction over chronological age7.

Furthermore, these biomarkers are influenced not only by interactions with other analytes but also by external health-related factors such as diet, physical activity, functional status, and cognitive performance8. Thus, to understand biological ageing, it is essential to consider the combined patterns of these markers rather than isolated values.

Given this complexity, machine learning (ML) offers a powerful approach to detecting hidden structures in biochemical datasets. While prior biological age models have typically relied on supervised optimisation of predefined outcomes7, unsupervised ML can identify latent phenotypes without predefined labels, enabling a data-driven exploration of biological heterogeneity9. However, its application to routine biochemical data in community-dwelling older adults remains scarce, as most studies rely on clinical records10.

Building on our previous work in physically active older adults from the EXERNET multi-centre study11—where unsupervised ML of routine biochemical markers identified metabolic phenotypes associated with long-term mortality— the present study extends this approach to a representative population-based cohort: the Toledo Study for Healthy Ageing (TSHA). The TSHA cohort provides extensive biochemical, functional, and clinical information with long-term follow-up.

This current study aims to identify latent biochemical phenotypes in older adults, characterise their functional and clinical profiles, map sex-specific health trajectories, and evaluate their prognostic value regarding mortality and healthcare usage over approximately 10–11 years. We also compared key characteristics with an independent cohort of physically active older adults (EXERNET). This approach may allow routine blood tests to serve as a scalable, clinically informative tool for precision risk stratification in ageing.

Results

These results are based on the TSHA, a population-based longitudinal study including 2488 people aged over 65 years residing in the province of Toledo, Spain. For the cluster analysis of routine biochemical data, 997 participants were excluded (Supplementary Fig. S1). Consequently, 1491 participants were included in the cluster and ANCOVA analyses, adjusted for age and stratified by sex (631 men, 860 women). During the 11 years of follow-up for disease, hospitalisation, and mortality (consulted in 2019), participants with incomplete follow-up data or who were unable to complete the physical performance tests were excluded. The longitudinal analyses, with mortality and disease outcomes, were assessed based on the data available for each participant.

Phenotype discovery from routine biochemistry

Cluster analysis of 39 routine biochemical variables was performed and reduced to 2 dimensions using Principal Components Analysis (PCA, Fig. 1). The main contributors to the variance were insulin-related variables and the Homeostatic Model Assessment (HOMA) index in one component, and haemoglobin and haematocrit in the other. Both K-means (elbow method) and hierarchical clustering indicated three clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2).

One cluster, comprising ~42% of participants, exhibited broadly optimal biochemical values, including intermediate red blood cell indices, hence referred to as the Healthy Cluster. Another cluster (44.5%) was characterised by higher glucose, triglycerides, HOMA index, and liver enzymes (gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (GPT/ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (GOT/AST), mostly within reference ranges, indicating subclinical dysmetabolism, and is therefore called the Metabolic Cluster. The third cluster (~13.5%) showed lower haemoglobin, haematocrit, and red blood cells (RBC) indices, reduced lymphocyte counts, and subtle renal alterations (creatinine, uric acid, urea) within normal limits, reflecting low erythroid reserve and giving rise to the name Haematological Cluster.

Socio-demographic, activity and independence characteristics

Table 2 shows the sociodemographic parameters of the phenotypes by sex. Among men, the Metabolic cluster predominated (60.9%), while the Healthy cluster accounted for 23.2% (p < 0.001). Among women, Healthy was predominant (76.8%), followed by Haematological (59.8%) and Metabolic (39.1%) (p < 0.001). Men in the Haematological cluster were older; similar in women (p < 0.001). Other socio-demographic variables showed no clear differences.

Beyond demographic factors, activity and independence measures also differed between clusters (Table 2). Men in the Haematological cluster showed lower physical activity (Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly, PASE, p = 0.016) compared with the Metabolic cluster. Both men and women in this cluster had reduced independence in basic daily activities (Katz, p < 0.001 and p = 0.03, respectively) and, among women, also in instrumental activities (Lawton, p = 0.02 and p < 0.001 vs. Metabolic and Healthy). Although men tended to score lower on Lawton overall, differences were not significant. No significant differences were observed in depressive symptoms (Geriatric Depression Scale, GDS) or cognitive function (Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE).

Physical functionality, anthropometric measures and frailty

In age-adjusted ANCOVAS stratified by sex (Table 3), differences emerged in physical function, frailty, and anthropometric measures. Men in the Haematological cluster had lower gait speed (p = 0.027) and completed fewer chair stand repetitions (p = 0.038) than those in the Metabolic cluster, while no significant differences were observed among women. Regarding muscle strength, only women in the Haematological cluster showed lower knee strength compared with the Metabolic cluster (p = 0.038). BMI was higher in the Metabolic than in the Healthy cluster in both sexes (men p = 0.008; women p = 0.004), whereas waist-to-hip ratio did not differ significantly. Frailty prevalence was also higher in the Haematological cluster, reaching statistical significance only among men (p < 0.001).

Long-term health trajectories

The comparison of disease progression across biochemical clusters, stratified by sex and adjusted for sociodemographic covariates and physical activity (PASE score) (Model 3), revealed heterogeneous associations. The Healthy cluster served as reference and the absolute number of events, and incidence rates per 1000 person-years for each cluster and sex are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

For hypertension, men in the Metabolic cluster showed no significant increase in risk (Hazard Ratio, HR = 1.077; 95% CI = 0.851–1.362; p = 0.539), whereas women had a higher risk (HR= 1.301; 95% CI = 1.081–1.567; p = 0.005). The Haematological cluster showed a borderline effect in men (HR = 1.354; 95% CI =: 0.951–1.927; p: 0.093) and no significant association in women (HR = 1.162; 95% CI = 0.896–1.508; p = 0.257).

For diabetes, the Metabolic cluster was not significantly associated in men (HR = 0.919; 95% CI = 0.703–1.201; p = 0.537) or women (HR = 1.120; 95% CI = 0.918–1.367; p = 0.264), though incidence rates were higher in both. The Haematological cluster showed no significant effect in men (HR = 1.230; 95% CI = 0.836–1.809; p = 0.293), but increased risk in women (HR = 1.456; 95% CI = 1.103–1.922; p = 0.008).

For thrombosis, men in the Metabolic cluster showed a strong but non-significant trend (HR = 2.879; 95% CI: 0.943–8.791; p = 0.063), while women showed none (HR = 1.072; 95% CI = 0.512–2.245; p = 0.854). In contrast, the Haematological cluster was significant in men (HR = 7.198; 95% CI = 1.395–37.146; p = 0.018) but not in women (HR = 2.734; 95% CI = 0.805–9.277; p = 0.107).

No significant associations were observed for asthma in the Metabolic cluster (HR = 0.932; 95% CI = 0.462–1.878; p = 0.843; women: HR = 1.614; 95% CI = 0.808–3.223; p = 0.175) or in the Haematological cluster (men: HR = 1.871; 95% CI = 0.707–4.951; p = 0.207; women: HR = 1.874; 95% CI = 0.609–5.764; p = 0.273). The Healthy cluster showed relatively high asthma incidence across sexes.

For anaemia, the Metabolic cluster showed no association (men: HR = 1.122; 95% CI = 0.601–2.093; p = 0.717; women: HR = 1.125; 95% CI = 0.714–1.772; p = 0.612), whereas the Haematological cluster was associated with higher risk in men (HR = 2.310; 95% CI = 1.071–4.984; p = 0.033) but not in women (HR = 1.314; 95% CI = 0.836–2.066; p = 0.237). These findings are consistent with the higher incidence rates observed in the Haematological Cluster for both sexes.

Finally, for syncope, no associations were detected in the Metabolic cluster (HR = 0.907; 95% CI = 0.418–1.970; p = 0.805; women: HR = 0.863; 95% CI = 0.607–1.227; p = 0.186). In the Haematological cluster, men showed no significant risk (HR = 1.350; 95% CI = 0.484–3.767; p = 0.566), whereas women showed a strong association (HR = 1.878; 95% CI = 1.168–3.018; p = 0.009), consistent with their higher incidence rates.

Mortality risk and hospital admissions

Cox regression analyses revealed significant differences in all-cause mortality and hospitalisations between clusters, stratified by sex and adjusted for sociodemographic variables and physical activity (PASE score) in Model 3 (Fig. 3). Event counts and incidence rates per 1000 person-years for hospitalisations are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Cox proportional hazards analyses of all-cause mortality and risk of hospital admission over 11 years in the three biochemical profiles.

Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, socioeconomic status, education, and marital status; Model 3: additionally adjusted for physical activity using the PASE score; Hybrid Model: model 3 additionally adjusted for HOMA-IR and haemoglobin as representative individual biochemical factors.

No significant differences in hospitalisation risk were observed between clusters after full adjustment. In contrast, mortality showed clear sex-specific patterns: women in the Metabolic cluster exhibited a significantly higher risk compared with the Healthy group, while men showed only a non-significant trend. The Haematological Cluster was not significantly associated with mortality in either sex.

These associations also remained unchanged when applying the landmark analysis excluding the first three years of follow-up and under the additional hybrid Cox models, confirming that the observed effects were not influenced by pre-existing risk conditions, supporting the absence of reverse causality and corroborating that the prognostic signal of the latent phenotypes is not solely driven by isolated biomarkers (Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. 3).

Comparison of biochemical profiles between TSHA and EXERNET cohorts

To assess external reproducibility, we compared the Metabolic and Healthy clusters between TSHA and the independent EXERNET cohort of physically active older adults (Table 5). Both clusters emerged independently in each cohort using the same analytical pipeline and displayed broadly similar biochemical patterns, supporting generalizability. Between-cohort differences were quantified using standardised effect sizes (Hedges’ g). The Haematological cluster identified in TSHA was not included in this comparison, as it was not present in the EXERNET cohort. In the Metabolic cluster, most biochemical differences between cohorts were small to moderate in magnitude (|g| < 0.5), indicating substantial structural similarity between TSHA and EXERNET. Core metabolic parameters such as glucose showed negligible differences (g ≈ 0), while triglycerides demonstrated a moderate effect size. Haematological parameters were generally higher in TSHA and showed large standardized differences, particularly haemoglobin, hematocrit, MCH, and mean platelet volume (MPV).

In the Healthy cluster, effect sizes were also predominantly small. TSHA showed higher glucose and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), with similar triglycerides, uric acid, electrolytes, and liver markers. Minor differences in haematological indices were observed (notably MCHC and MPV displayed large effect sizes), again indicating population-level variability in blood indices. Overall, the quantitative effect size analysis supports phenotype concordance, and previously reported links to mortality were also confirmed.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that unsupervised machine learning applied to routine blood tests can reveal latent biochemical phenotypes capturing meaningful heterogeneity in older adults. Three distinct profiles—primarily differentiated by metabolic and haematological parameters—showed strong associations with physical function, frailty, and long-term outcomes. The Metabolic phenotype, comprising nearly half of participants, was marked by elevated glucose, triglycerides, and liver enzymes, reflecting subclinical metabolic dysregulation consistent with early cardiometabolic ageing12. In contrast, the Haematological phenotype exhibited lower erythroid parameters and mild renal alterations, aligning with reduced physiological reserve13,14. The Healthy phenotype showed overall optimal biochemical balance and served as the functional baseline.

Sociodemographic and functional patterns differed across clusters. The Healthy group included younger participants and more women, consistent with sex-specific ageing trajectories15. The Haematological phenotype comprised older adults with lower scores in physical activity, independence, and cognition—especially men in activity levels and both sexes in independence— with women also showing greater limitations in instrumental activities. This pattern supports prior evidence linking anaemia and functional decline16, highlights the significance of anaemia as an indicator of underlying systemic disorders rather than an isolated condition, and emphasises the importance of considering sex-specific differences17. Regarding physical function, the Haematological cluster performed worse in physical tests: men showed slower gait speed and completed fewer chair stands, while women had lower knee extension strength18. Their higher frailty prevalence—especially in men—also supports previous evidence linking anaemia with frailty19. These results support that standard biochemical values, even within normal ranges, are meaningfully associated with functional decline and frailty when considered jointly using multivariate approaches20, rather than individually as typically done in clinical practice. Conversely, higher BMI in the Metabolic cluster did not translate to poorer performance, aligning with the “fit but fat” concept21,22.

Longitudinal analyses revealed sex-specific disease trajectories. Women in the Metabolic cluster showed a higher risk of hypertension, whereas men did not. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that older women may be more susceptible to blood pressure changes driven by metabolic alterations23. This cluster also showed higher diabetes incidence in both sexes, supporting prior links between metabolic dysregulation, excess weight, and diabetes risk24, despite non-significant HRs. The Haematological phenotype was linked to anaemia and thrombosis in men25 and to diabetes and syncope in women, reflecting sex-differentiated vulnerabilities. On the other hand, although hospitalisation rates did not differ significantly, metabolic abnormalities predicted mortality in women, even within normal laboratory ranges26,27. These findings are conceptually aligned with prior work demonstrating that composite biomarker-based ageing measures outperform chronological age in predicting adverse outcomes, reinforcing the clinical relevance of multidimensional biochemical profiling7. Importantly, these associations remained significant after full adjustment for sociodemographic and lifestyle covariates, were consistent when excluding participants with early events in the first years of follow-up, and were largely maintained in complementary hybrid models including key individual biomarkers, reinforcing the robustness of the findings and supporting the absence of reverse causality. Subsequently, these sex-specific patterns highlight that latent biochemical profiles, rather than isolated analytes, capture the systemic processes underlying frailty and disease progression, and may therefore help to anticipate risk and guide preventive measures.

Comparison with the EXERNET cohort confirmed external reproducibility. The Metabolic phenotype emerged independently, maintaining similar biochemical signatures and mortality associations28. Minor differences—such as those in the haematological parameters—underscore the protective role of physical activity. The additional Haematological phenotype in TSHA, absent in EXERNET, likely reflects population-specific differences in age, sex distribution and health status.

Importantly, the results should be interpreted within the framework of the observational design, which precludes causal inference. Furthermore, although partial reproducibility was observed in an independent cohort, the identified phenotypes and their prognostic value require further validation in other populations.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that biochemical phenotyping using routine blood tests may provide the basis for a scalable and low-cost approach for precision risk stratification in older adults. By capturing latent, co-varying patterns rather than isolated values, this method identifies clinically meaningful subgroups—such as the Metabolic and Haematological phenotypes—that are linked to functional decline, frailty, sex-specific disease trajectories, and long-term mortality. The approach could improve early identification of individuals at higher risk, informing targeted preventive strategies (e.g., lifestyle/metabolic interventions for the Metabolic group, or anaemia work-up and functional prehabilitation for the Haematological group); and offering a framework for integrating routine laboratory data into personalised geriatric care. Collectively, these results underscore the potential of multivariate biochemical profiling to enhance understanding of ageing biology and guide interventions before overt disease manifests.

Methods

Participants and study design

This study used data from the TSHA, a longitudinal, population-based cohort including community-dwelling and institutionalised adults aged ≥65 years from the province of Toledo (Spain). Participants (n = 2488) were selected through two-stage random sampling from the municipal census. Baseline data were collected between June 2006 and September 2009 and comprised sociodemographic, clinical, biochemical, and functional assessments29. All participants or legal representatives provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Virgen del Valle Hospital, Toledo (CEIC106), and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were followed from 2006 to 2019 (mean ≈11 years) to record incident diseases, frailty, functional decline, and mortality. Follow-up data were obtained through structured interviews, physical assessments, and linkage with medical and mortality registries.

Biochemical markers

Fasting venous blood samples were obtained from all participants at baseline to measure 39 biochemical and haematological variables. Variables were selected based on routine availability in the Spanish public healthcare system and a missingness threshold <10% to achieve a complete case analysis. This strategy aimed to maximise translational applicability while preserving sample size and multivariate integrity. Metabolic and lipid markers included glucose (mg/dL), insulin (µIU/mL), and the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as glucose × insulin/405, a validated surrogate of insulin resistance and β-cell function30; as well as triglycerides (mg/dL) and total, HDL, and LDL cholesterol (mg/dL). Renal function markers included urea, creatinine, and uric acid (mg/dL); electrolytes and minerals included sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, and chloride (mEq/L or mg/dL as appropriate); liver enzymes and proteins included AST, ALT, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, LDH, and total proteins (U/L or g/dL). Iron metabolism markers included iron and transferrin (µg/dL). Haematological parameters included leukocytes, erythrocytes, haemoglobin, haematocrit, platelets, mean corpuscular indices (MCV, MCH, MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), and major leukocyte subtypes (lymphocytes, granulocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils; 10⁹/L or 10¹²/L as appropriate).

Sociodemographic, psychosocial and independence variables

Sociodemographic data, including age, sex, marital status, education level, and living arrangements, as well as social support and health-related quality of life, were obtained via the structured, computer-assisted TSHA interview29, conducted face-to-face by a qualified neuropsychologist. Physical activity was assessed using the validated PASE31, and depressive symptoms were evaluated with the 15-item Yesavage Geriatric Depression Scale32. Independence in daily activities was measured with the Katz Index for basic activities of daily living (ADL)33 and the Lawton and Brody scale for instrumental activities of daily living (IADL)34. Cognitive status was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)35.

Physical functionality, frailty and anthropometric measures

Physical function was assessed using validated performance-based tests. Muscle strength was measured with a Jamar hydraulic hand dynamometer according to the standards of the Hispanic Established Populations for Epidemiologic Studies of the Elderly (EPESE)36. The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), including gait speed, balance, and a repeated chair-stand test, was administered37. Participants unable to complete the physical function tests due to poor health were excluded from these assessments (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Frailty was evaluated according to the Fried phenotype38, which includes self-reported exhaustion, weakness, unintentional weight loss, slowness, and physical activity, classifying participants as non-frail (0 points), pre-frail (1-2 points), or frail (3–5 points). In addition, the Frailty Trait Scale-5 (FTS-5) was used39, integrating BMI, PASE score, handgrip strength, static balance, and habitual gait speed over 3 m to yield a composite score from 0 to 50 points, with scores >25 indicating frailty. Unlike dichotomous classifications, the FTS-5 allows for more nuanced evaluation of health status and risk.

Anthropometrics included weight (SECA 884 floor scale, Germany), height (stadiometer on wall without skirting board, Medizintechnik seit 1890, KaWe, Germany), BMI (kg/m²), and waist and hip circumferences measured with an inelastic tape (Cescorf, Porto Alegre, Brazil) to the nearest 0.01 m following standardised protocols40.

Comorbidities, hospital admissions and mortality

Comorbidity data were collected from medical diagnoses and participant self-reports, including ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, stroke, syncope, hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, peptic ulcer, fractures, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, cancer, dementia, Parkinson’s disease, anaemia, and renal, thyroid, and liver disorders.

Hospital admissions during follow-up were obtained from medical records, and mortality data were retrieved from the Spanish National Death Index (Ministry of Health, Consumer Affairs and Social Welfare). Time to death was censored at death or at 11 years of follow-up.

Data preprocessing

A flow diagram summarising participant exclusion is provided (Supplementary Fig. S1). Variables with more than 10% missing values, such as D-dimer and C-reactive protein (CRP), were excluded from analyses, and participants with incomplete data on required variables were removed. To evaluate potential selection bias associated with the exclusion of participants in this complete-case analysis, available baseline demographic variables (age and sex) were compared between included and excluded individuals. Although excluded individuals were slightly older (mean difference = 1.30 years) and differed marginally in sex distribution (53.8% of included individuals were women, compared to 57.7% of excluded individuals), effect sizes were small (Cohen’s d = 0.21; Cramér’s V = 0.04). Logistic regression showed minimal variance explained (Pseudo R² = 0.009), suggesting limited impact on representativeness.

Participants with HOMA-IR > 10 or glucose >350 mg/dL, suggestive of non-fasting status, were excluded, as were those with white blood cell counts >20 ×10⁹/L, indicative of potential infection (Supplementary Fig. S1). Outliers were identified using biomarker-specific thresholds, set at approximately seven times the cohort mean, and removed when likely due to experimental or measurement error. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and selected biomarkers, were compared between included and excluded participants to assess potential selection bias, with minimal impact on mean biomarker values (Supplementary Table S1). Data cleaning was performed using Pandas: Python Data Analysis Library (v1.3.5), accessible at https://pandas.pydata.org.

Blood test data were normalised using the StandardScaler function (Python Sklearn package). Principal component analysis (PCA) was then applied to the 39 selected variables to reduce dimensionality and identify the most informative variables, with two principal components retained based on cumulative explained variance, accounting for 69.19% of the total variance.

Clustering analysis

Unsupervised machine learning methods were applied to classify participants based on routine blood analysis variables, using both k-means and hierarchical clustering implemented in Python (v3.9) and Orange Data Mining software (v3.32)41. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating the sum of squared errors (SSE) and the average silhouette coefficient across solutions from k = 2 to 8. The SSE curve (1–10 clusters) and the hierarchical dendrogram are presented Supplementary Fig. S2, while silhouette scores are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Both the elbow criterion and silhouette analysis supported a three-cluster solution (silhouette = 0.054), providing the best balance between statistical coherence and clinical interpretability. Although k = 2 yielded a slightly higher silhouette score (0.064), this partition was considered overly broad, whereas k > 3 produced fragmented clusters with limited interpretability. For visualisation, biochemical variables were normalised using Min–Max scaling to the interval [−1, 1] and grouped by functional category to enable clearer comparison between clusters (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Statistical analysis

Statistics were performed using SPSS v26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and STATA. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, percentages, and frequencies) were used to characterise each cluster. Cross-sectional associations between cluster membership and sociodemographic, functional, and anthropometric variables were analysed with general linear models (ANCOVA), including age as a covariate and applying Bonferroni post-hoc corrections. Analyses were stratified by sex. Categorical variables were studied using Chi-Square and cross-tabulations. A significance level of p < 0.05 was established to indicate significant differences.

Longitudinal analyses assessed the influence of cluster membership on disease incidence, hospital admissions, and all-cause mortality using Cox proportional hazards models. Cluster allocation was entered as an independent variable, and HRs with 95% CI were estimated. Analyses were stratified by sex, with results reported separately for men and women. Three hierarchical models were fitted: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for age, socioeconomic status, education, and marital status; and Model 3, additionally adjusted for physical activity (PASE score) to assess its influence on the longitudinal outcomes. Cumulative risk plots illustrated disease trajectories over the 11-year follow-up, and incidence rates were expressed per 1000 person-years (events/person-years × 1000) (Model 3). In addition, a landmark sensitivity analysis was performed excluding participants who died within the first three years after baseline, to minimize bias from pre-existing high-risk conditions. To further assess whether cluster membership provided prognostic information beyond key individual biochemical contributors, complementary hybrid Cox models were constructed including cluster allocation alongside HOMA-IR and haemoglobin levels into model 3. HOMA-IR and haemoglobin were selected as representative contributors based on their dominant loadings in the principal component analysis and their role in defining the metabolic and haematological dimensions of the identified phenotypes. This approach allowed us to assess whether the prognostic associations of latent phenotypes were independent of their main individual biochemical contributors.

Finally, cluster comparability was evaluated against that derived from the EXERNET cohort of active older adults. Only shared biochemical and haematological variables were included. Between-cohort differences were quantified using standardised effect sizes (Hedges’ g) calculated from reported sample sizes, means, and SDs were used to compare clusters with similar patterns (“Healthy” and “Metabolic”). Data for EXERNET were obtained from previously published results11, allowing external reproducibility of cluster profiles identified in the TSHA cohort.
