Correlative microscopy for in-depth analysis of calcium oxalate crystals in plant tissues
Authors: Martin Niedermeier, Sebastian J Antreich, Notburga Gierlinger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcium oxalate (CaOx) crystals are commonly found in many plant species. These crystals vary in distribution and morphology and to elucidate their role in plants multiple methods have been applied. Raman imaging and polarized light microscopy (PLM) easily visualize the crystals within plant tissues, but both methods are limited in spatial resolution by the diffraction of light. To unravel the distinctive shape and morphology of CaOx crystals down to the nanoscale and how they are embedded within cells, high resolution scanning electron microscopy is needed. To grasp the full potential of multiple methods in CaOx studies, a novel and easy-to-build correlative sampling approach is presented on different nut species (pecan (Carya illinoinensis), Turkish hazel (Corylus colurna) and black walnut (Juglans nigra)), including soft tissues (young developmental stages) as well as hard tissues (mature nutshells).ResultYoung seed coat tissues as well as mature nutshells included distinct morphological CaOx features, like druses and prismatic crystals. By Raman imaging the chemical composition of all investigated crystals was verified as calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) and Raman band intensity changed according to crystal plane orientation with respect to incident laser polarisation. Calcium oxalate dihydrate (COD) was only found in the young C. illinoinensis seed coat and was restricted to a few pixels adjacent to cell walls. These thin cell walls were identified as pectin-rich, while in the mature nutshells the crystals were surrounded by thicker and highly lignified cell walls. The Raman and light microscopy results were correlated with SEM images, which gave additional information on crystal surface structure and/or internal porosity on the nanoscale.ConclusionThe presented correlative approach preserved the structural integrity of crystals and cellular structures during cutting and transferring between microscopes. Analysing exactly the same sample (position) by Raman, polarized light microscopy and SEM opens the view on the distribution within tissues and cells as well as the molecular structure of the crystals and adjacent cell structures. Background

Calcium oxalate (CaOx) crystals are the most abundant mineral deposits in higher plants [1]. Their distribution inside plants and their tissues is highly variable and species specific [2], and therefore plays a role as a taxonomic character [3]. CaOx crystals are present in plants mainly in two different hydration states, calcium oxalate monohydrate (whewellite, CaC2O4. H2O, COM) and the less stable calcium oxalate dihydrate (weddellite, CaC2O4. 2 + xH2O, COD) [4]. As it is shown in cacti, some species solely contain COM or COD crystals, while others show both CaOx hydration states within one species [5]. In some plants COM and COD crystals can be even found together in one single cell [6]. Additionally, they show high variation in number and morphology, which is also depending on the hydration state of their constituents [2, 3, 7]. All these observations led to the proposal of numerous theories on the function of CaOx crystals in plants. They are supposed to play a role in defence against herbivores [8], detoxification of heavy metals or oxalic acid, Ca regulation [9], providing a dynamic Ca storage [10], an internal CO2 source [11], structural reinforcement in tissues, or taking part in the promotion of lignin polymerization and stomatal functioning [1].

Like in tissues of other higher plants, soluble and insoluble oxalate (mainly bound as calcium salt) can be found in many nut species in their kernels [12]. But studies concentrating on CaOx depositions in the essential protective layers surrounding the nut kernel, the nutshell and the husk, are rare. Micromechanical studies of pecan (Carya illinoinensis) CaOx crystals revealed them as very stiff and brittle support structures [13], while a negative effect on the tensile strength of the nutshell was discussed [14].

In higher plants, CaOx crystal formation usually takes place inside the vacuoles of idioblasts, specialized cells that differ from surrounding cells in morphology and cytoplasmic activity [2, 15]. Crystals, regardless of their morphology, often develop within intravacuolar membrane chambers [3], which can be found exclusively in vacuoles of idioblasts [1]. Occasionally, cell wall sheaths develop around vacuolar crystals [16], so called Rosanoffian crystals [17], which effectively and permanently exclude the crystal from the protoplast [1].

COM and COD crystals usually can be easily observed via light microscopy, which led to the first description of plant CaOx crystals by Leeuwunhoek in the late 1600 s [17]. The use of a light polarization filter drastically benefits the detection of COM crystals in most cases [18]. However, to gather information about crystal structure, morphology or chemical composition several techniques can be employed: X-ray diffraction (XRD; [19]), scanning electron microscopy (SEM; [1]), energy-dis=persive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX; [20]) or vibrational spectroscopy (Raman and infrared spectroscopy; [21, 22]). SEM imaging is an ideal tool to observe the morphology and the surfaces of CaOx crystals in high resolution [1, 3, 23], and SEM coupled EDX was already successfully employed to determine the chemical components of CaOx crystals in pecan nutshells [13]. Raman spectroscopy on the other hand bears benefits in detecting molecular composition and orientation of CaOx crystals (e.g., [24, 25]). and additional valuable plant substances [26]. During the last years, confocal Raman microscopy has shown great potential in plant research as a non-destructive approach detecting molecular configuration based on spectral signature and visualizing the distribution of all different components at once in-situ within plant tissues and cells [27–29].

The benefits of the combined use of Raman spectroscopy and electron microscopic techniques are known (e.g. [30]), also in relation to CaOx crystals [5, 25]. Nevertheless, no correlative in situ studies on plant material containing CaOx crystals exist. Herein, we show on three different nut species that such a beneficial correlative approach is possible with simple self-built sample holders, if different microscopes are available. Shell and husk sections of developing and mature individuals of Carya illinoinensis, Corylus colurna (Turkish hazel) and Juglans nigra (black walnut) are analysed via (polarized) light microscopy, confocal Raman microscopy and SEM to identify and compare distribution, molecular composition and morphological features of CaOx crystals in-situ within the plant cells, including insights into their surrounding cell wall structures.

Results

In previous works, Raman measurements and SEM pictures of nutshell tissue were presented, however never in a correlative approach. Due to the high variability in shape, size and position of CaOx crystals, a protocol was developed to measure exactly the same positions on a microsection transferrable from Raman to polarized light microscopy and SEM (Fig. 1). An InSnO2 coated microscope slide was chosen, as it is transparent and also electron-conductive and, therefore, suitable for light and electron microscopy. As this slide was smaller as a standard microscope glass slide, a carrier was designed and 3D-printed (PLA), so it could be fixed on the Raman microscope stage, but also on the SEM sample holder with two stubs (.stl file, Additional Fi=le 1). Additionally, the carrier was sputter-coated with gold to increase electric conductivity in the SEM. All prepared sections were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and placed on the slide in a drop of distilled water and sealed with a cover slip and nail polish and transferred to the light and Raman microscope. After polarization and Raman measurements the seal and the cover slip of the InSnO2 coated slide were carefully removed, and the sample was air dried for 3 h. For easier and damage-free removal of the sealing and the coverslip after Raman measurement, adhesive tape can be used on the edges of the slide. Some mature samples tend to deform during drying, so they were taped with adhesive copper tape on both sides on the slide. The slide holder was placed on the multipurpose holder of the SEM and fixed with two stubs on both sides of the holder.

The freshly cut section is placed on the slide and sealed with coverslip and nail-polish (3). The sample holder was placed under the light and Raman microscope, and spectra were obtained. After that, the coverslip was removed, and the sample was air-dried for 3 h. Mature samples were additionally fixed with copper-tape (4). Young shell of C. illinoinensis

The combination of light microscopy and scanning electron microscopy revealed detailed morphological features of the husk and the shell of C. illinoinensis on the same section (Fig. 2). The use of a light polarization filter benefited the observation of these crystals drastically. Several different tissues were identified in the shell and the husk of C. illinoinensis and the crystal distribution displayed (Fig. 2, illustration). Due to the drying process the husk tissue shrank, and, in some cases, remaining parts of protoplasts had collapsed. However, the overall shape of the tissue remained, and crystals analysed in the light/Raman microscope were easily located in the SEM image.

The husk tissue shrank due to the drying process prior to the mounting in the SEM. Colour theme in illustration is according to legend. For the uncropped correlated images see Supplementary Fig. 1a, Additional File 2

In the husk of C. illinoinensis, all observed crystals were druses (Fig. 2, pink; Fig. 3a–d). They were slightly smaller in the most outer part of the husk, referred to as epidermis. Beside the druses, clusters of big stone cells were observable as the thick cell walls deflected the polarized light (Fig. 2, blue; Fig. 3a). Measured druses had an average size of 19.6 (± 3) µm in length and 17.1 (± 3) µm in width (n = 19) and showed irregular shapes (Fig. 3b). Several druses, cut open and observed in the SEM, reveal a concentric layering of the crystal and no crystal sheath (Fig. 3c). Correlative Raman scans (Fig. 3e–g) reveal the molecular composition of crystals as COM, indicated by the two Raman bands at 1463 and 1489 cm−1 (Fig. 3h). True component analysis (TCA) differentiated four different COM spectra with changing band intensities. The platelets oriented in x-direction (Fig. 3e, f, blue and turquoise) coincided with the incident laser polarisation direction and had a high 1463 cm−1 band (Fig. 3h, blue and turquoise spectra), whereas the platelets oriented in y-direction (Fig. 3e, g, red and orange) had a high 1489 cm−1 (Fig. 3h, red and orange) and 502 cm−1 band (Fig. 3h orange).

In the young shell tissue of C. illinoinensis we found in the innermost part again druses, while in the outermost part of the shell styloid, prismatic or an aggregation of these crystal shapes was detected (Fig. 2). The prismatic crystals showed an increasing size from the outside to the inside of the shell (Fig. 4a). However, the biggest prismatic crystals (12.1 (± 4) µm in length and 10.5 (± 4) µm in width (n = 13)) were still smaller than the druses in the husk. Depending on orientation and thickness of the crystals, polarized light caused different colours in the pictures (Fig. 4b, c). SEM pictures of some cut open cells revealed pores and bigger cavities within the crystal (Fig. 4d). In a correlative way a prismatic crystal was visualized in the SEM (Fig. 4e) and analysed by Raman imaging, including the surrounding cell wall (Fig. 4f, g). The dense crystal was again confirmed as COM by its Raman signature (Fig. 4g, pink spectrum). Attached to the cell wall, a few pixels revealed spectra typical for COD with a band at 1477 cm−1 (Fig. 4f, g, green). Also, nearby the cell wall starch grains were visualized (Fig. 4f, yellow), with a characteristic band at 470 cm−1 (Fig. 4g, yellow spectrum). The crystal surrounding layer (Fig. 4f, white area) showed very noisy high fluorescing spectra with a typical protein band at 1002 cm−1 (Fig. 4g, black spectrum). The cell wall in the developing shell showed carbohydrate bands and the pectin marker band at 852 cm−1 (Fig. 4f, g, blue)

Mature shell of different nuts

The correlative approach was also used for sections of mature shell tissue of C. illinoinensis (Fig. 5). The SEM was able to distinguish between the dense crystals and the cell wall tissue. Contrary, with polarized light microscopy it was difficult to differentiate between the crystals and the thick cell wall due to the birefringence properties of cellulose. Like in the young shell tissue, crystals close to the exocarp were prismatic and became gradually bigger toward the inside. Closer to the endocarp occasionally druses were visible (Fig. 5).

Location and appearance of crystals in the mature nutshell of Carya illinoinensis by SEM, bright light, polarized light and the illustration of the crystal distribution. The shell tissue is too dense to distinguish individual cells in the illustration. For the uncropped correlated images see Supplementary Fig. 1b, Additional File 2

A more detailed close-up analysis confirmed that crystals are hard to distinguish from the cell wall with polarized light microscopy (Fig. 6a, b), while SEM gave a clear distinction and showed the dense crystal structure as well as the lamellar structure of the thick secondary cell wall (Fig. 6c, d). On a cell with the crystal almost covered by the cell wall the contrast was less in the SEM (Fig. 6e), but correlative Raman clearly distinguished the cell wall and the crystal surfaces (Fig. 6f, g). The crystal was revealed as two differently oriented COM surfaces (Fig. 6f, g, red and blue) tightly backed in secondary cell wall, as evidenced by typical lignin bands at 1599 cm−1 and 1657 cm−1 (Fig. 6f, g, turquoise). The lignin bands were even higher between the cells and in the layer around the crystal (Fig. 6f, g, orange). This circular layer showed high fluorescence (noisy spectra) and only weak bands around 1603 and 1558 cm1 (Fig. 6f, white area, Fig. 6g: black spectrum).

The same approach was used for two other nutshell tissues to compare distribution and chemical composition, as well as to demonstrate the universal application of the protocol (Supplementary Fig. 2a-b, Additional File 2). In Juglans nigra all kinds of differently shaped crystals were randomly distributed in the shell. Crystal shapes in J. nigra ranged from druses to aggregates of crystalline material (Supplementary Fig. 2c-d, Additional File 2). In the shell of Corylus colurna crystals were present in a layer along the innermost tissue (prismatic, Supplementary Fig. 2e, Additional File 2) and in cells next to the vascular bundles (druses, Supplementary Fig. 2f, Additional File 2). Additionally, single druses were occasionally observed throughout the shell. The majority of crystals found in mature nut species had a cell wall sheath around. The only general exception were the crystals in cells surrounding the vascular bundles in C. colurna, which were never surrounded by cell wall material.

A comparative correlative SEM and Raman analysis is shown exemplarily of the mature shell tissue of C. illinoinensis, C. colurna and J. nigra nuts (Fig. 7). In the SEM pictures structural details of the crystals as well as the surrounding cell walls are visualized (Fig. 7a, c and e). In all three nut species, the most intense Raman bands at ~ 1462 cm−1 and 1489 cm−1 were attributed to COM and therefore its integration outlined the crystal structure (Fig. 7b, d, e). This double peak was found in all developed visible crystals of all samples, regardless of their morphology or distribution (Figs. 3, 4 and 6). As the incident laser was always polarized in x-direction, changes in band heights (especially 1462 cm−1 and 1489 cm−1) in relation to molecular orientation of COM were observed. Molecular orientation can be linked to crystal shape and its orientation with respect to the laser orientation (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The suggested sample preparation (Polyethylenglycol (PEG) embedding, cryo-sectioning) and transfer approach paves the way for in-depth structural and chemical analysis of plant tissues on the micro- and nano level. All microsections were successfully measured in their wet state in the polarized light and Raman microscope, dried and transferred afterwards into the SEM without losing the integrity of the cell walls and cell contents (e.g. crystals). Cell shape was preserved in immature tissues by embedding, which led to a clear identification of the distinctive layers in the shell and husk. Despite the drying process prior to the mounting in the SEM, the overall shape of the different sections stayed the same so that correlative measurements, of e.g. the same crystal, were possible.

First light microscopic examinations revealed a species-specific distribution of crystals in the fruit coats (pericarp) of all observed nut species, ranging from a distinct cell layer in C. colurna to a sporadic and random distribution in J. nigra. Especially in the young C. illinoinensis, the separation of druses to the husk and the inner most shell as well as single crystals only in the outer shell layer was striking. This is in strong contrast to bark tissue of Betula ermanii which showed a mix of single crystals and spherulites (similar shape as druses) all over the bark tissue [31]. Morphologically different crystals are assumed to derive from different concentrations of reactants forming the crystals and growth conditions. Millan [32] predicted based on crystal structure and growth experiments that druses in leaves grow in high supersaturation and for a short time, whereas prismatic crystals in roots grow for long periods and in a moderate to high supersaturation. Based on this information, we hypothesize that the occurrence of prismatic crystals in combination with the observed gradient of crystal size points to a lower concentration of reactants in the outer shell tissues of C. illinoinensis compared to its husk tissue.

In general, crystal appearance in immature tissues was clearly visible using a polarization filter in a light microscope, as also described by Frey-Wyssling [18]. The use of a light polarization filter was more difficult to employ in mature samples due to thicker cell walls and birefringence of highly oriented cellulose fibrils, which outshines the CaOx crystals. While the location and shape of crystals were already observable via light microscopic techniques, Raman microscopy revealed molecular composition of cell inclusions and the cell walls. The crystal cell inclusions were identified as calcium oxalate monohydrate crystals (COM), indicated by the double peak at 1462 and 1488 cm−1 [4, 24, 33]. The relative intensity of the two typical COM bands differed between the investigated crystals, but also within one single crystal. These observed differences in Raman band heights can be explained by changes in molecular orientation of crystal planes with respect to laser polarisation. Therefore, in druse shaped crystals, COM molecules are orientated in several directions, while styloid and prismatic ones depend on the orientation of the crystal planes with respect to laser orientation.

Regardless of crystal morphology, location and species all visible crystals consisted of COM. Probably due to the higher stability of COM in comparison to COD [4], or the absence of supersaturation conditions which allows the formation of stable COD [34]. In case of immature C. illinoinensis the presence of COD could be proven by the Raman signature with a marker band at 1477 cm−1 [4, 24, 33]. Yet, COD was restricted to a few pixels (~ 2 μm) adjacent to the pectin rich primary cell wall. These crystals were not observed by light microscopic investigations maybe due to their very small size and as directly attached to the cell wall. The change in crystal shape could point to a controlled process of crystal production, which was suggested by Franceschi and Horner [7] or derive from other cell constituents, as suggested by Chien et al. [35]. Raman microscopy has the potential to follow crystal formation from scratch and include cell content and crystal sheath development and composition, which might also play an important role.

The combination of Raman and SEM gives insights into chemical composition as well as morphological details of the observed crystals and their surrounding cell wall structures. Recently, both methods were also used to characterize isolated CaOx crystals in cacti [36]. However, applying both methods correlative on intact plant tissues is even more informative as exactly the same position/crystal can be observed, which is in biological samples and their inherent variability of utmost importance. The gained information is often complementary and differs in spatial resolution. For example, a porous structure within several cut crystals was imaged by SEM, but not visible in the Raman images (~ 300 nm spatial resolution). The porous centre of druses found in mature as well as young shell tissue were like those found in bark tissue and derive from organic material which act as a nucleation side of the multiple single crystals forming the druse [31, 37]. Some druses from the husk tissue of C. illinoinensis revealed several layers within the crystal which might point to cyclic growth activity during the formation of this druse (Fig. 3c). For prismatic crystals, we observed cases where the centre was hollow or occupied by another prismatic crystal (Fig. 7c). This might be the result of twinning, where two crystals grow together causing one crystal to partly enclose the other [32]. During sample cutting, these individual crystals could have gotten separated partly or completely. Besides crystal shape and morphology, also cell content and the surrounding cell walls were visible in high resolution in the SEM revealing e.g. different cell wall layers. Some crystals were covered partially or completely by cell wall material and, therefore not visible via SEM. However, we were able to obtain information about molecular composition of these visually hidden crystals using confocal Raman microscopy.

Regarding the cell wall architecture, the walls in the developing shell show a high content of pectin, while in later stages cell walls are highly lignified, which was also already shown for walnut shells [38]. Via SEM imaging the laminar structure of the secondary cell wall material is revealed, while Raman microscopic investigations add the information on lignification. In the mature state, crystals in the shell of all three examined nut species were enclosed by secondary cell wall material. As described by Katayama et al. [16] and Arnott [17] the envelopment of crystals by cell wall sheaths is an effective process to exclude them from the protoplast and therefore from cell metabolism.

Conclusions

A self-designed sample holder enabled correlative imaging by polarized light microscopy, Raman microscopy and SEM without the need of transferring the sample to a different holder, which could lead to artefacts. Light (polarization) microscopy gave a complete overview of the distribution of crystals within the nutshell tissues. Further investigation of the same area or even the same cell led to a more complete picture of morphology, structure and composition of crystals in tissues and their development. While molecular composition and orientation of the crystals were observed via Raman microscopy, SEM led to morphological and structural details down to the nanoscale. All investigated nut species seemed to have developed distinctive ways to bind Ca and oxalic acid, leading to differently distributed and shaped calcium oxalate crystals. The shown correlative analysis of plant tissue with polarized light, Raman and SEM is a powerful tool which can be easily applied for other biological tissues to answer questions about morphology and molecular properties of cells, cell walls and/or cell content, especially in the context of biomineralization.

Methods

Nut species

Carya illinoinensis (Juglandaceae, Wangenh., K.Koch) were collected at the experimental garden at BOKU in Jedlersdorf, Vienna, Austria, end of August (young) and mid of October (mature) 2019. The younger developmental stage was chosen to show the ability to work with immature tissues and to pronounce the importance to show the differences in crystal distribution between husk and shell.

Mature individuals of Juglans nigra (Juglandaceae, A.Richard ex Kunth) were collected on the “Danube Island” in Vienna, Austria begin of October 2019.

Corylus colurna (Betulaceae, J.J. Furlow) were collected in their mature state begin of October 2019 in front of the BOKU building, 19th district, Vienna, Austria.

All nut species were collected freshly, frozen at −20 °C in our department and kept on that temperature until further preparation. Additionally, young C. illinoinensis were kept in their fresh state until the embedding process in PEG.

Sample preparation

To preserve tissues and cellular structure, pieces of young C. illinoinensis were embedded in PEG 600 by an ethanol series (30−50−70−90−100%) in 30 min steps at room temperature followed by 1:1 (v/v) ethanol: PEG 600 overnight at 37 °C and finally PEG 600 for 60 min at 37 °C [39].

After the embedding process the samples were transferred to a freezer (−20 °C) and kept in PEG blocks until further preparation. PEG embedded samples were cut with a Leica CM 3050 S cryo microtome (Leica Microsystems, Vienna, Austria) at −20 °C, to obtain 16–25 μm thick sections.

Nutshells of mature samples were soaked in distilled water over night and sectioned with a Leica RM 225 rotary microtome (Leica Microsystems, Vienna, Austria) at room temperature, to obtain 10–16 μm thick sections.

A transparent InSnO2 coated microscope slide (SPI Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA) was used which allows to place the obtained samples in the microscopes as well as in the SEM (Fig. 1). As the InSnO2 slide was smaller as a standard microscope glass slide, a frame was designed with FreeCAD 1.0.0 (www.freecad.org), sliced with Cura 5.9.1 (UltiMaker, Zaltbommel, Netherlands) and 3D-printed with a Voron 0.1 (www.vorondesign.com) in PLA, which fitted into the Raman microscope but also could be fixed in the SEM with two stubs (.stl file, Additional File 1). Additionally, the frame was sputter-coated with gold (Leica Microsystems, Vienna, Austria) to increase electric conductivity in the SEM. All prepared sections were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and placed on the slide in a drop of distilled water and sealed with a cover slip and nail polish and transferred to the Raman microscope. After Raman measurements the seal and the cover slip of the InSnO2 slide were carefully removed, and the sample was air dried for 3 h. For easier and damage-free removal of the sealing and the coverslip after Raman measurement, adhesive tape can be used on the edges of the slide. Some mature samples tend to deform during drying, so they were taped with adhesive copper tape on both sides on the slide. The slide holder was placed on the multipurpose holder of the SEM and fixed with two stubs on both sides of the holder.

Light microscopy with polarized light microscopy

A light polarization filter was used to quickly determine and localize crystalline structures inside sections. Light microscopic images with and without polarization filter were obtained with a POLYVAR microscope (Reichert AG, Vienna, Austria) and a Canon EOS M10 (Canon Inc., Japan) mounted on the microscope.

Raman microscopy

Raman spectra were acquired with a confocal Raman Microscope (alpha 300RA, WITec GmbH, Ulm, Germany), equipped with a 100x oil objective (NA = 1.4, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Two Raman setups were used to observe the chemical composition within the sections. Samples were excited with a sapphire SF laser (532 nm, WITec GmbH, Ulm, Germany), connected to a spectrometer (600 g mm−1 grating, UHTS 300, WITec GmbH, Ulm, Germany) and a CCD camera (DV401A-BV-351, Andor, Belfast, Northern Ireland) via a 50 μm optical fibre. Laser power and integration times were chosen to optimize signal output, without risking damage to the specimen. Integration times ranged from 0.05 to 0.2 s, laser power from 20 to 25 mW.

To reduce background noise caused by fluorescence in some cases, a 785 nm linear polarized laser (XTRA II, Toptica Photonics, Germany) was used, connected to a spectrometer (600 g mm−1 grating, UHTS 300) and a CCD camera (DU401A-BR-DD-351, Andor) via a 100 μm optical fibre. Integration times ranged from 0.1 to 1 s, laser power from 100 to 150 mW.

For both lasers, Raman mappings were acquired with a lateral resolution of approximately 0.3 μm.

Raman measurement, spectral analysis and cosmic ray removal were performed with the software Control FOUR 4.1 plus (WITec GmbH, Ulm, Germany). Raman images were generated by band integration and True component analysis (TCA, WITec, Plus 6.0). The later algorithm seeks for the most different spectra within the hyperspectral datasets and further on fits all retrieved component spectra as a linear combination at every pixel to result in distribution maps of the found components [40]. Raman spectra were baseline corrected with OPUS (v. 7.0, Bruker Optik GmbH, Billerica, MA, USA).

SEM

4k pictures were made in an Apreo SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., MA, USA) under high vacuum with 1 kV, 50 pA and 5 µs dwell time. The low voltage and the use of the in-lens detector allowed the acquisition of high-quality pictures without prior gold coating which would also make the indium-tin-oxide slide permanently opaque. Overviews of the whole shell tissue as well as of crystals measured a priori in the Raman were scanned. Scan rotation was set according to the orientation of the section from the Raman measurement.

Image processing

Where necessary slight adjustment of brightness and contrast and additional cropping of some images was performed with ImageJ 1.53t (Wayne Rasband and contributors, National Institutes of Health, USA). Arrangement of all images and additional labelling was performed with Inkscape 1.2.1 (9c6d41e410, 2022-07-14) (www.inkscape.org, distributed under the GNU General Public License, version 3) and CorelDRAW 24.5.0 (Corel Corporation, Ottawa, Canada).
