Exploratory clinical-CT machine learning characterization of CK7 expression in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Qiansen Lin, Gengyun Miao, Xi Jia, Yuxiu Zhang, Chunpeng Niu, Heqing Wang, Jieyun Chen, Liheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytokeratin 7 (CK7) expression in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) may reflect tumor phenotype and biological heterogeneity, but its clinical role remains exploratory. This study aimed to investigate associations between preoperatively available clinical and CT imaging features and CK7 expression and to develop an exploratory machine learning model for CK7 characterization.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 230 patients with pathologically confirmed ccRCC from three institutions, including 139 patients in the training cohort and 91 patients in the validation cohort. Clinical variables and qualitative and quantitative CT features were assessed by two radiologists. CK7 positivity was defined as cytoplasmic staining in at least 10% of tumor cells. After least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, four machine learning models using prespecified configurations were developed in the training cohort and evaluated in the validation cohort. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy, F1 score, calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis. Classification thresholds were determined in the training cohort using the maximum Youden index and then applied to the validation cohort. Model stability was assessed using 1000 bootstrap resampling iterations.ResultsAmong 230 patients, 67 had CK7-positive tumors. The final predictors included age, cystic component, corticomedullary attenuation, corticomedullary enhancement ratio, nephrographic attenuation, and tumor size. The logistic regression model was selected as the final model based on its discrimination, calibration performance, interpretability, and overall generalizability. In the validation cohort, the logistic regression model achieved an AUC of 0.714 (95% CI: 0.589, 0.828), with a sensitivity of 0.625 and a specificity of 0.627. Bootstrap resampling supported the stability of performance estimates.ConclusionThis exploratory machine learning model-development study suggests that preoperatively available clinical and CT imaging features may be associated with CK7 expression in ccRCC. Further prospective validation using larger cohorts is required before clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16567-1.Keywords: Clear cell renal cell carcinoma, Cytokeratin 7, Computed tomography, Machine learning

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) accounts for approximately 70%−80% of renal cell carcinomas and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. According to the World Health Organization/International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO/ISUP) classification, ccRCC is defined by characteristic morphologic features and remains the predominant histologic subtype of renal cancer [2, 3]. Tumor stage and WHO/ISUP grade are well-established prognostic factors and play central roles in risk stratification and postoperative surveillance [4, 5]. Beyond conventional clinicopathologic parameters, increasing attention has focused on biomarkers that may provide additional information on tumor phenotype and biological heterogeneity in patients with ccRCC.

Cytokeratin 7 (CK7), an intermediate filament protein expressed in simple epithelial cells, is an important immunohistochemical marker in the differential diagnosis of renal neoplasms [6]. Papillary and chromophobe renal cell carcinomas typically show diffuse CK7 positivity, whereas renal oncocytoma is usually negative or only focally positive [7–9]. Clear cell papillary renal cell tumor, an indolent entity with a distinct immunophenotype, also shows diffuse CK7 expression and can therefore be distinguished from conventional ccRCC on the basis of CK7 staining [10]. Although CK7 expression is relatively uncommon in ccRCC, previous studies have shown that CK7 status is associated with WHO/ISUP grade, Tumor-Node-Metastasis stage, and oncologic outcomes [11–13].

Preoperative characterization of CK7 status may therefore be biologically informative, but its clinical role remains exploratory. Compared with established clinicopathologic biomarkers, such as WHO/ISUP grade and Tumor-Node-Metastasis stage, CK7 status may serve as a complementary immunohistochemical biomarker that captures tumor phenotype and biological heterogeneity. However, these tissue-based biomarkers are usually determined after surgery or require invasive sampling before treatment. Percutaneous biopsy is limited by sampling error and may not adequately capture intratumoral heterogeneity [14, 15]. Thus, an imaging-based approach to estimate CK7 expression may provide complementary tumor-characterization information when biopsy is unavailable, inconclusive, or potentially unrepresentative of the whole tumor. Previous work has suggested that a multimodal ultrasound-based model can predict CK7 expression in ccRCC [16], whereas CT-based prediction of CK7 status remains insufficiently explored. Our study extends this prior ultrasound-based work by evaluating routinely acquired contrast-enhanced CT features in a multicenter cohort with validation. Because contrast-enhanced CT is routinely used for renal mass evaluation and provides information on tumor vascularity, internal architecture, necrosis, and heterogeneity [17, 18], it may offer imaging biomarkers associated with CK7 expression. In addition, machine learning techniques enable modeling of complex and potentially nonlinear relationships among clinical and imaging variables and may improve predictive performance [19–21].

Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to investigate the association between preoperatively available clinical and CT imaging features and CK7 expression in ccRCC and to develop an exploratory machine learning model for CK7 characterization.

Materials and methods

Study patients

This multicenter retrospective study was approved by the institutional review boards of the participating institutions, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. At Institution A, 410 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed ccRCC who underwent surgery between February 2019 and June 2021 were initially screened for model development. At Institutions B and C, 240 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed ccRCC who underwent surgery between June 2015 and June 2025 were screened for validation. The longer inclusion period in the validation cohort was used to increase the representativeness of real-world multicenter validation, although potential temporal heterogeneity in imaging protocols was considered in the interpretation of model performance. Patients at each center were identified through institutional surgical pathology databases and were cross-matched with radiology records to confirm the availability of preoperative contrast-enhanced CT examinations. No patient received systemic therapy, radiotherapy, ablation, or other tumor-directed treatment before CT examination or surgery. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: (a) absence of preoperative contrast-enhanced CT, (b) missing CK7 immunohistochemistry data, (c) history of another primary malignancy, (d) multiple primary ccRCC tumors, (e) inadequate CT image quality, or (f) an interval of more than 1 month between CT examination and surgery.

Clinical and pathologic data evaluation

Clinical information was extracted from the electronic medical record systems of the participating institutions, including patient age and sex. Immediately after surgical excision, specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by graded ethanol dehydration and paraffin embedding. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm for immunohistochemical evaluation. After deparaffinization, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using EnVision FLEX antigen retrieval solution. The primary antibody was a mouse anti-CK7 monoclonal antibody (clone OV-TL 12/30; DAKO; dilution 1:200), and the secondary antibody was a universal reagent. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using an automated immunostainer. CK7 staining was independently assessed by two senior pathologists who were blinded to CT features, model outputs, and clinical outcomes. Discrepant cases were resolved by consensus. CK7 expression was assessed semi-quantitatively according to the proportion of tumor cells showing cytoplasmic staining. CK7 positivity was defined as immunoreactivity in at least 10% of tumor cells [16]. Staining intensity was recorded as weak (+), moderate (++), or strong (+++), but CK7 status in this study was determined according to the proportion of positive tumor cells.

CT examination

All patients underwent multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT examinations using one of three multidetector CT systems (uCT 530, uCT 710, or uCT 760; United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). For the uCT 530 system, scans were obtained with a tube voltage of 120 kV and a tube current of 110 mAs, a gantry rotation time of 0.8 s, and a pitch factor of 1.075. Examinations performed on the uCT 710 utilized a tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 110 mAs, rotation time of 0.5 s, and pitch of 0.9875. For the uCT 760 scanner, imaging parameters included 120 kV tube voltage, 105 mAs tube current, 0.5 s rotation time, and a pitch of 0.9875. An intravenous nonionic iodinated contrast medium (Optiray; Guerbet, France) was administered at a weight-based dose of 1.2 mL per kilogram of body weight through a power injector at a flow rate of 3.0 mL/s. Following contrast injection, biphasic imaging was performed. The corticomedullary phase was acquired approximately 25–28 s after contrast administration, followed by the nephrographic phase at 65–70 s. Although scanners and acquisition settings varied across institutions and over time, all included examinations consisted of multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT with corticomedullary and nephrographic phase images. All CT features were re-evaluated using the same predefined feature definitions and measurement rules. Because this study used routinely acquired clinical CT images, no post hoc image harmonization or feature harmonization method, such as ComBat harmonization, was applied to the quantitative CT features.

CT feature evaluation

All contrast-enhanced CT images were retrieved from the institutional picture archiving and communication system and independently reviewed by two abdominal radiologists who were blinded to histopathologic findings and CK7 status. Both radiologists independently evaluated all qualitative CT features and performed all quantitative measurements. Interobserver agreement was assessed before consensus reading or averaging of quantitative measurements. Tumor location was recorded as left or right. Enhancement in the corticomedullary phase was categorized as homogeneous or heterogeneous according to the presence of visually apparent intratumoral hypoattenuating areas. Tumor contour was classified as regular (smooth, well-defined margin) or irregular (lobulated or ill-defined margin). Calcification on unenhanced images was recorded as present or absent. A cystic component was defined as a well-demarcated intratumoral fluid-attenuation area without perceptible enhancement. Discrepancies between readers were resolved by consensus. For quantitative analysis, maximum tumor diameter (mm) was measured on axial images at the largest cross-sectional level. Attenuation values (Hounsfield units, HU) were obtained by placing a manually drawn region of interest within the solid portion of the tumor while avoiding necrosis, calcification, and cystic areas on unenhanced, corticomedullary-phase, and nephrographic-phase images; the mean value was recorded. The region of interest was placed on the largest available solid tumor area, with care taken to avoid visible vessels, hemorrhage, calcification, cystic degeneration, and necrotic components. When possible, ROIs were placed in corresponding solid tumor regions across the unenhanced, corticomedullary, and nephrographic phases by referring to the same axial level and adjacent slices. The ROI was drawn as large as possible within the solid component while avoiding the tumor margin, partial-volume effects, and nonenhancing areas. Enhancement ratios were calculated as follows: corticomedullary enhancement ratio = (attenuation in the corticomedullary phase—unenhanced attenuation)/unenhanced attenuation, and nephrographic enhancement ratio = (attenuation in the nephrographic phase—unenhanced attenuation)/unenhanced attenuation. For quantitative variables, the mean of the two readers' measurements was used for analysis.

Feature selection and model development

All preprocessing steps used for model development, including feature scaling, correlation filtering, LASSO feature selection, and model fitting, were performed within the training cohort. The validation cohort was not used for feature selection or model fitting and was reserved for independent performance evaluation. Pairwise correlation analysis was performed among all candidate clinical and imaging variables to identify highly correlated features and assess potential multicollinearity. For variable pairs with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.9, the more clinically meaningful variable was retained to reduce redundancy. Categorical variables were encoded as binary variables. Continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization. The mean and standard deviation used for standardization were estimated from the training cohort and then applied unchanged to the validation cohort. Feature selection was then performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression within the training cohort to further reduce dimensionality and limit overfitting. All remaining variables were entered into the LASSO model, and the optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was determined using cross-validation within the training cohort. Variables with nonzero coefficients at the optimal lambda value were retained for model construction. Using the selected features, four machine learning algorithms were developed in the training cohort to discriminate CK7-positive from CK7-negative tumors: adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), logistic regression (LR), and naive Bayes. No class-imbalance correction method, including SMOTE, class weighting, or undersampling, was applied in the primary analysis. Because the number of CK7-positive cases was limited, we avoided resampling-based correction to reduce the risk of introducing artificial sample patterns or increasing overfitting. To improve interpretability under class imbalance, we reported multiple threshold-dependent metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1 score. No grid search or random search was performed for hyperparameter optimization. Instead, the models were trained using prespecified configurations, and the full model parameters are provided in Additional Table 1 to improve reproducibility. Briefly, the KNN model used five nearest neighbors, uniform weighting, and the Minkowski distance metric with p = 2, corresponding to Euclidean distance. The AdaBoost model used 10 estimators, a learning rate of 1.0, and the SAMME.R algorithm. The LR model used L2 regularization with C = 1.0 and the lbfgs solver. The naive Bayes model used a variance smoothing parameter of 1 × 10^−9. To improve model interpretability, Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) analysis was performed after model development. SHAP values were used to quantify the contribution of selected features to model output. A positive SHAP value indicated that a feature increased the model output toward CK7-positive prediction, whereas a negative SHAP value indicated that a feature decreased the predicted probability of CK7 positivity. Feature importance was ranked according to the mean absolute SHAP value across patients. For the final selected model, a SHAP summary plot was generated to illustrate both feature importance and the direction of feature effects.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using the Student t test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Interobserver agreement for categorical variables was assessed using the κ statistic and interpreted as poor (0.00–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), good (0.61–0.80), or excellent (0.81–1.00). For continuous variables, interobserver agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with ICC values interpreted as poor (0.00–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.76–0.90), or excellent (0.91–1.00). The optimal classification threshold for each model was determined in the training cohort by maximizing the Youden index and was then applied unchanged to the validation cohort. No classification threshold was re-estimated in the validation cohort. Based on this threshold, confusion matrices were generated to report true-positive, false-positive, true-negative, and false-negative classifications. In addition to area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, accuracy, and F1 score were calculated to provide a more comprehensive assessment of model performance. Bootstrap resampling analysis was performed to assess the stability of model performance. For each model and cohort, 1000 bootstrap samples were generated with replacement based on the model-predicted probabilities. The AUC and threshold-dependent performance metrics were recalculated in each bootstrap sample, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distributions were used to derive 95% confidence intervals. This internal resampling analysis was used to evaluate the stability of performance estimates. Differences in AUCs between models were compared using the DeLong test. Calibration was assessed by comparing predicted probabilities with observed outcomes using calibration curves. Clinical utility was further evaluated with decision curve analysis by estimating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities and comparing it with the treat-all and treat-none strategies. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 21.0; IBM), R software (version 3.6.1), and Python. Machine learning analyses, including LASSO feature selection and construction of KNN, AdaBoost, LR, and naive Bayes models, were performed using the Onekey package. Data processing and visualization were performed using pandas, NumPy, matplotlib, and seaborn. ROC analysis, DeLong testing, bootstrap resampling, calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis were performed using the Onekey package and custom scripts in R and Python. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 650 patients with pathologically confirmed ccRCC from three institutions were initially screened. At Institution A, 271 patients were excluded for the following reasons: absence of contrast-enhanced CT (n = 231), missing CK7 data (n = 6), history of other malignancies (n = 18), multiple primary tumors (n = 5), inadequate CT image quality (n = 5), and an interval between CT and surgery greater than 1 month (n = 6). Ultimately, 139 patients were included in the training cohort. At Institutions B and C, 149 patients were excluded, including 120 without contrast-enhanced CT, 7 with missing CK7 data, 12 with a history of other malignancies, 3 with multiple tumors, 3 with inadequate CT image quality, and 4 with an interval between CT and surgery greater than 1 month. Finally, 91 patients were included in the validation cohort, including 55 from Institution B and 36 from Institution C (Fig. 1). In total, 230 eligible patients were included in the study (median age, 59 years; interquartile range [IQR], 51–67 years), including 155 male patients. CK7 positivity was observed in 67 patients (29.13%), whereas 163 patients (70.87%) were CK7-negative. In the training cohort, 43 of 139 patients (30.94%) were CK7-positive and 96 (69.06%) were CK7-negative. In the validation cohort, 24 of 91 patients (26.37%) were CK7-positive and 67 (73.63%) were CK7-negative (Table 1). Tumor location differed significantly between the two cohorts (P = 0.025), with left-sided tumors more frequently observed in the validation cohort (58.24%) than in the training cohort (43.17%). No significant differences were observed between the two cohorts with respect to age (P = 0.059), sex (P = 0.213), enhancement homogeneity (P = 0.200), tumor contour (P = 0.064), calcification (P = 0.584), cystic component (P = 0.619), unenhanced attenuation (P = 0.906), corticomedullary attenuation (P = 0.848), corticomedullary enhancement ratio (P = 0.582), nephrographic attenuation (P = 0.727), nephrographic enhancement ratio (P = 0.615), tumor size (P = 0.192), or CK7 status (P = 0.457).

Clinical and CT imaging characteristics according to CK7 status

Compared with CK7-negative tumors, CK7-positive tumors were associated with younger age (55.00 years [IQR, 50.00–65.50 years] vs 60.00 years [IQR, 53.00–67.50 years]; P = 0.046), lower corticomedullary attenuation (92.00 HU [IQR, 64.00–116.00 HU] vs 115.00 HU [IQR, 92.50–145.00 HU]; P < 0.001), lower corticomedullary enhancement ratio (1.85 [IQR, 1.08–2.90] vs 2.48 [IQR, 1.69–3.62]; P < 0.001), lower nephrographic attenuation (90.00 HU [IQR, 66.50–103.00 HU] vs 102.00 HU [IQR, 88.00–118.50 HU]; P < 0.001), lower nephrographic enhancement ratio (1.83 [IQR, 1.02–2.73] vs 2.21 [IQR, 1.50–3.09]; P = 0.025), and smaller tumor size (25.00 mm [IQR, 20.00–35.45 mm] vs 39.00 mm [IQR, 27.00–57.00 mm]; P < 0.001) (Table 2). Cystic component was less frequently observed in CK7-positive tumors than in CK7-negative tumors (36 of 67 [53.73%] vs 124 of 163 [76.07%]; P = 0.001). No significant differences were observed between CK7-positive and CK7-negative tumors with respect to sex (P = 0.098), tumor location (P = 0.646), enhancement homogeneity (P = 0.408), tumor contour (P = 0.497), calcification (P = 0.342), or unenhanced attenuation (P = 0.192).

Interobserver agreement for CT features

Interobserver agreement was good to excellent for all evaluated imaging features. For categorical variables, κ values ranged from 0.724 to 0.864. Agreement was good for enhancement homogeneity (κ = 0.780; 95% CI: 0.678, 0.882) and tumor contour (κ = 0.724; 95% CI: 0.634, 0.813), and excellent for calcification (κ = 0.864; 95% CI: 0.766, 0.962) and cystic component (κ = 0.803; 95% CI: 0.720, 0.885). For continuous variables, ICC values ranged from 0.840 to 0.965, indicating good to excellent agreement. Agreement was good for unenhanced attenuation (ICC = 0.840; 95% CI: 0.798, 0.874), corticomedullary attenuation (ICC = 0.872; 95% CI: 0.837, 0.899), and nephrographic attenuation (ICC = 0.876; 95% CI: 0.843, 0.903), and excellent for tumor size (ICC = 0.965; 95% CI: 0.955, 0.973).

Selected features and model development

LASSO regression was performed to select the most informative predictors for CK7 status. The coefficient profiles showed gradual shrinkage of candidate variables as the regularization parameter increased (Fig. 2A). The optimal penalty parameter was determined by cross-validation, with the selected lambda value of 0.0339 (Fig. 2B). At this lambda value, six variables with nonzero coefficients were retained for subsequent model construction: age, cystic component, corticomedullary attenuation, corticomedullary enhancement ratio, nephrographic attenuation, and tumor size. These selected variables were then used to develop the four machine learning models.

Model performance and comparison

In the training cohort, AdaBoost showed the highest AUC of 0.938 (95% CI: 0.895, 0.970), followed by KNN with an AUC of 0.901 (95% CI: 0.848, 0.946), LR with an AUC of 0.849 (95% CI: 0.775, 0.914), and Naive Bayes with an AUC of 0.844 (95% CI: 0.770, 0.914) (Table 3). In the validation cohort, KNN and LR showed comparable discrimination, with AUCs of 0.716 (95% CI: 0.595, 0.822) and 0.714 (95% CI: 0.589, 0.828), respectively, followed by Naive Bayes with an AUC of 0.664 (95% CI: 0.538, 0.789) and AdaBoost with an AUC of 0.594 (95% CI: 0.460, 0.722) (Additional Fig. 1). Bootstrap-derived 95% CIs for threshold-dependent performance metrics are provided in Additional Table 2. Although KNN achieved a slightly higher AUC in the validation cohort, LR was selected as the final model based on its comparable validation discrimination, calibration performance, model interpretability, and overall generalizability. At the selected threshold of 0.318, the LR model yielded 39 true-positive, 72 true-negative, 24 false-positive, and 4 false-negative classifications in the training cohort, and 15 true-positive, 42 true-negative, 25 false-positive, and 9 false-negative classifications in the validation cohort (Additional Fig. 2).

Model clinical utility and interpretability

In exploratory decision curve analysis, KNN and LR showed higher net benefit than the treat-all and treat-none strategies across selected threshold probability ranges in both cohorts (Fig. 3). Calibration curves visually showed that the LR model had relatively closer agreement between predicted probabilities and observed CK7-positive fractions than the other evaluated models, particularly in the validation cohort (Additional Fig. 3). Calibration analysis showed that the LR model had no statistically significant evidence of poor calibration in either the training cohort or the validation cohort. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test for the LR model yielded P values of 0.120 in the training cohort and 0.093 in the validation cohort (Additional Table 3). By contrast, KNN showed poor calibration in the validation cohort (P < 0.001). Therefore, among the evaluated models, LR was selected as the final model based on comparable validation discrimination, acceptable calibration, exploratory net benefit, interpretability, and overall generalizability. The LR model was subsequently used for SHAP-based interpretation (Additional Fig. 4). Positive SHAP values indicated features that increased the model output toward CK7-positive prediction, whereas negative SHAP values indicated features that decreased the predicted probability of CK7 positivity (Figs. 4 and 5).

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we investigated the association between contrast-enhanced CT features and CK7 expression in clear cell renal cell carcinoma and developed four machine learning models for exploratory CK7 characterization. The LR model was selected as the final model based on its overall discrimination, calibration performance, interpretability, and generalizability, achieving an AUC of 0.849 (95% CI: 0.775, 0.914) in the training cohort and 0.714 (95% CI: 0.589, 0.828) in the validation cohort. These findings suggest that routinely acquired contrast-enhanced CT features, combined with clinical information, may provide complementary information for exploratory characterization of CK7 expression in ccRCC. Relative to prior ultrasound-based CK7 prediction research, this study explored the value of routinely acquired contrast-enhanced CT features in a multicenter setting with validation.

In the validation cohort, KNN and LR showed comparable discrimination, with AUCs of 0.716 and 0.714, respectively. Although KNN achieved a slightly higher validation AUC, its calibration performance was suboptimal in the validation cohort. In contrast, LR showed comparable discrimination, acceptable calibration, better probability reliability, and greater interpretability. Therefore, LR was selected as the final model based on overall validation discrimination, calibration performance, interpretability, and generalizability. The decrease in validation performance may reflect some degree of overfitting, the limited number of CK7-positive events, and differences in imaging protocols, reconstruction techniques, scanner-related attenuation measurements, and case composition across institutions. Tumor location differed between cohorts but was not significantly associated with CK7 status; therefore, this imbalance was considered likely to reflect sampling variation or cohort heterogeneity rather than a CK7-related biological factor. Because no harmonization method was applied to CT-derived quantitative features, scanner-related variation may have contributed to the reduced validation performance. To improve transparency and assess model stability, we performed bootstrap resampling, expanded classification metrics, used training-derived classification thresholds, and provided confusion matrices. Nevertheless, these analyses cannot fully compensate for the limited event count, and the model should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. SHAP analysis further quantified the relative contribution of the selected features and showed that enhancement-related CT parameters, tumor size, cystic component, and age contributed to the prediction of CK7 expression. These SHAP-based findings should be interpreted as model-derived associations rather than mechanistic evidence.

Contrast-enhanced CT is widely used in the evaluation of renal masses because it provides information on tumor vascularity, internal architecture, and necrosis [22, 23]. In ccRCC, these imaging characteristics may be influenced by angiogenesis and intratumoral heterogeneity. Enhancement-related parameters, including corticomedullary attenuation, corticomedullary enhancement ratio, and nephrographic attenuation, were retained in the model and may capture imaging phenotypes related to vascularity or perfusion. Although angiogenic signaling is relevant to ccRCC biology, this study did not directly assess angiogenesis, perfusion, or molecular heterogeneity. Therefore, the observed contribution of enhancement-related CT parameters should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating imaging association rather than evidence of an underlying biological mechanism. Cystic component and tumor size also contributed to model prediction. In ccRCC, cystic degeneration or necrosis is a recognized imaging finding and may reflect tumor heterogeneity in some cases [24]. Previous studies have linked cystic or necrotic imaging features and tumor size to tumor heterogeneity and adverse pathologic features [25–27]. Larger tumors were associated with lower SHAP values, indicating a decreased predicted probability of CK7 positivity. This finding indicates a model-derived association between tumor size and predicted CK7 status. However, the direction and magnitude of these associations should be interpreted cautiously, because they describe model-derived patterns rather than direct biological mechanisms. Age demonstrated a modest contribution to model output, with higher age values generally associated with decreased predicted probability of CK7 positivity. Although demographic variables were less influential than imaging characteristics, their retention suggests that patient-level factors may be associated with CK7-related tumor phenotype [28, 29].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective study may have selection bias, and the sample size was relatively limited. The limited number of positive events may affect the stability of feature selection and model comparison. Second, moderate class imbalance may have influenced the balance between sensitivity and specificity, although multiple threshold-dependent metrics were reported. Future studies should evaluate whether class-weighted learning or resampling strategies improve performance. Third, the validation cohort spanned a long period and multiple institutions, during which changes in CT acquisition, reconstruction protocols, scanner settings, and case composition may have introduced heterogeneity. No image or feature harmonization method was applied; therefore, scanner-related variation in attenuation values may have affected quantitative features and validation performance. Fourth, some overfitting cannot be excluded, although bootstrap resampling was performed. Pipeline-level reproducibility was not fully assessed because repeated feature selection and model training across resampled datasets were not performed. Finally, SHAP analysis explains model behavior but does not establish causality, and the biological explanations were not validated by molecular, perfusion, or spatial pathologic analyses.

In conclusion, contrast-enhanced CT imaging features showed an association with CK7 expression in clear cell renal cell carcinoma. A machine learning model based on preoperatively available clinical and CT imaging features demonstrated exploratory potential for CK7 characterization, with the LR model selected as the final model based on overall discrimination, calibration, interpretability, and generalizability. These findings may provide complementary information for understanding clinical-CT imaging associations with CK7 expression in ccRCC, but further prospective studies are warranted before clinical translation.
