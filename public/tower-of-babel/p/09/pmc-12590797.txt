The application and effectiveness of PBL combined with MDT and flipped classroom teaching method in acute coronary syndrome education for undergraduate medical students
Authors: Yuhui Lin, Xiaoqing He, Wenjun Dai, Wenpeng Tan, Yunhong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe development of medical education is rapidly evolving, with innovative teaching methods such as problem-based learning (PBL) and multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) combined with flipped classroom approaches gaining increasing attention in clinical medical education. However, the actual effectiveness of PBL combined with MDT and flipped classroom methods in the teaching of cardiovascular diseases has not been well studied. Considering that these which includedteaching methods can complement each other, this study explores the application and effectiveness of PBL combined with MDT and flipped classroom teaching method in the case-based teaching of acute coronary syndrome.MethodsA total of 100 undergraduate clinical medicine students aged 20–21 years from Guangzhou Medical University were selected as research participants. They were randomly divided into two groups via a random number table: the experimental group (PBL combined with MDT and flipped classroom teaching), which included 50 students, and the control group (traditional teaching methods), which also included 50 students. After the clinical training, the theoretical examination scores and skill assessment results of the two groups were compared, and a questionnaire survey was conducted to evaluate teaching effectiveness.ResultsThe theoretical examination scores and skill assessment results of the experimental group were significantly better than those of the control group (P < 0.05). The questionnaire survey revealed that the students in the experimental group outperformed those in the traditional teaching group in terms of learning motivation, theoretical learning outcomes, clinical thinking ability, literature retrieval skills, self-directed learning ability, practical ability, and classroom satisfaction, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings of this study suggest that the integration of PBL, MDT, and the flipped classroom teaching method may have a positive impact on the theoretical knowledge and clinical skills of undergraduate medical students in the context of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). However, due to the study’s limited sample size, short-term assessment period, and reliance on self-reported data, these results should be interpreted with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08150-2.

Introduction

The problem-based learning (PBL) teaching method is currently a popular educational model in the field of medical education and first emerged in medical education in the 1950s [1]. The PBL teaching method is centered around problem-oriented learning, where the teacher guides students through a process that includes problem identification, analysis, discussion, and resolution to complete the learning content. PBL effectively captures students’ attention and fosters their enthusiasm and initiative for learning. By encouraging students to participate in the formulation, analysis, and discussion of problems, PBL enhances effective classroom interaction between teachers and students as well as among students themselves. This approach not only aids in developing students’ independent learning and thinking habits but also hones their communication and expression skills [2, 3].

The flipped classroom, also known as the flipped classroom teaching method [4], was first introduced by two chemistry teachers, Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams, in 2006. The successful implementation of this approach has gained widespread attention from scholars both domestically and internationally, establishing it as an innovative teaching model across various disciplines. In medical education, the flipped classroom has led to significant changes in teacher and student roles, as well as in course teaching models. The core concept of this method is to encourage students to engage in knowledge acquisition outside the classroom while using class time for the internalization of knowledge and Introductory clinical discussion of the problems. This approach represents a “disruptive” or “transformative” shift from traditional teaching methods [5–7]. The flipped classroom model offers significant advantages in promoting personalized student development and enhancing students’ self-directed learning abilities. It moves away from the monotonous and repetitive nature of traditional teaching methods, transforming into a more engaging, dynamic, and diverse teaching approach. Before class, the teacher provides students with learning materials such as videos and slides through an online platform, encouraging them to engage in self-study. Students are expected to gain a certain understanding of the key concepts by researching the material and discussing it with their peers. In class, students bring up any questions or doubts they encountered during their self-study, and the class collaboratively discusses these issues, allowing for a deeper exploration of the topics and helping students better understand the related concepts.By integrating modern multimedia technology, the flipped classroom facilitates autonomous learning and improves students’ overall competencies, aligning with the developmental goals of modern medical education [8].

The concept of multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) was introduced by the MD Anderson Cancer Center in the United States. It advocates for the collaboration of multiple disciplines in the diagnosis and treatment decision-making process for cancer patients. This approach shifts the traditional disease-centered diagnostic and treatment model toward a more patient-centered model that emphasizes individualized, precise, comprehensive, and multidisciplinary care [9]. MDT has garnered widespread attention in both clinical practice and medical education because of its unique multidisciplinary approach to disease diagnosis and treatment, which aims to provide patients with the best treatment strategies. For medical educators, a long-standing challenge has been how to cultivate students’ ability to understand diseases holistically and analyze and manage the individualized characteristics of patients’ conditions. Applying the MDT model to case-based teaching helps overcome the limitations of the narrow and shallow knowledge often associated with a single-discipline perspective [10, 11].

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of combining these methods to enhance learning outcomes. For instance, a study by Zhou et al. integrated PBL and FC in a cardiology curriculum and found that students showed significant improvements in clinical reasoning and knowledge retention compared to traditional methods.Similarly [12], Feng et al. combined MDT with competency-based education in respiratory rehabilitation nursing and reported higher student satisfaction and better performance in clinical simulations [9]. These findings suggest that the integration of multiple teaching strategies can create a synergistic effect, addressing the gaps in traditional approaches and better preparing students for the complexities of real-world clinical practice.Traditional teaching methods for ACS have primarily focused on theoretical knowledge and individual clinical skills, but there is a lack of innovative approaches that combine active learning strategies like PBL with team-based and multidisciplinary perspectives.

Both PBL and the flipped classroom are designed to increase student engagement and foster critical thinking [12, 13]. The integration of the MDT approach reflects real-world clinical practice [14], where professionals from various disciplines collaborate to manage complex conditions like Acute coronary syndrome (ACS). ACS was chosen as the focus of our study due to its clinical complexity and its critical importance in cardiology education. ACS is a condition that requires not only a solid understanding of theoretical concepts but also the ability to integrate knowledge from multiple disciplines (e.g., cardiology, critical care, pharmacology, and imaging) to make timely and accurate decisions. A single teaching method may not adequately address these diverse learning needs.By incorporating MDT discussions into the learning process, our goal is to expose students to interdisciplinary perspectives early in their education, thereby enhancing their comprehensive understanding of patient care. Using a single strategy in isolation may not suffice to achieve the desired learning outcomes, as each method has its limitations. For example, PBL alone may lack the structured knowledge acquisition provided by FC, while FC may not fully simulate the collaborative decision-making required in clinical practice. Similarly, MDT discussions without a strong foundation in problem-solving or self-directed learning may fall short of fostering independent clinical reasoning. By combining these methods, we aim to create a synergistic effect that addresses the gaps in traditional teaching approaches.

In recent years, Guangzhou Medical University has vigorously promoted new teaching models such as “organ system-centered” education, PBL teaching methods, and flipped classrooms, achieving substantial results. These innovative approaches address the shortcomings of traditional medical education, significantly enhancing students’ innovative thinking and practical skills. However, the implementation of these models has also faced some challenges, particularly during clinical clerkships. A considerable number of students still struggle to integrate knowledge effectively from disciplines such as pathophysiology, diagnostics, radiology, and internal medicine. They often lack logical thinking and judgment skills and rely passively on teachers to impart knowledge. To address these issues, it is necessary to integrate these new teaching models, leveraging their strengths and compensating for their weaknesses. This has led to timely reforms in clinical clerkship teaching methods. Given that acute coronary syndrome (ACS) presents characteristic symptoms that are easy for students to learn and understand, a typical case of ACS was selected as the teaching case. This study explored the application and effectiveness of PBL combined with an MDT and the flipped classroom teaching method in clinical clerkships for acute coronary syndrome.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study selected 100 undergraduate clinical medicine students aged 20–21 years from Guangzhou Medical University. The students were randomly divided into two groups using a random number table: the experimental group (PBL combined with MDT and the flipped classroom teaching method), which included 50 students, and the control group (traditional teaching method), which included 50 students. The same instructor conducted the theoretical courses for both groups. A team of three instructors, each with over 10 years of teaching experience, was responsible for supervising the clinical clerkship. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the research was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University.

Study design

The students who participated in the study had already completed foundational courses such as Human Anatomy, Physiology, Pathophysiology, Pathology, Pharmacology, Biochemistry, Molecular Biology, Cell Biology, and Diagnostics, providing them with a solid base of medical knowledge. The teaching was conducted according to the overall requirements of the Internal Medicine Teaching Syllabus of Guangzhou Medical University. The course began with foundational theoretical teaching on cardiovascular system diseases, helping students build a comprehensive understanding of the theory and diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases to facilitate clinical clerkship practices.

Apart from the differences in teaching methods, the instructor, teaching content, and number of class hours for the experimental group were identical to those of the control group. The study was conducted over the course of a two-week clinical internship.The case-based teaching for acute coronary syndrome was conducted in two sessions, each lasting 120 min.

Control group

The traditional teaching method was implemented, covering key topics such as the pathophysiology of ACS, clinical manifestations, diagnostic approaches, treatment protocols, and long-term management. The content was delivered through instructor-led sessions using PowerPoint presentations and handouts. For a comprehensive description of the session details, please refer to the Supplementary file.Teaching was primarily didactic, with a focus on presenting information systematically and ensuring students gained an understanding of the theoretical aspects of ACS.

In the control group, ‘traditional teaching’ referred to large-group didactic lectures with limited case discussion, as typically used in our institution’s ACS module prior to this study. The structure included instructor-led explanations of theoretical content followed by brief question-and-answer sessions without pre-class preparation tasks. While flipped classroom elements could be incorporated into traditional teaching, they were not part of the existing standard practice in our setting.

Experimental group

PBL combined with an MDT and a flipped classroom teaching method was employed. Based on the teaching syllabus and the key points of acute coronary syndrome, the core content and difficult areas of teaching were identified, and a problem-based learning (PBL) lesson plan was developed. Lecture videos and Supplementary materials on acute coronary syndrome were prepared, and students were required to study these resources in advance. Additionally, the students were instructed to use databases such as PubMed and the university library to gather useful information and write study notes.

The students were divided into groups of 5 to 6 members, with one student acting as the group leader. Each member took on the role of a doctor from different specialties, such as cardiology, critical care medicine, respiratory medicine, ultrasound medicine, and pharmacy, forming an MDT (multidisciplinary team). The group simulated an MDT team discussion, focusing on key issues related to acute coronary syndrome from the perspective of their respective disciplines. These key issues are detailed in the Supplementary materials. The group discussed these questions and collected and recorded their findings. During the hospital clerkship, under the guidance of a supervising teacher, each group discussed how to “solve problems” by combining the patient’s medical history, physical examination, and auxiliary test results. They conducted Introductory clinical discussion on the diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and treatment plan for the patient. The group leader then presented their findings, with other members adding Supplementary information. The focus was on analyzing and summarizing the diagnostic and therapeutic logic and strategies of the case. After the presentations, the instructor evaluated each group’s report, pointed out areas for improvement, and guided the students to clarify the next steps in their learning, thereby stimulating their potential for self-directed learning.

Additionally, for some typical cases, a 40-minute discussion session was appropriately arranged during class. Specific questions are assigned in advance, with each group focusing on one question for research and analysis. Before the clerkship, students explored the optimal solutions through information gathering, team discussions, and literature reviews and then prepare a report. For example, in a case involving an elderly male patient with sudden chest pain and ST-T wave abnormalities on an ECG, the supervising teacher can include guiding questions in the PBL lesson plan, such as the following: What are the diagnostic criteria? What are the key points for differential diagnosis? What further tests are necessary? What is the best treatment strategy? During the preparation phase before the clerkship, group members can gather information through various means, such as online searches and library resources, to develop a solution. In the clerkship, after thoroughly understanding the theoretical knowledge and participating in classroom discussions, students can engage more deeply with patients, applying what they have learned to real clinical cases.

The PBL approach used in this study was a hybrid model adapted from the classical McMaster model. Key features included: small-group learning (5–6 students per group), real patient cases from clinical practice, structured facilitator guidance, and a two-session format (initial case discussion → independent research/self-study → follow-up session for problem resolution).

Evaluation of teaching effectiveness

A test was designed based on the teaching content, and a closed-book written exam was administered to assess the theoretical knowledge of the students in both groups. The exam consisted of multiple-choice questions, which were equally divided into two sections: basic theoretical knowledge (100 points) and clinical case analysis (100 points). All the questions in the test were based on Bloom’s Taxonomy, which categorizes cognitive activities into six levels: remembering(20%), understanding(30%), applying(25%), analyzing(15%), evaluating(7%), and creating(3%). The “remembering” and “understanding” categories were combined into one section, referred to as “basic theoretical knowledge,” while items from the other categories were classified under “clinical case analysis“ [15, 16]. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess the internal consistency reliability of the multiple-choice questions (MCQs), yielding a value of 0.82. Inter-rater reliability for the skill examination was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa, with a coefficient of 0.89, indicating a high level of agreement between evaluators. The pilot testing of the assessment tools was conducted on a small subset of students from the same academic year, who did not participate in the main intervention.

The survey questionnaire used in this study was specifically designed for research purposes by the author. To ensure unbiased feedback, the survey was administered anonymously to both the experimental and control groups. The questionnaire included the following seven questions: learning motivation, effectiveness of theoretical learning, clinical reasoning ability, literature search skills, self-directed learning ability, practical skills, and classroom satisfaction. Each question was in a multiple-choice format, with the options “Yes” or “No.” The proportion of “Yes” responses was calculated for each question. Finally, a statistical analysis was conducted on the exam results and the responses from the teaching effectiveness survey for both the experimental and control groups. The internal consistency reliability of the teaching effectiveness questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.78, indicating good reliability.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the study were statistically analyzed via SPSS software(version 23.0,Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data are expressed as frequencies and percentages n (%), and comparisons between groups were performed via the chi-square (χ²) test. Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and comparisons between groups were made via t- test. A p value of less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Traditional teaching methods in medical education, particularly in the context of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), primarily focus on delivering theoretical knowledge and training individual clinical skills. These approaches, often lecture-based, provide foundational knowledge but fail to actively engage students or simulate the collaborative, interdisciplinary nature of real-world clinical practice. As a result, students may lack the critical thinking, problem-solving, and teamwork skills necessary to manage complex conditions like ACS, which requires the integration of knowledge across multiple disciplines.To address these limitations, innovative teaching strategies such as Problem-Based Learning (PBL), flipped classroom models, and multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) approaches have gained increasing attention [17–19].

Acute coronary syndrome is a common and critical condition that involves multidisciplinary knowledge for its diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and treatment [20]. The present study investigated the combined use of Problem-Based Learning (PBL), flipped classroom, and Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) teaching methods in the education of undergraduate medical students about acute coronary syndrome (ACS). This innovative approach was designed to address limitations in traditional teaching methods. The results demonstrated significant improvements in students’ learning outcomes, clinical reasoning, and satisfaction, indicating the value of integrating these methods in medical education.

PBL facilitated active engagement by encouraging students to analyze and solve case-based problems collaboratively. This approach directly addresses the need for medical students to develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills essential for managing complex conditions like ACS. Similarly, the flipped classroom allowed students to prepare independently by reviewing lecture materials and research articles before interactive sessions. This preparation optimized in-class time for higher-order cognitive activities, such as applying theoretical knowledge to clinical cases.

The integration of MDT discussions added an essential interdisciplinary dimension. By simulating real-world clinical practice, MDT discussions required students to consider perspectives from cardiology, respiratory medicine, critical care, pharmacy, and imaging. This approach not only enhanced students’ understanding of ACS but also fostered teamwork and communication skills, which are critical for effective healthcare delivery. Among the three components of the combined strategy, MDT likely played the most central role in promoting clinical reasoning and problem-solving skills. PBL and flipped classroom approaches were incorporated to enhance active learning and student engagement.

The results of this study show that students in the experimental group outperformed those in the control group in both exam scores and classroom satisfaction. This confirms that the integration of MDT, flipped classroom, and PBL teaching methods can play a multifaceted role in clinical training for acute coronary syndrome (ACS).

Firstly, it enhances students’ self-directed learning abilities. The flipped classroom requires students to engage in self-directed learning of foundational knowledge through videos, reading materials, and other resources before class. This approach is highly effective in fostering students’ self-learning and self-management skills, transforming passive knowledge reception into active learning [21]. Students in the experimental group were highly proactive in preparing for class by searching for information and engaging in group discussions to develop their case reports. Due to their thorough preparation, they were able to better understand and absorb the relevant knowledge in clinical cases, and they were able to apply this knowledge confidently in real-life scenarios during the clinical discussions.

Secondly, it promotes the development of critical thinking. During the discussions, students in the experimental group were required to analyze problems, propose hypotheses, and engage in discussions, which helped develop their critical thinking and problem-solving abilities. Compared to the control group, they were significantly more eager to explore problems and were more willing to engage with teachers and peers to find the best solutions. In addition, the experimental group had more teacher-student interactions, which allowed for personalized guidance based on feedback and the needs of students. Unlike traditional teaching methods, the integration of MDT, flipped classroom, and PBL requires students to actively participate in classroom discussions, enabling better absorption of knowledge and skills during teacher-student interactions. Teachers could make real-time adjustments based on students’ comprehension levels.

Finally, students in the experimental group showed a significant improvement in clinical thinking and decision-making skills. Clinical thinking and decision-making cannot be acquired through simple knowledge transfer but require students to actively engage in clinical practice. The integration of MDT, flipped classroom, and PBL teaching methods effectively addresses this challenge by encouraging students to prepare in advance, independently identify problems, seek solutions, and interact with instructors. This proactive approach is beneficial for both knowledge acquisition and the development of clinical reasoning skills.

While the PBL combined with MDT and flipped classroom teaching method has demonstrated significant short-term benefits, its long-term sustainability and impact on student satisfaction remain important considerations. The novelty effect, workload management, and continuous adaptation are key factors that could influence students’ ongoing engagement and satisfaction. Future studies should explore the longitudinal effects of this approach and identify strategies to maintain its effectiveness over time.

The decision to combine PBL, MDT, and the flipped classroom was based on the complementary strengths of these methods. PBL fosters critical thinking and problem-solving skills, the flipped classroom promotes self-directed learning and active engagement, and MDT reflects the interdisciplinary nature of real-world clinical practice. By integrating these methods, we aimed to create a synergistic effect that addresses the limitations of using each strategy in isolation. This approach aligns with recent studies that have demonstrated the benefits of combining active learning strategies in medical education [1, 6]. The positive outcomes observed in the experimental group, including higher exam scores and greater student satisfaction, suggest that the combined teaching methods are effective in enhancing learning outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the benefits of active learning and interdisciplinary collaboration in medical education [9, 12].

This study introduces an innovative approach by integrating Problem-Based Learning (PBL), flipped classroom methods, and Multidisciplinary Treatment (MDT) discussions to address the current challenges in teaching acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Traditional teaching methods for ACS primarily focus on theoretical knowledge and individual clinical skills, often lacking the active learning and interdisciplinary collaboration needed to manage complex, real-world clinical scenarios. This research aims to bridge that gap through the combined use of these interactive and team-based strategies. This innovative teaching model addresses current gaps in ACS education and provides a framework that could be applied to other complex medical topics, setting a precedent for advancing medical education with active, interdisciplinary learning strategies.

It is acknowledged that the improved performance of the experimental group may partly reflect novelty effects, increased interaction, and increased teacher/student workload rather than solely the teaching method itself. Although the same instructor, identical content, and equal class hours were applied for both groups, the interaction style in the experimental group inherently differed. This potential influence has been added as a limitation, and future studies are recommended to control for novelty and attention effects.

While the results demonstrated statistical significance, statistical outcomes alone should not be regarded as the sole measure of educational value. Broader considerations, such as the feasibility of implementation in different institutional contexts, the sustainability of such teaching strategies, the additional workload for both faculty and students, and the overall impact on medical education, must also be taken into account.

Limitations

While this study highlights the benefits of combining PBL, MDT, and flipped classroom methods in teaching acute coronary syndrome (ACS).However, due to the study’s limited sample size, short-term assessment period, and reliance on self-reported data, these results should be interpreted with caution.The study was conducted with a relatively small sample size of 100 undergraduate medical students from a single institution. This limits the generalizability of the findings to other medical schools or educational settings with different resources, student demographics, or curricular structures. The study evaluated the effectiveness of the teaching method over a short period (two weeks). Long-term outcomes, such as knowledge retention, clinical performance in real-world settings, and the impact on students’ future careers, were not assessed. Future studies should include follow-up evaluations to determine the sustained benefits of this approach. It should also be noted that the observed improvements in the experimental group may partly reflect novelty effects and the increased effort required by both teachers and students, rather than solely the superiority of the combined teaching methods. Another limitation of this study is that the combined intervention included MDT, PBL, and flipped classroom simultaneously. While MDT may be the most influential component, its individual contribution could not be isolated in our design. Future research should consider evaluating MDT alone to clarify its unique impact.

The study did not collect pre-test data to assess the baseline knowledge and skills of the students before the intervention. This limits the ability to fully attribute the observed improvements to the PBL-MDT-flipped classroom method, as it is possible that the experimental group had a higher baseline level of preparedness. The study population consisted of students from a single medical school in China, which may not represent the diverse backgrounds and learning styles of students globally. Future studies should include participants from different regions, cultures, and educational systems to enhance the external validity of the findings. Although the same instructor conducted the theoretical courses for both groups, the involvement of multiple instructors in the clinical clerkship could introduce variability in teaching quality and student experiences. Standardizing the training and delivery of the intervention across instructors could help mitigate this issue. No additional support or compensation was provided to the control group after the study. This difference may raise ethical concerns regarding fairness. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the study and suggest that future studies consider offering the enhanced teaching methods to all students either before or after the experiment to ensure equitable learning opportunities.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that the integration of PBL, MDT, and the flipped classroom teaching method may have a positive impact on the theoretical knowledge and clinical skills of undergraduate medical students in the context of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). While the observed improvements in exam scores and student satisfaction are promising, further large-scale, multi-institutional studies with long-term follow-ups and more robust evaluation tools are needed to validate these findings and explore the generalizability of this approach. Future research should also investigate the potential challenges of implementing this method in different educational settings and its long-term effects on student performance and clinical practice.
