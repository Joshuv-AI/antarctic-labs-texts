Risk factors of sinus perforation after extraction of upper third molars in proximity with the sinus floor
Authors: Wei Qi, Jing Qian, Leihao Hu, Xiaoyan Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSinus perforation (SP) is a relatively common complication during upper third molars (UTMs) extractions. However, previous studies included a broad range of UTM extractions, potentially underestimating the true incidence of SP by incorporating unrelated cases. This study investigated the risk factors for SP. during the extraction of UTMs that were closely adjacent to or protruded into the maxillary sinus.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at the General Dentistry and Fifth Division of Peking University School of Stomatology from January 2019 to May 2025. The study included partially or fully impacted UTMs, classified according to the extent of protrusion into the sinus, depth of impaction, and type of inclination. Data were analyzed using chi-square tests and binary logistic regression, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 141 UTMs from 120 patients were included. SP occurred in 24 sites, yielding an SP rate of 16.5%. Of these, 7 perforations were iatrogenic, with all UTMs being mesially or horizontally impacted (p < 0.05). SP was not detected when less than half of the root tip protrude into the sinus. Most of the SP were repaired successfully with a modified trapezoid flap after reliable primary wound closure. Postoperative oral-antral fistula was detected in 1 case. Chi-square analysis revealed a significant correlation between the mesially inclined UTMs and the incidence of total and iatrogenic SP (p < 0.05). Binary logistic regression showed that meisal impaction (OR 4.12) and the type Ⅱc protrusion (OR 6.03) were significantly related to SP.ConclusionsFor the UTMs that closely related to sinus floor, mesially inclined UTMs was identified as the primary risk factor for intra-operative SP. Additionally, a modified trapezoid flap is recommended for optimal wound closure.Trial registrationThe research has been registered in the Chinese Medical Research Registration and Filing System with registration number MR-11-25-019459 on March 4th, 2025.

Introduction

Upper third molars (UTMs) extraction is a frequently performed procedure in oral and maxillofacial surgery. Due to the anatomical proximity of UTMs to the sinus floor, sinus perforation (SP) is a relatively common complication during their extractions, with reported incidence rates ranging from 1.4% to 13% [1–5]. In certain cases, the incidence of SP may rise to nearly 24% [5]. The proximity of the tooth roots to the sinus floor and the depth of impaction are generally considered the primary risk factors for SP.

However, previous studies included a broad range of UTMs extractions, potentially underestimating the true incidence of SP by incorporating unrelated cases. Additionally, earlier evaluations primarily relied on pantomography, which may not accurately reflect the spatial relationship between the tooth roots and the sinus floor. Furthermore, the management of extraction-induced SP has not been thoroughly documented, and the long-term outcomes of SP repair remain unclear.

In this study, we focused exclusively on UTMs with roots in close proximity to the sinus floor, with cortical bone discontinuity. All cases involved partially or fully impacted teeth. This report provides a detailed analysis of the surgical flap design, risk factors for SP based on cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), and the short-term prognosis of the patents with SP following intraoperative repair.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted at the General Dentistry and Fifth Division of Peking University School of Stomatology from January 2019 to February 2025. This study adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University School of Stomatology (PKUSSIRB-2025108046). The privacy rights of human subjects were observed and informed consent was obtained for experimentation with human subjects. The inclusion criteria were: healthy patients aged > 18 years without systemic diseases; fully or partially impacted UTMs requiring flap elevation during extraction; preoperative CBCT conducted to assess the relationship between UTMs and the maxillary sinus floor; and UTMs with either the crown or root directly adjacent to the sinus floor, where the cortical bone was discontinuous or protruded into the sinus cavity. Additionally, the adjacent upper second molars (USMs) had to be intact and capable of preservation following UTMs extraction. Cases involving tooth germs without root development were excluded to ensure consistency in evaluating root-related factors.

CBCT evaluation of factors associated with SP

CBCT was performed when a close relationship between the UTM roots and the sinus floor was suspected based on periapical or panoramic radiographs. Only teeth that met the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis. The extent of root protrusion into the sinus cavity was classified into different types based on CBCT findings to assess the risk of SP.

A surgeon and a radiologist assessed and categorized all diagnostic imaging data independently according to predefined diagnostic categories. Both physicians remained blinded to the specific surgical outcomes for each individual patient. Inter-rater reliability for the classification of medical images was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics software (IBM, Version 27.0). Kappa values were calculated to quantify the level of agreement between raters beyond that expected by chance. Values of Kappa coefficient above 0.80 (95% CI, 0.712–0.894) typically indicate excellent agreement.

Extent of the root protrusion into the sinus

The extent of root protrusion proposed by the authors was categorized based on the amount of bone surrounding the protruding root. If the protruding root was surrounded by at least two bony walls, it was classified as the lateral type (type I). Otherwise, it was considered the tip type (type II). For type I, the horizontal plane was adjusted to transect the long axis of the UTMs, and the corresponding coronal plane was set as the reference plane; for the tip type, the sagittal or the coronal plane was adjusted to transect the long axis of the UTMs and set as the reference plane. Subsequently, Type I and II were divided into three subcategories depending on the extent of protrusion. Type of impaction

UTMs were classified based on their angulation relative to the long axis of the adjacent USMs. Thus, the teeth were categorized as mesially, buccally, horizontally, distally, or palatally impacted according to Winter’s classification (Fig. 2; [6])Fig. 2Different types of UTM inclinations. UTM depth relative to the pterygomaxillary fissure

The depth of UTM impaction proposed by the authors was further categorized based on the relationship between the distal cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) and the pterygomaxillary fissure (PMF). In Type A, the distal CEJ was located below the lower border of the PMF, whereas in Type B, the distal CEJ was located above the lower border of the PMF (Fig. 1).

Archer’s classification of depth of impaction

Depth of impaction was also evaluated using Archer’s classification. In Type A, the inferior border of the UTM was at or below the occlusal plane. Type B referred to cases in which the inferior border of the UTM was located between the occlusal plane and the cervical line of the adjacent USM. In Type C, the inferior border of the UTM was positioned between the cervical line of the USM and the middle third of its root. Finally, Type D referred to cases where the inferior border of the UTM was at or above the apical third of the USM root.

Surgical protocol

All surgical procedures were performed by two senior maxillofacial surgeons. Local anesthesia was administered using 4% articaine. A modified trapezoid flap design was used, which included two vertical incisions made at the mesiobuccal and distolingual corners, with an incision along the gingival sulcus of the adjacent upper second molar, regardless of the type and depth of impaction (Fig. 3). For most UTMs, bone removal was conducted using a dental elevator. In cases where additional access was required, tooth sectioning was performed with a high-speed handpiece.

A nose-blowing test was performed intraoperatively to assess for SP before wound closure. The socket was irrigated with physiological saline and filled with resorbable hemostatic sponges. The flap was then repositioned and sutured using resorbable sutures. Primary wound closure was required for sockets with SP. Patients with confirmed SP were prescribed a 7-day course of antibiotics, including amoxicillin (0.5 g taken thrice daily, Zhongnuo Pharmaceutical (Shijiazhuang) Co., Ltd.) and metronidazole (0.3 g taken thrice daily, Grand Pharmaceutical (China) Co., Ltd).

Follow-up

Follow-up was conducted on the second and seventh days postoperatively. Patients with confirmed SP were required to return for an additional follow-up visit 4 weeks postoperatively. Postoperative CBCT was performed in cases where patients reported nasal obstruction or when maxillary sinusitis was suspected.

SP detection

SP was confirmed when any of the following conditions were observed: visible oral bubbles in the extraction socket during the nose-blowing test, direct visualization of the perforation through the socket, or nasal bleeding within 24 h postoperatively. Iatrogenic perforation was defined as cases where surgical instruments, such as dental elevators or high-speed handpieces, directly entered the sinus cavity during the procedure.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.). Chi-square test was used to evaluate the correlation between each classification category and SP. Besides, logistic regression analysis was used to further analyze the risk factors for intraoperative SP. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 141 UTMs from 120 patients were included in the study, comprising 84 females and 36 males. Of the included UTMs, 107 were fully impacted, and 113 exhibited root protrusion into the maxillary sinus. The mean patient age was 27.7 ± 6.3 years (range: 18–46 years). Abnormal sinus membrane adjacent to the roots was observed in 14 cases, all of which had unobstructed maxillary sinus openings.

SP occurred in 24 cases, with 22 cases identified intraoperatively through direct visualization or the nose-blowing test, and 2 cases diagnosed postoperatively due to nasal bleeding. Among the 24 SP cases, 7 were associated with dental elevators protruding into the sinus during tooth luxation. All 7 of these teeth were mesially inclined UTMs. In 3 of these cases, the UTMs were removed through tooth sectioning, while in 4 cases, they were extracted buccally using forceps. Furthermore, 16 SP were detected in fully impacted UTMs, and 8 SP were detected in partially erupted UTMs.

Flap elevation was performed in all surgical procedures. In 10 cases, tooth sectioning was necessary for UTM extraction, while the remaining UTMs were removed using a dental elevator directly with bone removal if necessary.

Postoperative wound healing was uneventful in cases involving fully impacted UTMs, where primary wound closure was achieved. Discussion

This study investigated impacted UTMs located in close proximity to the maxillary sinus, and proposed a new classification system for SP evaluation based on CBCT. Generally, periapical films or panoramic radiographs are the most common imaging modalities for impacted UTMs. However, two-dimensional imaging failed to display the positional relationship between the root and the adjacent sinus floor accurately. Therefore, three-dimensional imaging is recommended when root protrusion into the sinus floor is suspected. Due to its lower radiation dose, CBCT has been selected by most researchers, including in our department. We suggest that it should be utilized in higher-risk conditions as indicated in the present research.The results demonstrated that SP was more frequently observed in mesially impacted UTMS and when a significant portion of the root tip protruded into the sinus cavity, as assessed by preoperative CBCT. Additionally, mesially inclined UTMs were associated with a higher risk of iatrogenic SP. The majority of SPs were successfully managed using a trapezoid flap, highlighting that proper surgical technique can effectively address this complication. These findings provided valuable insights into the risk assessment and prognosis of UTM extractions performed near the sinus floor.

SP is a well-documented complication of UTM extraction, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0.8% to 13% in the literature [1–5]. A multicenter retrospective study involving various UTM types found SP in 46 out of 3,259 extractions [1]. In contrast, Lim et al. reported a much lower SP incidence of 0.8%, noting that most cases involved roots located less than 2 mm from the sinus floor, as seen on panoramic radiographs [5]. This difference in SP rates between our study and previous reports likely stems from variations in inclusion criteria. In our study, all UTMs had impacted roots with a discontinuous cortical sinus floor and required flap elevation, factors that may have contributed to the higher observed SP rate. Rothamel et al. reported an SP incidence of up to 13% in a multicenter prospective study focusing on surgically extracted UTMs, further supporting the notion that complex cases pose a greater risk [3].

Several risk factors for SP have been identified, including the type and depth of impaction, proximity of the UTM root to the sinus floor, number of roots, and the degree of root development [1–5]. Rothamel et al. reported that a higher degree of impaction was a significant risk factor, with an SP incidence of 24% in completely impacted teeth [3]. Similarly, Iwata et al. demonstrated through multivariate analysis that teeth classified as type D according to Archer’s classification presented a higher SP risk, particularly when the root protruded into the sinus [1]. Lim et al. also found that SP occurred only when the shortest vertical distance between the tooth and the sinus floor was less than 2 mm [5]. Consistent with previous studies, we observed that SP rates increased significantly when more than half of the root protruded into the sinus cavity. This finding indicated the importance of exercising particular caution during extraction in such cases. However, we did not find a clear correlation between impaction depth and the SP risk. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors. First, the need for extraction of type C and D impactions is relatively rare, meaning that most of the UTMs in our study were type B. Second, the smaller sample size for higher-degree impactions limited further analysis. Lastly, and most importantly, the depth of impaction does not accurately reflect the spatial relationship between the UTM and the sinus floor.

Lim et al. also reported a strong association between tooth inclination and SP risk, with mesially inclined UTMs showing a higher likelihood of perforation [5]. Similarly, we also observed an elevated SP rate in mesially inclined UTMs. We hypothesize that mesially inclined UTMs require a larger rotational force during extraction, which increases pressure on the sinus membrane. All cases of iatrogenic SP in our study involved mesially inclined UTMs. Insufficient distal bone removal may lead to SP during tooth rotation, as the thinner mesial bone adjacent to the sinus can serve as a fulcrum, resulting in sinus floor fractures when force is applied with an elevator. Therefore, adequate bone removal and tooth sectioning are essential to minimize SP risk, particularly for mesially inclined UTMs.

Although tooth sectioning is commonly used for lower third molar surgeries, sectioning UTMs remains challenging due to the limited intermaxillary space and the presence of hypertrophic buccal tissue [7, 8]. As a result, the most frequently used technique for UTM extraction involves adequate bone removal followed by tooth luxation using elevators to achieve distal or buccal rotation [3, 9]. The tuberosity bone is typically porous and can be easily removed [10–12]. However, the pterygoid lamina, which lies adjacent to the distal surface of the tuberosity base, serves as the attachment site for the pterygoid muscles [13, 14]. To prevent serious complications, we avoided bone removal in this region [15–17]. Consequently, a higher proportion of tooth sectioning was observed in cases where the UTMs were impacted superior to the inferior border of the PMF (Table 1).

Despite Ward’s flap being commonly used in UTM surgery, it is not suitable for teeth with a higher risk of SP [18]. Firstly, the distal part of the incision is challenging to suture due to poor intraoperative visibility. Secondly, the flap’s limited base may negatively impact its blood supply. Most importantly, the distal incision directly beneath the SP site increases the risk of postoperative oral-antral fistula. In contrast, a modified flap with longitudinal incisions at the mesiobuccal and distopalatal regions, combined with a sulcus incision, ensures adequate blood supply and effectively overlays the perforation. Thus, although the incidence of intraoperative SP was relatively high, postoperative oral-antral fistulas were not observed in most cases with reliable primary wound closure, unlike findings in previous studies [19]. We emphasize that achieving a primary closed suture is crucial in preventing postoperative complications when SP occurs intraoperatively.

While the role of antibiotic prophylaxis in lower third molar surgery is well-documented, the optimal strategy for UTM extraction remains unclear [20–25]. Compared to lower third molar surgery, UTM extraction is less traumatic due to reduced cortical bone resistance. However, deeply impacted UTMs, often located near the maxillary sinus and infratemporal space, pose a greater risk of postoperative infection, complicating treatment and increasing patient discomfort [26]. Consequently, antibiotic prophylaxis was administered to all patients. Amoxicillin, known for its safety and broad-spectrum activity, is commonly prescribed in third molar surgeries [21–23, 25]. It can be given preoperatively, postoperatively, or both [21, 23, 25]. According to a recent review by Camps-Font et al., postoperative amoxicillin administration appears to be the most effective approach to reduce surgical site infections [25]. In our study, we prescribed amoxicillin and metronidazole for 3 postoperative days, extending to 7 days in cases of SP. This protocol resulted in a low rate of sinusitis and no surgical site infections.

Preexisting pathology in the sinus floor has been identified as a risk factor for post-extraction sinusitis, particularly when combined with intraoperative SP [27–29]. However, none of the cases with SP and abnormal sinus membranes (5 sides in total) in our study progressed to sinusitis, likely due to perioperative antibiotic use. Given the limited sample size, the correlation between preexisting sinus pathology and SP, or between SP and post-extraction sinusitis, could not be definitively established. Nevertheless, our findings suggested that prophylactic antibiotics are effective and should be considered for UTMs in close proximity to the sinus floor, particularly when SP is suspected.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations of this study, our findings indicated that mesial impaction and the type Ⅱc protrusion were the primary risk factor for SP during higher-risk UTM extractions. Mesially inclined UTMs were also associated with a higher likelihood of iatrogenic SP, indicating the need for careful surgical planning in such cases.
