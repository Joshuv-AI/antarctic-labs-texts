A qualitative assessment of insomnia in recovering alcohol‐dependent patients
Authors: Olena Zhabenko, Igor V Linskiy, Oleksandr I Minko, Valeriy N Kuzminov, Meike Gmeinwieser, Lars P Kiefer, Deirdre A Conroy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsomnia is a common symptom that may interfere with the efforts of alcohol‐dependent patients to initiate and maintain sobriety. This study investigates the experiences of recovering alcohol‐dependent patients with insomnia as well as preferences for treatment strategies. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 27 patients in Ukraine and Germany. Most of the patients experienced negative psychological (n = 21), physiological (n = 14) effects and negative daytime consequences during insomnia. All 27 patients were engaged in nonpharmacological approaches to insomnia and 52% used pharmacological approaches to manage insomnia. This study provides insight for understanding how alcohol‐dependent patients in recovery perceive insomnia.Keywords: alcohol consumption, alcoholism, qualitative study, recovery, sleep problems

INTRODUCTION

The diagnostic guideline for alcohol dependence syndrome according to the ICD‐10 consists of at least three of six following criteria within the past year: craving, difficulties in controlling its use, persisting in its use despite harmful consequences, a higher priority given to alcohol use compared to other activities, increased tolerance, and a physical withdrawal state. 1 Insomnia is described by difficulties initiating, maintaining sleep and is related with significant distress or daytime impairments. The diagnosis is based on the presence of these symptoms during at least three nights per week, for at least 3 months. 2

The range of insomnia among alcohol‐dependent (AD) individuals is from 36% to 91%. 3 There is a growing biomedical research literature on sleep, substance use, and recovery, which find sleep quality to be associated with relapse and recovery. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Treatment of insomnia is important because of its role in relapse. 5 , 9

This study complements biomedical studies that explore the biological processes of sleep among AD patients which have documented a high prevalence of sleeping difficulties. The reported study contributes to an emerging literature on the patient's view, opinions, and feedback on the insomnia and its management among AD patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design and procedures

The qualitative study designed applied open‐ended, semi‐structured interviews with the patients suffering from alcohol‐dependence and insomnia. These in‐depth interviews applied an interview guide that included 16 prompts and was created by the study team based on the clinical review of qualitative studies of insomnia. 10 Specifically, the study examined experience of insomnia among alcohol‐dependent patients, its daytime consequences, interaction with healthcare professionals, (self)‐treatment coping strategies and relationship between insomnia and relapse. All interviews were digitally recorded and manually transcribed by the study team.

Subjects

The primary selection criterion for the study was a diagnosis of alcohol dependence and insomnia, which was determined by using ICD‐10 without regard for other diagnoses. Other selection criteria included psychiatric stability (not acutely psychotic, suicidal, or danger to others), at least 3 weeks of sobriety, 18–80 years old and ability to provide consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria for recruitment are: a significant medical and psychiatric illness, including psychosis, current severe depressive episode, heart disease, acute coronary syndrome, severe liver disease, seizure disorder (except seizures related to alcohol withdrawal), degenerative or demyelinating central nervous system disease, stroke, transient ischemic attacks, or recent loss of consciousness due to head trauma.

There were three centers from which participants were recruited: Kharkiv Regional Clinical Psychiatric Hospital N3 in Ukraine, neurological rehabilitation Clinic, Kliniken Schmieder in Gailingen and the outpatient addiction department (Fachstelle Sucht) in Singen, both in Germany.

We screened a total of 17 patients in December 2019 (before Coronavirus SARS‐CoV‐2 Pandemic and before Russia launched a large‐scale invasion on February 24, 2022) in Ukraine and 24 patients from May 2021 to September 2022 in Germany. The interview questions were pre‐tested for semantic and attitudinal feedback by two individuals in Germany, who were not included in the analysis. Five patients declined to participate in the study and seven did not meet the criteria for the study. Twenty‐seven patients selected for further interviews.

Assessment

To assess insomnia, participants completed the Sleep Problems Questionnaire (SPQ). 11 The four items were summed with total possible scores ranging from 0 to 20 with higher score indicating greater insomnia.

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS‐21), a 21‐item self‐administered scale which assesses the negative emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress. 12

Analysis

Thematic analysis was used, and codes and themes were developed iteratively as data were collected. This process included familiarization with the data, generating initial descriptive codes, and searching for underlying themes, reflecting deeper notions and conceptualization. 13 The first author developed initial codes and then together with the last author grouped the codes into similar concepts and categories for potential themes. Interviewing continued until we deemed data saturation to have been reached which was defined as the point at which no new themes were emerging.

Daytime consequences of insomnia among AD patients

Daytime consequences of insomnia were categorized according to the International classification of sleep disorders 14 (Table 3).

Patients describe fatigue. “Well, such a light fatigue. When a person doesn't get enough sleep, you know yourself, as might say a little bit ‘delayed’. I want to fall asleep for a couple of hours, I got it wrong days and nights, and then I get involved and everything is fine” (Ukrainian, Male, 36 years). Another participant experienced fatigue during the whole day: “Yes, such fatigue all the day and no strength” (Ukrainian, Male, 35 years). “I can't get a full life with insomnia, because I am so quickly tired, I can have a headache and fatigue in 4 hours after waking up, and who needs such an employee? When I study I can't get a full knowledge” (Ukrainian, Male, 21 years).

Interaction with health professionals due to insomnia

The majority of participants received information about insomnia from medical practitioners (n = 12), for example, physician, family doctor, psychiatrist, neurologist, psychologist, occupational therapist, psychiatric institute outpatient clinic. “I told a psychiatrist twice about my sleep problems and he then said ok, take the medication and then I often have the problem that the dose does help only a while and then I had to increase it” (German, Female, 38 years). “I [discuss this] with my colleagues or with my occupational therapist, she gave me a few good tips. And otherwise with my family doctor” (German, Male, 53 years). “Actually only with my psychologist” (German, Male, 45 years). Others received information from the pharmacy, Internet, TV, relatives, coworkers, friends, or did not receive any information at all.

(Self)‐treatment of insomnia among AD patients

More than half of the sample (n = 14) reported taking medication as the treatment option of insomnia. Most commonly used medications included: antihistamine (Doxylamine, Dominal), atypical antipsychotics (Clozapine, Quetiapine, Olanzapine), benzodiazepine (Diazepam), supplement (Melatonin), or antidepressant (Trazodone). At the time of the study (December 2019), in Ukraine, many medicines could be purchased without a prescription. In particular, the patients we examined could purchase Gidazepam (an atypical benzodiazepine derivative developed in the Soviet Union) as well as Barboval and Corvalol (combination drugs containing phenobarbital) without a prescription. All of these preparations were in tablet or liquid form.

The non‐pharmacological approaches were characterized by inadequate sleep hygiene such as, watching TV, eating at night, drinking (e.g., alcohol, water, milk, herbal or sleep tea), exercise, smoking (nicotine, cannabis), relaxation techniques (self‐hypnosis, meditation, autogenic training, progressive muscle relaxation) etc. “I turn on the TV and watch TV. Then I fall asleep while watching TV… Or watch an interesting movie… Action, melodramas, mystics” (Ukrainian, Male, 21 years). “Basically I watch TV… then only in the morning I can fall asleep” (Ukrainian, Male, 36 years). “I like watching movies, action movies or something like this… and for me the TV is a sleeping pill” (German, Male, 60 years).

How insomnia and alcohol interact

The majority of the participants had reported either negative effects of alcohol on sleep (n = 8) or reported a bidirectional relationship (n = 6) (Table 3). “Well I think alcohol negatively affects sleep… so, you woke up with a hangover in the morning and you feel bad. Well, if you wouldn't drink, you would sleep further, wake up, adequately get up, wash up, and go to work” (Ukrainian, Male, 21 years). “Yes, if a person suffers from insomnia, the cause is alcohol, then it is necessary to solve the problem with alcohol, then immediately treat insomnia…” (Ukrainian, Male, 56 years).

DISCUSSION

This is the first qualitative study explored the experience of insomnia among a mostly male alcohol‐dependent sample in recovery living in Ukraine and Germany. This research provides an insight into how alcohol‐dependent patients in recovery perceive insomnia, how they engage in (self)‐treatment, and what they understand about the relationship between insomnia and relapse.

Interviews and brief assessments show that insomnia is a problem during recovery from alcohol dependence. The average SPQ score was 9 (interquartile range: 8–15). This score is consistent with the mean SPQ score of 9.2 ± 5.6 at baseline of a 2.5‐year longitudinal survey of AD individuals in the USA. 15 Many of the patients were experiencing negative psychological experiences, which has been well established in other samples of alcohol use disorder patients with insomnia. 6 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 This experience of anxiety and depression is important to monitor during this time, as previous research showed that both quantity of drinking and depression predicted insomnia severity, controlling for time, age, and gender. Drinking also predicted depressive symptoms, and its effect on insomnia was mediated by depression severity. 15

This study showed that a variety of self‐treatment options were used by patients with insomnia and AD. The majority of participants were not aware of other helpful treatments that have been used in patients with insomnia and AD such as progressive relaxation therapy, cognitive‐behavioral therapy for insomnia, acamprosate, agomelatine, etanercept, carbamazepine, gabapentin, naltrexone, ramelteon, topiramate, trazodone, or quetiapine. 20 , 21 The majority of participants received information about insomnia from medical practitioners.
