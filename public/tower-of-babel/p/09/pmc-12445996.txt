Correlation of hindgut microbiome and fermentation properties with a history of gas and/or impaction colic in Japanese draft horses
Authors: Rintaro YANO, Tomoe MORIYAMA, Hisao ARAI, Andrew J. SCHEFTGEN, Garret SUEN, Takehiro NISHIDA, Masaaki HANDA, Naoki FUKUMA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTColic, a major gastrointestinal disease in horses, has a high recurrence rate and can lead to surgery or fatal outcomes, highlighting the need for effective prevention measures. Disruption of the microbiome is a multifaceted problem and can occur from a variety of factors, such as high-concentrate diets, which can then potentially cause colic. However, individual variation in the incidence of colic can occur when under identical management practices. The aim of this study was to characterize the fecal bacterial composition of Japanese draft horses with and without a history of colic in the past two years under identical feeding conditions to determine if specific bacterial taxa are associated with either phenotype. A fecal bacterial community analysis was performed via sequencing of the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. The fecal lactate concentration was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography. Our results show a significant decrease in microbial evenness in horses with a history of colic along with an increase in Streptococcus. The fecal lactate concentration was higher in the colic group compared with the non-colic group, which may be attributed to the higher abundance of Streptococcus. Horses without a history of colic were characterized by amplicon sequence variants belonging to bacteria associated with fiber degradation, including Rikenellaceae RC9, Kiritimatiellae, and Clostridium. Overall, our results align with previous studies on equine colic epidemiology and suggest that the bacterial microbiome composition, independent of diet, may be related to the recurrence of colic.

Herbivores harbor a diverse microbial community in their gastrointestinal tracts and rely on these symbiotic microbes for the digestion of feed. This microbial community is capable of metabolizing ingested plant fibers and converting them into short-chain fatty acids (SFCAs) that the host can then utilize [4, 25, 30]. While the majority of investigations into herbivore microbiomes focus on ruminants, there is renewed interest in monogastric herbivores, such as horses, as microbial compositions vary by species [46]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome has been linked to the development of gastrointestinal diseases in humans [19], and thus characterization of both healthy and sick gut microbiomes is imperative for the treatment and maintenance of health in livestock animals.

One example of such gastrointestinal diseases is colic, a general term that describes abdominal pain caused by complications in the gastrointestinal tract [2]. These complications are the result of a multitude of factors, including gas accumulation (gas colic), obstruction of ingesta (impaction colic), or mechanical twisting of the intestines (strangulation colic) [17]. Colic can lead to severe discomfort and behavioral changes and, in some cases, require surgery or euthanasia [42]. Importantly, colic is widely recognized as one of the most serious conditions in equine husbandry [41], with recurrence within the same animal representing one of the most expensive health costs. As a result, colic is known for its high recurrence rate, with a cohort study conducted in the United States, which included Thoroughbred, Warmblood, Standardbred, and other breeds, reporting 35 recurrences per 100 horses within one year after the initial episode [41]. Given the substantial economic losses [49], there is a need for developing effective preventive measures.

Previously, it has been reported that horses presenting with colic exhibit microbial disturbances compared with those without colic [45]. Dietary factors, such as overfeeding of high-concentrate diets and abrupt dietary changes, are thought to cause such microbial changes and have been identified as important risk factors for developing colic [12, 48]. Thus, maintaining a stable hindgut microbiome by implementing optimal feed management is important for the prevention of equine colic. However, even under identical feeding practices, there is considerable individual variation in the incidence of colic, and the underlying factors contributing to these differences remain unclear. Moreover, in high-performance racehorses, feeding high-concentrate diets is necessary to meet their energy requirements [37], which further complicates the development of effective prevention strategies. For instance, the Japanese draft horse used in Ban’ei racing (Hokkaido, Japan) consumes more than 10 kg of high-concentrate diet per day [51]. Previously, we reported that this feeding practice negatively affects the fecal microbial community and fermentation characteristics of these horses [51]. However, no study has been conducted on the relationship between microbial community structure and the incidence of colic in these Japanese draft horses.

To address this, we examined the differences in the composition of the fecal bacterial community in cohorts of Japanese draft horses with and without a history of colic. Only horses diagnosed with gas and impaction colic were included, as the focus of this study was to determine the relationship between microbial fermentation abnormalities and the recurrence of colic. This exploratory study aimed to determine if specific bacterial community members in horses with a history of colic could be identified and serve as biomarkers useful for identification of future episodes of colic.

Materials and Methods

Animal selection and sample collection

Experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine. The horses were fed 8.4 kg of oats, 5.2 kg of bran, and 3.2 kg of molasses-coated oats as part of a concentrate diet, along with 5.6 kg of alfalfa hay and 3.6 kg of timothy hay as roughage per day, resulting in the concentrate diet accounting for 65% of the total feed on an as-fed basis. The chemical composition of the feed was as follows: 43.1% neutral detergent fiber, 23.7% acid detergent fiber, 38.9% non-fiber carbohydrate, 23.9% starch, 13.3% crude protein, 2.8% ether extract, and 5.2% ash on an as-dry matter basis, as previously described in Yano et al. [51]. Based on clinical records collected from the Tokachi Ban’ei Clinic, the 10 horses were divided into two groups: a group with a medical history of colic in the past two years (colic, n=5) and another without a history of colic in the past two years (non-colic, n=5). All horses were considered clinically healthy, without any gastrointestinal disease for at least two months before fecal sampling. Horses diagnosed only with gas and impaction colic were included, as the focus of this study was on colic related to fermentation abnormalities in the hindgut. The period from the last colic episode to sampling varied among individuals, ranging from approximately 1 to 7 months. Fresh fecal samples were collected from the stall floor of each individual horse. The sampling was conducted at the same time point for all horses, with feces collected after the second feeding (horses were fed four times a day). The samples were then stored at –20°C until DNA extraction and measurements of pH and lactate concentration could be performed.

Bacterial DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Fecal bacterial DNA extraction was carried out following the method reported by Yano et al. [51]. Briefly, bacterial DNA from the lysates of the fecal samples was extracted using the repeated bead beating plus column (RBB+C) method [52] and purified using a QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Bacterial community sequencing was performed via amplification of the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene using universal bacterial primers (341F-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGA CAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG; 805R-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAG ACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC) as previously described [1]. Subsequently, index PCR, Illumina sequencing adapters, and dual-index barcodes were added to the amplicons using a Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The concentration of the PCR products was measured using a QuantusTM Fluorometer and the QuantiFluor® DNA System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA), and the final products were stored in equimolar concentrations. The resulting samples were then sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq, using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 600 Cycle PE (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Sequence analysis

The resulting bacterial sequences were processed with QIIME 2 v2023.9 [6]. Demultiplexed raw sequences were imported using the Casava 1.8 format and denoised using DADA2 (via qiime-dada2 plugin) [7] to generate a feature table containing amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Bacterial taxonomy was classified using the classify‐sklearn naive Bayes taxonomy classifier [5] against the SILVA (v138.1) 16S rRNA gene database [36]. Non-bacterial ASVs were removed, and sequences were rarefied to a depth of 22,680 reads per sample for diversity analysis. Alpha diversity metrics, including observed ASVs, Chao1 [8], Shannon’s index [43], Pielou’s evenness index [35], and Faith’s PD [16], as well as unweighted and weighted UniFrac [29] distance metrics for beta diversity, were calculated. Differential abundance analysis for ASVs was performed using an analysis of the compositions of microbiomes with bias correction (ANCOM-BC) [26]. Sequences were assessed using NCBI BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to estimate the closely related bacterial species of the differentially abundant ASVs determined by ANCOM-BC.

Measurement of pH and lactate concentration

Measurements of the fecal pH and lactate concentration were conducted as previously described [51] with slight modifications. Briefly, approximately 1 g of each fecal sample was aliquoted into a 5.0 ml sterilized tube (Bio-Bik, model number: ST-500, INA OPTIKA Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and diluted threefold with sterile distilled water. The mixture was then vortexed at room temperature for 1 minute and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µm filter, and the filtrate was subjected to high-performance liquid chromatography (SIL-20AC, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) for lactate concentration measurement. The pH of the remaining supernatant was measured twice using a pH meter (D-210P, Horiba, Kyoto, Japan), and the average pH value was calculated.

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare age, body weight, alpha diversity, pH, and the lactate concentration between the groups using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differentially abundant ASVs were identified using the ANCOM-BC plugin in QIIME 2, with a prevalence threshold ≥90% across all samples. Group (colic vs. non-colic), age_type (young, ≤4 years old, vs. mature, ≥5 years old), and sex (male vs. female) were incorporated as factors. ASVs were considered as bacterial biomarkers of a medical history of colic if they exhibited a log fold-change (log-FC) ≥1 with a Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected P-value <0.05 for the group factor, with no significant differences in age_type, sex, or both. The results of ANCOM-BC were visualized using RStudio (version 4.1.1) using the following packages: dplyr, ggplot2, ggtext, and readxl. The unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance metrics were compared using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) in QIIME 2. A principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plot was visualized using RStudio (version 4.1.1) with the following packages to evaluate the differences in microbial composition between the groups: ggplot2, dplyr, readr, and qiime2R. A stacked bar plot of the relative abundances of fecal bacterial communities at the family level was generated using RStudio (version 4.1.1) with the following packages to confirm the overall composition between individuals: ggplot2, dplyr, readxl, and tidyr. The obtained data, except for differentially abundant ASVs and the PCoA, are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Here we investigated the composition of the fecal bacterial community of Japanese draft horses with and without a history of colic to identify microbial members that may play a role in the recurrence of gas or impaction colic. We found that horses with a history of colic had lower abundances of fibrolytic bacteria, an increase in Streptococcus, and higher concentrations of lactate. Due to the horses being fed the same diet, these findings suggest that bacterial composition is a factor in the development and recurrence of colic.

Previously, Stewart et al. [45] reported that fecal bacterial diversity was lower in Thoroughbred, Warmblood, and other breeds of horses with colic compared with elective surgical horses (the aforementioned breeds and Standardbred). We found that Pielou’s evenness index was significantly lower in the colic group, but no significant differences were found with other measures of alpha diversity (Table 2). Similarly, beta diversity was significantly different when measured by weighted UniFrac but not by unweighted UniFrac (Fig. 2). While Stewart et al. [45] found that horses with colic had fewer bacterial species, the differences may be attributed to variation in feeding management, breed, and purpose of use among the horse populations studied. To address this, our study controlled for the dietary design, breed, and purpose of use. Although our comparison is based on a medical history of colic, when taken together with the findings of Stewart et al. [45], we posit that lower bacterial diversity in the equine gut may be an important factor in the development of colic.

Of the taxa we identified as differentially abundant, Streptococcus had the greatest log fold-change in the colic group. Streptococcus is a well-known amylolytic bacteria and lactic acid producer in the digestive tracts of herbivores [11, 39]. We previously reported that Streptococcus spp. are key agents of lactate accumulation, which can lead to a decline in pH and a reduction in fibrolytic bacteria, thereby resulting in lower microbial diversity in the hindgut [51]. In this study, we also observed that the colic group had a higher fecal lactate concentration and a decreased evenness, which may be attributed to the higher abundance of Streptococcus.

We note that Streptococcus has also been reported in the feces of horses presenting with colic [45] and has been associated with various gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., rumen acidosis [33], bloat [9], and simple colonic obstruction and distension (SCOD) [13]). Additionally, the accumulation of lactic acid in the gastrointestinal tract has been reported to be associated with acidosis and subsequent pathological conditions [13, 24, 28]. Therefore, horses in the colic group may have a higher potential risk of acidosis compared with those in the non-colic group. These findings highlight the significant role of Streptococcus spp. in the development of colic and its potential adverse effect on gastrointestinal health.

We also found that the non-colic group was characterized by multiple ASVs assigned to bacterial genera thought to be involved in fiber degradation, including Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, the NK4A214_group within Oscillospiraceae, and WCHB1-41 within the Kiritimatiellae. Previous studies have also reported these bacterial genera in the feces of horses on grazing or hay-based diets [50, 53]. Moreover, previous metagenomic analyses of the yak fecal microbiome [21] and moose rumen microbiome [47] revealed that metagenome-assembled genomes belonging to the Kiritimatiellae and Rikenellaceae RC9 encode for cellulases and hemicellulases. We noted that Clostridium butyricum, a closely related species of the ASV assigned to Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, can utilize galactomannan, pectin, and xylan [14, 34]. In contrast, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 was differentially abundant in the colic group and has previously been identified in the digestive tract when host animals were managed under fiber-rich conditions [18, 23, 27]. However, in our study, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 was the only fibrolytic bacteria differentially abundant in the colic horses, whereas in the non-colic group, there were 10 differentially abundant fibrolytic bacteria (Fig. 3). This suggests that the fibrolytic capacity of horses in the non-colic group is likely higher than that of the colic group. WCHB1-41 is recognized as one of the core bacterial groups in equine feces [15] and has previously been correlated with increased diversity [31]. Here, WCHB1-41 accounted for more than half of the ASVs of the non-colic group, suggesting that this bacterial group may contribute to equine gastrointestinal health. Our results suggest that a diverse gut microbiome with an abundance of fibrolytic species may reduce the likelihood of developing colic in horses.

An epidemiological survey and review on equine colic suggests that poor quality hay with low digestibility predisposes horses to colonic impaction, which is a contributing factor to the development of colic [10, 20]. Additionally, horses with colonic obstruction and distention that were maintained on concentrate feed rich in sugar and starch are known to have a decrease in the abundance of fibrolytic bacteria in their intestinal microbiomes [13]. By controlling for feed quality, our study found a potential association between the bacterial microbiome and a history of colic. The characteristics we observed in the colic group, including uneven bacterial species distribution, increased acidosis risk, and reduced fibrolytic bacteria, align with our current understanding of the impact of the microbiome on colic. Indeed, fiber-degrading bacteria are generally sensitive to acidic conditions [32, 38, 40], but it is beyond the scope of this study to determine if the decreases in fibrolytic bacteria we observed are the result of an increased abundance of lactate-producing bacteria such as Streptococcus or due to some other dysbiotic process. It is important to note that intestinal microbiome composition is impacted by the host’s diet, genetic background [22], antibiotic history [3], and management conditions. Apart from the intestinal microbiome, the horse’s age may be related to the likelihood of experiencing colic. The non-colic group tended to be younger than the colic group (Table 1), suggesting that their shorter durations of management as racehorses—typically associated with a high-concentrate diet—may have reduced their risks of developing colic. For instance, Non-colic_01 (3 years old), one of the youngest horses in this study, had a relatively high abundance of Streptococcaceae (5.166%) (Fig. 1), resembling the trend seen in the colic group. However, Non-colic_05 was the oldest horse among all the horses (10 years old). This data indicates that age may be a potential co-factor along with bacterial microbiome composition in the development of colic. Therefore, further long-term studies are warranted to elucidate whether microbial characteristics differ between groups before and after colic onset. Studies of this design may clarify both the onset and high recurrence rate of colic in horses.

Conclusion

Here, we provided insights into the microbial characteristics of Japanese draft horses both with and without a history of colic. Our findings suggest that horses that have experienced colic possess a microbiome associated with a higher risk of hindgut acidosis and colonic impaction, predisposing them to future bouts of colic. Therefore, understanding the functionality of the host’s hindgut microbiome would be essential for elucidating the causes of colic onset and the individual differences that arise under the same dietary conditions. Due to the low resolution of the taxonomic identities of the ASVs classified in this study, more functional analyses, such as shotgun metagenomics, would be necessary in the future to compare the fiber degradation capacity and other factors in great detail.
