The influence of larval temperature and nutrients on dog heartworm (Dirofilaria immitis (Spirurida: Onchocercidae)) transmission in Aedes albopictus (Diptera: Culicidae) and Aedes triseriatus (Diptera: Culicidae)
Authors: Kaylin Lewandowski, Skylar R Hopkins, Michael Reiskind, Bethany McGregor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe filarial nematode, Dirofilaria immitis (Leidy) (Spirurida: Onchocercidae), is an obligate mosquito-borne pathogen transmitted by at least 26 species of mosquitoes in the United States. Due to its size and migration through mosquito tissues during its first 3 larval stages (L1-L3), it can damage the mosquito to the point of death, thereby hindering transmission to its vertebrate host (eg canids and other carnivores). Mosquito species vary in competency for D. immitis, where some species facilitate the development of more viable, infectious L3 worms (host tolerance), while others produce few to no worms (host resistance). Here, we investigated how resistance and tolerance may be impacted by larval rearing conditions (ie temperature and nutrition), specifically quantifying D. immitis burden and development in 2 mosquito species (Aedes triseriatus (Say) and Aedes albopictus (Skuse)). Post-emergence, we offered either a D. immitis positive or negative blood meal to adult female mosquitoes and tracked their development for 15 days before quantifying worm burdens. For both mosquito species, growth rates increased and adult size decreased under conditions with warmer temperatures and lower nutrition. Larval conditions did not have consistent effects on the burden or development of D. immitis, but a higher proportion of Ae. triseriatus developed L3 worms than Ae. albopictus. Poor survival to the end of the incubation period and low numbers of blood-fed mosquitoes for each treatment combination may have hindered our ability to detect significant effects of larval environment on worm transmission, highlighting important considerations for future studies.Keywords: vector competence, immune response, mosquitoes, Culicidae, resistance, vector-borne pathogens animal

Introduction

Vector competence is the ability of an arthropod vector to transmit a pathogen, which is determined by a complex interplay between the vector, the pathogen, and the environment. Understanding how vector–pathogen–environment contexts shape transmission is critical for accurately predicting and effectively controlling vector-borne diseases (Randolph and Rogers 2010, Fouque and Reeder 2019, Brass et al. 2024). Correspondingly, substantial research effort has been dedicated to understanding how environmental conditions affect mosquito-borne transmission of microparasites, such as viruses (Alto et al. 2005, 2008, Alto and Lounibos 2013, Buckner et al. 2016, Evans et al. 2018, Herd et al. 2021). In contrast, relatively little research effort has considered how environmental conditions affect mosquito vector competence for macroparasites, like Dirofilaria immitis (Leidy, 1856), the causative agent of dog heartworm (Breaux et al. 2014). Because filarial worms and other macroparasites have large impacts on adult mosquito fitness, vector competence may be even more sensitive to environmental conditions that influence mosquito physiology, growth, and longevity.

Mosquitoes survive filarial worm infection by either resisting them through immune responses or tolerating the infection (Dharmarajan et al. 2019). Resistance to filarial worms can be via anatomical structures (eg midgut escape barrier, cibarial armatures) or physiological processes (McGreevy et al. 1978, Christensen 1981, Lai et al. 2000, Simón et al. 2012, Bartholomay 2014, Dharmarajan et al. 2019). For example, Aedes albopictus (Skuse, 1894) is suspected to readily melanize D. immitis, especially when high doses of microfilariae are ingested (Lai et al. 2000, Dharmarajan et al. 2019). Resistance to infection may decrease the likelihood of worm transmission by killing worms before transmission. On the other hand, tolerant vectors spend their energy to repairing damaged tissues without having direct effects on the pathogen (Raberg et al. 2009, Moller-Jacobs et al. 2014), such that the worms develop freely and may be more likely to be transmitted (Dharmarajan et al. 2019). Resistance versus tolerance for a given pathogen is both an intrinsic property of a species and a plastic trait known to be dependent upon environmental conditions experienced during immature development (Grimstad and Walker 1991, Reiskind and Lounibos 2009, Padmanabha et al. 2011, Reiskind and Zarrabi 2012, Alto and Lounibos 2013, Li et al. 2014, Moller-Jacobs et al. 2014, Chandrasegaran et al. 2020).

In this study, we considered plasticity related to 1 abiotic condition and 1 biotic condition that are important for larval mosquito development: temperature and nutrient availability (Padmanabha et al. 2011, Araújo et al. 2012, Reiskind and Zarrabi 2012, Christiansen-Jucht et al. 2015, Barreaux et al. 2016, Carvajal-Lago et al. 2021). Increasing nutrient availability supports greater immune responses and larger adult sizes (Murdock et al. 2012, Chandrasegaran et al. 2020), whereas increasing temperature has unresolved effects on immune responses and results in smaller adult mosquitoes (Kingsolver and Huey 2008, Murdock et al. 2012, Reiskind and Zarrabi 2012, MacKay et al. 2023). Furthermore, temperature and nutrients can interact (Reiskind and Zarrabi 2012, Moller-Jacobs et al. 2014). As adult mosquito size increases, the ratio of mosquito size to worm size increases, because worms are consistently between 250 μm at the microfilariae stage and 1 mm at the L3 stage (Simón et al. 2012, Bartholomay 2014). Therefore, larval environmental conditions that produce larger mosquitoes might allow mosquitoes to sustain less worm damage and/or allocate more energy resources to the appropriate immune responses (Murdock et al. 2012, Lang et al. 2018, MacKay et al. 2023). Additionally, MacKay et al. (2023) found that, under varying temperature and larval diet conditions, pupal weight, wing length, and longevity can vary in adult mosquitoes. Longevity of the adult is critical for the life cycle of D. immitis as the extrinsic incubation period (EIP) is 10 days when infected mosquitoes are held at 26 °C (Ledesma and Harrington 2015). Therefore, the lack of energy reserves and/or immune responses results in a poor vector simply due to the lack of survival through the extrinsic incubation period.

To explore this possibility, we studied 2 co-occurring mosquito species that are hypothesized to differ in their defense mechanisms against D. immitis infection. Aedes albopictus is thought to be resistant (Lai et al. 2000, Dharmarajan et al. 2019), and Aedes triseriatus (Say, 1823) is thought to be tolerant (Spence Beaulieu and Reiskind 2020). We addressed 3 primary research questions relating to the transmission of D. immitis: (1) does resistance-based melanization occur more in Ae. albopictus than Ae. triseriatus?; (2) how does mosquito body mass and wing length change in relation to larval rearing environment?; and (3) does adult size alter D. immitis melanization, burden, and/or development? We predicted that increasing temperature would decrease body size and mass, while increased nutrients would increase size, and that optimal rearing environments and larger sizes would allow both vector species to better resist (melanization) or tolerate (tissue repair) the worms due to an increase in energy reserves (Buckner et al. 2016).

Methods

We conducted a 3 × 3 × 2 full factorial design experiment with 3 temperatures (22 °C, 26 °C, and 30 °C), 3 nutrition levels (low, medium, and high), and 2 mosquito species, replicated 5 times (18 unique treatments and 90 total replicates). We sourced mosquitoes from an established colony of Ae. albopictus originating from Fayetteville, NC (F22) and an established colony of Ae. triseriatus originating from Michigan State University (MSU) of unknown generation postcolonization.

We stimulated hatching by placing a piece of seed germination paper with approximately 2,000 eggs of 1 species into a tray (53 cm × 28 cm × 5 cm) with 2 L of tap water, and 6 ml of a yeast solution for a final concentration of 0.03%. We prepared 6 trays for each species and placed the trays in an incubator set to 26 °C 16:8 L:D cycle for 48 h. After 48 h, we placed 70 larvae in a modified ULINE (Pleasant Prairie, WI, USA) round container (32 oz clear round wide-mouth plastic jars), replicated 90 times. We randomly assigned containers to treatment groups. We added nutrients (a mixture of oak leaf litter broth, albumin, and yeast) at either a low (0.075 g of yeast: albumin (1:1) + 280 ml of tap water + 120 ml of leaf litter water), medium (0.15 g of yeast: albumin (1:1) + 200 ml of tap water + 200 ml of leaf litter water), or high (0.3 g of yeast: albumin (1:1) + 120 ml of tap water + 280 ml of leaf litter water) concentration. After we fed the larvae, we secured a funnel lid with another round container on top of the bottom container, making an enclosed environment. The top container had a mesh hole at the top for air circulation and provided a dry environment to which emerging adults could move. We placed containers in their designated temperature treatment, and all temperatures followed a 16:8 L:D cycle. We monitored containers daily to record pupation, mortality, and emergence of adults.

We moved all adults from their larval containers to plastic cups (height, 4.8 in; diameter, 4.75 in) maintained at 26 °C in the incubator for 48 h prior to blood feeding. This time frame was sufficient to assume that all females had mated prior to blood feeding (Clements 1999, Dejesus and Reiskind 2016). The new container allowed the blood feeding apparatus to sit on the top of the container and allowed easy monitoring of adult feeding and mortality. As the larval environment was the experimental unit, we kept cohorts of females separate by larval container.

Before blood feeding, we randomly selected half of the females in each replicate and placed them in a new plastic cup. We then randomly fed half the females a D. immitis positive blood meal or a negative blood meal weekly with the use of a Hemotek P26 (Hemotek Ltd, Blackbyrn, UK) with 3 ml reservoirs (FU1-3) and the blood temperature regulated at 98.7 °C. We used Parafilm (M laboratory film, Amcor, Zürich, Switzerland) as the feeding membrane. We used D. immitis positive blood provided by a partner company (name withheld by non-disclosure agreement) diluted to a standard concentration of 6,750 microfilariae per milliliter with bovine blood (Spence Beaulieu and Reiskind 2020). Both positive and negative blood meals consisted of the same ratio of dog blood to bovine blood each week, where the ratio varied weekly depending upon microfilariae concentration in the D. immitis positive dog blood. We used D. immitis negative dog blood, supplied by North Carolina State University (NCSU) Center for Veterinary Medicine.

We provided all unfed containers with weekly blood meal opportunities for 2 consecutive days each week. Each container received a minimum of 20 min to feed. Females had access to a 10% sucrose solution when not given a blood meal opportunity, and we starved females of sugar for 24 h prior to blood meal opportunities to encourage feeding. If females chose not to feed the first week, we returned them to the 26 °C incubator and offered them another blood meal the following weeks for up to 2 weeks.

If the female successfully blood fed, we aspirated her into a 50-ml conical tube with a wet cotton ball and seed germination paper at the bottom. We covered the tube with a mesh cloth and secured a dental cotton wick soaked in 10% sucrose solution to a hole in the side of the tube, which was replaced ad libitum when the wick dried or molded. We held all blood fed adults at a constant 26 °C 16:8 L:D cycle, which should require a 10-day minimum for L3 D. immitis worms to develop, based on the calculation of heartworm developmental units (HDUs) (Kartman 1953, Ledesma and Harrington 2015).

We monitored blood fed females daily for mortality and processed any that died before the end of the experiment within 24 h. Using carbon dioxide asphyxiation, we euthanized all females that were alive at 15 days, which should have allowed an excess of time for worm development.

For all unfed dead mosquitoes, we immediately dried them in the 26 °C incubator for 48 hours, before being weighed with a Sartorius (Gottingen, Germany) ultramicro balance (±0.001 mg) and mounted both wings to a microscope slide with double-sided tape. For all fed females that died or were euthanized, we also weighed them and removed their wings. The left wing of each female was measured from the cubitus to the wing margin with an AmScope MU series (AmScope, Irvine, CA). If the left wing was damaged, we used the right wing (Packer and Corbet 1989, Lounibos et al. 1995). Dried weight and wing length were positively correlated for both Ae. albopictus (r = 0.388, P < 0.0001) and Ae. triseriatus (r = 0.689, P < 0.0001). Therefore, we only analyzed wing length as our proxy for body size.

For females fed positive blood, we dissected the head and Malpighian tubules and temporarily fixed these structures in Rosewell Park Memorial Institute 1640 culture medium (RPMI-1640; Lonza, Walkersville, MD), counted all worms and recorded worm location and condition via light microscopy (ie melanized, dead, alive). Melanized worms will appear white instead of clear, and the white represents melanin adhered to the outside of the worm (Bradley and Nyar 1985). Melanization was categorized as either no melanin present (none), approximately half the worm is covered in melanin (half), or the worm was completely covered in melanin (full). Some females (n = 19) could not be successfully dissected (ie Malpighian tubules could not be fully removed) because they had died on the wet cotton ball at the bottom of the tube and quickly began to decompose, so we removed these individuals from the worm burden dataset.

Data Analysis

We quantified how larval growth rates (days to adulthood) varied with temperature (categorical variable), nutrition (categorical variable), mosquito species, and all pairwise and 3-way interactions among these variables using a generalized linear model with a Poisson distribution. We quantified how these same predictors influenced wing length using a generalized linear model with a Gamma distribution, to account for the right skewed, zero-truncated, non-normal distribution of wing lengths (confirmed with a Shapiro–Wilk test). For both models, we used stepwise model reduction to determine which predictors to include in our final model. Models were then further analyzed with a likelihood ratio test, with the P valves derived from a chi-squared distribution.

For our feeding rates, we compared our rates to a previous study (Alto et al. 2003) with binomial test. For survival rates, we only quantified the percentage of blood-fed mosquitoes out of the total blood fed for each individual treatment that survived 10 days or more, which represents the extrinsic incubation period. We did not further analyze these proportions due to our low sample sizes across treatments.

We quantified how D. immitis positive blood-fed mosquito’s fecundity (total number of eggs) and worm burden (number of parasites per mosquito) in Aedes albopictus varied across treatment groups using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) with a zero inflated negative binomial (ZINB) distribution, because the count data were over dispersed with excess zeros. We included temperature and nutrition for our main effects, and chamber replicate and additional weeks from emergence to blood feeding as random effects. For both Ae. albopictus fecundity and worm burden models, the interaction of temperature and nutrition decreased the AIC by only 0.2, therefore we included no interaction in the final model. For Ae. triseriatus, we used generalized linear models with a ZINB distribution for fecundity, but a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution for worm burdens due to few zeros in the worm burden dataset. We only included the low nutrition and 22 °C and 26 °C temperatures in the models for Ae. triseriatus, since so few adults fed on positive D. immitis blood in the other nutrition and 30 °C treatment groups. We only included chamber replicate as a random effect in our models, as no Ae. triseriatus blood fed in subsequent weeks. Melanization of worms was only occasionally observed in medium nutrition Ae. albopictus, and thus it was not further analyzed.

For longevity, we recorded the number of days lived post-blood feeding, however we processed all females after 15 days. Therefore, we adjusted days lived by subtracting 15 to analyze the overall reduction in longevity. For Ae. albopictus, we used a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution. This model included temperature and nutrition as the main effects and chamber replicate and additional weeks from emergence to blood feeding as random effects. The interaction did not increase the fit of the model (AIC = 397.3), so the final model did not include an interaction (AIC = 392.1). For Ae. triseriatus, survival post-blood feeding on positive D. immitis blood was also analyzed with a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution. Additionally, we again only used females rearing in low nutrition and 22 °C and 26 °C temperatures conditions due to insufficient sample sizes in the remaining rearing conditions.

All statistical tests were conducted in R 4.3.1 statistical software (R Core Team 2023) with the MASS (Venables and Ripley 2002), glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017), tidyverse (Wickham et al. 2019), and knitr (Xie 2023) packages.

Results

Mosquito growth rates decreased with nutrition and increased with temperature, and there was a significant interaction, where nutrition tended to have a smaller effect with increasing temperature (Fig. 1, Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). This led to substantial variation in emergence times, where Ae. albopictus in the 30 °C low nutrition treatment emerged twice as fast as the 22 °C high nutrition, and Ae. triseriatus emerged 1.5 times faster in the 30 °C low nutrition treatment than the 22 °C high nutrition treatment. Correspondingly, wing lengths increased with nutrition with medium nutrition increasing wing length by 12% and High by 8% when compared to low nutrition, as well as a decreased with temperature with 26 °C and 30 °C 9% and 8% smaller than the 22 °C (Fig. 2, Table 2, Supplementary Table S2). Wing lengths were affected by the interaction between temperature and nutrition, but the effect was smaller than for emergence dates, because the effect of nutrition saturated at medium levels (Fig. 2).

Adult mosquito emergence rates ranged from 9–70% across all treatments and replicates (Supplementary Fig. S1), resulting in 1,175 females that survived to blood feed. For Ae. albopictus, 18.5% (89/481) took a positive blood meal, and 12% (53/439) took a negative blood meal. For Ae. triseriatus, 14.5% (20/138) took a positive blood meal, and 6% (7/117) took a negative blood meal. These rates were not significantly different (binomial test, all P values > 0.05, Supplementary Table S3) from the 30% of the mosquitoes that blood fed when offered a meal during artificial feeding using a similar method (Alto et al. 2003). For Ae. triseriatus, no mosquitoes blood fed in treatments 26 °C medium, 30 °C medium, and 30 °C high (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Temperature and nutrition did not significantly affect fecundity for either species for females that fed on positive D. immitis blood (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Tables S4, S5). Approximately half of Ae. albopictus infected females laid no eggs, with the remainder following a negative binomial distribution laying a median of 40 eggs. A portion of positive blood-fed Ae. albopictus females survived past 10 days in all treatments, except for 30 °C medium nutrition. Overall, 60% of positive blood-fed Ae. albopictus survived past 10 days, while 76% of Ae. triseriatus survived past 10 days. When comparing days lived post feeding for positive blood-fed mosquitoes in varying rearing conditions, we observed a significant reduction of 1.3 times in longevity for medium nutrition reared mosquitoes when compared to low nutrition positive blood-fed Ae. albopictus. Additionally, positive blood-fed Ae. albopictus had a 6.3-fold significant reduction in longevity when reared at 30 °C compared to 22 °C conditions (Supplementary Table S6). For Ae. triseriatus, we only looked at low nutrition mosquitoes reared in 22 °C and 26 °C and no significant difference was observed (Supplementary Table S7). We could not compare these survival rates to the negative blood treatments, because so few mosquitoes fed on the negative blood (see above). We successfully had 8 of the Ae. albopictus treatments feed on negative blood and we observed unusually high mortality and absence of eggs 2 days post feeding in those control mosquitoes (2.7% mortality in 2 days post feeding in positive blood fed versus 52.6% in negative blood fed). For negative blood-fed Ae. triseriatus, we had mosquitoes feed in all nutrition levels of the 22 °C only, and out of the 6, only 1 died within 2 days post feeding.

Temperature did not significantly affect the total worms in Ae. albopictus (Supplementary Table S8, Fig. 2), but there was a significant 55% increase in worm burdens in the medium nutrition treatments when compared to the low nutrition treatments (P < 0.05). For low nutrition Ae. triseriatus in the 22 °C and 26 °C, temperature and nutrition did not significantly affect the total worm burdens (Supplementary Table S9, Fig. 2). Worm development rates were low overall and thus difficult to compare. Out of the 89 positive blood fed Ae. albopictus, 66 (74%) had successful dissections, and of these, 79% (n = 52) had worm(s) present either in the Malpighian tubules or head. A total of 8 Ae. albopictus developed L3 worms, with a median burden of 2 worms per mosquito, and the highest recorded burden of 5 worms in an individual reared in the 26 °C high nutrition environment. Out of the 20 positive blood fed Ae. triseriatus, 14 (70%) had successful dissections, and 13 of those (93%) had worms present in their head or malpighian tubules. A total of 4 developed L3 worms, all with 1 to 2 worms (Fig. 3). When comparing the 2 host species, more Ae. triseriatus tended to develop L3 worms than Ae. albopictus (20% (4/20) vs 9% (8/89)), but this difference was not significant (X2 (1, 109) = 1.05, P > 0.05).

Discussion

Consistent with many previous studies, temperature and nutrient conditions in larval environments interacted to determine larval mosquito growth rates and subsequent adult sizes. (Briegel and Timmermann 2001, Juliano 2009, Lounibos et al. 2002, Nayar 1969). However, this variation in size, the interaction of temperature and nutrition had no effect on D. immitis burdens, although a significant increase in worm burdens was observed in Ae. albopictus reared in medium nutrition environments when compared with those reared under low nutrition, suggesting host tolerance may increase in a more nutrient-dense larval environment. This was not, however, a linear relationship with nutrition as high nutrition did not significantly increase worm burdens, although we observed more mosquitoes and higher worm burdens in Ae. albopictus in the 26 °C high nutrition. The medium level of nutrition may represent an optimal environment for larval development (Merritt et al. 1992, Brady et al. 2013), suggesting that conditions that are best for larval mosquitoes might also be best for D. immitis. Surprisingly, worm melanization was only occasionally observed in Ae. albopictus, all in medium-nutrition treatments, and never in Ae. triseriatus. Either melanization is not a common resistant strategy, or different methods are needed to consistently observe melanization. More detailed work is required to further disentangle the relative influence of the complex interaction between nutrition and temperature on resistance and tolerance to D. immitis.

While nutrition and temperature had no clear effects, we observed potential species-level differences in vector competence, consistent with a prior study (Spence Beaulieu and Reiskind 2020). Only 9% of the Ae. albopictus that fed on an infectious blood meal developed L3 worms, some of which had melanization, while 20% of Ae. triseriatus developed L3 worms, none of which had melanization. These non-significant trends that may suggest the species-level differences in resistance and tolerance that we hypothesized and warrant future investigation. In contrast, only 60% of Ae. albopictus that fed on infectious blood survived beyond 10 days, whereas 80% of Ae. triseriatus that fed on infectious blood survived beyond 10 days. Lower survival of Ae. albopictus could be due to several factors, including resource limitations after mounting a larger immune response to resist infection, a potentially important trade-off between resistance and survival (Moret and Schmid-Hempel 2000, Gwynn et al. 2005, Bascuñán-García et al. 2010), or a lack of tolerance causing the mosquitoes to succumb to infection. If Ae. triseriatus invest less in immune defenses in the wild, resulting in both more L3 worms and longer mosquito survival, it would make Ae. triseriatus a more competent vector species. Additionally, structural differences in the pyloric armature between the 2 species have been noted by Tuten et al. 2012 and Aedes spp. have a poorly developed cibarial armature compared with other genera of mosquitoes like Anopheles, as described by Lee and Craig (1983). These structures may contribute to parasite mortality, affecting vector competence. As structural components, they may be influenced during the larval growth phase, and they should be considered in future studies to understand the full extent of mosquito immune response in the system of D. immitis. Rearing diverse mosquito species under similar artificial conditions continues to challenge vector-borne disease research (Munstermann 1997). In this study, Ae. albopictus had survival rates greater than 60% in most treatments and Ae. triseriatus had survival rates <60% in all treatments. This is consistent with differences in survival observed in other studies (Hwa-Jen and Apperson 2000) and can likely be attributed to differences in physiology and ecology. In particular, Ae. albopictus has wider temperature tolerance with an optimum 29.7 °C (Delatte et al. 2009, Reinhold et al. 2018), whereas Ae. triseriatus has a narrower temperature range with an optimum of 21 °C (Munstermann and Wasmuth 1985, Paulson and Grimstad 1989), which is outside the range used in our study. Another important aspect could be the size of the container, and we suggest that future studies consider trays instead of pupal rearing chambers (Qureshi et al. 2023). Any improvements that increase survival would increase sample sizes and the ability to robustly test hypotheses about species-level differences in vector competence.

Artificial blood feeding is another common challenge when working with laboratory-reared mosquitoes (Alto et al. 2003, Gunathilaka et al. 2017, Costa da Silva 2023). In our study, only 15.4% of all adult females successfully fed, which reduced our overall sample sizes and statistical power. Furthermore, we used negative D. immitis dog blood to standardize our dog: bovine blood mixture for our control, but we do not know the exact provenance of that blood. We observed unusually high mortality in those control mosquitos, which leads us to hypothesize that the negative dog blood may have come from dogs administered D. immitis preventives, because Ivermectin can also reduce fertility, survival, egg hatching, larval survival, and adult emergence rates in mosquitoes, as can other flea/tick/and worm prophylaxis (Fritz et al. 2012, Duncan et al. 2023, Eba et al. 2023, Evans et al. 2023, Knape et al. 2025). Therefore, we recommend that future studies test for the presence of mosquito feeding deterrents or mosquitocidal components in blood used for artificial feeding and continue to improve methodology for increasing feeding rates.

Ultimately, improved methodology and more studies are needed to understand how abiotic and biotic factors influence mosquito hosts and transmission of filarial pathogens. To our knowledge, the only previous work in this area investigated how larval crowding affects Brugia pahangi infections in 1 vector species (Breaux et al. 2014). Far more research has considered how temperature, nutrition, or crowding effects virus infections (Alto et al. 2008, Westbrook et al. 2010, Buckner et al. 2016, Kang et al. 2017) or Plasmodium infections (Linenberg et al. 2016), but these results may not apply to the larger filarial worms. Given the wide diversity of mosquito species that can serve as vectors for D. immitis (Ledesma and Harrington 2015, Ledesma et al. 2019, Spence Beaulieu and Reiskind 2020) and the increasing incidence and spread of D. immitis in the United States (Drake and Wiseman 2018, American Heartworm Society 2023), understanding how environmental conditions impact D. immitis transmission remains an important topic for One Health connections between animal, human, and environmental health.
