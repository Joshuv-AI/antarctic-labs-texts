The J-shaped association between the hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score and all-cause mortality in patients with cardiometabolic multimorbidity: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiayi Chen, Han Yan, Linghua Pei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, platelet (HALP) score integrates nutritional, inflammatory, and immune markers. While its prognostic utility is known in other diseases, its significance in cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) remains unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the association between the HALP score and all-cause mortality in a nationally representative CMM cohort. We analyzed 1479 individuals with CMM from NHANES (1999–2018). Survival and mortality risk were assessed using Kaplan–Meier methods and weighted Cox regression. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to model the non-linear relationship. Exploratory time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed on subgroups stratified by the RCS-identified inflection point to further explore this association. During a median follow-up of 68 months, survival was lowest in HALP quartile 1 (Q1). In fully adjusted models, higher quartiles (Q2–Q4) had lower mortality risk versus Q1, with hazard ratios (HRs) of 0.61, 0.65, and 0.63, respectively (all P < .01). RCS analysis revealed a J-shaped association (P for non-linearity < .001), with a nadir at 50.01. Below this nadir, HALP was inversely associated with mortality (HR per unit increase = 0.981, P < .001); above it, a small-magnitude positive association was observed (HR = 1.002, P = .018). This pattern was consistent across most subgroups, though an interaction with drinking status was noted (P for interaction = .028). The HALP score has an independent, J-shaped association with all-cause mortality in CMM patients. This highlights the complex role of the nutrition-inflammation-immunity axis. Rather than a simple prognostic tool, HALP may serve as a nuanced indicator that helps differentiate between chronic frailty and potential acute systemic failure, guiding clinical interpretation. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the observational design precludes causal inference and the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains.

1. Introduction

Multimorbidity, defined as the co-occurrence of 2 or more chronic conditions within an individual, represents a substantial challenge to global public health, particularly within aging populations.[1] Among diverse comorbidity patterns, the clustering of cardiometabolic diseases – such as diabetes, angina pectoris, and stroke – termed cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), is increasingly recognized due to its rising prevalence and significant adverse impact on patient prognosis.[2,3] Individuals with CMM face a markedly elevated risk of major cardiovascular events, functional decline, and all-cause mortality compared to those with single conditions.[4–6] Addressing the intricate clinical challenges and considerable socioeconomic burden imposed by CMM necessitates the development and validation of simple, reliable, and readily accessible tools for effective risk stratification and prognostic assessment, thereby informing clinical decision-making.

While existing biomarkers for CMM, such as the waist-to-height ratio and triglyceride glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI), primarily reflect obesity and insulin sensitivity, they overlook other critical prognostic dimensions.[7,8] The pathophysiology and adverse outcomes of CMM are profoundly influenced by the complex interplay of malnutrition, systemic inflammation, and compromised immune function.[9,10] Therefore, a more comprehensive prognostic tool should integrate these interconnected states. The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, platelet (HALP) score, a composite biomarker derived from routine blood tests, is designed to provide precisely this holistic reflection.[11,12] Although emerging evidence links the HALP score to mortality in cancer and some cardiovascular conditions, its specific prognostic role and the nature of its association with mortality in the CMM population remain largely unexplored.[13–15]

This knowledge gap is significant. We hypothesized that the relationship between the HALP score and all-cause mortality in CMM patients is non-linear. While a lower score intuitively reflects poor nutritional and immune status, the protective effect of a higher score may not be limitless. Since the HALP score is a composite index, extreme values at either end could signify distinct pathological states. Therefore, we aimed to investigate this potential non-linear association and characterize its shape. By doing so, we seek to validate HALP as a more precise prognostic tool and offer novel insights to refine risk management strategies for this vulnerable patient group.

2. Method

2.1. Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort analysis drew upon publicly available, de-identified data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). NHANES, conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, employed a multistage probability sampling design to achieve national representativeness regarding U.S. civilian health and nutrition. Original NHANES protocols received ethical approval, and all participants provided informed consent.

2.2. Study Cohort assembly

This analysis utilized data spanning ten NHANES survey cycles, from 1999 to 2000 through 2017 to 2018. The initial sample comprised all 101,316 participants surveyed during this period. To specifically examine the association between the HALP score and all-cause mortality in individuals with CMM, we applied rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria aimed at minimizing selection bias. Exclusion criteria were as follows: age < 20 years, documented pregnancy, or missing essential survey weight data required for generating nationally representative estimates; absence of CMM; missing data for any component required for HALP score calculation; and missing mortality follow-up data (vital status or follow-up duration). After applying these criteria, a final analytical cohort of 1479 participants with CMM was established for the present survival analysis (Fig. 1).

2.3.2. Ascertainment of CMM status

This study focused on individuals with CMM, which was defined operationally as the confirmed presence of ≥2 specific cardiovascular diseases (e.g., angina pectoris, stroke) or metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes) within an individua.[17] Ascertainment of CMM status was based on a combination of self-reported physician diagnoses, relevant physical examination findings, and laboratory test results obtained from the NHANES dataset. Diabetes mellitus status was assigned based on meeting established diagnostic thresholds (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L [126 mg/dL] or glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5% [48 mmol/mol]) or self-reported use of antidiabetic treatment (confirmed via “yes” responses to questions about taking diabetes pills or insulin).[18] Similarly, participant confirmation (“yes”) to specific survey questions asking about prior diagnoses by a health professional determined the presence of angina (“Has a doctor... angina?”) or stroke (“Has a doctor... stroke?”).

2.3.3. Outcome assessment: all-cause mortality

Mortality from all causes was the primary outcome. Probabilistic record linkage to the National Death Index public-access files yielded vital status and date of death, with data complete through December 31, 2019. For each individual, the follow-up period extended from their NHANES mobile examination center assessment to the date of death or the study end date (December 31, 2019), whichever was sooner. Baseline measurement of the HALP score preceded the outcome assessment, ensuring the correct temporal sequence between exposure and outcome.

2.3.4. Selection and collection of covariates

To account for potential confounding in the association between the HALP score and mortality, a comprehensive set of covariates was selected from the NHANES dataset. These included: Age (<60/ ≥60 years), sex, race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Race/Ethnicity), education level (less than high school, high school graduate/GED or equivalent, some college or above), family poverty income ratio (categorized as ≤1.3, 1.3–3.5, >3.5), and marital status (married/cohabiting, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2, categorized as <25.0, 25.0–29.9, ≥30.0), current smoking status (yes/no), drinking status (yes/no), total cholesterol (mg/dL), uric acid (mg/dL), serum creatinine (Scr, mg/dL), and a history of hypertension (yes/no), the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), use of hypoglycemic agents (yes/no), and use of lipid-lowering agents (yes/no).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted with proper NHANES weighting protocols to compensate for the complex sampling methodology and generate estimates applicable to the broader U.S. population. Missing data for covariates were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations, under the assumption that the data were missing at random. The impact of HALP score quartiles on mortality was evaluated using weighted Cox regression, producing hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals with reference to the lowest quartile. For analytical robustness, results were generated from 3 distinct models: Model 1 provided unadjusted estimates, while Model 2 controlled for sociodemographic characteristics including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, poverty income ratio, and marital status. The final model (Model 3) was further adjusted for all variables in Model 2 plus BMI, smoking status, drinking status, total cholesterol, uric acid, Scr, history of hypertension, the CCI, and the use of hypoglycemic and lipid-lowering agents. The potential non-linear relationship between the continuous HALP score and all-cause mortality was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles), based on the fully adjusted Model 3. A likelihood ratio test was used to test for non-linearity. To quantitatively identify the inflection point of the J-shaped curve, a two-piecewise Cox regression model was fitted. The threshold value was algorithmically determined by selecting the point that yielded the maximum model log-likelihood. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the stability of this relationship by repeating the RCS analysis after excluding participants with extreme HALP values (those in the lowest and highest 2.5% of the distribution).

A time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of the HALP score for 1-, 5-, and 10-year mortality, stratified by the inflection point identified from the primary piecewise regression model. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted using the fully adjusted model to assess for potential effect modification across various demographic and clinical characteristics. The significance of interactions was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test.

Results yielding P-values <.05 (two-sided) were deemed statistically significant, and this criterion was applied consistently across all analyses.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study population

The final study cohort comprised 1479 eligible participants with CMM. Their baseline characteristics, stratified by HALP score quartiles, were presented in Table 1. The cutoff values defining the HALP score quartiles were: Q1 (≤33.12), Q2 (33.12–47.00], Q3 (47.00–63.94], and Q4 (>63.94). Overall, 74% were older than 60 years; 56.6% were male, and 71% were non-Hispanic White. During a median follow-up of 68.0 months (IQR: 33.8–114.0), a total of 718 deaths (45%) occurred. Several significant trends were observed across the HALP quartiles. Participants in higher HALP quartiles were more likely to be male (P < .001), married or living with a partner (P = .008), and have a higher BMI (P = .005). Notably, individuals with higher HALP scores exhibited a significantly lower comorbidity burden, as evidenced by a decreasing mean CCI across the quartiles (P = .024). They also had lower mean Scr levels (P = .007). Conversely, the mortality rate was markedly higher in the lowest HALP quartile (60.2% in Q1 vs 35.1% in Q4, P < .001). In contrast, characteristics such as age, race/ethnicity, education level, and poverty-income ratio did not differ significantly across the HALP quartiles. Interestingly, the proportions of participants using hypoglycemic drugs (P = .362) and lipid-lowering drugs (P = .123) were also similar across the groups.

3.3. Association between HALP score and all-cause mortality

The association between HALP score quartiles and all-cause mortality was evaluated using 3 sequential multivariable Cox regression models (Table 2). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), higher HALP quartiles were strongly associated with lower mortality. Compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), the HRs were 0.59 (95% CI: 0.47–0.78) for Q2, 0.57 (95% CI: 0.43–0.76) for Q3, and 0.52 (95% CI: 0.40–0.68) for Q4 (all P < .001). This association remained significant after adjusting for sociodemographic factors in Model 2. Crucially, the protective effect of a higher HALP score persisted even after comprehensive adjustment in Model 3. In this fully adjusted model, participants in the second, third, and fourth HALP quartiles still demonstrated a significantly lower risk of mortality compared to those in the lowest quartile. The corresponding HRs were 0.61 (95% CI: 0.48–0.78, P < .001) for Q2, 0.65 (95% CI: 0.49–0.87, P = .004) for Q3, and 0.63 (95% CI: 0.48–0.83, P = .001) for Q4. These results robustly indicated that the HALP score is an independent prognostic marker for all-cause mortality in patients with CMM, beyond the influence of established risk factors and overall disease burden.

3.4. Non-linear association and threshold effect analysis

RCS analysis demonstrated a significant non-linear association between the continuous HALP score and all-cause mortality among CMM patients (P for non-linearity < .001), consistent with a “J”-shaped curve, as illustrated in Figure 3. The mortality risk was highest at the lowest HALP levels, decreased progressively to a nadir, and then began to rise again at higher HALP values.

RCS curves of HALP score and all-cause mortality. The shaded region represents the 95% CI. The solid line indicates the estimated HR. The dashed line indicates HR = 1.0. The identified nadir is at HALP = 70.28. CI = confidence interval, CMM = cardiometabolic multimorbidity, HALP = hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, platelet, HR = hazard ratio, RCS = restricted cubic spline.

To quantitatively define this J-shaped curve, a two-piecewise Cox regression model was applied to identify the inflection point (Table 3). The analysis identified a HALP score of 50.01 as the optimal threshold where mortality risk was at its minimum. On the left side of the threshold (HALP ≤ 50.01), each unit increase in HALP was associated with a 1.9% reduction in mortality (HR = 0.981, 95% CI: 0.974–0.988, P < .001). Conversely, on the right side (HALP > 50.01), each unit increase was associated with a slight but significant 0.2% increase in risk (HR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.000–1.003, P = .018). The log-likelihood ratio test (P < .001) confirmed that the two-piecewise model provided a significantly better fit than a standard linear model.

3.5. Time-dependent ROC analysis

The results of the exploratory time-dependent ROC analysis were presented in Figure 5. The analysis revealed markedly different predictive characteristics between the 2 groups. For participants in the low-HALP subgroup (HALP ≤ 50.01), the HALP score demonstrated poor discriminative ability for mortality at all time points, with areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.37 for 1-year, 0.38 for 5-year, and 0.41 for 10-year mortality (Fig. 5A). In stark contrast, for the high-HALP subgroup (HALP > 50.01), the score showed moderate utility in predicting short-term mortality, with an AUC of 0.63 for 1-year mortality. However, its predictive power diminished substantially for longer-term outcomes, with the AUC dropping to 0.49 for 5-year mortality before recovering slightly to 0.58 for 10-year mortality (Fig. 5B).

3.6. Subgroup analyses

To assess the consistency of the prognostic value of the HALP score, we conducted subgroup analyses across a wide range of demographic and clinical characteristics using the fully adjusted model (Table 4). The protective association of higher HALP scores (quartiles 2–4 vs quartile 1) with all-cause mortality was remarkably consistent across most subgroups, including those based on age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, and the use of hypoglyc emic or lipid-lowering drugs (all P for interaction > .05). This indicated that the prognostic utility of HALP is broadly generalizable and not significantly modified by these factors. An exception was observed in the subgroup analysis stratified by drinking status, where a significant interaction was detected (P for interaction = .028). Specifically, the protective effect of a higher HALP score was pronounced and significant among nondrinkers, but this association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant among drinkers.

4. Discussion

Our investigation, drawing upon a large, nationally representative cohort, is the first to reveal a distinct, non-linear J-shaped relationship between the HALP score and all-cause mortality in patients with CMM. This finding moves beyond prior studies in other diseases that often reported linear associations, suggesting that in the context of CMM, the balance of the nutrition-inflammation-immunity axis is uniquely critical. The nadir identified at a HALP score of approximately 50.01 represents a potential physiological tipping point, below which markers of frailty dominate risk, and above which other pathological processes may emerge.

The steep descending limb of the J-curve, where lower HALP scores conferred a markedly higher mortality risk, is consistent with the concept of systemic frailty in the CMM population.[19] We propose that this reflects a self-perpetuating cycle, where the chronic inflammatory state inherent to CMM acts as a central driver.[20] It is plausible that this inflammation induces the anemia of chronic disease by dysregulating iron metabolism,[21] which in turn places an increased hemodynamic load on the myocardium.[22] Simultaneously, the inflammatory milieu, along with associated poor nutritional status, may impair effective immune responses and contribute to lymphopenia.[23,24] This potential immunodeficiency could then perpetuate the cycle, as unresolved infections or impaired cellular clearance can exacerbate chronic inflammation,[25] further suppressing albumin levels and bone marrow function.[26] If this hypothesis holds, the descending limb would quantify the momentum of a downward spiral where these factors mutually reinforce each other, leading to systemic collapse.

Intriguingly, the ascending limb of our J-curve revealed a paradoxical increase in mortality risk at very high HALP scores. This finding does not suggest that superior nutritional or immune status is harmful. Instead, a closer examination of the HALP formula (hemoglobin × albumin × lymphocytes/ platelets) provides a compelling explanation. An extremely high HALP score is mathematically driven by a disproportionately low platelet count in the denominator. In clinical practice, severe thrombocytopenia is a well-established surrogate for grave underlying pathologies that carry a high mortality risk, such as decompensated liver disease or sepsis-induced coagulopathy.[27–29] These conditions represent a state of acute systemic failure. This hypothesis is further corroborated by our time-dependent ROC analysis, which demonstrated that the HALP score had moderate predictive utility for 1-year mortality (AUC = 0.63) specifically within this high-HALP subgroup, while its predictive power completely diminished for 5-year outcomes (AUC = 0.49). This striking temporal pattern – effective for short-term prediction but failing in the medium-term – is precisely what one would expect if the risk is driven by acute, life-threatening events (reflected by thrombocytopenia) rather than chronic decline. Therefore, the ascending limb of the J-curve likely does not identify a risk from a “high HALP state” itself. Rather, it effectively unmasks a distinct subgroup of CMM patients whose immediate prognosis is dictated by severe pathologies reflected by their dangerously low platelet counts, underscoring the need to interpret the score with careful consideration of its individual components.

Beyond its prognostic value, our findings on the J-shaped association of the HALP score have significant clinical implications, potentially guiding more nuanced and personalized management of CMM patients. The HALP score can serve as a simple, cost-effective indicator to prompt deeper etiological exploration. For instance, a low HALP score should alert clinicians to look beyond standard cardiometabolic targets and actively assess for underlying malnutrition, systemic inflammation, and immunosenescence. These interconnected states are increasingly recognized as core components of a frailty phenotype that accelerates cardiovascular aging and multimorbidity.[30] This could trigger interventions such as nutritional support or anti-inflammatory therapies. Indeed, optimizing nutritional status is considered a crucial component in managing critically ill cardiac patients and has been linked to improved outcomes,[31] suggesting its potential benefit for high-risk CMM individuals in the ambulatory setting as well. Conversely, an exceptionally high HALP score acts as a paradoxical red flag. It should compel an immediate review of the patient’s platelet count, as it may unmask severe thrombocytopenia secondary to life-threatening acute conditions like sepsis or decompensated liver disease.[27–29]

This dual nature of the HALP score provides a strong rationale for personalized, risk-stratified therapeutic strategies. For patients on the descending limb of the J-curve, the therapeutic focus might be on reversing the chronic wasting state through interventions like nutritional support and physical exercise. This concept is central to managing conditions like cardiac cachexia and sarcopenia, where targeted nutritional treatment has been highlighted for its relevance in counteracting the disease progression.[32] In contrast, for those on the ascending limb, the priority shifts entirely to aggressively managing the acute underlying pathology driving thrombocytopenia. Therefore, the HALP score, interpreted within the context of its J-shaped curve, transforms from a static prognostic marker into a dynamic tool that can help clinicians tailor treatment priorities, distinguishing between patients who need chronic, supportive care and those who require urgent, life-saving interventions.

The prognostic utility of the HALP score was initially established in oncology,[33–35] but its relevance to cardiovascular diseases is a rapidly emerging area of investigation. Prior studies have linked the HALP score to in-hospital mortality in acute heart failure and to all-cause mortality in patients with comorbid acute ischemic stroke and type 2 diabetes.[36] More recently, research using the same NHANES dataset as our study has further solidified this connection. For example, Zou et al demonstrated an association between HALP and mortality in adults with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.[37] Most strikingly, a study by Dong et al identified a J-shaped relationship between the HALP score and mortality in a cancer cohort, mirroring the non-linear pattern we observed.[38] While these studies underscore the broad prognostic relevance of HALP, our research provides a novel and crucial contribution by being the first to specifically characterize this distinct J-shaped association within the CMM population. This highlights a unique pathophysiological interplay in CMM and reinforces the HALP score’s potential as a nuanced prognostic tool for this particularly vulnerable group.

This study has several notable strengths. First, its use of a large, nationally representative NHANES dataset enhances generalizability. Second, our study is the first to establish a non-linear, J-shaped association between the HALP score and all-cause mortality in patients with CMM, repositioning it as a nuanced prognostic indicator. A key strength is the rigor of our multivariable adjustment. Our final model controlled for a comprehensive set of confounders, including the CCI and the use of major cardiometabolic medications. The persistence of the J-shaped association after these adjustments supports HALP as an independent prognostic factor beyond general disease burden. Finally, the robustness of our findings was confirmed through sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Excluding extreme values in a sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the J-shaped curve and its turning point. Furthermore, the prognostic association was consistent across most subgroups, underscoring the broad applicability of the HALP score.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the observational design precludes any causal inference between the HALP score and mortality. Second, the use of a single baseline measurement for HALP and other covariates prevents the analysis of their longitudinal dynamics and how changes over time might influence outcomes. Third, while our models adjusted for a wide range of covariates, the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains. Fourth, our subgroup analysis identified a significant interaction by drinking status, suggesting the prognostic utility of HALP may be attenuated in drinkers. The underlying mechanisms for this observation are unclear and were beyond the scope of this study, highlighting an area for future research. Lastly, the operational definition of CMM relied on survey data, which may be subject to misclassification, and our findings, derived from a US population, require further validation in prospective cohorts and diverse ethnic groups to confirm their generalizability. In conclusion, this research provides initial evidence regarding the independent, J-shaped association connecting the HALP score with mortality outcomes in the context of CMM. The observed relationship advances our understanding of HALP as a clinically relevant prognostic parameter and illuminates the substantial impact of nutritional-inflammatory-immune dysregulation on survival in these medically complex patients.
