CLINICAL OUTCOMES OF SCLERAL BUCKLING IN ELDERLY PATIENTS WITH RHEGMATOGENOUS RETINAL DETACHMENT
Authors: Xinlei Zhu, Mengying Xu, Nan Chen, Lingxiao Lun, Lei Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.With proper surgical indications, scleral buckling achieves high anatomical and visual success in elderly rhegmatogenous retinal detachment with fewer complications. Key outcome predictors include initial visual acuity, macular attachment status, and proliferative vitreoretinopathy.

Retinal detachment (RD) is a potentially blinding ophthalmic condition if left untreated.1,2 Among its causes, retinal breaks are the most common contributors.3 Surgical intervention remains the mainstay of RD management, involving techniques such as pars plana vitrectomy (PPV), scleral buckling, and pneumatic retinopexy (PR), either alone or in combination.4,5 However, the optimal surgical approach for rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) is still debated, as the choice depends on a complex interplay of clinical factors and surgeon preferences, with no universally accepted consensus.

Historically, scleral buckling surgery has served as the cornerstone treatment for RD, especially in cases involving retinal breaks.6,7 However, the advent of microsurgical techniques, particularly PPV, has substantially altered the landscape of RD management.8–10 Over the past two decades, PPV has rapidly gained prominence, dramatically reducing scleral buckling from approximately 90% to less than 10%.11,12 Evidence suggests that younger surgeons receive insufficient training in scleral buckling, potentially accelerating its decline in usage.13–15

Despite the widespread adoption of PPV, scleral buckling remains a valuable technique, particularly for younger patients with crystalline lenses.7,16,17 However, its use in elderly patients has significantly diminished as PPV has largely replaced it.18 Elderly individuals, frequently burdened by systemic comorbidities, may struggle with the stringent postoperative positioning required for successful PPV outcomes, a factor often overlooked. While previous studies have compared scleral buckling and vitrectomy for RRD in general populations, few have systematically evaluated the clinical efficacy, safety, and outcome predictors of scleral buckling, specifically in elderly patients.18,19 As a result, surgical decision making in this population remains empirical and lacks evidence-based guidance. In addition, scleral buckling offers distinct advantages, including reduced medical resource requirements and cost-effectiveness, making it a viable option in appropriately selected cases.

Our study aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes of scleral buckling in elderly patients over 60 with RRD. By focusing on this patient demographic, we hope to provide evidence-based insights into the role of scleral buckling in older populations, contributing to more informed decision making in clinical practice.

Methods

Patients

The retrospective, observational cohort study included patients with RRD who underwent scleral buckling surgery at Qingdao Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University between August 2018 and August 2024. The primary focus of the study was on patients aged 60 years and older (referred to as the elderly group). Because of the relatively minimal controversy surrounding the use of scleral buckling surgery in younger patients with RRD, individuals under the age of 30 were included as controls (referred to as the young group). The study adheres to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University.

The study exclusively focused on scleral buckling surgery, excluding other surgical interventions for RRD, such as PPV and PR. Furthermore, RRD associated with trauma, retinovascular diseases, or inflammation were excluded. The study required a minimum follow-up period of 3 months, and cases that did not meet this criterion were excluded.

The indications for scleral buckling in elderly patients were broadly consistent with those for younger patients. It was performed in cases of RRD with identifiable peripheral breaks, limited proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR, Grade A, B, and C Type 1 and 3), and no significant vitreous traction or posterior segment pathology. Special consideration was given to the presence of pseudophakia or subretinal membranes, ensuring that the detachment characteristics were amenable to external indentation. Cases with diffuse PVR (Grade C Type 2, 4, and 5) or posterior breaks were excluded, favoring PPV or combined approaches when necessary.

Baseline Assessment

Baseline demographic characteristics and preoperative ophthalmic characteristics were recorded for all patients. Initial visual acuity was measured using a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart and expressed as the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR). For recording purposes, multiple consecutive atrophic retinal tears within the same grid-like area of retinal degeneration were counted as a single tear. The predominant quadrant of RRD was identified based on the primary quadrants through which the retinal detachment extended. The extent of retinal detachment was quantified using clock hours. The presence of PVR was graded according to the classification system defined by Machemer et al20 and documented accordingly.

Surgical Procedure

A single experienced ophthalmologist performed all surgical procedures to ensure consistency in technique and outcomes. Scleral buckling surgery was performed according to standard protocols, ensuring proper placement and securing of the buckle to achieve optimal retinal reattachment. Ancillary procedures, including cataract extraction, laser photocoagulation, transscleral cryotherapy, and external drainage, were performed at the surgeon's discretion based on intraoperative findings and the patient's condition. In selected elderly patients with significant lens opacity, cataract extraction, and intraocular lens implantation were performed concurrently with scleral buckling as part of a triple procedure. The external drainage of subretinal fluid (SRF) was performed in cases where a large amount of SRF prevented the proper placement of the buckle.

Outcomes Assessment

The primary outcome measure for this study was the single surgery anatomical success (SSAS), defined as the maintenance of retinal reattachment for at least 3 months after surgery. Failure to retain reattachment within this period was classified as a recurrence of retinal detachment. In cases where local subretinal fluid accumulation was observed but did not increase during follow-up, it was not classified as a surgical failure.

Secondary outcome measures included final visual acuity, the presence of postoperative complications, and the proportion of final retinal reattachment. Final visual acuity was assessed using a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart, and values were recorded as LogMAR scores. Common complications included raised IOP requiring drops, subretinal hemorrhage, and residual subretinal fluid. Transient postoperative high IOP that resolved without pharmacologic intervention was not considered a complication. Residual subretinal fluid was defined as fluid that had not been absorbed within 1 month.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons made using the chi-square test or Wilcoxon signed rank test. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between potential influencing factors and final visual outcomes in patients aged over 60 years. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Demographic Characteristics

During the period from August 2018 to August 2024, a total of 1,342 eyes from 1,323 patients with RRD underwent scleral buckling, PPV, or combined procedures at Qingdao Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University. Among them, 443 eyes (33.01%) received scleral buckling surgery. Age-related details of surgical distribution are presented in Supplemental Digital Content 1 (see Table S1, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C632).

In this study, 290 eyes from 278 patients satisfied the inclusion criteria. Table 1 lists the comparison of demographic characteristics between the young and elderly groups. A total of 177 eyes from 167 patients were included in the young group, whereas the elderly group comprised 113 eyes from 111 patients. The mean age of patients in the young group was 21.21 ± 4.94 years, while in the elderly group, it was 66.86 ± 4.42 years. Gender distribution differed substantially, with 59.88% of the young group being male and 67.57% of the elderly group being female (χ2 = 20.104, P < 0.001). The laterality (distribution of right vs. left eyes) was relatively balanced in the young group, with 47.46% of cases involving the right eye and 52.54% involving the left eye. In contrast, the elderly group exhibited a higher prevalence of right-eye involvement, with 60.18% of cases involving the right eye and 39.82% involving the left eye (χ2 = 4.474, P = 0.034). The mean duration of symptoms before surgery was significantly longer in the elderly group (23.52 ± 21.18 days) compared with the young group (14.15 ± 17.23 days, t = −3.929, P < 0.001). Systemic comorbidities were markedly more prevalent among elderly patients, with 64.86% affected, in contrast to only 2.99% in the young cohort (χ2 = 128.004, P < 0.001). Finally, the follow-up duration was significantly longer for young patients (15.87 ± 15.92 months) compared with their elderly counterparts (6.09 ± 7.14 months, t = 7.102, P < 0.001).

Preoperative Ophthalmic Characteristics

Initial visual acuity was slightly better in the young group (0.53 ± 0.65) compared with the elderly group (0.67 ± 0.66), although the difference did not achieve statistical significance (t = −1.705, P = 0.089). Similarly, initial IOP in the young group (14.02 ± 2.71 mmHg) was significantly higher than that in the elderly group (13.08 ± 2.30 mmHg, t = 3.056, P = 0.002), but both values remained within the normal range. High myopia was notably more prevalent in the young group, affecting 44.63% of patients, while only 2.65% of elderly patients had this condition (χ2 = 59.926, P < 0.001). In contrast, the lens status was consistent between groups, with most patients in both groups being phakic (99.44% of young patients and 97.34% of elderly patients, χ2 = 0.945, P = 0.331). Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) was significantly more common in the elderly group, occurring in 71.68% of eyes, compared with only 17.51% in the young group (χ2 = 66.990, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Ophthalmic Characteristics Before Scleral Buckling in Various Age Groups

As the primary focus of this study, RD-related indicators also exhibited differences between the young and elderly groups. Specifically, the mean number of retinal breaks was significantly higher in the young group (1.43 ± 0.87) compared with the elderly group (1.15 ± 0.45, t = 3.585, P < 0.001). The specific number of retinal breaks was further analyzed. In the young group, 75.14% of eyes had a single break, 12.43% had two breaks, and 12.43% had three or more breaks. In contrast, among elderly patients, the corresponding proportions were 87.61%, 10.62%, and 1.77%, respectively. The distribution differed significantly between the 2 age groups (χ2 = 11.002, P = 0.004). In addition, retinal breaks were classified as either retinal holes or retinal tears. Retinal holes were significantly more prevalent in the young group (86.44%), whereas retinal tears predominated in the elderly group (59.29%). The difference in break types between the 2 groups was statistically significant (χ2 = 66.990, P < 0.001). Meanwhile, the distribution of retinal breaks differed significantly between the 2 cohorts (χ2 = 26.106, P < 0.001). In the young group, the most frequent break location was inferotemporal (50.28%), followed by superotemporal (42.94%). Conversely, in the elderly group, most breaks were located in the superotemporal quadrant (53.98%), with fewer in the inferotemporal quadrant (25.66%). Further analysis revealed that the location of retinal breaks and the predominant quadrant of RRD were significantly associated with the presence of PVD (χ2 = 18.692, P < 0.001; χ2 = 20.662, P < 0.001, respectively). In addition, the extent of RRD, measured in clock hours, was marginally greater in the elderly group (4.29 ± 1.97 hours) compared with the young group (4.03 ± 2.04 hours), although this difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.062, P = 0.289). A significant difference was observed in macular attachment status, with the young group having a higher incidence of macular-off RRD (57.06%) compared with the elderly group (33.62%, χ2 = 15.176, P < 0.001). Interestingly, young patients had a significantly higher proportion of PVR Grade C, with 27.68% exhibiting Grade C, compared with only 8.85% in the elderly group (χ2 = 15.633, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Surgical Intervention

Table 3 presents a detailed analysis of the surgical management strategies used in the treatment of RRD across young and elderly patient cohorts. The choice of illumination source during surgery was consistent between the two groups (χ2 = 0.001, P = 0.976). In both groups, most patients underwent surgery using an intravitreal fiber-optic probe (76.84% in the young group and 76.99% in the elderly group). Cataract extraction was performed exclusively in the elderly group, affecting 15.04% of these patients (χ2 = 28.286, P < 0.001). Notably, external drainage procedures were marginally more frequent in the elderly group, used in 64.60% of cases compared with 53.11% in the young cohort, although this difference was not statistically significant (χ2 = 3.731, P = 0.053). Finally, the mean length of the buckle used during the scleral buckling procedure was significantly longer in the young group (17.47 ± 7.89 cm) compared with the elderly group (13.98 ± 4.96 cm, t = 4.610, P < 0.001).

Postoperative Outcomes and Complications

Postoperative outcomes were largely favorable in both groups, with SSAS noted in 97.18% of young patients and 98.23% of elderly patients (χ2 = 0.032, P = 0.858). Visual acuity outcomes, assessed by LogMAR scores, showed no significant difference between the two groups, with the young cohort achieving a mean final visual acuity of 0.27 ± 0.35 and the elderly cohort 0.33 ± 0.29 (t = −1.749, P = 0.081). The distribution of visual outcomes was similar, with 59.89% of young patients showing improved visual acuity, compared with 64.60% in the elderly group (χ2 = 0.678, P = 0.713). A decrease in visual acuity was infrequent in both groups but slightly more prevalent among young patients (9.60%) than elderly patients (7.96%). Notably, the final IOP differed significantly between groups, with young patients exhibiting higher levels (18.37 ± 7.16 mmHg) than elderly patients (14.27 ± 4.07 mmHg, t = 6.189, P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Complications were infrequent, although significant differences were observed between groups. The incidence of raised IOP requiring drops was observed in 25.99% of young patients versus 7.08% in the elderly cohort, with a significant statistical difference (χ2 = 16.274, P < 0.001). Subretinal hemorrhage occurred in 9.60% of younger patients and 2.65% of elderly patients, as an intraoperative complication of subretinal fluid drainage (χ2 = 5.188, P = 0.023). Residual subretinal fluid and other complications were similarly distributed between the groups, with no significant differences observed (χ2 = 2.079, P = 0.149 and χ2 = 0.476, P = 0.490, respectively). Only a tiny proportion of patients required secondary surgical intervention, with 2.82% in the young group and 1.77% in the elderly group (χ2 = 0.032, P = 0.858). Finally, 100% of eyes in both the young and elderly groups achieved successful retinal reattachment (Table 4).

Clinical and Anatomical Predictors of Final Visual Outcomes

We subsequently focused on the visual outcomes after scleral buckling surgery in patients aged over 60 years. This cohort's median final visual acuity was 0.30, which served as the threshold to divide patients into two subgroups. Patients with a final visual acuity of 0.30 or better were classified into the better visual acuity group. In comparison, those with a final visual acuity worse than 0.30 were assigned to the lower visual acuity group. Statistical analysis was conducted on various parameters for both groups, with results summarized in Supplemental Digital Content 2 (see Table S2, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C633).

Logistic regression analysis identified factors influencing final visual outcomes in patients aged 60 years and older. The crude model indicated that a larger extent of RD, measured in clock hours, was significantly associated with a poorer final visual outcome (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.03–1.55). Conversely, external drainage was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving better final visual acuity after scleral buckling surgery (OR = 3.00, 95% CI: 1.25–7.19). However, after adjusting for other factors, these associations were no longer statistically significant, suggesting that RRD extent and external drainage may not be independent determinants of final visual acuity. Initial visual acuity, macular attachment status, and Level C PVR emerged as significant predictors of final visual outcomes in both the crude and adjusted models. In the crude analysis, patients with poorer initial visual acuity were less likely to achieve favorable postoperative visual outcomes (OR = 23.56, 95% CI: 6.88–80.64). In contrast, a macula-on status was significantly associated with favorable final visual outcomes (OR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.02–0.15). However, patients with Level C PVR had an increased risk of poor visual outcomes after scleral buckling surgery (OR = 8.71, 95% CI: 1.74–43.72). In contrast, Levels A and B did not significantly affect final visual acuity. These three associations remained significant after adjusting for other variables (OR = 10.87, 95% CI: 2.57–45.92, OR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.66, and OR = 16.75, 95% CI: 1.96–142.89). Other factors, such as laterality, systemic diseases, and surgical interventions, did not show statistically significant associations in the logistic regression analysis and were, therefore, not included in Table 5.

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical outcomes of patients aged over 60 years with RRD who underwent scleral buckling surgery. Among this cohort, 98.23% achieved SSAS, and 92.03% experienced stable or improved postoperative visual acuity. Elderly patients demonstrated fewer postoperative complications than younger cohorts, reinforcing the feasibility and safety of scleral buckling in this population. Initial visual acuity, macular attachment status, and PVR Grade C were identified as significant predictors of final visual outcomes. In contrast, factors such as RRD extent and the use of external drainage played a lesser role. Unlike previous studies that often generalize surgical choices based on lens status alone, our data support a more nuanced, evidence-based approach to surgical decision making in elderly patients with RRD.

We acknowledge that scleral buckling requires careful patient selection, as it is not appropriate for all cases of RRD.6,21 The indications for scleral buckling are more restricted than PPV, which is now widely used because of its applicability across diverse RRD types.11,22 Nevertheless, despite the decreasing prevalence of scleral buckling in current practice, we contend that treating all RD cases exclusively with PPV is neither clinically justified nor cost-effective.23,24 Scleral buckling is relevant in cases with identifiable peripheral breaks, limited PVR, and favorable lens status, particularly among elderly patients. This perspective underpins the rationale and motivation for composing this article.

Pars plana vitrectomy for RRD commonly involves intraocular tamponade with gas or silicone oil to stabilize retinal reattachment.25,26 However, this approach typically necessitates strict postoperative positioning, which can be particularly challenging for older adults. Although recent studies have investigated more lenient positioning protocols after PPV,27 a standardized consensus remains to be established. For elderly patients, prolonged positioning can be both challenging and uncomfortable. In contrast, scleral buckling eliminates the need for strict postoperative positioning, offering a more feasible and patient-friendly option for older adults. The reduced postoperative burden is particularly beneficial for elderly patients, further supporting the clinical practicality of scleral buckling in select older individuals.

The status of the vitreous plays a critical role in shaping the characteristics of RRD. Vitreous liquefaction is an age-related process leading to PVD, commonly associated with retinal tears, vitreoretinal traction, and bullous retinal detachments. In contrast, RRD can also occur in the absence of PVD, typically presenting as shallow detachments with atrophic holes and slower progression. In our study, the higher prevalence of multiple breaks and retinal holes in younger patients may reflect a distinct pathophysiologic mechanism involving lattice degeneration and high myopia. In contrast, retinal tears in elderly patients are likely associated with PVD-induced vitreoretinal traction. Furthermore, our data suggest that the variation in break locations between age groups is likely mediated by the presence or absence of PVD, consistent with known differences in vitreoretinal interface changes across ages.

Recent advancements in surgical techniques have substantially improved outcomes in treating RD. While existing literature emphasizes the superior visual outcomes of scleral buckling over PPV in specific populations, such as phakic patients and children,16,17 the continuous evolution of vitrectomy techniques must also be acknowledged. Innovations in microsurgical instrumentation have increased PPV's efficiency, minimized surgical trauma, and enabled more precise interventions. Similarly, scleral buckling must adapt and evolve to remain clinically relevant. In our study, certain elderly patients benefited from integrating advanced tools traditionally associated with PPV, such as wide-angle viewing systems (WAVS) and intravitreal fiber-optic probes, into their scleral buckling procedures. These enhancements identified small, otherwise undetectable retinal breaks, particularly in elderly patients with media opacities, without significantly increasing postoperative complications.28 Although fiber-optic–assisted scleral buckling converts the procedure into an intraocular one, it preserves the core advantages of scleral buckling while improving intraoperative visualization. These findings highlight the potential of integrating modern surgical technologies to optimize outcomes in scleral buckling procedures.

Final visual acuity is a critical parameter for assessing postoperative outcomes in RRD surgery and is often influenced by various independent risk factors. The Primary Retinal Detachment Outcomes (PRO) Study Group identified predictors of poor visual outcomes after RRD repair, including PVR, antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) injections, vision loss exceeding 1 week, ocular comorbidities, presenting visual acuity, and advanced age.29 Similarly, the Scleral Buckling versus Primary Vitrectomy in Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment (SPR) study found a significant association between postoperative BCVA and the number of preoperative retinal breaks, a risk factor common to both phakic and aphakic patients.30 Our study specifically investigated risk factors affecting postoperative visual outcomes in patients aged over 60 years who underwent scleral buckling surgery. We identified initial visual acuity, macular attachment status, and PVR Grade C as significant predictors of final visual outcomes and superseding factors such as RRD extent or external drainage. These results may encourage surgeons to reconsider scleral buckling in cases where it was previously deemed less favorable, especially in patients with systemic comorbidities or limited tolerance for postoperative positioning required by PPV. This finding emphasizes the critical role of early detection and timely intervention in optimizing visual outcomes for patients undergoing scleral buckling surgery for RRD.

This study has certain limitations. Its retrospective design and exclusion of elderly patients undergoing PPV limit direct comparisons between scleral buckling and PPV in this demographic. In addition, the influence of lens opacity on postoperative BCVA necessitates cautious interpretation of visual outcomes. Future research incorporating randomized controlled trials and broader patient populations would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the comparative efficacy of scleral buckling and PPV in elderly patients.

In conclusion, scleral buckling demonstrated favorable outcomes in patients over 60 with RRD. Initial visual acuity, macular attachment status, and PVR Grade C were key predictors of final visual outcomes. These findings support the continued use of scleral buckling in select elderly patients, highlighting its value as an evidence-based, patient-centered option in managing RRD.
