Peroxisomes orchestrate metabolic flexibility and longevity via an interorganelle cascade
Authors: Arpit Sharma, Aditi Prabhakar, Miriam Valera-Alberni, Jamie D. Kraft, Abigail E. Ellis, Ryan D. Sheldon, Meeta Mistry, Pallas Yao, Yanshan Liang, Sheng Hui, William B. Mair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging impairs coordinated organelle dynamics essential for lipid metabolism, causing a decline in intracellular metabolic flexibility. However, the drivers of organelle collapse and their temporal order remain unclear. Here we identify peroxisomal function as a critical regulator of metabolic flexibility during youth and low-energy states. Using Caenorhabditis elegans, we show that fasting robustly induces peroxisomal function in youth, whereas this response is blunted during aging. Loss of peroxisomal import via PRX-5 declines over age, causing pathological lipid droplet expansion, dysfunctional mitochondrial bioenergetics and metabolic inflexibility. Although targeted PRX-5 degradation recapitulates metabolic aging, its overexpression preserves lipid dynamics and mitochondrial integrity. Notably, dietary restriction maintains peroxisomal pathways and organelle coordination into late life and peroxisomal function causally underpins dietary restriction-mediated longevity. Our findings highlight peroxisomes as central upstream regulators of a dynamic interorganelle cascade driving metabolic plasticity and highlight peroxisomal maintenance as a key determinant of metabolic flexibility during aging.

Main

Cells at their prime are in constant dynamism and maintaining this dynamic response to fluctuating cellular conditions, that is, ‘homeodynamics’, is the truest form of a youthful system. A fundamental example of homeodynamics is metabolic flexibility, the ability to alternate between different metabolic substrates appropriately to meet the energetic needs of cells, tissues and the organism1–5. With age, metabolic flexibility declines, specifically the responsiveness to fasting and feeding becomes blunted, leading to dysregulated induction of lipid oxidation in older organisms across the evolutionary spectrum6,7. Consequently, there is an over-reliance on carbohydrate as fuel, accompanied by increased fat deposition and lipid toxicity8,9. These changes collectively contribute to the emergence of age-associated metabolic dysfunction, culminating in chronic metabolic syndrome10. Preserving metabolic flexibility is therefore critical for promoting healthy aging and mitigating the onset of age-related metabolic disorders.

Existing studies on loss of metabolic flexibility with age have largely focused on intertissue hormonal signals that maintain glucose homeostasis, such as insulin production, secretion and sensitivity11,12. Yet, far less is understood about cell-intrinsic mechanisms that sustain metabolic flexibility during aging. Intracellularly, lipid mobilization underpins metabolic plasticity and this complex process relies on coordinated dynamics across lipid droplets (LDs), mitochondria and peroxisomes13–15. Although emerging studies suggest that these organelles form a metabolic network, whether aging disrupts this network coordination, and whether failure of one node critically triggers the collapse of others, remain unknown16–18.

Here we identify peroxisome protein import as a causal regulator of age-related metabolic inflexibility and organelle collapse. Using multi-omic profiling, we found that aging selectively impairs peroxisome function. Central nodes regulating peroxisomal protein import, such as PEX5, rapidly decline with age, rendering peroxisome import defective and metabolically incompetent. Loss of peroxisome import causally drives pathological LD expansion and propagation of lipolysis-resistant LDs enriched in long-chain and poly(unsaturated triglycerides) (PUFA-TGs). Temporally, these defects precede and precipitate mitochondrial dysfunction, including hyperpolarization, swelling and bioenergetic failure, suggesting that peroxisomal dysfunction initiates a sequential, cross-organelle failure culminating in metabolic inflexibility.

Mechanistically, we find that peroxisomal pathways are regulated via dietary restriction (DR) and the conserved nuclear hormone receptor and regulator of fatty acid oxidation (FAO), NHR-49 or peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-α). NHR-49 modulates peroxisomal gene expression and its activity declines with age, underpinning the collapse in peroxisomal activation. Low-energy states, such as DR, which preserve NHR-49 activity, sustain peroxisomal function and maintain both LD and mitochondrial dynamics. Importantly, loss of peroxisomal import fully abolishes DR-mediated longevity and overexpression of prx-5 is sufficient to induce beneficial effects on organelle dynamics and longevity. We establish that peroxisomal dysfunction acts as a driver of metabolic inflexibility and define why intracellular architecture unravels with age and how its preservation may enable metabolic and physiological resilience.

Results

Aging results in loss of transcriptional flexibility and peroxisomal induction during fasting

We characterized metabolic changes in male mice aged 4 months (young) and 23 months (aged) via measurements of the respiratory exchange ratio (RER). Aging resulted in an attenuation of FAO, reflected in blunted decreases in the RER in aged mice during fasting periods (Extended Data Fig. 1a). To confirm whether aging impedes intracellular lipid mobilization during fasting, we subjected day 1 (young) and day 8 (aged) adult wild-type (WT) C. elegans to acute fasting and quantified lipid abundance. In young animals, lipid quantification using Nile Red (NR) dye revealed a rapid utilization of lipid stores on fasting. In contrast, aging resulted in a remarkable loss of cellular lipid mobilization during fasting and induced metabolic inflexibility, marked by an abundance of lipolysis-refractory lipid stores (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1b). Next, to directly measure neutral lipid stores, we quantified triglycerides (TGs) in day 1 (D1) and day 8 (D8) adult animals under fed and acute fasting conditions. In young animals, fasting induced a significant reduction in TGs, consistent with efficient lipid mobilization (Extended Data Fig. 1c). To identify mechanisms underpinning age-associated metabolic flexibility loss, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in young (YA) and aged (D8) WT C. elegans under freely (ad libitum (AL)) fed and 6-h fasted conditions (Fig. 1b). Principal component analysis (PCA) segregated samples according to age (component 1) and feeding state (component 2) (Fig. 1c). A 6-h fast induced targeted changes in the C. elegans transcriptome, whereas aging induced broad transcriptomic changes19,20. Although the transcriptional response to an acute fast was robust and distinct in youth, this distinction was ablated in aged animals (Fig. 1c). Crucially, this loss of transcriptional distinction between fasting and feeding with age was not a result of aged animals not feeding, because animals still ingest food at day 8 (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e). Next, an intercorrelation analysis revealed that hierarchical clustering could not distinguish between the fed and fasted transcriptomes, specifically in aged animals (Fig. 1d). We validated that, in youth, clustering across nutritional status (component 2) is representative of physiological pathways expected to be induced or repressed in response to fasting (Fig. 1c). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis verified that young animals induce fatty acid catabolic processes and suppress an array of lipid biosynthetic processes in response to a 6-h food deprivation21 (Extended Data Fig. 1f).

Using the likelihood ratio test (LRT) clustering, we focused on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) induced by fasting during youth but refractory to fasting in D8 adults. Gene set enrichment analysis of one gene cluster, the ‘differential fasting cluster’ (DFC) of 41 transcripts (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 1a), revealed that induction of peroxisome-specific genes in response to fasting is specifically lost with age (Fig. 1f). Of note, we identified all critical genes related to peroxisomal protein import and lipid oxidation enriched in this DFC cluster, such as prx-5, ech-8 and daf-22, all corresponding to distinct steps in peroxisomal import and FAO22 (Supplementary Table 1a). Direct analysis of normalized counts and log2(fold-change) (log2(FC)) across the four biological groups confirmed that, in addition, several conserved peroxins in C. elegans show induction with fasting in youth (Extended Data Fig. 1g) and that transcriptional responses to fasting are specifically diminished in aged animals (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1h).

Unlike peroxisomal genes, mitochondrial transcripts were predominantly regulated only with age and mitochondrial β-oxidation transcripts were regulated only with fasting, and neither was enriched in the DFC cluster (Supplementary Table 1b). Together, these data indicate that the blunted response to acute fasting with age is associated with peroxisomal dysfunction and that acute induction of peroxisomal function might precede metabolic alterations in other organelles. We confirmed that peroxisomal genes transcriptionally induced with fasting in C. elegans are also induced in HepG2 cells on serum and nutrient withdrawal23 (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 1h).

To assess lipid catabolism during fasting, we performed a lipidomic analysis between WT fed and 12-h fasted, D1 animals and found decreased TGs and a concomitant increase in diglycerides (DGs), indicating lipolysis of stored TGs into DGs in response to fasting (Fig. 1h,i). A significant proportion of TGs utilized with fasting contained fatty acid chain lengths corresponding to long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs), C > 18 and very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), C > 20, both known to be peroxisomal FAO substrates in C. elegans24–26 (Supplementary Table 1c). Given that nutrient sensors, such as mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), link cellular energy status to both downstream transcriptional and metabolic responses, we confirmed that loss of transcriptional flexibility with age occurs concurrently with an attenuated response of mTORC1 to fasting in older animals as measured by pS60K phosphorylation (Extended Data Fig. 1j,k), together suggesting that both metabolic and transcriptional responses to fasting are altered with age and loss of peroxisomal gene induction emerges as a key node of fasting dysregulation.

Peroxisomal protein import declines with age and coincides with lysosomal degradation

In C. elegans, cargo to be imported into peroxisomes is recognized by the sole peroxisomal targeting signal 1 (PTS1) and imported via the peroxin regulating peroxisomal protein import, PRX-527–29. Indeed, this critical import regulator, prx-5, was among the top differentially expressed peroxisomal genes in the DFC cluster (Supplementary Table 1a). To investigate whether a gradual failure of peroxisome import occurs during aging, we utilized a strain expressing a single-copy GFP-SKL, a GFP fused to the tripeptide PTS1 (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). We then quantified changes in peroxisomal count and morphology in the metabolically active C. elegans intestine (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Although peroxisomes exist in various tissues, such as the epidermis (Extended Data Fig. 2d), in C. elegans peroxisome import factors like prx-5 and prx-10 are primarily expressed in the intestine, where metabolic functions analogous to both liver and fat tissues in mammals actively occur30.

In young, D1 animals, we saw a robust sequestration of the GFP signal inside punctate peroxisomes (Fig. 2a). However, late life animals, at day 8 and day 10, showed a significant loss of GFP peroxisomal foci count, indicating an age-associated failure in peroxisomal protein import and induction of peroxisomal dysfunction (Fig. 2a,b). Recent studies show that peroxisomes undergo pexophagic turnover during aging and changes to peroxisomal morphology, specifically, tubulation of peroxisomes, occur, resulting in their merging with lysosomes31,32. Thus, we reasoned that changes to peroxisomal import with age occur simultaneously with alterations in peroxisomal shape. However, in contrast to peroxisomal count, the GFP-SKL did not show changes in peroxisome morphology, that is, length or area with age (Fig. 2b–d). To better visualize peroxisomal morphology changes that coincide with the merging of acidic lysosomes, we utilized a RFP, RFP-SKL strain (Extended Data Fig. 2b), which, unlike GFP, remains unquenched with changes to pH. Similar to the GFP-SKL probe, we identified punctate RFP foci count in young, D1 animals (Fig. 2e). However, starting at age day 5, we saw increasingly tubulated structures, concurrently increasing extraperoxisomal RFP fluorescence, lengthening of peroxisomes and increasing peroxisomal area, potentially due to lysosome merging (Fig. 2f–h). To investigate whether peroxisome import loss coincides with lysosomal merging, we used a dual peroxisome RFP–lysosome GFP reporter and found that, although young animals showed punctate peroxisomes and some lysosomal RFP engulfment, only midlife animals exhibited a clear co-recurrence of peroxisomes and lysosomes merging over tubule-length distances, up to 2 μm, in the anterior gut, a region specialized for nutrient sensing (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Line scan analysis confirmed coinciding tubules, aligning with previous observations of tubular lysosomes during starvation or midlife nutrient alterations32 (Extended Data Fig. 2f). Notably, loss of peroxisome import occurred post-midlife (Fig. 2b–d), suggesting that tubular lysosome-mediated degradation may precede import decline but also coincide over specific temporal windows or regions31,33. Supporting this, peroxisome-like puncta persisted in aged animals where lysosomal engagement appeared minimal and these structures exhibited markedly reduced matrix protein import, as indicated by a specific loss of RFP retention in a dual peroxisomal import–RFP and peroxisome–membrane GFP reporter (Fig. 2j,k) and a distinct presence of peroxisome–membrane GFP puncta, even with aging, together indicating that, although lysosomal degradation contributes to peroxisome turnover, loss of import is a distinct contributor to dysfunction with age. Prior work in C. elegans and Drosophila spp. has shown that peroxisomal aging is marked by import competency loss and includes turnover33,34. Consistently, we found reduced lumen fluorescence of another lumen-resident enzyme, DAF-22, in peroxisome-shaped puncta with age (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h).

Last, to investigate whether PRX-5 loss is a cause for protein import failure in peroxisomes over age, we generated a separate strain with an amino-terminally 3xFLAG-tagged PRX-5. Western blotting confirmed a pronounced decline in PRX-5 protein levels from young, D1 animals to aged, D12 animals (Fig. 2l,m). Specifically, this robust decline occurred after midlife, supported by our previous data showing that, temporally, peroxisome import failure begins around midlife and is a distinct mechanism of peroxisome dysfunction.

Loss of peroxisomal import disrupts LD dynamics and fasting-induced lipolysis

To investigate the metabolic consequences of peroxisome import dysfunction with age, we probed its role in lipid catabolism using mutants with loss of function (LoF) for import genes prx-5 and prx-10. PRX-10 is known to regulate PRX-5 protein function through monoubiquitylation35 and, similar to prx-5 and other peroxins, prx-10 was induced by fasting in our RNA-seq dataset. In WT, prx-5 and prx-10 animals, we measured lipid stores under basal, fed conditions using fixed lipophilic NR staining. Supporting a direct role for peroxisome protein import in lipid utilization, fat stores were significantly increased in prx-5 and prx-10 mutants (Fig. 3a,b). We next visualized LDs in conjunction with peroxisomes through a strain co-expressing RFP-SKL and DHS-3::GFP, which labels LDs with a GFP fluorophore (Extended Data Fig. 3c). In both prx-5 and prx-10 animals, the RFP signal was extraperoxisomally diffuse16 (Fig. 3c,d). Importantly, in accordance with lipid staining, LD abundance was significantly increased in prx-5 and prx-10 mutants (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, the extreme intestinal LD volume per animal was significantly increased (Fig. 3f). These pathologically sized LDs were absent in prx-11 null animals, confirming that altered peroxisome import, not scission, underlies LD dynamic aberrations and formation of these superstructures (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Using lipid staining together with a feeding–fasting paradigm, we further identified that peroxisome import mutants, prx-5 and prx-10, are entirely refractory to fasting-induced lipolysis, displaying the marked characteristic of intracellular metabolic inflexibility, that is, the inability to mobilize lipids (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 3e).

To investigate whether peroxisome import loss over age induces pathological LDs, we used targeted protein degradation (TPD) via a degron-tagged PRX-5, initiating peroxisome import loss beginning at day 1 of adulthood38. Although in control animals we identified an age-associated increase across LD size, in animals lacking peroxisome import we identified gross alteration of LD dynamics with age. Around late life, day 8, we observed the emergence of maximal supersized LDs with PRX-5 loss, whereas these remained absent in control animals (Fig. 3h,i). Accordingly, fasting refractory LDs in the peroxisome import mutants also appeared supersized and irregular (Extended Data Fig. 3e), confirming that peroxisome import is an acute regulator of lipid mobilization with gross effects on LD dynamics. Although we identified peroxisome tubulation on fasting (Extended Data Fig. 3f), loss of prx-11, which regulates peroxisomal abundance, only affected peroxisome morphology (Extended Data Fig. 3f–i) without any effects on fasting-induced lipolysis31,39 (Extended Data Figs. 3e,j), together, suggesting that age-associated peroxisome import failure is uniquely linked to the onset of metabolic flexibility and represents a causal regulator of both lipolysis and LD dynamics that is rendered dysfunctional with age.

Previous studies have found that prx-5 or PEX5 loss significantly impairs lipolysis during fasting39. However, enhanced TG re-esterification post-fasting can potentially influence LD growth, independent of lipolysis. To distinguish between these possibilities, we inhibited dgat-2, the terminal step in re-esterification and TG resynthesis (Extended Data Fig. 4a). As expected, in WT animals, dgat-2 RNA interference (RNAi) suppressed refeeding-induced LD expansion, whereas prx-5 mutants harbored enlarged LDs that were, first, not mobilized during fasting and, second, completely unresponsive to dgat-2 inhibition during refeeding, consistent with defective lipolysis upstream of TG re-esterification and resynthesis driving LD persistence, together supporting previous findings showing that LD accumulation on peroxisome import loss is driven primarily by lipolysis defects rather than re-esterification (Extended Data Fig. 4a).

Fasting requires peroxisome-dependent lipid remodeling and PUFA catabolism

We next asked whether these supersized LDs are associated with the selective accumulation of specific lipids. To determine whether lipolysis defects during conditions of peroxisome import loss reflect altered utilization of specific lipid species, we performed lipidomic profiling in control animals and those with inducible peroxisome import loss via PRX-5 TPD, under fed and fasted conditions (Fig. 3j and Extended Data Fig. 4b). Differential fasting lipidomic profiles of significantly altered lipid species revealed distinct patterns of fasting-induced lipid remodeling in control and peroxisome import defective animals (Fig. 3k).

A limited number of lipid species increased on fasting in both control and PRX-5 loss animals and, in contrast, a significantly larger subset of lipid species decreased with fasting (Fig. 3k), consistent with lipid mobilization and utilization as an energy substrate during nutrient deprivation. It is interesting that fasting predominantly induced an enrichment of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylcholine plasmalogens in control animals (Extended Data Fig. 4c). In contrast, PRX-5-deficient animals accumulated VLCFA TGs (Extended Data Fig. 4d) during fasting, which are typically catabolized via peroxisomal β-oxidation. This reciprocal shift suggested a disruption in normal lipid utilization pathways in the absence of functional peroxisomes and prompted us to next investigate which specific TGs are refractory to utilization in animals with PRX-5 disruption.

We identified 413 lipids significantly decreased on fasting in control animals, whereas only 243 lipids decreased in animals with PRX-5 loss, indicating a diminished capacity for lipid mobilization and consumption in the absence of functional peroxisomes (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Table 2a). Next, to define how these lipids caused LD expansion, we focused on TGs, the most abundant lipids packaged into LDs. Analysis of fasting utilized TGs revealed that 207 TGs were utilized with fasting in control animals, in contrast to only 114 TGs in animals with PRX-5 loss (Supplementary Table 2b). We analyzed TGs uniquely utilized on fasting in control animals (Fig. 3l), because they represent TGs mobilized in a peroxisome-dependent manner and TGs inaccessible with loss of peroxisome import. A significant portion of 97 TGs was uniquely utilized with fasting only in control animals, representing the pool of peroxisome-mobilized TGs (Fig. 3m). Strikingly eight of the ten highest fasting-depleted TGs in control animals contained the VLCPUFA 20:5 (Fig. 3m), implicating peroxisome-dependent utilization of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA)-rich TGs (20:5 PUFA) as a key mechanism underlying effective lipid clearance during fasting.

Given that there is an established role of peroxisomes in breaking down VLCFAs via peroxisomal β-oxidation, our data aligned with this canonical function, showing that PRX-5 loss animals do not effectively utilize EPA-containing TGs. Strikingly, specific PUFAs such as EPA with three or more double bonds (n ≥ 3 PUFAs), which become enriched in PRX-5 loss animals, are highly unsaturated. Thus, n ≥ 3 PUFAs, including EPA, have been shown to promote LD coalescence via increasing LD fusion propensity40,41 and, beyond defective lipid breakdown, our data implicate n ≥ 3 PUFA enrichment and altered lipid biophysical properties as a secondary mechanism driving the emergence of oversized, lipolysis-resistant LDs induced on the absence of functional peroxisomes. We also found a remarkable increase in PUFA to monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) ratios with PRX-5 loss, suggesting a global shift toward PUFA enrichment induced on peroxisomal dysfunction (Fig. 3n). Together, this reveals that peroxisomal import loss not only impairs fatty acid catabolism but also reshapes the lipidome in ways that biophysically reinforce organelle dysfunction and metabolic inflexibility. Loss of peroxisome import over age might, thus, contribute to a shifting lipidome, enhancing metabolic inflexibility.

Mitochondrial fragmentation and bioenergetic collapse are induced on peroxisome import dysfunction

Although it has previously been hypothesized that peroxisomes and mitochondria also age interdependently, the initial step in this interorganelle failure has remained unclear23,42–48. Thus, to dissect the crosstalk between peroxisomes and mitochondria, we utilized PRX-5 TPD and monitored mitochondria using a tomm-20(1-49aa)::GFP label (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). Strikingly, we identified signatures of mitochondrial swelling within 48 h of PRX-5 degradation, suggesting an acute mitochondrial response on peroxisomal import loss (Fig. 4a). Next, we imaged mitochondria from young, D1 animals to aged, D8 animals with PRX-5 degradation initiated at day 1 of adulthood. As expected, in WT animals, we observed age-associated mitochondrial fragmentation, which has been widely described15,49–51. Importantly, in animals lacking peroxisome import, these effects appeared accelerated. Mitochondrial swelling and blebbing with heterogeneous mitochondria appeared as early as day 3, indicating that peroxisome collapse accelerates age-related mitochondrial damage (Fig. 4b). Animals lacking import-competent peroxisomes had, overall, fewer mitochondria but an increased proportion that were hyperfragmented and swollen (Fig. 4c–e). To investigate whether peroxisome failure induces mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction and consequently mitochondrial swelling, we quantified mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), ΔΨm, and its attenuation in response to FCCP with tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) in WT animals52 (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Although WT animals maintain relatively stable mitochondrial polarity in early adulthood (days 1 and 3), polarity decreases with age, as previously reported (Fig. 4f,g). However, peroxisome import loss induced robust mitochondrial membrane hyperpolarization across age (Fig. 4f,g). Critically, ΔΨm varies within a relatively narrow range, −130 to −180 mV, and premature deviation from this range, via either depolarization or hyperpolarization, can induce mitochondrial defects53,54. Indeed, MMP hyperpolarization has also been hypothesized to precede subsequent depolarization53,55,56. The mitochondrial hyperpolarization observed on peroxisome import failure with twofold maximal changes in ΔΨm represented maladaptive bioenergetics likely to induce age-associated mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction. Importantly, auxin alone did not affect TMRE staining and ΔΨm in WT animals, showing that hyperpolarization of mitochondria is a result of PRX-5 loss and peroxisomal dysfunction (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e).

As changes to ΔΨm have direct implications on mitochondrial metabolism, we next investigated functional alterations induced on peroxisome import loss. Using WT and PEX5-knockout (KO) cells we identified a distinct metabolic signature on PEX5-KO (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 5f,g). First, the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) on the mitochondrial oxidative source pyruvate was significantly decreased in PEX5-KO cells, corroborating perturbed mitochondrial function (Fig. 4i). PEX5-KO cells showed a marked reduction in basal and FCCP-induced maximal respiration, suggesting significantly reduced oxidative capacity (Fig. 4j–l). Second, in contrast to oxidative metabolism, extracellular acidification rate in PEX5-KO cells was significantly greater with glucose as the sole substrate, supporting increased reliance on extramitochondrial glycolysis and a tendency to bypass mitochondrial metabolism for ATP generation in PEX5-KO cells (Fig. 4m). Thus, in agreement with mitochondrial dysfunction, PEX5-KO cells harbored bioenergetically perturbed mitochondria with increased reliance on a glycolytic metabolism57.

We reciprocally observed a clear disruption of peroxisomal morphology in mutants where we selectively disrupted either mitochondrial fission (drp-1) or mitochondrial fusion (fzo-1). Given the shared fission machinery between the two organelles, drp-1 deletion resulted in distinctly elongated peroxisomes (Extended Data Fig. 5h). We next tested whether disruption of other peroxisomal functions beyond import could similarly impact mitochondrial integrity. Using inhibition of two key peroxisomal FAO enzymes, the terminal thiolase daf-22 and peroxisomal dehydrogenase dhs-28 via RNAi, we evaluated the effects on mitochondrial morphology. First, inhibition of daf-22 and dhs-28 yielded distinct effects on peroxisomal morphology, displaying that inhibiting peroxisomal β-oxidation at distinct steps disrupts their structural integrity uniquely, underscoring a link between peroxisomal form and function (Extended Data Fig. 5i). Second, we observed increased mitochondrial area over age, which is indicative of swelling, similar to peroxisomal import loss with daf-22 but not dhs-28 inhibition (Extended Data Fig. 5j).

It is interesting that the effect of daf-22 RNAi on peroxisomal morphology also mimicked a partial decrease in import (Extended Data Fig. 5i). Furthermore, its distinct effect on mitochondrial morphology suggested that potential accumulation of unmetabolized substrates at the terminal step of peroxisomal β-oxidation might more severely impact both peroxisomal and mitochondrial homeostasis with age (Extended Data Fig. 5i,j), overall suggesting that peroxisomal metabolism feeds back to regulate both peroxisomal and mitochondrial forms. Together, these findings establish peroxisome function, specifically import, as a critical upstream regulator of mitochondrial integrity and indicate that mitochondrial dynamics and metabolic defects commonly associated with age might be induced downstream of age-induced peroxisomal dysfunction.

DR preserves peroxisomal function and mitigates age-induced organelle collapse

Next, we tested whether the effects of DR on mitochondrial dynamics depended on peroxisomal state. We examined ΔΨm in WT and eat-2 (ad1116) mutants, a genetic DR model. We found that DR animals have reduced ΔΨm, suggesting that mitochondria may be hypometabolic under low-energy states58,59 (Fig. 5e,f). To test whether mitochondrial network maintenance during DR requires functional peroxisomes, we analyzed mitochondrial dynamics in WT and eat-2 animals at days 1, 5 and 11 of adulthood when adaptations to DR occur (Fig. 5g,h). DR failed to maintain mitochondrial count and network structure when peroxisome function was perturbed; we observed striking mitochondrial fragmentation and swelling in eat-2 animals on PRX-5 loss with age, supporting the key role of peroxisomes in maintaining a homeostatic mitochondrial network, which is required for metabolic adaptations for DR longevity (Fig. 5h).

Given this requirement of functional peroxisomes for DR, we examined whether genetic pathways regulating peroxisomal function are transcriptionally upregulated in this context. Indeed, expression of prx-5 was elevated in eat-2 (DR) animals compared to WT controls (Fig. 5i). Supporting cross-species conservation, a prior study in nonhuman primates similarly found upregulation of peroxisomal pathways under DR60. Conversely, chronological aging from youth to midlife in rodents was associated with a decline in PEX5 expression across key metabolically flexible tissues, liver and white adipose tissue (Fig. 5j), suggesting that DR and aging exert opposing, evolutionarily conserved effects on peroxisomal pathways (Fig. 5k).

As peroxisomal function is regulated by an array of genes encoding several peroxisome components, we analyzed expression across peroxisomal genes in WT and eat-2 (DR) animals in early (day 3) and late (day 15) life61. In addition to increased prx-5 expression under DR, using this dataset we found multiple peroxisomal genes selectively upregulated under DR. Notably, many of these genes remained elevated across ages, especially in late life (Figs. 5l,m and Extended Data Fig. 6), suggesting that DR sustains peroxisomal function by broadly maintaining transcriptional activity across multiple peroxisomal genes. Indeed, knockdown of either ether lipid synthesis gene, ads-1 or fard-1, also suppressed the longevity of eat-2 animals, in addition to peroxisome import regulator prx-5, whereas other pathways showed variable effects (Extended Data Fig. 7a–i), together suggesting that several peroxisomal pathways are transcriptionally maintained under DR and, specifically, peroxisomal ether lipid synthesis enables DR longevity via mechanisms that remain to be fully elucidated62.

Previous studies have directly linked prx-5 expression to NHR-49 activity64. To investigate whether NHR-49 regulates prx-5 expression, we utilized strains with either nhr-49 overexpression (Extended Data Fig. 8b) or reduced nhr-49 expression due to an LoF mutation. We found that NHR-49 regulated prx-5 expression bidirectionally, with nhr-49 overexpression increasing prx-5 levels and nhr-49 mutants failing to induce prx-5 expression in response to fasting (Fig. 6b and Extended Data Fig. 8c). Indeed, the hallmark phenotype of nhr-49 LoF mutants is decreased lipid mobilization and increased lipid stores, indicative of lipid dysregulation. Next, to confirm that NHR-49 can regulate prx-5 expression, we utilized a prx-5p::GFP transcriptional reporter and targeted nhr-49 using RNAi and visualized prx-5 induction29. In animals fed nhr-49 RNAi, we observed a significant reduction in prx-5 induction (Fig. 6c), showing that NHR-49 also acts upstream of prx-5 to control its transcriptional activation and positively regulates its expression, along with other key peroxisomal genes64 (Fig. 6d).

Given that NHR-49 nuclear localization reflects its transcriptionally active state, we hypothesized that age-related loss of NHR-49 activation may contribute to impaired induction of peroxisomal pathways during fasting, driving metabolic inflexibility65. Consistent with this, we found that NHR-49 nuclear enrichment decreased under AL conditions over age but was preserved under DR (eat-2), suggesting that sustained NHR-49 activation under low-energy states like DR maintains peroxisomal gene expression, protects against metabolic inflexibility during aging and underpins the presence of more import-competent peroxisomes under DR (Fig. 6e,f). Notably, the metabolic shift in AL animals occurred around midlife at day 5, when NHR-49 nuclear localization declined and coincided with hallmark features of metabolic inflexibility, including accumulation of lipolysis-resistant LDs (Fig. 6f and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e). Reciprocally, DR (eat-2) animals retained both NHR-49 nuclear localization and lipid mobilization capacity (Fig. 6f and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e), supporting a model in which preserved NHR-49 active state under DR sustains peroxisomal function and import competence into late life (Fig. 6d).

To test whether prx-5 expression could promote physiological benefits during aging and mitigate the age-related defects in metabolic flexibility, we examined its effect on longevity. Using the prx5-p::GFP transcriptional reporter, at day 1, we sorted animals into high and low GFP expressors as a proxy of prx-5 expression (Fig. 6g). We found that higher prx-5 expressors showed significantly increased lifespan compared to animals with low prx-5 expression (Fig. 6h). To validate this longevity phenotype, next we generated three independent prx-5 overexpression lines (Extended Data Fig. 8f). Notably, overexpression of prx-5 was also sufficient to extend the median and drove up to 24% maximal lifespan extension, showing that increasing prx-5 expression can itself promote longevity (Fig. 6i). Last, we explored whether prx-5 overexpression also mitigates age-associated organelle dysfunction and preserves metabolic flexibility. Indeed, prx-5 expression in aging animals attenuated LD expansion at midlife (Fig. 6j–l) and preserved mitochondrial architecture in late life, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 8g,h), consistent with observed temporal periods of organelle dysfunction, together highlighting peroxisomal function as a critical node maintaining organelle homeodynamics, preserving metabolic flexibility and demonstrating that restoring this function is sufficient to preserve organelle integrity and promote longevity.

Discussion

Here we identified peroxisomal protein import as an early and causal regulator of metabolic flexibility, organelle homeodynamics and longevity. Our findings position peroxisomes not as ancillary organelles in lipid metabolism but as upstream nodes in an interorganelle cascade that coordinates nutrient sensing, lipid remodeling and organelle resilience with age. Disruption of peroxisomal import precipitates lipolysis-resistant LD expansion and mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in an organelle homeostasis collapse that underlies age-associated metabolic inflexibility (Extended Data Fig. 9). Peroxisomes have traditionally been studied in the context of rare metabolic disorders or VLCFA catabolism defects66,67. Our data broaden this framework to aging and reveal that peroxisomes, particularly during fasting and low-energy states, play a critical role in both organelle and metabolic homeodyanmics68–70.

PRX-5 emerges as a key regulator of metabolic flexibility, specifically enriched in our DFC transcript cluster, with other genes regulating peroxisomal β-oxidation. Indeed, a previous proteomics analysis has also shown that PRX-5 protein levels and peroxisomal import decline over age33. Our data suggest a cascade of metabolic alterations emerging from age-associated PRX-5 loss, specifically inducing cellular lipolysis-resistance. PEX10 monoubiquitinates PEX5 on a conserved N-terminal, proximal cysteine residue to enable recycling, such that PEX5 can continue import activity71. Indeed, cysteine residues have also been shown to become increasingly oxidized over age, rendering them nonfunctional to regulatory modifications72. Therefore, mechanisms at both the transcript and the protein levels that might alter prx-5 induction, abundance and function possibly contribute to the decline in peroxisome metabolism over age30.

PEX5, the mammalian ortholog of PRX-5, has been shown to escort the neutral adipose TG lipase on to increased contact points between peroxisomes and LDs to regulate lipolysis39. In addition, adipose-specific loss of peroxisome function has been shown to impair fat thermogenesis, decrease energy expenditure in mice and increase diet-induced obesity, through specifically modulating mitochondrial fission73–76. These recent studies support our model, suggesting that peroxisomes play a previously unrecognized yet critical role in maintaining LD homeostasis. Indeed, we uncover that peroxisomal dysfunction not only impairs β-oxidation of VLCFAs but also reshapes the lipidome in ways that alter LD dynamics.

Although direct biochemical assessment of peroxisomal activity, particularly in aged animals, remains technically challenging, independent lipidomic studies have identified age-associated lipid signatures that align closely with our model of peroxisomal dysfunction over age. Notably, comparative lipidomic analyses have shown only selective accumulation of VLCFAs, including C20:2, C22:1, C22:5, C22:6 and C24:1, between D1 and D10 WT animals, closely mirroring the VLCFA profile observed on loss of peroxisomal import77. Similarly, another study reported that long-chain TG species, composed of LCFAs (for example, TG57:3, TG59:1 and TG58:3), increase with age, whereas, conversely, short-chain TGs selectively decrease in D10 animals compared with YA WT animals26, supporting age-associated lipid remodeling involving an impaired handling of VLCFAs, consistent with the model of declining peroxisomal function during aging. Lipids have been linked to different effects on healthy aging. Previous work has shown that increased LDs are beneficial for longevity, yet LDs have also been shown to accumulate with age and in conditions of systemic metabolic dysregulation16,78–80, together confounding their effects on metabolic health. Our data support LDs being dynamic organelles interacting with other metabolic counterparts and, more than their number, their size, volume and enriched lipids might be critically indicative of their effect.

The mitochondrial fragmentation and bioenergetic collapse observed on peroxisomal impairment underscore the interdependence of organelle health. Mitochondria rely on tightly regulated lipid flux and shared metabolic networks with peroxisomes to maintain membrane composition, redox balance and ATP production80. Our work adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that aging may involve a domino-like failure of organelle systems, in which the loss of a single metabolic function, such as peroxisomal import, propagates dysfunction across cellular compartments. A unique insight from our data is that mitochondrial hyperpolarization arises on peroxisome dysfunction. Although increased mitochondrial potential is often equated with increased energetic capacity, emerging evidence suggests that sustained hyperpolarization can represent both a maladaptive and a pre-degenerative state. In this context, we propose that mitochondrial hyperpolarization might represent an early and sensitive response to disrupted lipid flux, potentially serving as a bioenergetic warning that precedes mitochondrial dysfunction81–83.

From an aging theory perspective, our data align with concepts of homeodynamic resilience, proposing that longevity depends not just on reducing damage but also on preserving the dynamic adaptability of intracellular networks84,85. The preservation of peroxisomal function in DR animals, coupled with its requirement for DR-mediated longevity, suggests that low-energy states maintain organelle coordination by sustaining core molecular regulators such as NHR-49 or PPARα. This supports a model in which DR extends lifespan by buffering against age-induced collapse in organelle and lipid homeostasis, a form of hormetic adaptation that enhances systemic metabolic flexibility. Similarly, the DR-mimetic and AMP-activated protein kinase activator, metformin, simultaneously increases peroxisome abundance86, together highlighting how conserved metabolic regulators such as PPARs, AMP-activated protein kinase and others might regulate peroxisome function in varying nutrient states and during aging.

Our findings show that peroxisomes are central orchestrators in the complex interorganelle network regulating lipid homeostasis and that loss of peroxisome function drives age-associated metabolic inflexibility. Given the conservation of peroxisome function and lipid metabolism across species, our findings highlight a critical cellular mechanism regulating how efficiently youthful organisms mobilize lipids, originating at the peroxisome. Future studies will be needed to test whether augmenting peroxisomal import can delay metabolic dysfunction in mammalian aging or serve as a therapeutic strategy in age-related diseases characterized by lipid imbalance and mitochondrial failure. In summary, this study identifies peroxisomal function as a critical determinant of organelle coordination and metabolic plasticity during aging.

Methods

All experiments were performed in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health (protocol no. IS00001512). C57BL/6 mice, young (<4 months) and aged (<23 months) were acquired from the National Institute on Aging (NIA).

C. elegans strains and husbandry

N2 WT, eat-2 (ad1116), MH5239 (prx-5(ku517)), SOZ259 (prx-10(ssd68)), VS10 (hjIs37 (vha-6p::mRFP-PTS1 + Cbr-unc-119(+))) C. elegans strains were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetic Center, which is funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Office of Research Infrastructure Programs (grant no. P40 OD010440). The strain vha-7p::mKate2::linker::pxmp-4 was provided by S. G. Rolland. The strain ABR161 (vha-6p::mRFP–SKL;dsh-3p::dhs-3::GFP) was provided by A. Brunet. Worms were maintained on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) seeded with Escherichia coli OP50-1 and maintained at 20 °C. All strains are described in Supplementary Table 3.

Home-cage measurements of free-living, whole-body metabolism

C57BL/6 male mice, young (n = 8, age 4 months) and aged (n = 8, age 23 months), were acclimated for 24 h and then monitored for 60 h in an environmentally controlled Sable Systems Promethion Core metabolic cage system fitted with indirect open circuit calorimetry, food consumption monitors and activity monitors. The RER = VCO2/VO2 was plotted using CalR v1.3 (calrapp.org). Animals were maintained on a Purina 5L79 (6722) chow diet.

Microbe strains

OP50-1 bacteria were cultured overnight in Luria–Bertani broth at 37 °C, after which 100 μl of liquid culture was seeded on NGM plates to grow for 2 d at room temperature. Unless otherwise noted, worms were grown at 20 °C on the strain E. coli OP50-1 for all experiments.

Solid plate-based DR assays

Solid DR assays were performed as previously described87. Briefly, AL plates were prepared with a bacterial concentration of 100 ml of 1011 colony-forming units (c.f.u.) ml−1 and DR plates with 200 ml of 108 c.f.u. ml−1 of OP50-1 bacterial concentration. Then 100 μl of a kanamycin and carbenicillin solution was added after the plates were completely dried (5 mg ml−1 and 10 mg ml−1, respectively) to arrest bacterial growth at respective AL and DR concentrations. These plates were prepared in advance and stored at 4 °C. 5-Fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine (FUDR, 100 μl of 1 mg ml−1 solution in M9) was added on top of the bacterial lawn 24 h before the worms were introduced to the plates for lifespans. FUDR was utilized only for lifespans involving dietary restriction and the treatment was the same across AL and DR conditions. Survival was scored every 1–2 d and a worm was deemed dead when unresponsive to three taps on the head and tail. Worms were censored due to contamination on the plate, leaving the NGM, eggs hatching inside the adult or loss of vulval integrity during reproduction. FUDR was used to prevent progeny production during lifespan experiments involving DR. Although FUDR can have known effects on germline physiology, its use was restricted to conditions where progeny prevention was essential for assay reproducibility. Non-lifespan assays, including metabolic flexibility and imaging, were performed without FUDR wherever possible.

C. elegans feeding assays

To analyze C. elegans feeding, 0.5-µm yellow–green GFP beads were used to visualize intake and feeding88. Before the feeding assay, NGM plates were seeded with 100 µl of 0.5 µm GFP beads diluted 1:400 in Luria–Bertani broth. On the next day, the worms were picked from their respective plates and washed 3× with 1 ml of M9 at 500g and transferred on to unseeded NGM plates. Washed animals from the unseeded NGM plates were then picked on to NGM plates seeded with diluted 0.5-µm GFP beads overnight. Animals were allowed to graze on the GFP bead lawn for 3 h at 20 °C. After 3 h, animals were rapidly anesthetized using 20 mM tetramisole in M9 and imaged at ×20 magnification The mean fluorescence intensity of GFP was then calculated using Image J or FIJI and normalized to animal size to measure direct feed intake into the animals.

Auxin-inducibleTPD

Strain PRX-5::degron/AID (auxin-inducible degradation) was generated using CRISPR gene editing and outcrossed. The strain was then crossed into a strain expressing ruby-tagged TIR1 under the control of the somatic eft-3 promoter. To induce degradation of PRX-5, animals were transferred at day 1 of adulthood on to NGM plates containing auxin at a final concentration of 0.15 mM auxin.

Western blotting

Around 500 adult worms were used per sample for each appropriate age per replicate. Worms were collected in M9 buffer and washed 3× using centrifugation. Liquids were removed after centrifugation and samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen. For worm lysis, radioimmunoprecipitation buffer containing protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 8340) and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, cat. no. 04906845001) was added to each sample at the same volume as the worm pellet. Worms were lysed via sonication (Qsonica, cat. no. Q700). Protein concentration was measured using Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. PI23227) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, 4× Leammli sample buffer (BioRad, cat. no. 1610747) was added to denature the proteins and samples were heated to 95 °C for 5 min. Samples containing 30–40 mg of protein were loaded to 10–20% Tris(glycine) gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. XP10205BOX) for sodium dodecyl sulfate–poly(acrylamide gel) electrophoresis. Proteins were transferred to poly(vinylidene fluoride) membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. LC2005) and blocked with 5% nonfat milk powder in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20. The following primary antibodies were used at the indicated dilutions: monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 antibody produced in mice (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. F3165-1MG, 1:1,000) and β-actin antibody produced in rabbits (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 4967, 1:1,000). The secondary antibody was α-mouse, immunoglobulin, G horseradish peroxidase linked (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 7076S, 1:1,000) and α-rabbit, immunoglobulin G, horseradish peroxidase linked (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 7074, 1:1,000). Antibody signals were developed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 34577) and bands were quantified using Fiji ImageJ.

Microinjection and CRISPR–Cas9-triggered homologous recombination

All CRISPR edits and insertions required to generate the strains were performed using the previously described CRISPR protocol89. Briefly, homology repair templates were amplified by PCR, using primers that introduced a minimum stretch of 35-bp homology at both ends. Single-stranded oligo donors (ssODNs) were also used as repair templates. CRISPR injection mix reagents were added in the following order: 0.375 µl of Hepes, pH 7.4 (200 mM), 0.25 µl of KCl (1 M), 2.5 µl of trans-activating CRISPR RNA (4 µg µl−1), 0.6 µl of dpy-10 CRISPR RNA (2.6 μg μl−1), 0.25 μl of dpy-10 ssODN (500 ng μl−1) and PCR or ssODN repair template(s) up to 500 ng µl−1 final in the mix. Water was added to reach a final volume of 8 µl. Then 2 µl of purified Cas9 (12 μg μl−1) was added at the end, mixed by pipetting, spun for 2 min at 13,000 rpm and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Mixes were microinjected into the germline of D1 adult hermaphrodite worms using standard methods.

Fixed NR imaging and analysis

NR imaging was performed as previously described90. Briefly, at least 100 worms per condition, fed or 12-h fasted, were buffer washed to remove bacteria and fixed with 40% isopropanol before staining with NR stain for 3 h at room temperature. Post-staining, three buffer washes were used to remove excess NR stain. Animals were mounted on a 2% agarose pad and imaged under the bright-field and GFP channel using identical exposure for all conditions imaged. Images were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Imager.M2 microscope equipped with an ApoTome.2 system and an AxioCam MRc camera. The fluorescence intensity was normalized for each animal to their selected area and arbitrary units or pixels were obtained. For feeding and fasting NR assays, animals were either freely (AL) fed or fasted overnight to induce lipid utilization.

Confocal microscopy of mounted worms

For imaging, D1 adults or appropriate aged adults were mounted on 2% agarose pads and anesthetized with 20 mM tetramisole in M9. For confocal microscopy of organelles such as peroxisomes, mitochondria and LDs, images were taken in the Sabri Ulker imaging lab using a Yokogawa CSU-X1 spinning confocal disk system (Andor Technology) combined with a Nikon Ti-E inverted microscope (Nikon Instruments). Images were taken using a ×100 objective lens, Zyla cMOS (Zyla 4.2 Plus USB3) camera and 488-nm laser for GFP or 561-nm laser for RFP.

Image analysis for peroxisomes and mitochondria

The images were analyzed using macros called PeoxiMAPR for peroxisomes and MitoMAPR for mitochondria in ImageJ or FIJI91. Briefly, representative regions of interest were selected from the tissue of interest, intestine for peroxisomes and muscle for mitochondria, processed and filtered using the CLAHE plugin with a median filter and unsharp mask to increase the local contrast and particle distinctiveness. The region of interest was then converted to a binary image to generate a two-dimensional skeleton using the Skeletonize3D plugin. The skeleton image was then dissected using the AnalyzeSkeleton function in FIJI to generate tagged and labeled skeletons. Values obtained from AnalyzeSkeleton functions were used to quantify aspects of peroxisomal and mitochondrial networks, respectively. Between images, all parameters were kept constant to ensure unbiased analysis through the macros. For extraperoxisomal RFP diffusion, a uniform area of 4 × 4 µm2 was drawn in distal intestinal cells containing no fluorescence from peroxisome punctate and RFP intensity was quantified, summed and plotted for each worm to minimize signal masking from RFP foci (peroxisomes).

Imaging and analysis of LDs

For LD imaging, the mid-intestinal region was imaged using a ×100 objective lens and Zyla cMOS camera. The images were taken using the same exposure time and/or laser power. LD numbers were analyzed in ImageJ or FIJI by applying the same threshold to all images and manually counting the LDs in the defined area. The LD diameter was analyzed in ImageJ or FIJI by applying the same threshold to all images and manually measuring the diameter of all the LDs in focus. LD volume estimate measurements were then made from the LD diameter.

Cell culture

HepG2 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (cat. no. HB-8065) and cultured in surface-treated dishes in high glucose (4,500 mg l−1) Eagle’s minimum essential medium supplemented with 1 mM glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. For fasting assays, HepG2 cells were cultured in Hank’s balanced salt solution with Ca2+ and Mg2+ and 10 mM Hepes. Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline at respective fasting collection times, plates were rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen immersion and RNA was isolated using the TRIzol method.

A549 control and PEX5-KO cells were obtained from C. Metallo’s laboratory at the Salk Institute for Biological Sciences. A549 cells were cultured in American Type Culture Collection-formulated F-12K medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Real-time quantitative PCR

RNA was isolated from HepG2 cells under fed or respective fasting timepoints using the TRIzol method68. Complementary DNA was synthesized from 30 μg of RNA with SuperScript VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 11755050) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then 5 ng of cDNA was used as template for each quantitative (q)PCR reaction. Each qPCR probe was run along with the control probe as well as no reverse transcriptase (RT) samples (cDNA synthesis reaction set up with RNA but without RT) to be able to detect genomic DNA contamination, if any. No significant genomic DNA contamination was found for any runs. The following probes were used to quantify gene expression on a 96-well plate format using Taqman Universal Master Mix II (Life Technologies, cat. no. 4440040). Taqman probes used to target each gene of interest are as follows: ACOX1: Hs01074240_m1; PEX5: Hs00165604_m1; PEX6: Hs01122110_m1; PEX10: Hs00538216_m1; PEX12: Hs01558848_g1; PEX13: Hs00968434_m1; PEX14: Hs00992867_m1; and PEX19: Hs01005928_m1. Actin was used as the control probe to normalize gene expression: ACTB: Hs99999903_m1.

High-resolution respirometry assays

A Seahorse Bioscience, XF-96 extracellular, flux analyzer was used to monitor mitochondrial oxygen consumption similar to how it was described in refs. 92,93: 4 × 104 cells per well were plated on to seahorse cell culture v3 PS plates in their respective culture medium overnight and allowed to attach. A three-injection mito-stress test protocol in the presence of 10 mM pyruvate in Agilent seahorse medium was administered the following day, with three replicate measurements taken between each injection. Each replicate consisted of a 1-min mix step, a 1-min equilibration step and a 3-min measurement step. After basal measurements were acquired, oligomycin inhibited (1.5 µM final concentration, port A injection) and FCCP stimulated (1 µM final concentration, port B injection), rotenone + antimycin A (5 µM + 4 µM final concentration) was used to measure mitochondrial carbohydrate oxidation. The OCR was normalized to protein per well quantified using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit after respirometry completion.

MMP (ΔΨm) measurements using TMRE

TMRE assays were first performed to validate appropriate MMP staining. A 20 mM TMRE stock in dimethyl sulfoxide was used to make a final 10 µM dilution in filtered S-basal buffer. NGM plates previously seeded with 100 µl of OP50-1, were seeded with 100 µl of 10 µM TMRE. Appropriate aged animals were transferred to TMRE or control plates overnight. On the next day, animals were moved to NGM plates without any TMRE for 1 h before imaging. Animals were then anesthetized with 20 mM tetramisole in M9 and imaged using a ×63 objective lens, Zyla cMOS (Zyla 4.2 Plus USB3) camera and a 561-nm laser for TMRE fluorescence or a 488-nm laser for GFP nonspecific background detection. The effect of FCCP on depolarization was also confirmed using the same TMRE staining protocol as above, but transferring animals on to NGM OP50-1 plates with or without TMRE in addition to 25 µM FCCP or control treatment on the top of the plates. All images were collected at the same exposure time and processed in the same manner. For each image, the animal area was selected in ImageJ or FIJI and denoised and deconvolved for better visualization of TMRE-stained mitochondria. The fluorescence intensity of TMRE was then quantified and normalized to WT controls to obtain fold differences in TMRE staining to represent proportional changes in the MMP. Normalization did not affect the outcome of the experiments.

RNA-seq and differential gene expression analysis

RNA collection

Four biological replicates were collected for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in the following manner: N2, WT animals were grown on OP50-1 bacteria on 10-cm NGM plates to YA and D8 age. On collection days, fed worms were washed from their respective plates with OP50-1 using 15 ml of M9 + 0.01% Tween. The fed population was washed rapidly 3× with M9 buffer using centrifugation. Worms were then reduced to a minimal volume and separated on to NGM OP50-1 seeded (fed) plates and NGM unseeded (fasted) plates and monitored briefly for movement and grazing. After 6 h of fed and fasted treatment, worms were washed off fed and fasted plates using M9 + 0.01% Tween, washed 3× using M9 buffer, pelleted and flash-frozen in 600 μl of QIAzol in liquid nitrogen. Four individual biological replicate samples were thus collected on separate days from separate thaws of N2 strains. Samples were stored at −80 °C. RNA extractions were performed using QIAGEN RNeasy Mini Kit and eluted in RNase-free H2O. Samples were sent to the Harvard University Bauer Core Facility for library preparation and RNA-seq.

RNA-seq

Libraries were prepared using a SciClone G3 NGSx workstation (Perkin Elmer) and the Kapa mRNA HyperPrep kit (Roche Applied Science). Poly(adenylated) messenger RNAs were captured using oligo(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads (Kapa mRNA HyperPrep kit, Roche Sequencing) from 500 ng of total RNA on a Perkin Elmer SciClone G3 NGSx automated workstation. Poly(adenylated) mRNA samples were immediately fragmented to 200–300 bp using heat and magnesium. First-strand synthesis was completed using random priming followed by second-strand synthesis and A-tailing. A dUTP was incorporated into the second strand to allow strand-specific sequencing of the library. Libraries were enriched and indexed using nine cycles of amplification (Kapa mRNA HyperPrep kit, Roche Sequencing) with PCR primers, which included dual 8-bp index sequences to allow for multiplexing (IDT for Illumina unique dual 8-bp indices). Excess PCR reagents were removed through magnetic bead-based cleanup using Kapa Pure magnetic beads on a SciClone G3 NGSx workstation (Perkin Elmer). The resulting libraries were assessed using a 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies) and quantified by qPCR (Roche Sequencing). Libraries were pooled and sequenced on one Illumina NovaSeq SP flow cell using paired-end, 75-bp reads.

Differential gene expression analysis

Raw reads were examined for quality using FastQC (v0.11.5) to ensure that library generation and sequencing were suitable for further analysis. To perform additional quality checks, all reads were aligned to Ensembl assembly of the C. elegans (accession no. PRJNA13758) genome WBcel235_release104 using STAR (v2.7.0)57. Alignments were checked for biases, evenness of coverage, ribosomal RNA content, genomic content of alignments, complexity and other quality checks using a combination of FastQC, Qualimap and MultiQC. To quantify the abundance of reads corresponding to each transcript, alignment and quantification were performed using Salmon (1.4.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Wald’s test and significant genes were obtained using a FDR threshold of 0.01. Comparisons of the differential gene expression between the various conditions was performed using DESeq2 from tximport with a P-value cutoff of 0.01. PCA was performed using DESeq2.

Likelihood ratio test

To derive gene clustering information to identify subsets of genes that all change with fasting in young, but not aged, animals, LRT culturing was used. DEGs obtained using a FDR > 0.01 were separated into clusters based on similar expression profiles across YA fed, YA fasted, D8 fed and D8 fasted using a function from the DEGreport (v1.20.2) package. Gene lists for each cluster were used as input to the R bioconductor package clusterProfiler (v3.14.3). As a quality check, normalized counts were also manually plotted for select genes in clusters to ensure that changes in the fold-change followed the appropriate clustering profile.

Lipidomics

Methyl-tert-butyl ether extraction was used for fast extraction of total lipid extracts from 2,000 fed and 12-h fasted N2 worms each. A 12-h fast was chosen to induce changes to the lipidome subsequent to acute transcriptional changes. Three biological replicates of N2 worms, both fed and fasted, were collected for the lipidomics analysis. The extracted lipids were reconstituted in solvent volumes proportional to protein concentrations for normalization. Supernatant, 100 μl, was used for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-based lipidomic profiling. Lipid peaks passing quality control were used for LipidSearch-based annotation.

TG assay

Approximately 2,000 D1 and D8 adult, fed and 8-h fasted worms were harvested in three independent biological replicates using M9 buffer, washed 3× with M9 buffer, pelleted and resuspended in 75 μl of 1% NP-40 lysis buffer. Samples were transferred to Revvity ceramic bead tubes (cat. no. 2175318) and sonicated for 3 min total at 60% amplitude. Sonicated samples were transferred to Eppendorf tubes and incubated at 90 °C for 5 min to solubilize TGs. After solubilization, samples were cooled down at room temperature for 5 min followed by centrifugation at 21,000g for 10 min. Aqueous phases of the samples were collected and TGs were measured using the Randox Triglyceride assay kit (cat. no. TR 210), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total TGs were quantified based on a quinoneimine dye-based reaction at 500 nm using a plate reader.

NHR-49 imaging

Control and eat-2 mutant C. elegans expressing an extrachromosomal array of NHR-49::GFP translational reporter (kindly provided by A. Ghazi, University of Pittsburgh Medical Center, Pittsburgh) were synchronized by bleaching. Animals were aged by daily transfer on to NGM, semi-solid agar plates freshly seeded with OP50-1 E. coli. On days 1, 3, 5, 8 and 10 of adulthood, animals were anesthetized using 10× tetramisole diluted in M9 buffer and mounted on 2% agarose pads on glass slides. To quantify NHR-49::GFP nuclear localization, z-projections of entire worms were generated and the nuclear signal was manually scored for each animal. Image brightness and contrast were adjusted in Adobe Photoshop for generating micrographs.

Lipid extraction for LC–MS

Then 4 biological replicates of 2,500 animals per sample for each fed and fasted condition were rapidly collected, washed 3× times with M9 buffer, pelleted by centrifugation into beadmill homogenizer tubes (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 19-627) and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. 2:2:1.8 chloroform, methanol and water by volume was used for worm extraction. Samples were homogenized in ice-cold 1:1 chloroform (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 1024441000) to methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A456) such that final extraction solvent-to-sample ratios were 2,500 C. elegans to 1 ml of extraction solvent. After solvent addition, extracts were vortexed for 10 s, agitated in a liquid nitrogen-chilled, bead mill homogenizer for 30 s at 6 m s−1, sonicated in a water bath for 5 min and incubated on wet ice for 30 min. Incubation was followed by the addition of 0.9 volumes of water to achieve the final 2:2:1.8 ratio. Samples were then vortexed for an additional 10 s and followed with incubation on wet ice for 10 min. This was followed by a 10-min centrifugation at 17,000g and 4 °C, in which polar metabolite and nonpolar lipid fractions separate so the liquid nonpolar layer sits at the bottom, the liquid polar layer sits on top and the solid protein pellet rests as the interphase between the two liquid layers. For lipidomics analysis, 725 C. elegans equivalents were aliquoted from the bottom nonpolar layer, dried under vacuum and resuspended in 50 µl of ice-cold 1:1 acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A955) to isopropanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A461). Deuterated PE 14:0 (14:0 PE-d54, Avanti Research, cat. no. 860371P) was added as an internal standard to all lipidomic samples at a final concentration of 3 µg ml−1 to monitor instrument performance across each injection. For metabolomics analysis, 2,030 C. elegans equivalents were aliquoted from the top polar layer and centrifuged a second time to pellet any residual solid particles. Finally, 1,916 C. elegans equivalents were aliquoted from the second centrifugation, dried under vacuum and resuspended in 50 µl of LC–MS-grade water (for ion-paired analysis) or 50 µl of 1:1 water to acetonitrile (for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) analysis). Deuterated glutamate (2,3,3,4,4-D5, 98%, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, cat. no. DLM-556) was added as an internal standard to all metabolomic samples at a final concentration of 25 µg ml−1.

LC–MS lipidomics

Samples were analyzed with a Thermo Vanquish dual LC system utilizing two alternating methods, referred to as HILIC and lipidomics, coupled to an Orbitrap ID-X (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a heated electrospray ionization source in positive mode. Then 2 µl of each sample was injected. HILIC used an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide Column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm, Waters, cat. no. 176001909) combined with an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide VanGuard Pre-column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 5 mm, Waters, cat. no. 186004799). Mobile phase A consisted of 90% LC–MS-grade water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. W6), 10% LC–MS-grade acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A955), 0.2% LC–MS-grade acetic acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A11350) and 10 mM ammonium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 73594) and mobile phase B consisted of 90% LC–MS-grade acetonitrile, 10% LC–MS-grade water, 0.2% LC–MS-grade acetic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate. The column temperature was kept at 40 °C, the flow rate was held at 0.4 ml min−1 and the 14-min chromatography gradient was as follows: 0–9 min from 95% B to 70% B; 9–13 min from 70% B to 30% B; and 13–14 min held at 30% B. A 30-min wash gradient was run between every injection (in parallel with lipidomics chromatography) to flush the column and re-equilibrate solvent conditions as follows: 0–20 min held at 65% B and 0.4 ml min−1; 20–21 min from 65% B to 100% B and held at 0.4 ml min−1; 21-22 min held at 100% B and ramp to 0.8 ml min−1; 22-28 min held at 100% B and 0.8 ml min−1; 28–28.5 min from 100% B to 95% B and held at 0.8 ml min−1; 28.5–29.5 min held at 95% B and 0.8 ml min−1; 29.5–29.6 min held at 95% B and ramp to 0.4 ml min−1; and 29.6–30 min held at 95% B and 0.4 ml min−1.

Lipidomics chromatography used a reversed-phase Accucore C30 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 27826-152130) combined with an Accucore C30 guard column (2.6 μm, 2.1 mm × 10 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 27826-012105). Mobile phase A consisted of 60% LC–MS-grade acetonitrile, 40% LC–MS-grade water, 0.1% LC–MS-grade formic acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A117) and 10 mM ammonium formate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 70221) and mobile phase B consisted of 90% LC–MS-grade isopropanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A461), 8% LC–MS-grade acetonitrile, 2% LC–MS-grade water, 0.1% LC–MS-grade formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. The column temperature was kept at 50 °C, the flow rate was held at 0.4 ml min−1 and the 30-min chromatography gradient was as follows: 0–1 min held at 25% B, 1–3 min from 25% B to 40% B, 3–19 min from 40% B to 75% B, 19–20.5 min from 75% B to 90% B, 20.5–28 min from 90% B to 95% B, 28–28.1 min from 95% B to 100% B and 28.1–30 min held at 100% B. A 14-min wash gradient was run between every injection (in parallel with HILIC) to flush the column and re-equilibrate solvent conditions as follows: 0–6 min held at 100% B, 6–8 min ramp from 100% B to 25% B and 8–14 min held at 25% B.

For HILIC, the mass spectrometer parameters were: source voltage 3,500 V, sheath gas 60, auxiliary (aux) gas 19, sweep gas 1, ion transfer tube temperature 300 °C and vaporizer temperature 250 °C. Full scan data were collected using the orbitrap with a scan range of 105–1,000 m/z at a resolution of 120,000 and RF lens at 35%. Data-dependent MS2 fragmentation was induced in the orbitrap using assisted higher-energy collisional dissociation collision energies at 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100%, as well as with collision-induced dissociation (CID) at a collision energy of 35%. For both MS2 fragmentations, orbitrap resolution was 30,000, the isolation window was 1.5 m/z and total cycle time was 0.6 s. For lipidomics, the mass spectrometer parameters were: source voltage 3,250 V, sheath gas 40, aux gas 10, sweep gas 1, ion transfer tube temperature 300 °C and vaporizer temperature 275 °C. Full scan data were collected using the orbitrap with a scan range of 200–1,700 m/z at a resolution of 240,000 and RF lens at 45%. Data-dependent MS2 fragmentation was induced in the orbitrap using assisted higher-energy collisional dissociation collision energies at 15%, 30%, 45%, 75% and 110% as well as with CID at a collision energy of 35%. For both MS2 fragmentations, orbitrap resolution was 15,000 and the isolation window was 1.5 m/z. An m/z 184 mass trigger, indicative of phosphatidylcholines, was used for CID fragmentation. Data-dependent MS3 fragmentation was induced in the ion trap with the scan rate set at rapid using CID at a collision energy of 35%. MS3 scans were triggered by specific acyl chain losses for detailed analysis of monoglycerides, DGs and TGs. Total cycle time was 1.5 s. Lipid identifications were assigned using LipidSearch (v5.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was used to generate a compound list, and lipid peak areas were quantified in Skyline as described above.

Statistics and reproducibility

Fiji ImageJ was used for image quantifications. Graphpad Prism was used for statistical analyses. Statistical details of experiments are included in the figure legends. The log(rank) method was used for survival curve analyses. An LRT was used for RNA-seq analyses. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for experimental statistical analyses. For all experiments, P < 0.05 was considered significant. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. No method of randomization was used to assign experimental groups. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
