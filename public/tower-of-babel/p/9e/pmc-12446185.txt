A literature-based dataset on volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions from multiple grapevine varieties during berry ripening
Authors: Giulia Carriero, Maria Roberta Bruno, Roberta Calone, Simone Bregaglio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Volatile organic compounds (VOC) emitted by plants are valuable mediators of intra- and interspecific communication within ecosystems. These compounds play an important role in plant physiology and ecology, especially for defence responses, communication with neighbouring individuals, and attraction of beneficial organisms. Therefore, VOC are fundamental to plant survival, especially for facing biotic (such as pests and pathogens) and abiotic stressors (especially temperature extremes, drought, and flood). Grapevine plant (Vitis vinifera L.) emits a diverse array of VOC compounds such as monoterpenes, C13-norisoprenoids, alcohols, and aldehydes, and other secondary metabolites. These compounds play an important role in determining the sensory qualities of grapes and wine, thereby directly impacting agricultural and enological outcomes. Grapevine VOC have been detected in various organs, especially in foliage and berries and their biosynthesis and emission patterns are influenced by numerous factors such as plant genotype, developmental stage, environmental conditions, and even diurnal variation. Understanding the composition and regulation of these emissions is increasingly important in the context of climate change, which alters plant metabolism and may affect both grapevine health and the quality of grape-derived products. VOC profiles are thus emerging as promising indicators of plant physiological status and potential predictors of yield and fruit quality. To address this, we conducted a comprehensive review and synthesis of existing literature focusing on VOC emissions from grapevine berries. The resulting dataset consolidates information on VOC profiles from both pulp and skin tissues across various phenological stages, as well as from grape must. It includes about 800 unique VOC, systematically categorized into 14 chemical classes, along with aroma descriptors for 184 of these compounds. This extensive compilation offers a detailed and structured view of grapevine VOC diversity and distribution. The database we present provides a valuable knowledge base for functional, ecological, and physiological research, as well as for modeling studies. Furthermore, it offers insights into the biosynthetic pathways of VOC and their role in grape aroma chemistry.

Background

Grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) is one of the most cultivated fruit crops in the world for wine production and fresh consumption [1]. There are endless grapevine varieties, each characterized by different morphological features such as leaves, flowers, and fruits which can vary in shape, colour and flavour [2]. During fruit development, grapes undergo a series of biochemical transformations, including the synthesis of VOC that contribute to their aroma profile [[3], [4], [5]]. Understanding VOC emission is essential for predicting wine quality, as many VOC compounds are present in trace amounts [6]. Today, only a few studies [[7], [8], [9]] have reported comprehensive datasets on VOC emissions from different grape varieties, especially during ripening as VOC compounds have very low sensory detection thresholds, and their quantitative analysis is challenging due to their complex chemical composition. Given the analytical difficulty of characterizing VOC, the scarcity of complete data, and the need for deeper insight into grapevine physiology, a curated dataset compiling current knowledge on VOC emissions from different grapevine varieties during ripening process would be highly valuable for both research and practical applications. It allows scientists to assess how genotype, environmental factors, and agricultural practices influence grape aroma and wine quality [10].

Data Description

The dataset is publicly available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16410077 and comprises four sheets each containing four tables detailing information on VOC emissions from various red and white grape varieties during different ripening stages. The data were compiled through a literature review of scientific publications and is released as a Microsoft Excel file (extension .xlsx). Additionally, table and figures presented in the paper were generated with R statistical software (R Development Core Team, http://www.r-project.org), and a dedicated script is released for each table. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data selection

The data presented in this study were extracted from peer-reviewed scientific articles identified through SCOPUS. From a systematic review, 40 studies were selected as sources of data on grape-derived VOC emissions and aroma profiles. Studies were selected using keywords such as 'grape', 'VOC emissions', and 'fruit ripening', covering approximately 30 years of data (1995–2022), with the aim of including as many white and red grape varieties as possible. Only articles reporting numerical values for VOC emissions (mean ± SD) from grape berries (specifically from skin, pulp, and must) were included. The number of replicates from original studies was recorded to allow users to assess the statistical robustness of each measurement (see the ‘Metadata’ -Table 1- and the ‘Data’ sheet). Additional inclusion criteria required the availability of information on ripening stages, publication year, country, and experimental site. When geographical coordinates (latitude and longitude) were not explicitly reported, they were inferred from the description of the location of the experiment using Google map. These geographic data allow for a more precise characterization of the experimental area and enable analysis of the influence of climatic conditions on grape VOC emissions. General information related to each study was cleaned and standardized in terms of format, including variables such as publication year, country, and experimental conditions. All emission data were carefully verified for consistency and converted to a common unit of measurement (µg kg⁻¹) before being included in the final dataset. This harmonization step ensured the comparability of data across different studies and experimental setups. The workflow and methodological steps followed in this study are illustrated in Fig. 1. In total, 17 red and 20 white varieties from nine countries (Italy, France, Spain, Portugal, USA, Australia, Canada, China, and Turkey) were included. The distinction between white and red grapevine varieties was included to examine differences in VOC emissions related to berry color. Among red varieties, some of the most widely cultivated for wine production were selected, including Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot Noir, Primitivo, Shiraz, Sangiovese, and Tempranillo, with the addition of regionally important cultivars like Cannonau [15]. For white varieties, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, and Moscato Bianco were included, along with aromatic varieties such as Muscat Hamburg and Muscat of Alexandria. In addition to the major cultivars, several less globally prominent but still widely cultivated varieties were considered, such as Aglianico, Nebbiolo, Gewürztraminer, Aleatico, Cannonau, Nerello Mascalese, Airèn, Macabeo, Monastrell, Labrusca, Grüner Veltliner, Albariño, Loureira, Moristel, Baga, Guzum, Pinot Gris and Glera. Table grape varieties were also considered including Bronx Seedless, Superior Seedless and Italia. Study site selection was guided by the availability of VOC emissions data and carefully considered the world's leading wine-producing countries to ensure representation from key regions with significant contributions to global wine production. The geographical distribution of the experimental sites is shown in a global map of all study sites (Fig. 2) along with three continent-based maps (Fig. 3) to aid interpretation (Script 1: ’Metadata and Maps‘). Data collection

The dataset was compiled into an Excel file organized into four sheets (see the ‘Data Description’ section). VOC emissions data with almost 800 individual VOC compounds were categorized into 14 chemical classes, including monoterpenes, alcohols, aldehydes, esters, sesquiterpenes, C13-norisoprenoids, ketones, phenols, carboxylic acids, furans, ethers, alkenes, alkanes, and methoxypyrazines. Individual VOC compounds are the specific molecules within those classes with a distinct chemical formula, structure, and often a unique aroma or role. They have been distinguished in free and bound form (see Table ‘VOC’): free VOC are volatile and directly contribute to aroma, while bound VOC are glycosidically linked to sugars and can be released during ripening or winemaking. Reporting both VOC forms could provide insights into the development and evolution of grape aroma, its variation during ripening and storage, and its response to environmental factors. Among the 14 chemical classes, monoterpenes were the most abundant, with almost 200 individual identified compounds (25 % of total), followed by alcohols (19 %), esters (17 %), and aldehydes (8 %) (Fig. 4; Script 2: ‘Compound Class & Aroma’). The 14 classes were further grouped into five macro-categories based on similarities in their chemical structure, biosynthetic origin, and functional roles in aroma and flavor profiles: Group 1 includes terpenoid compounds (monoterpenes, C13-norisoprenoids, sesquiterpenes) belonging to the biochemical class of isoprenoids; Group 2 comprises oxygenated compounds (alcohols, esters, carboxylic acids); Group 3 contains carbonyl compounds (aldehydes, ketones); Group 4 includes diverse aromatic compounds (phenols, furans, ethers, methoxypyrazines); and Group 5 covers hydrocarbons (alkanes, alkenes) known also as non-methane hydrocarbons (NMHCs) [49]. Data analysis and response variables

In the aroma analysis each VOC compound with known sensory attributes (n = 184) was assigned to a corresponding aroma category (e.g., floral, fruity, herbal) based on information from aroma libraries and relevant literature sources [11,12] (see the ‘VOC’ sheet in the dataset). To support the sensory interpretation of the chemical data, ten macro-categories of aroma descriptors were defined, each associated to chemically related compound classes. This classification aimed to simplify the complex relationship between chemical structure and sensory perception. These aroma macro-categories were then used to construct radar charts that visually compare the sensory profiles of different chemical classes (Fig. 5; Script 2: ‘Compound class & Aroma’). In each radar chart, the axes represent distinct sensory descriptors, while the blue shaded area illustrated the aggregated aromatic contribution of each compound class across those descriptors. To ensure robust and interpretable visualizations, only chemical classes linked to more than three aroma macro-categories were included in the radar chart analysis. This threshold was applied to avoid overinterpretation of limited or sparsely distributed data and to focus on compound classes with broader and more representative sensory impacts.Fig. 5Radar charts of main VOCs chemical classes provided in this study.Fig 5

Radar charts of main VOCs chemical classes provided in this study.

The mean number of VOC compounds per grapevine variety across phenological stages provides a comparative overview of VOC richness and helps identify general trends in compound accumulation over time (Script 3 ‘VOC Ripening’). In the dataset VOC emissions show a distinct trend throughout the various phenological stages of berry development, reflecting the dynamic biochemical changes associated with grape maturation. These variations are closely linked to physiological processes such as cell expansion, sugar accumulation, and the activation of specific metabolic pathways involved in aroma compound biosynthesis [14]. The data cover the developmental period from fruit set (BBCH 71) to harvest (BBCH 89) and include measurements from different berry tissues (such as pulp and skin) as well as from must. Statistics

No inferential statistical analyses were performed on the original data, in order to preserve the integrity and traceability of values as reported in the source studies. However, a set of descriptive aggregations and data visualizations was conducted with the aim of supporting exploratory interpretation and communicating trends within the dataset. All calculations and visualizations were performed using R (version 2025.05.0) in RStudio (R Development Core Team, http://www.r-project.org). The scripts used for data aggregation and plotting are provided alongside the dataset to ensure full transparency and reproducibility.

Limitations
