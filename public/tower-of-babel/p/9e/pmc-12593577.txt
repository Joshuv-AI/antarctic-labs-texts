Protocol to measure the kinetics of MHC class II-influenza A binding using biolayer interferometry
Authors: Matias Cardenas, Sasha Compton, Ashkon Hunt, Pradeep Chopra, Daniel R. Perez, Geert-Jan Boons, Daniela S. Rajao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryRecent work has shown that major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II is a functional receptor for some influenza A virus (FLUAV) subtypes. Here, we present a protocol to measure the kinetics of MHC class II-influenza A binding using biolayer interferometry. We describe steps for producing recombinant human leukocyte antigen DR isotype (HLA-DR) MHC class II using a baculovirus expression system. The HLA-DR antigen is then modified for its utilization as a biolayer interferometry (BLI) probe for kinetic experiments using whole FLUAV viruses. This protocol allows for the screening of different FLUAV subtypes and their affinity for MHC class II.

Before you begin

Previously, Influenza A viruses (FLUAV) were believed to exclusively utilize sialic acid (SA) as an entry receptor.1 However, in recent years it has been demonstrated that some hemagglutinin (HA) subtypes (H17-H19) do not use SA but instead recognize the Major Histocompatibility Complex Class II (MHCII) as the receptor.2,3,4 Moreover, some HA subtypes can utilize either SA or MHCII receptors to gain cell entry such as some H2N25 and H3N26 viruses. This has challenged our knowledge about FLUAV and the mechanisms allowing cross-species transmission and infectivity. MHCII is a heterodimer formed by an α and β chain.7 It is normally found on the surface of professional antigen-presenting cells although its expression has also been observed in non-professional antigen-presenting cells in the intestine and the respiratory tract.8 Dimerization of the complex is mediated by the transmembrane domain (TMD).9 Therefore, to obtain a soluble MHCII dimer, the TMD must be replaced with an exogenous dimerization motif. Current methods to evaluate MHCII recognition involve a series of functional in vitro and in vivo experiments that can be both costly and time-consuming, which ultimately limits the ability to screen different viruses for MHCII recognition. These assays mainly involve generation of sialic-acid depleted cells by knocking out specific genes such as SLC35A1.5 Nonetheless, biolayer interferometry (BLI) has been used as a qualitative and quantitative tool to assess FLUAV preference for α2,3-linked or α2,6-linked sialic acid.10,11,12 This label-free technique allows for real time assessment of biomolecules interactions based on the wavelength shift produced by binding of the immobilized molecule on the sensor and the free analyte in solution.13 In this report, we expand its role in Influenza research to evaluate FLUAV-MHCII binding.

Here we provide a detailed step-by-step method on how to produce HLA-DR using a baculovirus expression system to prepare functionalized BLI probes. We further describe the use of BLI as a screen tool to detect FLUAV-MHCII interactions. We performed the binding kinetics of H3N2 and H1N1 viruses with HLA-DR. These viruses exhibited different steady state affinity for MHC class II in which A/California/04/2009 (human H1N1) did not show any binding to HLA-DR, while A/Victoria/361/2011 (human H3N2) binds strongly to the receptor. Similarly, a swine-adapted H3N2 virus (A/turkey/Ohio/3134053/2004) exhibited less affinity to MHCII compared to the human H3N2. These results are in line with previous findings6 and support the use of BLI as a preliminary screening tool for MHCII recognition by FLUAV.

Innovation

This protocol describes the utilization of biolayer interferometry to evaluate the ability of FLUAV to bind MHC class II, which has been recently shown to act as a novel entry receptor. Biolayer interferometry has been widely used to analyze and quantify the affinity of FLUAV for α2,3- and/or α2,6-linked SA. We have adapted BLI for the screening of MHC class II binding by FLUAV. This technique provides valuable information regarding MHCII usage by FLUAV that can be used before assessing MHCII utilization by a specific FLUAV virus in vitro and/or in vivo.

Expected outcomes

This protocol has been successfully used to produce a soluble version of the human MHC class II homolog HLA-DR. The versatility of this system allows for the expression of practically any MHC class II dimer. Transposition from the transfer pFastBac Dual plasmid to the baculovirus bacmid is relatively efficient with nearly 70% of white colonies being true recombinants (Figure 4). Rescue of recombinant baculoviruses was confirmed 5 days post transfection of Sf9 cells by marked and classical CPE characterized by cell size enlargement, rounding, and presence of inclusion bodies (Figure 5). Baculoviruses obtained after transfection were immediately amplified in Sf9 cells. Although an average titer of 106 PFU/mL was observed after 1 passage, the highest titers were typically obtained after performing a second passage (≈107 PFU/mL). Infection of Sf9 cells with a high-titer baculovirus stock with an MOI of 10 yield nearly 90% of HLA-DR-expressing cells (Figure 6). HLA-DR production in High Five cells yielded an average of 1–4 mg per L of culture or 1–4 mg/mL after concentration. These samples exhibited high purity and were confirmed dimeric (Figure 7). No protein should be observed when using supernatant from High-Five cells infected with a wild type baculovirus after Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography (IMAC) purification. The biotinylation process and further IMAC purification did not affect the dimer stability as confirmed by NATIVE-PAGE analysis (Figure 8A). HLA-DR biotinylation was confirmed by forming a complex with streptavidin (Figure 8B).

Utilization of these HLA-DR molecules as functionalized BLI probes was demonstrated as they efficiently bound the sensors in a practically non-reversible interaction (Figure 10A). By analyzing A/Victoria/361/2011 (H3N2) and A/California/04/2009 (H1N1) binding to HLA-DR, we demonstrated the specificity of the system and its ability to distinguish between viruses that use/do not use HLA-DR as a receptor (Figure 10B). Moreover, affinity for HLA-DR was successfully determined by using two H3N2 viruses: A/Victoria/361/2011 and A/turkey/Ohio/313053/2004. Both viruses bound to HLA-DR and allowed to determine the steady state affinity (KD) (Figure 11). We found that the human virus A/Victoria/361/2011 bound approximately three times stronger than the swine-adapted virus A/turkey/Ohio/313053/2004 (A/Victoria/361/2011 KD= 4.2 pM vs A/turkey/Ohio/313053/2004 KD=11.7 pM). This effect was also observed when the association rate of both viruses to HLA-DR was calculated, in which A/Victoria/361/2011 had an association rate three times higher than the swine-adapted H3N2 virus. This is in agreement with our previous data in which we demonstrated that A/turkey/Ohio/313053/2004 poorly infected cells lacking sialic acid and expressing HLA-DR, contrasting with A/Victoria/361/2011 that efficiently replicated in these cells.6

Overall, this protocol can be used as a screening tool to determine whether a virus binds to a specific MHCII homolog. The affinity of the interaction can be determined and used to compare binding between different FLUAV subtypes and/or strains.

Limitations

BLI machines exhibit low sensitivity for low-affinity or hard-to-dissociate samples, allowing detection of KD between 1 mM-10 pM. Since FLUAV and MHCII exhibit a virtually non-reversible binding, full kinetic analysis using whole virus is not possible.

Unspecific binding can yield a signal consistent with virus binding. Therefore, as suggested in the main text, buffer controls with no virus and non-functionalized sensors must be included to ensure the accuracy of the data.

The OctetRED96 instrument allows the utilization of 8 probes per run, limiting the number of samples that can be analyzed simultaneously to 7 plus a negative control.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

No white colonies.

Potential solution

Competent cells might have been stored incorrectly. Prepare new competent cells by your method of choice such as calcium chloride, rubidium chloride, etc.

Problem 2

Low baculovirus titer after 1 passage.

Potential solutions

Make sure baculovirus stocks are stored at 2°C–4°C and always protected from light. Storage at higher or lower temperatures will result in a significant drop of titer in a short period of time.

Problem 4

Little or no biotinylation.

Potential solution

High salt concentrations such as NaCl can inhibit BirA activity. Perform a buffer exchange and store MHCII samples in PBS.

Problem 5

No virus binding to MHCII.

Potential solution

Whenever testing a virus, a positive control should be included. To date, all H17, H18, H19, and a subset of H2N2, and H3N2 viruses have been shown to bind to MHCII.2,3,4,5,6 If the controls do not show binding, ensure there is no biotin carried over from the biotinylation step as free biotin will reduce MHCII loading onto the sensor. To remove free biotin, perform a buffer exchange.
