Waste eggshell derived CaCO3 modified MIL-101(Cr) for response surface methodology optimized photocatalytic malachite green degradation
Authors: Asmaa S. Morshedy, Howaida M. Abd El Salam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic dyes used in food products and the textile industry are a major source of environmental contamination, as they are nonbiodegradable, highly toxic to aquatic life, and carcinogenic to humans. A novel visible-light-sensitive CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite photocatalyst has been fabricated by unique calcium carbonate from eggshell waste modified with a chromium metal–organic framework for applying photocatalytic degradation of malachite green (MG) dye as a representative pollutant under the influence of visible light irradiation and providing a cost-effective and environmentally friendly approach. Synthesized samples MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) were characterized using a variety of techniques, including XRD, FT-IR, BET, SEM, XPS, and UV–Vis spectroscopy analysis. The improved photocatalytic activity of the CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between CaCO3 and MIL-101(Cr), which enhanced visible-light absorption, facilitated interfacial charge transfer, and suppressed electron/hole recombination. Using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) according to Central Composite Design (CCD), process parameters such as starting catalyst dosage, oxidizing agent, and dye concentration were optimized. The photocatalytic oxidative degradation of (MG) model dye (the optimized conditions were catalyst dose: 2.0 g L−1, oxidizing agent: 2.25%, and dye concentration: 200 mg L−1) resulted in a remarkable 98.86% efficiency via a waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. Furthermore, the optimized conditions were employed in treating real textile wastewater, and the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite achieved a removal efficiency of 96.20%. Additionally, with no decline in efficiency, the catalyst showed outstanding durability and reusability over six cycles. The potential of waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite as a viable and recyclable photocatalyst for the effective removal of resistant organic contaminants from wastewater is demonstrated in this work.

Introduction

Water pollution is one of the major environmental challenges and is caused by the presence of toxic materials or excessive heat, which has an adverse effect on humans, animals, and ecosystems in or adjacent to hydrological bodies.1 This is one of the reasons that industrial plants have to condition water before using it, and wastewater needs treatment so as not to cause pollution. Water pre-treatment emerges as an inevitable solution to a variety of issues concerning water pollution.2 Organic matter can be a contaminant to water, leading to undesirable tastes and odors. Organic pollutants also produce coloured colloidal suspensions, posing an additional challenge for water treatment. Organic compounds have emerged as some of the most challenging water contaminants that can last in the environment for a long time. Among them, organic pollutants can lead to different diseases in the human body even in trace concentrations. Industrial synthetic dyes are the major sources of organic water pollutants due to their extensive use and high resistance to biodegradation, which can cause various environmental sustainability issues.3,4

Malachite green (MG) is a cationic triphenylmethane dye extensively used in Polymeric fibers, leather, and paper industries due to its bright color and excellent stability.5 However, even at low concentrations, MG is a major pollutant in wastewater, posing serious environmental and health risks. Its high solubility and intense color reduce light penetration in water, disrupting aquatic photosynthesis and harming aquatic organisms. Exposure to MG can cause allergies, dermatitis, and skin irritation, while prolonged exposure has been linked to genotoxic and carcinogenic effects.6,7 Because of its cationic nature, MG strongly interacts with anionic functional groups on substrates, making it difficult to remove through conventional treatment methods. Therefore, developing efficient and sustainable approaches for MG removal from wastewater is critically important.

To confront this challenge, optimized conditions were applied to enhance the removal efficiency, photocatalysis and other advanced oxidation processes have attracted a lot of interest as an eco-friendly water treatment method. Using light energy to activate a catalyst and produce electron–hole (e−/h+) pairs on its surface is known as photocatalysis or photocatalytic degradation. Participating in redox reactions, these charge carriers (e−/h+) generate superoxide radicals (˙O2−) when electrons reduce dissolved oxygen, and hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) when water oxidizes. These species of reactive oxygen are quite good at breaking down MG's complex aromatic structures into less harmful forms. The mineralization of pollutants into harmless byproducts like carbon dioxide and water is the result of these photocatalysis reactions. A photocatalyst with a narrow band gap that corresponds to the absorption of visible light and low electron–hole recombination rates should be taken into consideration for the practical application of photocatalysis.5

In recent years, it has been shown that metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are a noteworthy starting point for a diverse array of functional materials,8 notably those that are engineered with species that are catalytically efficient on porous carbon substrates.9 Diverse researchers have modified the MOFs by aziridine,10 amino group11 and thiol group12 for the removal of various categories of organic and inorganic pollutants. Chen Li et al. (2015)13 have prepared two types of MOFs (MIL-53(Al)-NH2 and MIL-53(Al)) and utilized for the removal of MG and MB through adsorption. Zhang et al.14 have synthesized a MNPC (g-Fe2O3/C) which was applied for the remove of MG dye from water. MIL-101(Cr) is a type of MOF documented for its exceptional hydrothermal, water, and chemical robustness. MIL-101(Cr) is highly interconnected three-dimensional network structure, which also contributes to its exceptional toughness and structural robustness. It is appropriate for use in adsorption, catalysis, and environmental remediation.15 Two of the most topical solids among the several MOFs that have been identified thus far are the porous chromium-benzenedicarboxylates (Cr-BDCs), namely MIL-53 (ref. 16) and MIL-101,17,18 which are being extensively researched for possible uses. MIL-101(Cr) characterized by acidic coordinatively unsaturated sites (CUSs) and its framework composed of quasi-spherical cages with large pore windows that facilitate the incorporation of active species and photocatalytic applications.19 Because of its enormous porosity and mesoporous structure, MIL-101 is a highly significant material that is being researched extensively for drug delivery,19 adsorption,20 and catalysis.21

This study elucidates new applications of MOF structures, particularly emphasizing on terephthalic acid-based MOFs with Cr as central metals and modified with waste eggshell-derived CaCO3, employed as innovative photocatalysts for degradation processes in wastewater treatment. The integration of waste eggshell-derived CaCO3 into the MIL-101(Cr) framework is predicated on its documented capacity to encourage oxidative degradation stability, and reusability, suggesting that it improves the effectiveness of MG removal. Furthermore, by exploiting the positive effects of both metals in promoting degrading processes, the combination of CaCO3 and Cr aims to enhance catalytic activity in a synergistic manner. MIL-101(Cr) was previously introduced in small amounts to improve the oxidative degradation characteristics of materials such as metal oxides. This design dramatically improves visible-light-driven photocatalytic oxidation processes while maintaining the large surface area characteristic of MOFs. Although MIL-101(Cr)-based materials and CaCO3 have been individually investigated in environmental applications,22–24 the use of eggshell-derived CaCO3 as a low-cost bio-waste modifier for MIL-101(Cr), combined with RSM-CCD optimization of photocatalytic operating parameters and validation using real textile wastewater, has not been sufficiently explored. Therefore, this study contributes not only to photocatalyst development but also to circular-economy-based wastewater treatment by converting an abundant domestic waste into a functional component of an efficient recyclable photocatalyst.

The results reveal that waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite mitigate the leaching of MIL-101(Cr), delivering better stability as compared with single-metal MOFs like MIL-101(Cr) and CaCO3, yielding remarkable MG reduction (96.2%) throughout six cycles.

Experimental

Materials and characterization methods

Chromium(iii) nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO3)3·9H2O, ≥99%), 1,4-benzenedi-carboxylic acid (H2BDC ≥ 98%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, >99.8), ethanol absolute (C2H5OH, vol. >99.5%). Analytical-grade Malachite Green (MG, ≥99% purity), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, ≥99%), and sodium sulfate (≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Water used in all experiments was deionized. All chemicals were of analytical grade and were used as received without further purification. Detailed characterizations methods, have been thoroughly depicted in the SI.

Synthesis of eggshell-based CaCO3 nanoparticles

Waste chicken egg shells were collected from local domestic kitchen waste in Cairo, Egypt. Eggs purchased from a local retail market in Egypt, shells were stored over clean polyethylene bags away at room temperature for not more than one month before processing. The eggshells were washed several times with tap water followed by deionized water to remove any adhering organic residues before use. The general preparation procedure begins with the thorough washing of eggshells to remove impurities, followed by membrane separation achieved via boiling in water for 10 minutes.25 Then, the eggshells were treated with an acetic acid solution at a solid-to-liquid ratio of (10 g eggshell powder per 1 L of (10%) acetic acid solution) for 1 h by magnetic stirring under room temperature. Then the acid-treated eggshells were separated by filtration, thoroughly washed with deionized water until the filtrate approached neutral pH and dried at 100 °C for 24 h, then grinded as a powder, and heated in a muffle furnace to get calcium carbonate from eggshell held at 500 °C for 24 h.26 The mass yield of eggshell-derived CaCO3 was determined for each synthesis batch. Briefly, 10.0 g of cleaned and dried eggshell precursor produced 8.5 g of CaCO3 powder after thermal treatment at 500 °C for 24 h, corresponding to a yield of 85% based on three independent batches.

Synthesis of photocatalyst MIL-101(Cr)

Following the process described by Kayal et al., MIL-101(Cr) was created by hydrothermally reacting chromium nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO3)3·9H2O) (4 g) with terephthalic acid (H2BDC) (1.66 g) in an environment of hydrochloric acid (HCl) (1 mL) as a mineralizing agent and deionized water (50 mL).27 The reaction mixture was heated to 200 °C for 10 hours under autogenous pressure while enclosed in a Teflon-lined autoclave. After centrifugation, the green MIL-101(Cr) crystals were collected, and unreacted terephthalic acid was removed from the pores by washing three times with ethanol and DMF. The filtered product was then dried at 80 °C for the entire night.27,28

Synthesis of photocatalyst CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr)

The CaCO3 modified MIL-101(Cr) photocatalytic composite was synthesized using the same technique as the MIL-101(Cr) synthesis, with the exception that, 10 wt% of CaCO3 was incorporated into the synthesis process, the dispersion method employed to achieve homogeneous dispersion. The reaction mixture was heated to 200 °C for 10 hours under autogenous pressure while enclosed in a Teflon-lined autoclave. After centrifugation, the crystals were collected, and unreacted terephthalic acid was removed from the pores by washing three times with ethanol and DMF. The filtered product was then dried at 80 °C for the entire night.

Optimization process based on response surface methodology

Working stock solution of MG dye with a concentration of 1000 mg L−1 was prepared by dissolving 1 gram (MG) in distilled water, and then diluting to the mark in a 1000 mL volumetric flask. The (MG) was serially diluted with distilled water, producing solution concentration from 10 to 100 mg L−1 in order to begin the POD process. The initial pH of MG solution was measured using a calibrated pH meter with 0 to 14 scales as (pH = 4.8). The pH of the solution was not adjusted in RSM experiments, as the photocatalytic degradation tests were performed at the natural pH value of MG solution so as not to introduce a new experimental variable. The pH value was continuously monitored to produce reproducible degradation experiments.29

A glass double jacket connected to a water-circulation system was put on as the photocatalytic reactor for maintaining irradiation holder temperature. The temperature of the reaction was measured and kept at (25 ± 1 °C), demonstrating that primarily photocatalytic oxidation was responsible for degradation over lamp-induced heating. The flask is stuffed with this solution and 0.1 g L−1 of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, or CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) catalyst. Prior to photocatalytic irradiation, the catalyst/MG solution was magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. This 30 min dark-equilibration step was applied before all photocatalytic degradation and RSM optimization experiments. In addition, a separate dark-control adsorption experiment was conducted for 24 h in the absence of light to evaluate the adsorption contribution of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) toward MG removal. Then, the system was illuminated with broadband visible-light irradiation from a 500 W linear halogen lamp at different intervals while 0.25% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is added. The wavelength of LHL was around 550 nm refers only to the approximate visible emission region spectral contribution and not to monochromatic green-light irradiation. The irradiation distance and light intensity were controlled during the experiments, as described in the photocatalytic performance section. Finally, the product is measured using the UV-visible spectrometry system (GBC Cintra 3030, Australia) to evaluate the MG dye concentration. Results and discussion

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was applied for evaluating the crystalline structures of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In agreement with the reported crystalline planes of MIL-101(Cr), the MIL-101(Cr) diffraction pattern showed distinctive peaks at 2θ = 5.7°, 8.6°, 9.2° and 16.0° corresponded to the (440), (822), (911) and (1644) planes. These peaks confirmed the successful assembly of the metal–organic framework with high crystallinity.34–36 The XRD pattern's intense peaks of MIL-101(Cr) are located at small angles (2θ), suggesting that the material has an abundance of pores. The crystalline phase of the pure CaCO3 sample was clearly identified by the presence of discrete peaks at 2θ = 23.13° (012), 29.4° (104), 36.1° (110), 39.5° (114), 43.3°(202), 47.6° (018), 57.5° (112), and 61.2° (119), which corresponded to CaCO3 (JCPDS No. 001-0799).37,38 The successful integration of CaCO3 into the MIL-101(Cr) was confirmed by the XRD pattern of the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite, which included the primary reflections of both MIL-101(Cr) and CaCO3. Significantly, the composite's diffraction peaks showed evidence of partial interactions and potentially lattice distortion forced on by CaCO3 modifying, appearing somewhat broader and less intense. The results obtained show that CaCO3 was distributed uniformly throughout the MIL-101(Cr) structure without negatively impacting its overall structural integrity or crystallinity.

FT-IR

For identifying the functional groups and confirm that the composite was successfully assembled, FT-IR spectra of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite were acquired (Fig. 2). The very broad peak development at 3537 cm−1 demonstrated the presence of loosely bound water molecules in spectra of MIL-101(Cr) and the two distinctive sharp absorption peaks at 1576 cm−1 and 1368 cm−1, which correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the carboxylate (–COO−) groups from the H2BDC organic linker, respectively.39 The coordination between chromium ions and organic linkers was confirmed by the assignment of a weak band at 735 cm−1 to the Cr–O stretching vibration.40 A peak at wavenumber 1400 cm−1 in the CaCO3 spectra was ascribed to carbonate functional group.41 Moreover, the non-bridging bending vibrational modes of O–Ca–O at 422 cm− 1 and non-bridging bending modes in the range of 455 cm−1 were observed. The carboxylate group's asymmetric stretching vibration shifted from 1576 cm−1 in pristine MIL-101(Cr) to 1564 cm−1 in the CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. The MIL-101(Cr) framework and the CaCO3 particles may interact, as suggested by this 12 cm−1 shift. FT-IR spectrum of CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) retained the characteristic vibrations of the MIL-101(Cr) framework along with new carbonate-related bands and low-frequency vibration located at approximately 568 cm−1. The latter is possibly due to Ca–O/metal–oxygen lattice vibrations linked with the calcium species. The FT-IR signature peaks are consistent with the CaCO3 diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern and the Ca distribution by SEM-EDX mapping, confirming that MIL-101(Cr) composite has incorporated eggshell from which most of the Ca distributes.42,43

BET analysis

Fig. 3 N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and BJH pore size distribution curves were used for assessing the textural characteristics of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. The MIL-101(Cr) showed a typical type I isotherm, which is typical of microporous materials. Its total pore volume was 0.8871 cm3 g−1, and its specific surface area was 1780 m2 g−1 (Fig. 3a). While Fig. 3c shows that CaCO3 is typical of type IV isotherm, which is typical of mesoporous materials, containing slit-like or interparticle pores. The sharp uptake at high relative pressure suggests capillary condensation inside larger mesopores and possibly voids between aggregated particles. The absence of strong uptake at very low pressure means the material likely has limited microporosity. The pore size distribution Fig. 3d shows a major peak centered on ∼35–40 nm, confirming that the dominant pores are in the mesopore range.44 According to IUPAC classification, mesopores are between 2 and 50 nm, so this material is mainly mesoporous. A small contribution appears at very low pore diameter, suggesting a minor fraction of smaller pores, but the main pore volume is clearly contributed by larger mesopores. Due to partial pore blocking by CaCO3 nanoparticles inside the MIL-101(Cr) framework, the CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite displayed a minor decrease in surface area to 1107 m2 g−1 and pore volume to 0.6304 cm3 g−1 after CaCO3 incorporation (Fig. 3e). BJH pore size distribution curves Fig. 3b and f showed one type of pore size for MIL-101(Cr) and CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite at (17 Å) and (29 Å), respectively. The waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite managed to retain a porous structure that was advantageous for adsorption and catalysis. In comparison to pristine MIL-101(Cr), the average pore diameter of waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite reduced marginally, indicating that CaCO3 was successfully distributed on the MOF surface and interior channels without substantially changing the porous structure.

SEM

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to investigate the surface morphology of the generated MIL-101(Cr) and waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. As is prevalent in chromium-based MOFs produced hydrothermally, the MIL-101(Cr) displayed a distinct octahedral crystalline morphology with smooth exteriors and a uniform particle distribution45 as seen in Fig. 4a. After CaCO3 was modified into the MIL-101(Cr) framework, the morphology of waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite emerged as a hybrid composite, with CaCO3 nanostructures evenly distributed and anchored on the MIL-101(Cr) crystals' surface and inside their pores. The successful inclusion of CaCO3 nanoparticles was confirmed by the somewhat rougher surface and decreased particle homogeneity that resulted from this structural integration when compared to pure MIL-101(Cr). Fig. 4b and c illustrate the SEM and EDS elemental mapping of CaCO3@MIL-(101) Cr reveal specific bright regions that correspond to CaCO3-rich domains, indicating that the CaCO3 molecules are dispersed throughout the MIL-101(Cr) matrix but show some degree of aggregation.

UV-vis reflectance

The optical properties and bandgap energies of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite were evaluated. Waste eggshell-derived CaCO3 exhibits 350 nm. While, pure MIL-101(Cr) showed a higher absorption edge in the longer-wave region 525 nm, typical for its wide bandgap, the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite demonstrated significant red shifts with its edge spreading in the visible region (435 nm), Fig. 6a. This shift indicates the promoted visible-light absorption, and suggests that an improved photocatalytic activity can be expected when a visible light is used as an irradiation source.

(a) UV–Vis reflectance spectra and (b) Tauc-plot energy values of MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite.

In accordance with the above finding, Fig. 6b demonstrates the calculated bandgap energies become gradually decreased from 3.61(eV) for CaCO3 to 2.2, and 2.1 (eV) for the CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite, and pure MIL-101(Cr), respectively. This directionally-excremental bandgap energy indicates a serious electronic interaction between the MIL-101(Cr) and CaCO3, facilitating wider light absorption and superior photocatalytic performance. Based on the UV-Vis DRS and derived band gap data, the synthesized materials demonstrate tailored optical properties suitable for applications in photocatalysis and solar energy harvesting.

Photocatalytic performance

The process was conducted using a 500 W linear halogen lamp (Orange-CT, USA) as the irradiation source. Activity was assessed by degrading organic dye in a model compound (200 ppm MG), employing 25 mL of dye solution with 0.1 g of materials in a quartz-glass vessel equipped with a reflux unit and placed on a magnetic stirrer (500 rpm) within a light-shielded enclosure.49 MG degradation was confirmed by the continuous decrease in its characteristic UV–Vis absorption band, together with control experiments distinguishing adsorption and light effects.

Key operational parameters, including catalyst dosage, percent of the H2O2, and MG dye concentration were systematically investigated to optimize performance. This methodological focus on parameter optimization was crucial for enhancing photocatalytic activity, demonstrating the process's adaptability and potential for efficient, application-specific design.

Effect of light

Initially, the adsorption behavior of the synthesized catalysts toward MG dye degradation was evaluated in the dark for 24 h prior to assessing photocatalytic performance. The findings revealed that, under identical conditions, waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite exhibited the maximum adsorption capacity of 29% dye degradation. In contrast, waste eggshell-derived CaCO3 alone removed 10% and pure MIL-101(Cr) catalyst extracted only 22% of the (MG) dye (Fig. 7). Under subsequent visible light irradiation (500 W halogen lamp, 550 nm, 1 hour), the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite demonstrated superior photocatalytic degradation efficiency, achieving 38% dye removal at 1 g L−1 catalyst loading (Fig. 7) compared with waste eggshell-derived CaCO3 and MIL-101(Cr) alone which achieve removal 13% and 26% respectively under the same conditions. This performance is attributed to its favorable 2.2 (eV) band gap and effective charge separation, in addition to low photoluminescence intensity as will discuss in Fig. 10 for eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. The newly waste eggshell-derived CaCO3 embedded in the porous framework of MIL-101(Cr) not only impart new function, but also serve as an electron acceptor, and act as electron conductor to decrease charge recombination, and offer stabilizing porous skeleton to increase porosity. As a result, the MIL-101(Cr) structure forms an integral functional unit that allows for synergistic Ca–Cr effects on activity; thus, waste eggshell-derived CaCO3 composite was chosen for further parametric optimization studies using (200 ppm) MG dye. The outstanding performance is related to the synergistic effect of CaCO3 and high specific surface area MIL-101(Cr) catalyst, which provide abundant accessible active sites for MG dye adsorption. The active Cr3+ species that overall improved the coordination structure of composite also greatly improved its catalytic activity. This cooperated effect significantly facilitates the rapid and efficient photocatalytic degradation of MG (88% after 60 minutes reaction under visible light irradiation).

Tests in dark & under LHL for MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite.

The photocatalytic degradation performance of (MG) dye was comparatively evaluated for MIL-101(Cr), CaCO3, and waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. The pure MIL-101(Cr) and CaCO3 components demonstrated moderate activity in the removal of MG dye under visible light irradiation, attributed to their respective semiconducting and porous adsorptive properties. However, the integrated waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite exhibited a significant enhancement in dye degradation efficiency, a synergy resulting from the effective coupling of its constituent phases. Also, the corresponding kinetic plots and calculated rate constants were studied as included in SI section (Fig. S1). The waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite showed the highest apparent rate constant of 0.0353 min−1, compared with 0.0050 min−1 for MIL-101(Cr) and 0.0023 min−1 for CaCO3, confirming the superior photocatalytic efficiency of the composite. This enhancement is attributed to the synergistic interaction between CaCO3 and MIL-101(Cr), improved visible-light absorption, enhanced charge separation, and reduced electron–hole recombination. The kinetic results show pseudo-first order model, further support the degradation efficiency findings and strengthen the photocatalytic performance evaluation of the developed composite.

Thus, the RSM optimization performed here may be seen as a conditional optimization of chosen operating variables for a fixed irradiation time rather than complete kinetic optimization where exposure duration would have been used independently.

In this study, the role of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as an oxidizing agent was investigated by gradually increasing its concentration from 0.5% to 4.0% within 10 mL malachite green (MG) solution containing 0.25 g of the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite. As illustrated in Fig. 8a, the degradation efficiency of MG dye rose significantly with higher H2O2 levels, climbing from 51% to a peak of 73% at an optimal concentration of 2.25%. This marked enhancement can be attributed to the efficient generation of reactive radicals at this ideal oxidant dosage. Beyond this concentration, only a negligible further improvement in activity was observed, most likely due to limited interfacial availability of H2O2 in the aqueous phase to attack the malachite green (MG) solution. The observed decline in efficiency at elevated H2O2 concentrations, despite the potential for greater radical generation, can be outlined by the saturation of active sites on the exposed catalyst surface by extra oxidant molecules, which aligns with conclusions reported in prior investigations.51 Furthermore, catalytic activity typically increases with higher catalyst loading, as this provides a greater number of active sites, as a result of that creating a more favorable conditions for the reaction to proceed.2

As shown in Fig. 8b, raising the concentration of the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite from 0.25 to 2.25 g L−1 led to a corresponding rise in malachite green (MG) degradation, suggesting that higher catalyst loadings enhance peroxidase-like (POD) activity. Examining the interplay between MG dye concentration and photocatalyst dosage (Fig. 8b) revealed that the most influential region for degradation response fell within 100–300 ppm of MG dye and 0.25–2.25 g L−1 of catalyst. This improvement can be ascribed to an augmentation of available reactive sites and the generation of hydroxyl radicals from the illuminated catalyst. Nevertheless, exceeding the optimal catalyst loading caused a decline in degradation efficiency, primarily due to heightened suspension turbidity. This turbidity promotes light scattering and reduces photon penetration, thereby limiting the formation of excited states in the photocatalyst. Moreover, at excessively high catalyst loadings, nanoparticle agglomeration proceeds, which reduces the abundance of accessible surface-active centers.51

Increasing the initial concentration of MG dye negatively impacts the photocatalytic degradation performance for two primary reasons. Firstly, higher dye concentrations lead to greater adsorption of MG molecules onto the catalyst surface, which can block active sites and suppress the formation of reactive radicals. Secondly, the elevated concentration makes the reaction medium more opaque, thereby limiting the abundance of photons reaching the photocatalyst surface and subsequently reducing its photo-activity.52 As previously noted, the strongest interactive effects between MG dye concentration and H2O2 percentage were observed within the ranges of 100–300 ppm and 0.5–4%, respectively, yielding a degradation efficiency nearly 83% (Fig. 8c). The optimal conditions were identified as a catalyst dosage of 2.0 g L−1, a hydrogen peroxide concentration of 2.25%, and an initial MG concentration of 200 ppm. Under these optimized parameters, the degradation efficiency of MG reached a maximum of 98.86%.53

Collectively, these results highlight the robustness and effectiveness of the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite, underscoring its strong potential for widespread industrial use in the treatment of various organic pollutants.

Proposed mechanism

The photocatalytic mechanism of MG dye degradation by waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite is depicted in Fig. 12. The photoactive component of CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) is MIL-101(Cr), which has a very narrow band gap making it suitable to act as the primary component under visible-light irradiation. Photoexcitation of MIL-101(Cr) produces electron–hole pairs, with the photogenerated electrons being promoted to its conduction band, whereas holes remain in the valence band. Upon photoexcitation, electrons are promoted across the band gap to the conduction band, leaving behind holes in the valence band. Nevertheless, CaCO3 is a semiconductor with a wider band gap and cannot be directly excited by visible light. This suggests that, while CaCO3 is mainly an interfacial electron-trapping component rather than contributing as the primary light absorber. The presence of CaCO3 acts as an electron acceptor, facilitating the migration of conduction band electrons toward its surface, thereby effectively suppressing electron–hole recombination.56 The e− photogenerated in MIL-101(Cr) can diffuse into CaCO3 surface/interfacial trap states that inhibits electron–hole recombination rates and promotes the reduction of dissolved oxygen to superoxide radicals. On the other hand, upon MIL-101(Cr) remaining unfilled in holes can convey out oxidation of water or surface hydroxyl groups to generate hydroxyl radicals. The reactive species like h+, ˙OH, and ˙O2− then thermodynamically react to oxidatively decompose malachite green into smaller oxidation intermediates and eventually remove to CO2 and H2O.

The trapped electrons subsequently react with adsorbed oxygen to form superoxide radicals (˙O2−), while the photogenerated holes react with water or surface hydroxyl groups to produce hydroxyl radicals (˙OH). In the case of malachite green degradation, a substantial reduction in degradation efficiency was detected when ammonium oxalate was used as the scavenger (Fig. 13), indicating that holes (h+) play a critical part in the organic dye removal method under visible light, monitored by other active species. Conclusions

In this study, three photocatalysts MIL-101(Cr), waste eggshell-derived CaCO3, and the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite were successfully synthesized and evaluated for their efficacy in degrading malachite green (MG) under visible light irradiation. Comprehensive characterization using XRD, FT-IR, BET, SEM mapping-EDX, UV-vis diffuse reflectance, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy, and XPS was conducted to analyze morphological features, crystal structure, porosity, photo-activity, and surface chemical states of the prepared materials. Under the optimized practical conditions, 2.0 g L−1 of CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite, 2.25% H2O2, 200 mg L−1 MG concentration, and 1 h irradiation using LHL, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency reached 98.86%. While its application to real textile wastewater yielded a removal rate exceeding 96% with consistent performance over six recycling cycles. The incorporation of CaCO3 was found to enhance activity by acting as an electron acceptor, thereby improving the composite's photocatalytic efficiency. Overall, the waste eggshell-derived CaCO3@MIL-101(Cr) composite designed in in this study exhibits superior performance photocatalytic activity, good stability, and significant potential for practical applicability in the remediation of hazardous contaminants.
