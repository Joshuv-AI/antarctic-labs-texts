Serological and molecular detection of hepatitis C virus in Amran governorate, Yemen
Authors: Ali Ahmed Al-Hadheq, Bashir Ahmed Al-Ofairi, Adam Dawoud Abakar, Azhari Mohammed, Wadhah Hassan Edrees, Wadee Abdullah Al-Shehari, Ahmed Salem Al-Jendy, Yosria Mohammed Elsiddig, Abdulrahman Abdullah Humaid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) is a blood-borne virus that causes acute or chronic hepatitis, depending on its severity, and its development leads to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma. This study aimed to detect HCV using the serological and molecular techniques among blood donors and clinical visitors in Amran governorate, Yemen.MethodsA total of 800 blood specimens were collected from blood donors (500 specimens) and clinical visitors (300 specimens). Blood specimens were screened for HCV using an immunochromatographic technique (ICT) and the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and the final positive cases for HCV were confirmed by nested polymerase chain reaction (nPCR).ResultsOut of 800 specimens, only 21 (2.6%) were reported as positive for HCV by molecular analysis. According to diagnostic techniques, 18 (2.3%), 22 (2.7%), and 21 (2.6%), respectively, of specimens were reported as positive for HCV by ICT, ELISA, and PCR techniques. Moreover, a higher rate of HCV was found among participants aged 21–30 years (4.3%), females (2.5%), urban residents (2.7%), single individuals (3.1%), and uneducated individuals (6.1%). In addition, the proportion of HCV was significantly higher among participants with a family history of HCV infection (11.6%), previous surgery (30.8%), no blood transfusion (2.9%), prior cupping (9.1%), and perinatal injury (4.6%).ConclusionThis finding indicates that the prevalence rate of HCV infection in the study area is high. To protect the general public from the spread of HCV infection, it is essential to develop a public health approach including surveillance and monitoring, strengthening infrastructure, training healthcare workers in early detection and treatment of cases, and increasing public awareness.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV), which infects the liver and causes acute and chronic hepatitis, is a challenge to the health system globally [1, 2]. HCV is an encapsulated, spherical, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus and belongs to the Flaviviridae family and Hepacivirus genus. In addition, seven genotypes of HCV have been identified worldwide [3–5]. Furthermore, HCV is usually spread from person to person through transfusions of contaminated blood and blood products or through exposure to contaminated needle pricks or sharp objects. Additionally, it can be transmitted from an infected mother to her fetus during pregnancy or childbirth and rarely through sexual contact. HCV is not transmitted through food, water, or breast milk [6].

Symptoms of HCV infection include high fever, tiredness, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, stomach pain, joint pain, dark urine, and yellow skin or eyes (jaundice). Newly acquired HCV infection is usually asymptomatic. Furthermore, infected individuals (30%) clear the virus spontaneously within 6 months, without treatment. Additionally, chronic HCV infection develops in 55–85% of infected individuals, and 15–30% of those who are infected are at risk for cirrhosis and cancer [6].

An estimated 50 million (1.8%) people worldwide suffer from chronic HCV infection, with approximately one million new cases reported annually. Furthermore, approximately 242,000 people died in 2022 as a result of HCV, the majority of whom have cirrhosis and liver cancer [6–8]. Only 36% of HCV-infected individuals worldwide have received a diagnosis, and 20% have received treatment [9].

Furthermore, the overall prevalence rate of HCV was reported to be between 0.07 and 8.5% among the general population in Yemen [10]. In addition, the prevalence of HCV was recorded to be between 40.0% and 62.7% among dialysis patients [11], 6.4%–38.2% among special clinical groups, 0.5%–3.5% among healthcare workers, 37.1% among patients with acute and chronic liver disease [12], 38.2% among patients with hepatocellular carcinoma [13], 8.5% among pregnant women [14], and 1.0%–1.6% among blood donors [15].

Yemen, which was classified among the poorest countries in the world, continues to face political and financial difficulties, and this situation consequently has led to a decline in the provision of health services and effective plans and programs to combat epidemics in various regions [16–18]. In addition, the continuation of the war since the beginning of 2015 has essentially destroyed the health sector, leading to the spread of various pathogens, especially infectious viruses, among the population [19–25]. To date, there is no published data on the molecular detection of HCV in the Amran governorate, Yemen. This study aimed to detect HCV using molecular and serological techniques among blood donors and clinical visitors to Amran governorate, Yemen. The results of this study are extremely important for health policymakers and healthcare providers in the study area, and they enable them to develop plans and programs to prevent and control infections for healthier blood donations.

Materials and methods

Study area

This cross-sectional study was conducted among blood donors and clinical visitors attending hospitals and health centers in Amran governorate, Yemen, during the period from February to November 2023. Moreover, Amran is considered a small city located in the west and center of the country, approximately 50 km north. Furthermore, the Amran governorate has a population of approximately 1,221,908 people and a total area of ​​9,587 square kilometers (3,702 square miles). Additionally, the Amran Governorate is divided into 20 administrative districts (Fig. 1) [26].

Sociodemographic and clinical data

The necessary data of participants was registered by using a standard predesigned questionnaire that was created with some modifications based on an earlier study [27]. Face-to-face interviews were used to gather the demographic information such as age (in years), sex, residency, marital status, education level, and occupational status, as well as clinical data such as family history, surgery, blood transfusion, types of specialist visitors, cupping, and perinatal injuries. The information was written on a questionnaire sheet that was originally made in Arabic and then translated into English after the data was collected.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included all healthy blood donors and clinical visitors attending hospitals and health centers who agreed to sign an informed consent form and allow blood samples to be collected. Participants with a history of chronic diseases or diagnosed with immunocompromise who did not agree to sign an informed consent form were excluded.

Specimens collection and serological analysis

Five mL of whole blood was collected aseptically by syringes from each individual in this study. Serum was separated by centrifugation following clotting of blood and stored at − 70 °C until time of laboratory investigations. Anti-HCV screening in human serum was performed using an immunochromatographic technique (ICT) with the Accu-Tell Rapid Anti-HCV Test (Accu Bio Tech Co., Ltd., Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Moreover, the third generation of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using the Cobase 411 analyzer (Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Bazel, Switzerland) was used for the quantitative detection of anti-HCV in human serum (Biokit Co., Barcelona, Spain). Additionally, the positive results of ELISA were confirmed by nested polymerase chain reaction (nPCR) for amplification of HCV RNA using GeneAmp™ PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems Applera Co., Norwalk, US).

Molecular detection

RNA extraction

Viral RNAs was extracted from the serum specimen (140 µL) using a QIAamp viral RNA kit (QIAGEN Sciences, Maryland, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Synthesis of cDNA

The RT reaction solution was prepared by exposing a microtube (0.5 mL, thin-walled sterilized tube) containing the RNA solution to 70 °C for 2 min, followed by the addition of 25 µL of mineral oil (DNase/RNase-free). The prepared solution was thoroughly mixed and then allowed to react for 10–30 s to clear the inner wall of the tube. Furthermore, DNA amplification was conducted using a PCR thermal cycler, which involved denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s. Additionally, this process was repeated for 35 cycles, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min and a hold at 40 °C (QIAGEN Sciences, Maryland, USA).

Nested-polymerase chain reaction (nPCR) protocol

The sequence of primers for the amplification of the HCV segment was as follows: forward 5’-TGAGGAACTACTGTCTTCACG-3’ and reverse 5’-AGCACCCTATCAGGCAGTACC-3’ primers were used for a first-round PCR that amplified a 258 bp fragment. The forward 5’-GGGAGAGCCATAGTGGTCTG-3’ and reverse 5’-CACTCGCAAGCACCCTATC-3’ primers were used for a second-round amplification of a 186 bp fragment according to a previous description by Wu et al. [28].

The amplification mixture consisted of 15.0 µL cDNA, 2 µL premixed solution, and 0.3 µL of each primer (P1 and P2) (QIAGEN Sciences, Maryland, USA). The enzyme was diluted with 2.4 µL of water, resulting in a total reaction volume of 20.0 µL. The response parameters were as follows: Initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 62 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. In the second cycle, the amplification mixture consisted of 12 µL of product from the first round, 10 µL of premixed solution, and 0.3 µL of each primer (P3 and P4). The enzyme was diluted with 2.6 µL of water, resulting in a total reaction volume of 25.0 µL. The reaction conditions were: 45 °C for 1 h and 95 °C for 45 min. (QIAGEN Sciences, Maryland, United States).

Furthermore, the size of amplified viral RNA with PCR was determined by loading 12 µL of each PCR onto 1.5% agarose gel (1.5%) electrophoresis that was stained with an ethidium bromide stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) and visualised under UV light with DNA Ladder ranges from 100 to 1000 bps. Interpretation of results: A result of more than 100 pb is positive, and a result of less than 100 pb is negative.

Data analysis

The results were statistically analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24 (IBM, USA), and they were presented descriptively in subgroups as frequencies and proportions in tables and figures. Moreover, Sensitivity and specificity tests were calculated by the following equations: Sensitivity % = No. of true positives divided by No. of true positives + No. of false negatives. Specificity % = No. of true negatives divided by No. of true negatives + No. of false positives. In addition, Chi-square test (χ2), confidence interval (95% CI), and the odds ratio (OR) used to assess the relationship between HCV infection and risk factors. Furthermore, the probability P value was considered statistically significant at a value less than 0.05.

The results of this study showed differences in detection capacities depending on the diagnostic techniques used to detect HCV in human serum. ICT detected HCV in only 18 (2.3%) samples, whereas ELISA detected HCV in 22 (2.7%) samples. In addition, molecular analysis using nPCR detected HCV in 21 (2.6%) samples, as shown in Fig. 2.

Discussion

The overall prevalence of HCV was recorded at 2.6%, and this finding is higher than the reported prevalence of HCV infection at 0.9% in Sudan [11], 0.60% in Ethiopia [29], 0.60% in Qatar [30], 0.50% in Saudi Arabia [31], and 0.4% in Iraq [32]. On the other hand, this finding is lower than reported at 6.4% in Egypt [33], 4.3% in Kuwait [34], and 4.5% in Pakistan [35]. Moreover, previous studies conducted in Yemen revealed that the prevalence of HCV was 0.79% in Hodeidah Governorate [36], 0.2% in Sana’a [24], 0.6% in Aden [37], and 5.1% in Sogotra Island [38], and the highest rate was recorded at 7.3% in Hadhramout [39].

The difference between our results and those of previous reports may be due to factors such as geographic location, sample size, study population, survey duration, and techniques used to detect the virus. In addition, the difficult economic situation, low public awareness, poor adherence to appropriate blood transfusion practices for patients, and high costs of hepatitis virus detection may have contributed to the increase in HCV prevalence in this study [40]. Therefore, national efforts are needed to develop effective strategies for HCV control through continuous monitoring and surveillance, strengthening health infrastructure, and training health care workers capable of early detection and therapeutic interventions.

The current results showed that the age group 21–30 years and the age group 41–50 years had a higher prevalence of HCV, and this result was in agreement with some studies [15, 24, 29] and contradicts previous studies that showed that older people are more susceptible to HCV infection [32, 41–43].

The prevalence of HCV was slightly higher among females compared to males. These results are consistent with the study by Gacche and Kaid [44] and inconsistent with other studies [29, 42]. Although the prevalence of HCV in this study was higher among participants from urban areas, no statistical differences were observed, as has been previously documented [23, 32]. Overcrowding in urban areas may be a factor contributing to the increased prevalence of HCV, which necessitates a comprehensive study to determine the factors contributing to the spread of this type of virus in such environments. Married participants were more likely to be infected with HCV, and these results are consistent with studies conducted in Yemen [41] and Brazil [43]. This increase may be due to partner infection, and further study is needed to clarify the relationship between HCV prevalence and marital status. Therefore, premarital screening for infectious diseases is recommended.

Furthermore, uneducated participants had a higher rate of HCV infection than educated participants, a finding that is inconsistent with previous studies [28, 43]. The educational level is a critical factor in the development of healthy behaviors and the increase of awareness to mitigate the transmission of infectious diseases [45]. Therefore, including topics related to ways to prevent communicable diseases in the curriculum at the early stages of education is important for promoting healthy behaviors and reducing the spread of diseases.

These results indicated that HCV infection was significantly more prevalent among participants whose families had a history of it. This finding is consistent with the studies of Al-Waleedi and Khader [41]. Therapeutic interventions should be implemented, and health awareness should be promoted regarding ways to prevent transmission of infection among family members.

In the current study, participants who had previously undergone surgery were found to be more likely to be infected with HCV, which is consistent with previous studies [41, 46]. Possibly, inadequate sterilization of surgical instruments played a role in the increased spread of HCV. Therefore, healthcare workers should adhere to standard protocols for sterilizing surgical instruments to limit the spread of viruses.

The results of this study show that the highest rate was recorded among individuals who did not receive a blood transfusion, a finding that contradicts previous studies [41, 46]. Acquisition of HCV through blood transfusion is a life-threatening health problem for patients, especially those with weakened immune systems, as they are more susceptible to infection and the development of complications [47, 48]. Therefore, the selection of blood and blood products is of paramount importance for the safety and protection of the lives of patients most at risk. This requires the early detection of HCV among blood donors using more accurate and reliable techniques, such as molecular detection.

These results revealed that participants who underwent cupping had higher rates of HCV infection compared to those who did not. Previous study has confirmed these findings [41]. Medical cupping is traditionally practiced in various regions of Yemen without following standard protocols to avoid pathogen transmission, which explains the high prevalence of HCV in this study. Therefore, cupping clinic practitioners must follow preventive and health safety measures to help reduce the spread of infectious diseases among cupping clinic visitors.

In this study, positive cases were examined by ELISA, and positive samples were confirmed by nPCR. Regarding the evaluation of the diagnostic test in this study, it was found that the validity of the rapid diagnostic test (cassette) for HCV was high, with a sensitivity and specificity of 81.8% and 100%, respectively. This result is in agreement with previous studies [39]. Additionally, PCR techniques have demonstrated greater sensitivity and specificity for HCV RNA detection. This outcome is supported by previous reports [49–51]. A specific issue with the HCV RNA assay is that HCV RNA can temporarily be undetectable due to the immune response, which controls viral replication transiently; patients during periods of nonviremia may be found to be anti-HCV positive while negative for HCV RNA. Consequently, HCV RNA tests must be repeated with a new specimen a few weeks later in these circumstances, and this need for retesting is one of the disadvantages of this test [52].

Strength and limitation of this study

One of the strengths of this study is that it is the first to use PCR to detect HCV in human serum, which is a more accurate and reliable confirmatory test. In addition, this study provides up-to-date data on the prevalence of HCV in the governorate, which helps decision-makers develop effective strategies and implement therapeutic interventions to mitigate the spread of the virus and its associated complications. The results of this study also contribute to enhancing early diagnosis of HCV by developing medical laboratory infrastructure in the study area and beyond. Despite the importance of the findings, there are some limitations. Due to limited resources, this study was unable to identify HCV genotypes, which would have added significance to this study. The results of this study do not provide a true picture of the prevalence of HCV in the study area, as this study was limited to specific population groups and over a period of time, which may affect the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the results in the long term. Additionally, this study did not assess participants’ knowledge, practices, and attitudes toward HCV prevalence and risks.

Conclusion

This study indicated a high prevalence of HCV infection in Amran Governorate, with the majority of cases occurring among females, urban residents, married individuals, the uneducated, and students. Participants with a family history of HCV, previous surgery, cupping therapy, and perinatal injuries were also more likely to be infected with HCV. Therefore, it is necessary to apply a more reliable molecular test, such as PCR, in all laboratories, health centers, and blood banks to decrease the public’s and patients’ transmission of HCV infection, as well as to implement effective strategies to protect the general public from the spread of this type of virus. Furthermore, health institutions in Yemen must intensify their efforts and support rapid response to ongoing, widespread surveillance and investigation of hepatitis C cases, which is critical to avoiding a HCV outbreak in Yemen. In addition, a study should be conducted to assess the level of awareness of the risk of HCV infection among the general population. Based on these findings, ongoing awareness campaigns should be implemented to reduce the burden of HCV transmission in the general population. Additionally, strengthening international cooperation is crucial to intensifying efforts to expand studies and conduct trials aimed at developing effective vaccines to prevent HCV.
