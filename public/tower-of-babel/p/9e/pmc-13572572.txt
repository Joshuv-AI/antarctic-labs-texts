AST/ALT, APOE ε4, and Alzheimer's disease progression: A longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Young Hyeon Ahn, Chan‐Mo Yang, Dae‐Jin Kim, Sae Hwan Lee, Young Hyun Jung, Sang‐Yeol Lee, Sung‐Hoon Yoon, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT) ratio is a routinely available liver‐enzyme index. Whether apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 modifies associations of baseline AST/ALT with longitudinal Alzheimer's disease (AD) trajectories is unclear.METHODSIn 2709 Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative participants, linear mixed‐effects models estimated AST/ALT‐associated longitudinal change in Mini‐Mental State Examination, Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale 13‐item Cognitive subscale, and AD‐signature cortical thickness; Cox models evaluated continuous AST/ALT and an extreme‐tertile APOE ε4 × AST/ALT comparison for clinical progression.RESULTSHigher baseline AST/ALT was associated with faster cognitive decline and AD‐signature cortical thinning, with larger associations among APOE ε4 carriers (interaction p = .0006–0.040). In clinical‐progression analyses, continuous AST/ALT was not significantly associated with risk (hazard ratio [HR] 1.07 per standard deviation, p = 0.12). In the extreme‐tertile comparison, APOE ε4 carriers with high AST/ALT had the highest adjusted hazard (HR 3.08, 95% confidence interval 2.18–4.34), but multiplicative and additive interactions were null and incremental discrimination was minimal (ΔC = +0.003).DISCUSSIONBaseline AST/ALT was associated with faster cognitive and cortical decline, particularly among APOE ε4 carriers, but provided limited incremental information for clinical progression beyond APOE ε4 and baseline diagnosis. These findings represent a large longitudinal extension of prior liver‐enzyme observations and require external validation.Keywords: alzheimer's disease, alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, apolipoprotein E ε4, aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐alanine aminotransferase ratio, biomarkers, clinical progression, cognitive decline, cortical thinning, liver‐enzyme markers, longitudinal

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia; the number of people living with dementia was estimated at ≈ 57 million in 2019 and is projected to reach 153 million by 2050. 1 AD is defined neuropathologically by amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and tau‐based neurofibrillary tangles, operationalized in the amyloid/tau/neurodegeneration (A/T/N) framework. 2 Considerable inter‐individual variation in clinical trajectories given equivalent amyloid burden 3 suggests that additional modifiers contribute to progression. Among candidate modifiers, systemic and metabolic stress—peripheral metabolic dysfunction, insulin resistance, and lipid dyshomeostasis—has been linked to reduced brain volume, 4 altered AD biomarker signatures, 5 and accelerated cognitive decline. 6

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late‐onset AD, conferring 3‐ to 12‐fold elevated risk in heterozygotes and homozygotes, respectively, while APOE ε2 is protective. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Beyond its central role in amyloid clearance and lipid handling, 7 apoE has substantial peripheral metabolic functions: hepatocytes are major sites of apoE synthesis, with the brain producing a separate astrocyte‐ and microglia‐derived pool. 11 APOE isoforms differ in their lipoprotein–particle associations, 11 and APOE ε4 has been linked to altered hepatic mitochondrial responses in preclinical models. 12 These observations position the liver as a potential site through which APOE ε4‐related alterations in peripheral lipid and metabolic homeostasis may intersect with brain vulnerability. 13 Because both APOE ε4 and hepatic–metabolic dysfunction have been implicated in AD, routinely measured liver‐enzyme signals may provide an accessible window into this peripheral axis.

The aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT) ratio summarizes the relative pattern of two routinely measured aminotransferases. A lower ratio reflects relative ALT predominance, whereas a higher ratio reflects relative AST predominance; in chronic liver disease settings, higher ratios have traditionally been associated with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis. 14 , 15 , 16 More recently, AST/ALT and related liver‐enzyme indices have been investigated in relation to cognition and AD‐related phenotypes within the liver–brain axis framework. In the Dementia Platform Korea Trial‐Ready Registry, AST/ALT was associated with cognition, while related hepatic indices showed APOE ε4‐dependent associations with cognition and amyloid burden. 17 Korean memory clinic and Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) studies similarly reported associations of liver‐enzyme measures with cognition and cortical atrophy, 5 , 18 and a community‐based study linked higher AST/ALT to subsequent cognitive impairment over 7 years. 19 Large‐scale longitudinal evidence jointly evaluating cognitive and cortical trajectories, particularly according to APOE ε4 carrier status, remains limited.

Our primary objective was to test whether APOE ε4 carrier status modified associations between baseline AST/ALT and longitudinal cognitive and AD‐signature cortical trajectories in 2709 ADNI participants. As a secondary objective, we evaluated continuous and extreme‐tertile associations of AST/ALT with clinical AD progression, including incremental discrimination and multiplicative and additive interactions.

METHODS

Study cohort

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the ADNI database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public–private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early AD. For the present analysis, data from ADNI1, ADNI‐GO, ADNI2, ADNI3, and ADNI4 were integrated through the April 22, 2026, data release. 20 All ADNI protocols were approved by participating sites’ institutional review boards (IRBs) and written informed consent was obtained; the present analysis was granted exemption by the Wonkwang University Hospital IRB (WKUH 2026‐05‐017) as it used de‐identified, publicly available data.

Participants were eligible if they had (1) APOE genotype, (2) baseline serum AST, ALT, and platelet measurements, and (3) at least one cognitive assessment (Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale 13‐item Cognitive subscale {ADAS‐Cog 13] or Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE]). Exclusion criteria were ALT below detection, self‐reported cirrhosis or liver transplantation, and visit offset > 15 years from baseline. Application yielded n = 2709. Baseline diagnosis (cognitively normal [CN], MCI, or AD dementia) followed standardized ADNI criteria. Laboratory measures

Serum AST, ALT, glucose, triglycerides, and platelet count were obtained at the baseline ADNI visit using standardized protocols. Implausible values (AST > 500 U/L, ALT > 500 U/L, platelet < 50 × 109/L) were excluded. The primary exposure was the AST/ALT ratio, a routinely available liver‐enzyme index. 14 , 15 , 16 Secondary indices were the fibrosis‐4 index (FIB‐4 = age × AST / [platelet × √ALT]), 21 the AST‐to‐platelet ratio index (APRI = [AST/40] / platelet × 100), 22 and the triglyceride–glucose index (TyG = ln [triglyceride × glucose / 2]). 23 AST/ALT was specified as the primary liver‐enzyme index because it is directly derived from the two routinely measured aminotransferases. FIB‐4, APRI, and TyG, which incorporate additional demographic, hematologic, or metabolic components, were evaluated as comparators. All indices were standardized (mean 0, SD 1) within the analytic cohort before modeling. AST/ALT was modeled as a continuous standardized exposure; top‐versus‐bottom AST/ALT tertile subgroups were used only for the Cox progression‐risk analysis (see section 3.5). Extreme tertiles provided an interpretable contrast for subgroup description and Kaplan–Meier visualization rather than validated clinical thresholds; the middle tertile was retained in a six‐group sensitivity analysis, and continuous modeling evaluated the full AST/ALT scale.

APOE ε4 genotyping

APOE genotype was obtained from ADNI records. APOE ε4 carrier status (≥ 1 ε4 allele) was the primary grouping and effect‐modification variable. 8 , 9 , 10 Additive APOE ε4 dosage (0/1/2, continuous per allele) was evaluated in sensitivity analyses (Table S2 in supporting information).

Cognitive assessments

The ADAS‐Cog 13 (0–85; higher = worse) 8 , 24 and MMSE (0–30; lower = worse) 8 , 25 were obtained at baseline and all follow‐up visits per ADNI standard schedule.

Structural MRI

Structural T1‐weighted MRI was acquired on 1.5T or 3T scanners per ADNI standardized protocols and processed by the ADNI imaging core (University of California San Francisco) using FreeSurfer 7.1.1 with the Desikan–Killiany atlas, 26 yielding cortical thickness for 68 bilateral regions. An AD‐signature composite was computed as the unweighted mean thickness across bilateral entorhinal, parahippocampal, inferior temporal, middle temporal, and supramarginal cortex (10 regions of interest [ROIs]). 2 , 27 All 68 regions were also examined individually.

Exploratory amyloid PET, plasma phosphorylated tau 217, and tau PET

Amyloid PET (Centiloids [CL]; 24 CL positivity threshold), plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)217 (Fujirebio Lumipulse), and 18F‐flortaucipir tau PET (meta‐temporal composite standardized uptake value ratio; positivity threshold 1.3) were processed by the ADNI PET core (University of California Berkeley). Full acquisition, processing, and longitudinal trajectory protocols are provided in Supplementary Methods 1 in supporting information. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31

Statistical analysis

Longitudinal mixed‐effects models

Longitudinal outcomes were modeled using linear mixed‐effects regression (statsmodels v0.14, REML) 32 with participant‐specific random intercepts and random slopes for visit time (years from baseline). Fixed effects included visit time, standardized AST/ALT ratio, APOE ε4 carrier status; their interaction terms; and baseline age, sex, education, and race/ethnicity. The primary estimand was the time × AST/ALT term within APOE ε4 carrier and non‐carrier strata; the time × AST/ALT × APOE ε4 carrier‐status term tested effect modification. Outcomes were standardized (mean 0, SD 1) within analytic samples; full model‐selection and standardization procedures appear in Supplementary Methods 2 and 3 in supporting information. 3 , 33 , 34 Longitudinal models used all available repeated outcome observations without imputation of missing outcomes.

Additional sensitivity analyses examined alternative random‐effects and covariate specifications; additive APOE ε4 dosage; restriction to CN participants; exclusion of AST/ALT values > 2.0; additional adjustment for statin use, ADNI phase, and MRI field strength; and heterogeneity by sex and age group (< 70 vs. ≥ 70). Comparator models evaluated FIB‐4, APRI, and TyG.

The time × AST/ALT association was examined across 68 Desikan–Killiany cortical thickness regions, with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied within the regional scan. 35 Regional concordance between AST/ALT‐associated cortical and tau‐associated regions was assessed as an exploratory analysis (see Supplementary Methods 4 in supporting information for exploratory regional concordance and baseline tau‐positivity testing procedures).

After excluding baseline AD dementia, the analytic cohorts were defined as follows: the full survival‐eligible cohort included 2243 participants with 440 events; continuous adjusted analyses included 2225 complete cases with 436 events; and for the extreme‐tertile comparison, exclusion of the middle AST/ALT tertile yielded 1570 participants with 306 events, of whom 1559 (304 events) had complete covariates for adjusted four‐group analyses. Time‐to‐event was the first visit meeting clinical AD criteria; non‐progressors were right censored at the last diagnostic assessment. Survival analyses used complete cases for the required baseline covariates (see analytic cohort flow in Figure S1). Kaplan–Meier and log‐rank tests compared APOE ε4 × AST/ALT tertile subgroups. Cox proportional‐hazards models adjusted for baseline age, sex, education, and diagnosis, with assumptions checked by scaled Schoenfeld residuals. AST/ALT was additionally modeled as a continuous standardized exposure (per SD), including an AST/ALT × APOE ε4 interaction and a restricted cubic spline for non‐linearity. Nested Cox models sequentially added APOE ε4 carrier status, AST/ALT‐high, and their interaction to a baseline clinical model (age, sex, education, baseline diagnosis); Harrell C‐index and likelihood‐ratio tests quantified incremental discrimination, with 2000‐resample percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs). Because baseline diagnosis and age showed evidence of non‐proportionality, sensitivity analyses stratified by baseline diagnosis and by baseline diagnosis plus age group were performed. Survival models used the Python lifelines package. 36 Adjusted absolute risks at 9 years were estimated by marginal standardization (g‐computation) from the fitted four‐group Cox model; for each subgroup, individual predicted progression risks at t = 9 years were averaged over the observed covariate distribution.

APOE ε4 carrier‐status interaction and sensitivity

Under the primary random‐slope specification, the time × AST/ALT × APOE ε4 carrier‐status interaction was significant for AD‐signature cortical thickness (β = –0.015, p = 0.04), MMSE (β = –0.039, p = 0.006), and ADAS‐Cog 13 (β = +0.021, p = 0.04). Diagnosis adjustment preserved all three signals; under diagnosis × time adjustment, MMSE and ADAS‐Cog 13 remained significant (p = 0.006 and p = 0.04) while AD‐signature thickness attenuated to borderline (p = 0.06). Sensitivity models using additive APOE ε4 allele dosage yielded weaker evidence (AD‐signature p = 0.13; MMSE p = 0.06; ADAS‐Cog 13 p = 0.21). Generalized estimating equation (GEE) robust‐standard error (SE) sensitivity tests did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.11–17). The associations observed in carriers and non‐carriers were directionally preserved after adjustment for ADNI phase, MRI field strength, and both combined. Formal four‐way (time × AST/ALT × APOE ε4 carrier × subgroup) interaction tests showed no evidence of heterogeneity by sex (all p ≥0.32); age‐group heterogeneity was observed for MMSE (p = 0.005) but not for AD‐signature cortical thickness (p = 0.09) or ADAS‐Cog 13 (p = 0.49; Table S2).

Comparison to FIB‐4, APRI, and TyG

In comparator analyses among APOE ε4 carriers, FIB‐4 reached nominal significance for MMSE decline (β = –0.029, p = 0.03) but showed a weaker association than AST/ALT with AD‐signature cortical thinning (β = –0.011, p = 0.07); APRI and TyG were null (Table S2, Panel C). Among the evaluated indices, the longitudinal association among APOE ε4 carriers was most consistent for AST/ALT, while FIB‐4 showed an intermediate pattern.

Frontotemporal regional cortical thinning

Among APOE ε4 carriers, AST/ALT‐associated thinning was concentrated in frontotemporal and temporoparietal association cortices (Figure 2; Table S3, Panel A). APOE ε4 carriers had a higher prevalence of baseline tau positivity than non‐carriers (40.9% vs. 14.4%, p < 0.001; Figure S2 in supporting information). Clinical progression and incremental discrimination

We grouped participants into four mutually exclusive subgroups based on APOE ε4 carrier status and baseline AST/ALT tertile (cutpoints ≤ 1.00 and ≥ 1.26; median [IQR] AST/ALT 0.88 [0.77–0.96] in the low tertile and 1.44 [1.33–1.62] in the high tertile; mid‐tertile excluded for contrast clarity; n = 1896 of 2709). The extreme‐tertile survival cohort comprised n = 1570 (511, 414, 320, and 325 in non‐carrier × AST/ALT‐low, non‐carrier × AST/ALT‐high, carrier × AST/ALT‐low, and carrier × AST/ALT‐high subgroups, respectively) with 306 incident AD‐progression events during follow‐up.

Per‐subject longitudinal slopes of MMSE decline and AD‐signature cortical thinning were ordered across the four subgroups (Table S4 in supporting information). The carrier × AST/ALT‐high subgroup exhibited the steepest declines (mean MMSE slope –1.54 points/year; mean thickness slope –0.044 mm/year), exceeding the non‐carrier × AST/ALT‐low reference (–0.45 points/year MMSE; –0.017 mm/year thickness). Across the full observed follow‐up, crude AD‐progression event proportions were 36.0% (117/325) in the carrier × AST/ALT‐high subgroup and 10.0% (51/511) in the non‐carrier × AST/ALT‐low subgroup, with intermediate proportions in the remaining subgroups (Figure 3; four‐group log‐rank p < 0.001).

Cox proportional hazards regression adjusted for baseline age, sex, education, and baseline diagnosis (CN vs. MCI) yielded an ordered pattern of progression risk across the four subgroups (Table 3). Multivariable adjustment, including baseline diagnosis, attenuated the carrier × AST/ALT‐high estimate from 4.57 to 3.08 (Table S4); baseline disease stage was strongly associated with progression (MCI vs. CN hazard ratio [HR] 10.13, p < 0.001). A six‐group sensitivity retaining the mid‐tertile yielded a near‐identical carrier × AST/ALT‐high adjusted HR of 3.03.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

In this longitudinal ADNI cohort, higher baseline AST/ALT was associated with faster cognitive decline and AD‐signature cortical thinning, with larger estimates among APOE ε4 carriers. The APOE ε4 carrier/high‐AST/ALT subgroup also showed the highest model‐estimated 9‐year cumulative incidence.

The findings differed according to the outcome scale. APOE ε4 modified the association of AST/ALT with continuous cognitive and cortical trajectories, whereas continuous AST/ALT was not significantly associated with clinical progression. The extreme‐tertile comparison identified risk enrichment in the carrier/high‐AST/ALT subgroup, but neither multiplicative nor additive interaction was significant, and incremental discrimination beyond APOE ε4 and baseline diagnosis was minimal.

These findings extend previous liver‐enzyme and APOE ε4 observations by showing stronger longitudinal cognitive and cortical associations among APOE ε4 carriers in a large multimodal cohort.

Prior human evidence and the APOE modifier framework

Prior studies have linked liver enzymes, particularly AST/ALT, with cognition, cortical atrophy, and AD biomarkers, 5 , 17 , 18 , 19 with some reporting stronger associations among APOE ε4 carriers. 5 , 17 , 18 The stronger associations among APOE ε4 carriers are consistent with a broader APOE modifier framework in which APOE ε4 shapes the cognitive consequences of both central pathology and peripheral vascular–metabolic exposures. APOE ε4 has been shown to amplify amyloid–cognition associations across multiple cohorts 37 and to modify the cognitive impact of cardiovascular and metabolic exposures. 38 , 39 Our results raise the possibility that this framework also extends to peripheral liver‐enzyme signals.

Biological plausibility and candidate mechanisms

The stronger longitudinal associations among APOE ε4 carriers provide a basis for considering potential hepatic–metabolic mechanisms. The observed associations are consistent with a model in which hepatic–metabolic processes interact with peripheral APOE biology to influence brain vulnerability. Because AST and ALT were largely within routine clinical ranges, the AST/ALT ratio may capture relative aminotransferase patterns and subclinical systemic metabolic variation rather than overt liver injury.

Hepatocytes contribute substantially to circulating apoE; APOE isoforms differ in lipid‐particle association; and hepatic APOE ε4 may influence systemic lipid handling, mitochondrial function, insulin signaling, and brain integrity. 11 , 12 , 40 Potential liver–brain pathways include altered hepatic Aβ production and clearance, 41 , 42 , 43 apoE‐dependent amyloid handling, 7 , 44 and broader systemic metabolic signaling. Serial assessment of liver enzymes alongside lipid, metabolic, and inflammatory mediators may help clarify which pathways contribute.

Regional and tau‐associated findings

The AST/ALT‐associated thinning pattern involved frontotemporal and temporoparietal association cortices and spatially overlapped with tau‐associated regions in the same cohort. Previous tau PET studies have linked regional tau burden to cortical thinning and clinical disease severity, 45 , 46 while preclinical work suggests that APOE ε4 may increase vulnerability to tau‐related neurodegeneration. 47 Together, these findings raise the possibility that AST/ALT‐associated cortical changes preferentially involve networks susceptible to tau pathology. Longitudinal studies combining serial tau PET and cortical‐thickness measures will be needed to clarify temporal ordering and assess whether tau burden contributes to this association.

Clinical progression

The extreme‐tertile analysis identified APOE ε4 carriers with high AST/ALT as the subgroup with the greatest model‐estimated 9‐year cumulative incidence. This pattern suggests that risk may concentrate when APOE ε4 carrier status and high AST/ALT coexist. Because neither the multiplicative nor the additive interaction was significant, the observed pattern is compatible with accumulation of risk rather than statistical synergy. The small gain in discrimination further indicates that AST/ALT may be more informative for characterizing risk concentration at the group level than for materially improving individual‐level prediction.

Limitations

ADNI is a selected, highly educated, predominantly non‐Hispanic White cohort; effect magnitudes may differ in more diverse community populations with greater liver‐metabolic variability. AST/ALT was measured once at baseline and is biologically non‐specific, precluding assessment of within‐subject enzyme trajectories or isolation of a liver‐specific mechanism. In older adults, the ratio may also be influenced by extrahepatic tissue injury, medication use, and relatively low ALT associated with frailty or sarcopenia. 48 Larger estimates among carriers may partially reflect their more advanced baseline disease stage; diagnosis adjustment preserved this pattern but cannot fully exclude residual stage‐related confounding. The observational design precludes causal inference; continuous AST/ALT was not significantly associated with clinical progression, and its incremental discrimination beyond baseline clinical factors and APOE ε4 was minimal. Interaction evidence varied by model specification and by whether APOE ε4 was modeled as carrier status or additive allele dosage: the cognitive interactions remained significant in diagnosis‐adjusted mixed‐effects models, whereas GEE sensitivity tests were not statistically significant; the cortical‐thickness interaction attenuated under diagnosis‐by‐time adjustment, and additive allele–dosage models yielded weaker evidence (Table S2). Findings should therefore be interpreted as associations that differed between carriers and non‐carriers rather than as a linear allele–dose response; larger cohorts with sufficient ε4 homozygote representation are needed. Age‐related heterogeneity was observed only for MMSE, not for AD‐signature thickness or ADAS‐Cog 13, and should therefore be considered exploratory. Although Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied within the regional scan, the possibility of false‐positive findings arising from multiplicity across primary, comparator, sensitivity, and exploratory analyses cannot be fully excluded, and observed magnitudes are modest. Replication in more diverse cohorts with serial liver‐enzyme measurements and longitudinal metabolic and AD biomarkers will be important for clarifying the biological and prognostic relevance of these findings. Accordingly, these observational findings should be considered hypothesis generating.

Conclusions

Baseline AST/ALT was associated with longitudinal cognitive and cortical decline in ADNI, with larger estimates among APOE ε4 carriers. These findings support further evaluation of AST/ALT as an accessible peripheral correlate of APOE ε4‐associated longitudinal decline in independent cohorts and mechanistic studies.
