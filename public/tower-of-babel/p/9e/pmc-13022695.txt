Integrated metagenomic sequencing and phage display-based immunoprecipitation sequencing for presumptive viral infection: a case report
Authors: Jessica G Shantha, Cindi Chen, Armin Hinterwirth, John A Gonzales, Nisha R Acharya, Thomas M Lietman, Thuy Doan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a case of uveitis in which unbiased metagenomic sequencing (MDS) and phage immunoprecipitation sequencing (PhIP-Seq) were performed on intraocular fluid.ObservationsA female patient with a past medical history of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1) who presented with chronic active anterior and intermediate uveitis with cystoid macular edema in the left eye. Introduction

Uveitis is a heterogeneous collection of inflammatory eye diseases with etiologies that range from infectious to non-infectious/autoimmune. Uveitis can cause significant visual morbidity and has a high potential for vision impairment and blindness due to the array of ocular complications associated with intraocular inflammation.1 The ability to accurately differentiate between infectious and inflammatory etiology is critical in mitigating poor clinical outcomes.

Molecular approaches such as pathogen-directed polymerase chain reaction (PCR), coupled with cultures and stains, have been the mainstay for infectious-associated diagnostics for uveitis. Recently, metagenomic sequencing (MDS), which is an unbiased approach for pathogen detection, has been used as an unbiased approach to identify possible unexpected pathogens not on the differential diagnosis. Directed serological testing, such as Toxoplasmosis or CMV IgM/IgG, can help confirm a subacute infection where the pathogen load may be below the threshold of detectability with culture or nucleic acid/genomic testing. Phage immunoprecipitation sequencing (PhIP-Seq) is a technology that can query human fluids and tissues, such as intraocular fluid, for seroreactivity of any target that is programmed into the assay. For example, a comprehensive programmable phage-display viral proteome will allow for the unbiased detection of thousands of viral-associated antibodies.2,3 This can be used in presumed infectious cases, but can also query the potential of molecular mimicry of viruses contributing to the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases.4 Understanding the local antibody repertoire in intraocular fluid may lend itself to a more precise and accurate diagnosis, illuminate underlying pathogenesis, and lead to the discovery of novel biomarkers that could influence the development of therapeutics in uveitis patients.

Here, we report a case in which unbiased sequencing for pathogen detection using MDS was performed in combination with PhIP-Seq to interrogate pathogen-associated antibodies in the intraocular fluid of a patient with presumed infectious uveitis. The results confirmed clinical suspicion of immune recovery uveitis due to CMV, as well as additional insights into systemic diseases associated with the patient.

Case report

A 68-year-old female presents with blurry vision in the left eye with a past medical history of diabetes mellitus, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1), and a remote history of a stage 4 diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of the lung and neck that is currently in remission. She also had respiratory symptoms in which she was diagnosed with COVID. The patient was initially referred for evaluation for cystoid macular edema (CME) in the left eye in the setting of a presumed history of viral retinitis. The outside provider initially noted new CME in the left eye without ocular inflammation and treated this with 3 intravitreal 0.7mg dexamethasone implants. The CME initially responded and resolved, but a few weeks after the most recent injection, she developed a panuveitis with a new area of supertemporal retinitis in the left eye. She had an anterior chamber paracentesis in which the sample was lost and was treated for presumed cytomegalovirus (CMV) retinitis with an intravitreal foscarnet injection and started on valganciclovir 900mg 2 times per day. This was in the setting of being off her prophylaxis HIV medications with a reported low CD4 count. The panuveitis and retinitis resolved, and the patient was subsequently placed on a maintenance dose of valganciclovir 450 mg daily. Her CME returned, and she was promptly referred to the F.I. Proctor Foundation for evaluation.

At her initial visit, the visual acuity was 20/25 in the right eye and 20/150 in the left eye with normal eye pressure and pupil exam in both eyes. Anterior segment exam revealed a nuclear sclerotic cataract in the right eye. In the left eye, she had scattered stellate keratic precipitates with 1+ anterior chamber cell and flare with a nuclear, cortical, and posterior subcapsular cataract, and 1+ anterior vitreous cell. Dilated fundus exam was normal in the right eye, and in the left eye, there was macular edema with scarring of the peripheral retina with associated fibrosis without active frank retinitis (Fig. 1A).

At her initial presentation, our differential diagnosis included infectious with CMV, immune recovery uveitis, and masquerades. An anterior chamber paracentesis was performed and sent for viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing, including CMV, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and Toxoplasma gondii. Additionally, systemic laboratory testing was performed, including tuberculosis testing with a QuantiFERON-TB Gold and syphilis antibody testing. The patient consented and was enrolled in a research study to perform deep sequencing on leftover aqueous fluid for pathogen detection and PhIP-Seq using a viral proteome-wide programmable phage display to characterize the intraocular viral humoral response. The patient was initially treated with difluprednate 4 times/day in the left eye and acetazolamide 500mg 2 times/day. She was maintained on valganciclovir 450mg daily.

The laboratory investigations were negative, including tuberculosis and syphilis testing, and aqueous PCR testing for CMV, HSV, VZV, and Toxoplasma gondii.

Laboratory methods

Deep sequencing was performed on aqueous fluid as previously described.5 Briefly, extracted RNA was first converted to cDNA, and a sequencing library was prepared using the NEBNext RNA ULTRA II kit (New England BioLabs) according to the manufacturer's instructions and sequenced on the NovaSeq X (Illumina) using 150-nucleotide paired-end sequencing. PhIP-Seq was performed on the remaining aqueous fluid. Our laboratory has created a pathogen proteome-phage display library that includes Chlamydia trachomatis and all known vertebrate viruses. This was created using the entire human and animal virus protein sequences downloaded from UniProtKB (January 31, 2024). All open reading frames for each pathogen were divided into sequences of 62-aa peptides, with consecutive peptides sharing a 25-aa overlap. Sequences with 99% identity were collapsed and then converted to DNA sequences. The complete viral cDNA library was cloned, packaged, and amplified in T7 phages. Titers were determined with plaque assays. Five microliters of intraocular fluid were incubated with 500 μL of the bacteriophage T7 library. Antibodies were captured using protein A/G magnetic beads, and antibody-bound phages were eluted and amplified in E. coli. DNA was extracted and processed with sequencing using 150-nucleotide paired-end sequencing on the NovaSeq X (Illumina). Sequencing reads were aligned to the reference comprehensive human and animal virus proteome database and aligned peptide counts are normalized to count per million.

Laboratory results

Deep sequencing for pathogen detection was negative for microorganisms, including viruses such as CMV. Phageimmunoprecipitation sequencing of the sample revealed abundant and notable seroreactivity (antibody detection) to CMV, HIV, SARS-CoV-2, and Chlamydia trachomatis (Fig. 1B and C). These findings confirmed the clinical suspicion of prior intraocular infection with CMV. It also highlighted the patient's distant exposures to various pathogens. The patient was diagnosed with immune recovery uveitis based on the testing results.

Discussion

This case demonstrated the clinical utility of combining MDS and PhIP-Seq for patients with presumed intraocular infections. The amount of fluid used was only 60 μL, of which 50 μL was used for MDS and 10 μL was used for PhIP-Seq (5 μL per replicate). The negative MDS results indicated that the patient no longer had an active CMV infection. However, the notable presence of CMV-associated antibodies in the aqueous fluid was consistent with a robust humoral response intraocularly, and thus likely responsible for her chronic macular edema that prompted the referral to a tertiary center.

In addition to confirming the diagnosis of immune reconstitution uveitis, our pathogen phage programmable peptidome was able to identify antibodies to HIV-1, SARS-CoV-2, and even Chlamydia trachomatis, consistent with the patient's known prior exposures.

The detection of antibodies to infectious organisms in intraocular fluid is not new. Before the development of molecular testing, such as PCR, the Goldmann-Witmer coefficient was used to determine the etiology of intraocular infections.6 The assay requires both intraocular fluid and serum and compares the local antibody levels to those of the blood to determine the etiology for infections. Indeed, it was successfully applied in what is now a landmark study that identify rubella as the etiologic agent causing Fuchs’ heterochromic iridocyclitis.7 Over time, PCR testing and cultures have become the more popular methods for detecting pathogens in intraocular fluid in the United States. As technology continues to advance, MDS has also been demonstrated to be an effective technique to evaluate microorganisms in ocular fluids and tissues such as the conjunctiva,8 cornea,9 and intraocular space.5

Now, PhIP-Seq offers yet another complementary approach to interrogate intraocular specimens and provide unique information from both an autoantigen discovery in non-infectious uveitis to pathogen-associated antibodies in infectious cases. Instead of using traditional testing with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for antibody detection, which would not be possible given the fluid requirement for testing for multiple pathogens and the limited amount of intraocular fluid. PhIP-Seq can analyze a small amount of intraocular fluid and interrogate for pathogens in an unbiased manner. Conclusion

In summary, the combination of MDS and phage-programmable peptidome immunoprecipitation sequencing allows for unbiased and complementary interrogation of pathogen-associated genomic and antibodies for patients with presumed intraocular infections and requires minute amounts of intraocular fluids. In this patient, it offered evidence for a previous CMV-associated retinitis and was also able to provide the local antibody repertoire reflective of prior infectious exposures.
