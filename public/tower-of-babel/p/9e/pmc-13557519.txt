A pathological analysis of pediatric pulmonary cystic/cavitary lesions
Authors: VL Nanaware, PA Sathe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPediatric pulmonary cystic/cavitary lesions are uncommon. The spectrum of cystic/cavitary pulmonary lesions in pediatric patients includes congenital and acquired lesions. They have similar clinical presentation and overlapping radiologic features. Histopathology plays an important role in diagnosis. This study was planned to describe the spectrum of surgically resected cystic/cavitary pulmonary lesions in pediatric patients (age <12 years) and correlate clinical features with pathology findings. This is a single-center 10-year retrospective descriptive study. The cases were divided into two categories: congenital and acquired. Demographic details, clinical presentation, and investigational data were correlated with pathology findings.The study included 40 cases of surgically resected cystic/cavitary pulmonary lesions received from pediatric patients. Congenital lesions (22 cases, 55%) predominated over acquired lesions (18 cases, 45%). Cystic pulmonary airway malformation (29.2%, 12 cases) was the most common lesion and the most common cystic lesion overall. Acquired lesions included hydatid cyst (nine cases) and post infectious (post abscess) cavitary lesions (nine cases which included two cases of tuberculosis). Most congenital lesions were found in the neonates and infants and none found in late childhood, while most acquired lesions belonged to late childhood with no case found in neonates. Though radiology plays an important role in the diagnosis of cystic/cavitary lesions, histopathology is the key investigation for confirmation. Very few studies that describe the clinico-pathologic spectrum of these rare lesions in children were found in the literature.

Introduction

Pediatric pulmonary cystic/cavitary lesions are uncommon. Traditionally, the term cyst is used to describe an air-containing space surrounded by a relatively thin wall (less than 4 mm) and the term cavity is used to describe an air containing space with a relatively thick wall (more than 4 mm).[1] The pathologic spectrum of these lesions is wide and different in children as compared to adults.[2] These lesions can be developmental (congenital) or acquired in origin.[34] As the clinical and radiologic features can be overlapping, histologic examination is required for definite diagnosis. This study aimed to depict the varied spectrum of these uncommon lesions in children.

Materials and Methods

A single-center, 10-year retrospective observational study at a tertiary referral hospital attempted to analyze cystic/cavitary pulmonary lesions in pediatric patients. Prior permission of the institutional ethics committee was obtained for this study along with waiver of consent. All surgically resected cystic/cavitary pulmonary lesions in the pediatric age group (≤12 years) were included. Two cases in which clinical and/or pathology details were not available were excluded from the study. The lesions were divided into two groups: congenital [developmental lesions like bronchopulmonary foregut malformations (BPM) and congenital lobar emphysema (CLE)] and acquired (hydatid cyst, chronic abscess, and tuberculous cavity). The lesions were diagnosed based on their histopathological findings. Demographic details, clinical presentation, and investigational data were correlated with pathology findings (obtained from department records). Descriptive statistical methods were used (percentage). Necessary conclusions were drawn.

Results

A total 40 cases were included in the study. Congenital lesions (22 cases, 55%) predominated over acquired lesions (18 cases, 45%) [Table 1]. All congenital lesions were seen below the age of 5 years, 8 of 22 cases (36%) being found in neonates. Majority acquired lesions (11 out of 18 cases) were seen in late childhood. Congenital lesions were slightly more common in males (male: female ratio = 1.3:1).

Distribution of cases (n=40)

Among the congenital lesions [Table 1], cystic pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) (30%, 12 of 40 cases) was the most common lesion and the most common cystic lesion overall [Figure 1a]. The other congenital lesions are mentioned in Table 1. All congenital lesions (22 cases = 55%) were found in neonates and early childhood. No case was found in late childhood. Presenting complaints in congenital lesions were dyspnea and cough along with recurrent respiratory infections and fever in a few cases. Five cases of CPAM were diagnosed antenatally. Among congenital lesions, 12 cases underwent only computed tomography, six cases underwent roentgenogram and computed tomography, two cases underwent only roentgenogram, and two cases underwent ultrasonography and computed tomography. In three cases, radiology suggested a diagnosis of infected CPAM, whereas histology was diagnostic of post infective cyst. In one case, radiologic diagnosis was infected bronchogenic cyst, whereas the histologic diagnosis was an infected CPAM. All cases of CLE and CPAM underwent a lobectomy. Among the 12 cases of CPAM, seven cases were found on the right side. Maximum cases were seen in the upper lobe. The maximum size of the lobectomy specimen was 8 × 6 × 2 cm. The following subtypes of CPAM (revised Stocker classification) were found: Type 1, 7 cases (58.33%) [Figure 1b]; Type 2, 2 cases (16.67%); and Type 4, one case. In two cases, histological subtype could not be identified as these lesions were infected. All cases of CLE were found in the upper lobe and predominated on the left side. The maximum size of the lobectomy specimen was 10 × 7 × 3 cm. Grossly, cases had the appearance of an overinflated lung [Figure 1c], and microscopically, the lung parenchyma appeared overinflated [Figure 1d]. Cystectomy was performed in all bronchogenic cysts. Among the bronchogenic cysts, there was predominance on the left side and upper lobe. The cysts were smooth-walled, and the largest cyst measured 6.5 cm in diameter and the maximum wall thickness was 2 cm. Histologically, all cases had a pseudostratified ciliated lining epithelium and had cartilaginous nodules in the wall [Figure 1e and f]. One bronchogenic cyst was infected, thus altering the histology. A single case of benign cyst had unclassified histology.

Acquired lesions [Table 1] included hydatid cyst (nine cases) [Figure 2a and b] and chronic abscess [seven cases, Figure 2c and d] and tuberculous cavity [two cases, Figure 2e and f]. Most cases were found in late childhood (11 cases) and none in neonates. All cases presented with fever; other symptoms were recurrent respiratory infections, cough, dyspnea, and hemoptysis. Two cases of hydatid cyst had associated hepatic hydatid cyst. Among the acquired cases, eight cases each underwent only computed tomography and roentgenogram and computed tomography, respectively; one case underwent ultrasonography and computed tomography; and one case underwent roentgenogram, ultrasonography, and computed tomography. All cases of hydatid cysts were diagnosed on radiology and underwent cystectomy. They were received as multiple tissue pieces. The cysts had a classic tender coconut appearance [Figure 2a] and were slightly more common on the left side with the lower lobe being the most common location. The microscopy showed a lamellated membrane (ectocyst) in all cases and an associated germinal layer in a few cases. Lobectomy had to be performed in two cases each of chronic abscess and tuberculous cavity as there were diffuse cavitation and parenchymal damage. Cystectomy with removal of adjacent parenchyma was done in other cases of post-infective cysts. All cases in the chronic abscess group showed abscess formation with cavitation; two cases of these had associated bronchiectasis. Left lung and upper lobe involvement was common. Tuberculous cavity was seen in two cases [Figure 2c, 2d]. Both cases involved the lower lobes of the right lung. The maximum size of the lobe was 7 × 6 × 0.5 cm. Histology showed dilated bronchioles surrounded by caseating granulomas, lymphocytes, and Langhans giant cells. Special stains were not contributory. The limitation of the study was the inability to correlate microbiology findings.

Discussion

Causes of cystic lung lesions in the newborn mainly include congenital cystic BPMs and rare cystic neoplasms, whereas the differential diagnosis broadens in infants and children and includes infectious lesions, cystic changes associated with collagen or connective tissue abnormalities, vasculitic lesions, and tumors.[5] In adults, the common causes are infections and malignancies.[4] In our study, congenital lesions were more common as compared to acquired lesions. This contrasts a few studies in the literature.[6] As expected, most congenital lesions were found in the neonates, and early childhood with none found in late childhood, while it was the reverse in the acquired lesions.

The most common congenital cystic lesion is CPAM (incidence = 1 in 25000–30000 births), and the most common subtype is Type 1.[1] A similar finding was seen in our study. Bronchogenic cysts form from islands of bronchogenic cells left along an “abortive” tract arising from the foregut which takes years to develop into a cyst. Thus, they are common in older children and adults and rare in infants, which is in congruence with our study. Except the single infected case, all cases had typical histology. CLE typically presents with dyspnea and manifests in infancy as also seen in our study with rare asymptomatic cases presenting in adulthood.[17] Grossly, it has the appearance of an overinflated lung, and microscopically, the lung parenchyma shows overinflation of alveoli. All cases of bronchogenic cysts and CLE had a good correlation with radiology. Pulmonary sequestration has a separate systemic blood supply with extra lobar (25%) and intralobar (75%) subtypes. Intralobar sequestration can present as a cystic lesion following recurrent infections.[8] We did not find any case of pulmonary sequestration in our study. Other rare causes of congenital cystic lesions include aneurysmal dilatation of small pulmonary arteries.[4] It is well known that antenatal cystic lesions can present later with respiratory complications, malignancy, or sudden infant death. The cystic form of pleuropulmonary blastoma may be indistinguishable from CPAM on radiology and gross examination. The key histologic feature is immature mesenchyme or islands of cartilage in the wall of the cysts. Retrospectively examined cases of CPAM have shown foci of bronchioloalveolar carcinoma. This emphasizes the need for adequate sampling of all cystic lesions. The consensus or recommendation about which cysts need prompt removal is not very clear, but a few studies emphasize the need for early surgical resection in congenital cystic lesions.[910]

Pulmonary hydatid cysts in children (10–20% cases of hydatid cyst) are generally asymptomatic. Secondary infection can lead to cough, fever, hemoptysis, and hypersensitivity reactions.[11] All cases in our study presented with cough, fever, and breathlessness, and a single case had hemoptysis. All cases underwent a cystectomy, though segmentectomy, lobectomy, or pneumonectomy may be performed in some cases. The classic gross appearance of ‘tender coconut’ was seen in all cases, and the histology was characteristic. Cavitary pulmonary lesions occur due to infective diseases, inflammatory/immunologic diseases, and neoplasms and are uncommon in the absence of an associated underlying pulmonary disease.[12] They follow necrosis which can be suppurative, caseous, or ischemic in nature.[12] Cavities can be formed in complicated pneumonias (chronic abscess) following Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial infections like those with Streptococcus pneumoniae, Hemophilus influenzae, and so on. Histology shows inflammation with abscess formation. Cavitation in pulmonary tuberculosis (tuberculous cavity) is uncommon in children and is seen in the posterior aspect of upper lobes (post primary tuberculosis) or the upper lower lobe usually in adolescents. Rarely, these lesions may show extensive cavitation as seen in one of our cases. This could probably be because of poor immunity.[13] Various forms of Aspergillus fumigatus infections can present as cavitary lesions following colonization of an existing cavity.[12] Though rare in children, Pneumocystis jerovecii infections can develop cystic/cavitary lesions specially in the upper lobe.[7] Pulmonary teratoma is a rare site for extragonadal teratoma and should be included in the differential diagnosis of cystic/cavitary pediatric pulmonary lesions.[14]

Differentiation of infected cystic lesions from cavitary lesions can be confusing and difficult.[3] Radiological differentiation is decided by the thickness of the wall and the presence of air fluid levels.[15] Pathologically, a cyst has a thin translucent wall, clear contents, and clean inner aspect. The cavity, on the other hand, has thick irregular walls, purulent/thick contents, and adherent shaggy material. The adjacent lung parenchyma shows consolidation in postinfectious lesions. As a part of the repair process, the central cavitation gets relined by columnar epithelium. Such a lesion may erroneously be interpreted as an infected true cyst. Histologic differentiation may be difficult and requires extensive sampling.

Though radiology plays an important role in the diagnosis of cystic/cavitary lesions in children, histopathology is the key investigation for confirmation in overlapping cases. Very few studies were found in the literature which have dealt with clinico-pathologic aspects of pulmonary cystic/cavitary lesions.
