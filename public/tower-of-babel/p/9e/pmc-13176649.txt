A Qualitative Evaluation of Eat, Learn, Grow: A Digital Microlearning Programme to Promote Responsive Feeding Practices
Authors: Kimberley A. Baxter, Jeffrey T. H. So, Danielle Gallegos, Rebecca Byrne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResponsive feeding supports nurturing caregiving and encourages healthy eating in early childhood. Food insecurity can hinder families' ability to adopt positive practices and access support. This qualitative study evaluated participants' experiences and behavioural impacts of Eat, Learn, Grow, a co‐designed digital microlearning programme that promotes responsive feeding among families facing food insecurity. Nineteen participants completed semi‐structured interviews after completing the programme, which consisted of 12 brief digital modules and a hard‐copy resource pack. Data were analysed thematically guided by the COM‐B model of behaviour change. Participants found the intervention to be accessible and engaging. The brief, flexible modules aligned with their learning needs and preferences, and the multimedia format and social media‐style interface supported engagement. While the physical resources offered behavioural reinforcement, the digital component was described as more impactful. The programme enhanced participants' capability by increasing knowledge and confidence, reducing stress and mealtime tensions. It created opportunity through flexible learning and priming family discussions on child feeding. Motivation was driven by observable improvements in children's eating behaviours, reduced stress, and alignment with parenting values. Behavioural changes included more positive food experiences and shared family meals, pressuring children to eat less often, and increased repeated food exposure. Eat, Learn, Grow was well‐received and regarded as accessible, practical, and supportive. The design and delivery facilitated the adoption of responsive feeding within challenging family contexts of limited time, high mental load, and stress. Findings provide insight into how theory‐informed microlearning can promote responsive feeding among families facing food insecurity.

Introduction

Responsive caregiving is a fundamental component of the Nurturing Care Framework that supports optimal growth and development (WHO 2018). It involves recognising and responding to a child's cues, fostering learning, emotional regulation, and positive relationships (WHO 2018). Consistent, responsive caregiving is critical for healthy brain development and has been linked to positive cognitive and socioemotional outcomes (Black et al. 2017). Feeding babies and children provides regular opportunities for responsive caregiving throughout the day. Parental feeding practices are the behaviours and strategies that parents use to influence their child's food and eating behaviours, which are powerful determinants of children's long‐term health, food preferences, and eating habits (Daniels 2019; Russell et al. 2018). These practices can either support or undermine children's eating behaviours, diet quality, and overall well‐being. Responsive feeding is consistent with responsive caregiving and emphasises reciprocal and nurturing interactions, where parents provide an appropriate balance of structure and autonomy (Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2021). This supports children's innate ability to self‐regulate food intake and reduces mealtime stress. Conversely, non‐responsive practices, such as pressuring and restriction, are associated with feeding challenges and poor dietary outcomes. Promoting responsive feeding practices is a key component of early childhood nutrition and public health initiatives.

Environments that are pleasant, calm, and low in distraction are an important precursor of effective responsive parenting (Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2017). Feeding environments in the early years are shaped by various factors, including time pressures, competing demands, and the significant mental load of caregiving (Black and Aboud 2011). These challenges can be exacerbated for families facing disadvantage, such as those experiencing financial hardship and food insecurity. In these contexts, access to sufficient, safe, nutritious, and culturally appropriate food becomes more difficult, which can limit parents' opportunities to adopt responsive feeding strategies (Nambiar et al. 2024; So et al. 2024a). Families facing food insecurity may also experience higher household chaos and toxic stress, characterised by home environments with increased noise, disorganisation, and dysfunction (Fiese et al. 2016). These household factors can impact parents' ability to establish family routines and enact responsive parenting, including through child feeding (Fiese et al. 2016; Knowles et al. 2016). Additionally, families, whether they experience food insecurity or not, may face barriers engaging with health services and parenting programmes, such as a lack of time, capacity, transport, or cultural misalignment.

Despite the imperative to promote responsive feeding, existing interventions have demonstrated variable success, and there remains limited consensus regarding which specific intervention components, delivery modes and theoretical approaches are most effective in supporting responsive feeding under conditions of financial and food insecurity (Arlinghaus and Laska 2021; Baxter et al. 2022). The Responsive Feeding in Tough Times (R‐FiTT) research project was initiated to address this gap. A key output of R‐FiTT was the development of an intervention to promote responsive feeding practices using a design thinking and co‐design approach. The intervention was named Eat Learn Grow, as parents didn't like ‘Tough Times’, which emphasised hardship, and wanted a more empowering, positive name for the programme (Baxter et al. 2024). This intervention utilised a digital microlearning format and was informed by responsive feeding (Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2021) and cognitive load theories (Sweller 1994). Cognitive load theory suggests that learning is most effective when information is presented in a way that avoids overloading an individual's working memory; microlearning is an educational strategy that breaks down information into smaller, simpler parts to build understanding of a complex topic over time. Utilising cognitive load theory can optimise engagement and efficacy among populations with reduced working memory due to the impacts of stress, hardship, and high mental load (Baxter et al. 2025). Eat, Learn, Grow was evaluated using a randomised controlled trial (RCT) design, which demonstrated significant improvements in feeding practices at a 6‐month follow‐up (Baxter et al. 2026). Linear mixed model analyses showed that parents who received the intervention reported significantly lower levels of using of food to calm and persuasive feeding practices, along with a reduction in household chaos compared to the control group (Baxter et al. 2026). Qualitative evaluation is a crucial component in assessing the effectiveness of RCTs, offering insights into the feasibility and acceptability of interventions within the target population that quantitative data alone cannot provide (Lewin et al. 2009). This study conducted a qualitative evaluation of the Eat, Learn, Grow programme to understand participant experiences with the programme and examine key drivers of behaviour change to complement the quantitative findings.

Methods

Study Design

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted as an evaluation component of a randomised controlled trial of the Eat, Learn, Grow intervention. This trial was prospectively registered with the Australia New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry on 8/05/2023 [ACTRN12623000513617]. A qualitative approach was selected for this study to gain in‐depth insights into how participants experienced the programme and how it influenced behaviour, as well as identify key features of the intervention that resonated with participants. The reporting of this research has been guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Tong et al. 2007) (see Supporting Information: S1). The research team comprised four academics—three cisgender women and one cisgender man—with teaching and research backgrounds in dietetics, child nutrition and feeding. Three authors are parents of children ranging in age from early childhood to adulthood. All authors have prior experience in qualitative research.

The Intervention

Eat, Learn, Grow was created using a web application (7taps OpCo LLC 2026) and included twelve brief modules delivered over 6 weeks via SMS. Participants could click on the link in the SMS to complete the module, with no login or additional software downloads required. Modules featured plain language text, videos, audio stories, images, and interactive activities such as quizzes and polls. Details of the intervention design have been published previously (Baxter et al. 2024). Briefly, the intervention incorporated behaviour change strategies on topics such as responsive feeding cues, parental role modelling, family mealtimes, and food variety. Behavioural techniques included instructional content, behavioural modelling via videos and parent stories, self‐reflection, prompts for practice and action planning. Modules also featured environmental restructuring, practical strategies (such as structuring mealtimes, neutral food language, supporting sensory and repeated exposure), and reinforcement of key messages. The module links remained active throughout the study, allowing participants to revisit content. Additionally, a hard‐copy package of materials was mailed to participants to complement the digital component. The package included a booklet with key intervention messages, contact details, and QR codes linking to credible online information, an infant toothbrush, stickers, and a children's storybook about vegetables.

Participants and Recruitment

Participants were enroled in the Eat, Learn, Grow trial. The inclusion criteria for the RCT were parents or adult caregivers of a child aged 6–24 months who self‐identified as experiencing economic hardship by responding positively to the screening question, ‘Do you sometimes struggle to pay the bills?’. Participants for the present study were recruited by a purposive, opt‐in sampling strategy from the intervention group. Potential participants were invited to take part in an interview by expressing interest in response to a question in the post‐intervention survey. Participants were followed up via email and telephone; those who confirmed their continued interest were sent a link to the online participant information statement and consent form. On completion, participants were contacted to arrange an interview. No minimum engagement with the intervention was required to participate in the interview. This sampling approach aligned with the aim of this qualitative study, which was to explore in depth the experiences and perceptions of participants with some exposure to the intervention to meaningfully reflect on its acceptability and perceived behavioural impact. Of the 74 participants who received the intervention, 67 completed the post‐intervention survey, and 36 expressed interest in completing an interview. From this group, 20 consented, and 19 completed an interview. This sample size was deemed sufficient to provide insight into the topics of interest and adequate data to fulfil the study's aims. The individual who consented but did not complete the interview cited a lack of time as the reason. Participants were given a 20 AUD e‐gift voucher for their involvement.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted by telephone. Telephone interviews were selected based on the authors' prior qualitative research with the same population, in which participants preferred telephone interviews over video conferencing because they offered greater flexibility to participate while managing daily responsibilities (Baxter et al. 2024). Videoconferencing interviews would have been accommodated if requested. In‐person interviews were not feasible given the participants dispersed geographic locations. The interviewer was the first author, who led the development of the Eat, Learn, Grow programme. The nature of the interviewer's role in the programme design was not disclosed to the interviewees. This was a pragmatic decision based on resource constraints, as no additional personnel or funding were available to support an independent interviewer. Given the digital, remote delivery of the Eat Learn Grow programme, participant contact with the research team was minimal.

Demographics and food insecurity status were obtained from the baseline survey using REDCap (Harris et al. 2009, 2019), which was completed on enrolment into the RCT. Participants completed the 18‐item United States Department of Agriculture Household Food Security Survey Module (USDA‐HFSSM) (Bickel et al. 2000). Responses were coded according to the USDA coding scheme and then summed to provide a continuous food‐security scale to measure the severity of household food insecurity when a child is present.

The interview guide is outlined in Table 1. The average interview length was 36 min (range 22–47). At the end of the interview, participants were invited to provide additional feedback on the topics discussed via email or phone. One participant provided additional comments via text message, which were added to the interview transcript for analysis. Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim using an automated speech recognition service (Amazon Web Services Inc. 2026). The generated transcriptions were anonymised and corrected against the audio recording by a co‐author (JS). Finalised transcripts were not provided to participants for review before analysis.

Theoretical Framework: The COM‐B Model

The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation‐Behaviour (COM‐B) Model is central to the Behaviour Change Wheel and is an established framework used to guide the design and evaluation of behavioural interventions (Michie et al. 2011). The COM‐B Model provides a framework for understanding human behaviour by examining the contributions and interplay of an individual's capability (physical and psychological), opportunity (social and environmental), and motivation (reflective and automatic) in producing specific behaviours (the targeted behaviour/s). The COM‐B Model provided a structured approach for understanding the behavioural determinants targeted by the Eat, Learn, Grow intervention, which was co‐designed with end‐users. The COM‐B model was used to inform the interview questions and the deductive analysis. Psychological and physical capability were considered in relation to parental knowledge and skills related to responsive feeding. Opportunity was conceptualised to encompass both physical access to programme content and social influences that could facilitate or hinder engagement and behaviour change. Motivation included reflective and automatic processes that may drive or sustain feeding behaviours.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using the Framework method of thematic analysis. Framework analysis employs a robust and interconnected approach to data analysis, while thematic analysis seeks to identify patterns across a dataset to provide a descriptive or explanatory interpretation (Gale et al. 2013). This method facilitated a structured process for identifying and interpreting key categories, codes, and sub‐codes across the dataset, while allowing for both inductive insights from the data and deductive coding informed by the COM‐B Model.

The analysis team (KAB, RB and JS) first familiarised themselves with the transcripts. Three transcripts were independently double coded by two research team members. An initial coding framework was created through open coding of the subset of transcripts using the comment function in Microsoft Word, incorporating both deductive coding based on the constructs of the COM‐B domains (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation and Behaviour) and inductive coding (data‐driven). Following this initial round of coding, the analysis team met to compare coding decisions and discuss areas of difference. Differences were resolved through discussion and consensus, with codes refined, merged or refined as needed.

To further refine the coding framework and ensure consistent application, three additional transcripts were double‐coded. The analysis team met again to review coding alignment, resolve discrepancies through discussion and develop a final structure of codes and sub‐codes with descriptions. This iterative process yielded a stable, agreed‐upon analytical framework. The coding framework was recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and developed iteratively through the outlined process. The remaining transcripts were divided among the analysis team and coded independently using the comment function in Microsoft Word. Another analysis team meeting was held to review the coded data, confirm the suitability of the coding framework, confirm no additional codes were required, and discuss initial patterns and findings.

Coded data were then charted into a matrix (in Microsoft Excel), organised by cases (rows) and codes and sub‐codes (columns), allowing comparisons within and across participants. This matrix supported the identification of patterns, relationships between programme components and behavioural outcomes and illustrative quotes. Themes were subsequently developed and mapped to the COM‐B components to enhance understanding of the factors influencing participants' experiences and the intervention's impact on behavioural change.

Results

Participant Experiences of Eat, Learn, Grow

Participants reported that the Eat, Learn, Grow format aligned with the demands of parenting in the early years. The use of microlearning, with brief, flexible modules received on mobile phones, was seen as a key strength of the programme. Participants reported that they appreciated the brevity, accessibility, and relevance of the content, which supported engagement without becoming overwhelming.I really like how the modules were structured, very easy to digest it. I wasn't spending too much time reading small paragraphs… It's not overwhelming. It's very accessible, and it doesn't come out in a judgy way…I don't think it will put off people that are reading about this for the first time.(ID4)

The multimedia design, including audio, video, interactive components, and storytelling, was well received. Participants appreciated the variety of formats, which catered to different learning preferences and helped maintain interest. Videos were especially valued.It works well in terms of having the change [of media] because often, if you get too much information, it's just like an overload and you can quickly bore, I suppose. Or it can just become too hard or too complex to sit down and get it done. Whereas, like, you have that little bit of information you'd read through, and then the next slide would be a video.(ID16)

Capability: Building Knowledge and Confidence in Responsive Feeding

The programme enhanced participants' psychological capability by increasing their understanding of responsive feeding principles. Participants described establishing a home food environment more favourable to responsive feeding.It certainly helped, like relaxing with the idea of understanding that you don't need to stress about the food all the time. Another thing was the way that you might not realise how you're making a view of food… which I'd never thought about. That stood out for me.(ID5)

It certainly helped, like relaxing with the idea of understanding that you don't need to stress about the food all the time. Another thing was the way that you might not realise how you're making a view of food… which I'd never thought about. That stood out for me.

For some participants, the programme prompted them to acknowledge previous negative feeding experiences, such as challenges with feeding their older child, or unlearning non‐responsive practices from intergenerational or cultural norms.[There is] a lot of deconditioning that a lot of us have to do from our parents' generation, with the information that they were given. So, I found it quite refreshing to be presented with information that was coming from a caring and nurturing point of view, not forcing the kids to eat.(ID12)

[There is] a lot of deconditioning that a lot of us have to do from our parents' generation, with the information that they were given. So, I found it quite refreshing to be presented with information that was coming from a caring and nurturing point of view, not forcing the kids to eat.

Refinements and Future Directions

Participants responded positively to the delivery style and structure of Eat, Learn, Grow, valuing its brevity and accessibility. While some expressed interest in additional content, many emphasised that the concise format with a focus on feeding was a key strength. To maintain this appeal while enhancing relevance, participants recommended targeted microlearning courses tailored to specific developmental stages—for example, introducing solids (4–8 months), toddler feeding (14–24 months), and preschoolers (24+ months). Enhancing customisation was another key recommendation. For example, they suggested a flexible programme timeline (e.g., 4, 6 or 10 weeks), adjustable SMS delivery times, and co‐enrolment options for other caregivers. This reflects the diverse caregiving and feeding roles within families and preferences for learning schedules. Participants also suggested optional advanced content and ongoing access to a digital resource library of all the modules:It would be handy to have, like, an opt‐in for more information… rather than the whole programme being extended.(ID15)

It would be handy to have, like, an opt‐in for more information… rather than the whole programme being extended.

Ensuring the content reflects diverse family structures was seen as important. One participant noted:I'm a single parent… I felt that it was more catered towards complete families.(ID4)

I'm a single parent… I felt that it was more catered towards complete families.

Participants proposed additional interaction opportunities—such as check‐ins or preloaded discussions—to increase engagement. Overall, participants found the programme highly valuable and suggested that greater flexibility, inclusivity, and optional depth could enhance its reach and impact.

Discussion

This qualitative evaluation described participants' experiences with a digital microlearning programme, Eat, Learn, Grow, and employed the COM‐B Model to examine the programme's behavioural impact. Eat, Learn, Grow was co‐designed with parents using a mixed‐methods approach and was designed to promote responsive feeding among families experiencing food insecurity (Baxter et al. 2024). The RCT evaluation of the intervention indicated that the programme effectively improved responsive feeding behaviours and reduced household chaos, with impacts maintained at the 6‐month follow‐up measure (Baxter et al. 2026). This qualitative study offers insights into the factors contributing to this effectiveness, with participants reporting that the programme was accessible, engaging, and aligned with the needs of parenting in resource‐constrained settings. It broadens understanding of key features of digital responsive feeding interventions for food‐insecure families, such as using a familiar mobile format and a microlearning approach, which allowed flexible engagement with the content. The brief content and the inclusion of parents' stories and quotes support social learning and help to normalise food insecurity and feeding challenges. The design and delivery supported the adoption of responsive feeding in challenging family environments characterised by limited time, high mental load, and stress.

The COM‐B Model has previously been employed in qualitative studies to evaluate health interventions or explore barriers and facilitators (Blebil et al. 2022; Litterbach et al. 2017; So et al. 2024b) and was beneficial in this study for exploring the mechanisms of behavioural change from the end‐user perspective. Mapping themes to the COM‐B domains demonstrated how the intervention supported behaviour change by enhancing capability, increasing social and physical opportunities, and reinforcing motivations. Participants described implementing responsive feeding strategies across diverse family contexts, highlighting how the intervention increased physical and social opportunities to enact the targeted behaviours. Improvements in knowledge and confidence regarding responsive feeding after engaging with the programme, align with a 2020 systematic review of digital nutrition interventions targeting parents, which identified knowledge and self‐efficacy as key mechanisms underpinning improved nutritional outcomes (Zarnowiecki et al. 2020). Participants described recognising how their feeding practices were shaped by cultural or intergenerational norms, illustrating how the intervention supported reflective motivation and reduced automatic or habitual behaviours. This finding is consistent with prior research highlighting the importance of addressing social norms to influence effectively caregiver feeding behaviours (Russell et al. 2016). The application of the COM‐B Model strengthened the interpretation of how participants interacted with the digital intervention.

Eat, Learn, Grow supported the translation of knowledge into action. Participants were able to embed responsive feeding practices into daily life by restructuring mealtimes and adjusting routines. Asynchronous engagement with the content was a key facilitator, critical for families experiencing overwhelm, unpredictability or chaos because it enabled access in ‘between moments' of time, such as commuting on public transport and while settling young children to sleep. Improvements in mealtime experience, reduced stress, and increased child agency reinforced participants' intrinsic motivation to continue with these new practices. Enhanced confidence and reported alignment with parenting beliefs and feeding goals‐ further reinforced commitment to responsive feeding through a sense of value congruence, which is recognised as an important factor in behaviour change (Ryan and Deci 2000). Eat, Learn, Grow also leveraged social opportunity by prompting discussions about responsive feeding with partners, extended family and friends. Narrative‐driven health interventions have been shown to be effective, particularly in reinforcing social norms and parents' self‐efficacy (Hinyard and Kreuter 2007). The inclusion of real‐life parent stories, alongside expert context, enhanced relatability and trust, resulting in shared understanding and collective behaviour change within households. The microlearning format, consisting of structured chunks of content, enabled participants to build knowledge progressively, an approach shown to be effective in managing cognitive load and enhancing retention in digital learning contexts (Alias and Razak 2025). This appeared particularly important for parents managing multiple responsibilities and the high mental load of caregiving, consistent with evidence that brief and mobile‐optimised interventions can facilitate engagement among underserved populations (Baxter et al. 2025; Grodberg and Smith 2022). These findings echo prior work demonstrating the feasibility and acceptability of mobile health (mHealth) tools in promoting early nutrition behaviours in a range of settings (Laws et al. 2018).

The combination of digital and hard‐copy components, including the digital modules and mail‐out packs, enhanced engagement with the intervention content across different modalities. Although not used consistently by the cohort, some participants found these hard‐copy resources effective for reinforcement of key programme messages, especially for involving children in food‐related conversations. Further evaluation of the programme could consider the cost‐effectiveness of time taken to prepare and mail‐out the pack, the type and number of items included, and whether this is an essential part of the intervention design that contributes to behaviour change.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study was the use of theory to inform the interview guide and analysis, enabling a structured qualitative exploration of the behavioural mechanisms behind the programme, which complements the findings of the RCT. Direct measures classified food insecurity using the USDA HFSSM 18‐item tool, and a high proportion of the group (84.2%) fell within the target population of experiencing very low, low, or marginal food security. However, the findings should be assessed considering that the sample completed the post‐intervention survey, potentially introducing a positive bias towards more engaged participants. It stands to reason that participants who completed the final survey of the intervention and accepted an invitation to provide additional feedback in an interview may respond more positively. Considering the reasons participants choose not to participate is a separate issue worthy of investigation, but is outside the scope of this analysis. Additionally, sample characteristics, such as the high percentage of female participants (95%), may limit the applicability of the results to male caregivers. The high proportion of participants with a university education (74%) limits our ability to apply these findings to people with lower levels of education and/or digital literacy. Although the intervention content was designed using plain language principles, further consultation may be necessary to ensure it is accessible to parents with low literacy. Participants' suggestions, including flexible delivery schedules and increased inclusivity for diverse family structures, could inform future improvements to the programme. Additionally, the researcher who led the co‐design of the programme also conducted the interviews. This may have influenced participant responses through social desirability bias. This was mitigated by participants having minimal contact with the research team during the programme, given its remote delivery, and by the co‐authors' involvement in the analysis of the data.

Conclusion

This study provided insight into how theory‐informed digital microlearning can effectively support responsive feeding among families experiencing food insecurity. Eat, Learn, Grow was well received by participants, who perceived it as accessible, practical, and supportive. Its flexible design and delivery supported the adoption of responsive feeding practices, even in challenging circumstances characterised by limited time, high mental load, and family stress. Findings suggest that responsive feeding interventions for food‐insecure families should be co‐designed with parents, offered in flexible formats, and informed by theory that considers the emotional and structural challenges shaping feeding practices. Future research should explore accessibility for caregivers with varying literacy and digital capabilities and the inclusion of diverse family structures. The success of this digital format highlights its potential as a scalable, cost‐effective public health approach, enabling broad reach for those who may face barriers to attending in‐person programmes.
