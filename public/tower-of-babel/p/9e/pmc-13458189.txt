Analysis of CT and MRI imaging features of ectopic pancreas
Authors: Si-Miao Chen, Guang-Wen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEctopic pancreas (EP) is a rare congenital anomaly that is often difficult to diagnose preoperatively due to its nonspecific clinical manifestations and imaging overlap with other gastrointestinal subepithelial lesions. Several characteristic imaging findings have been reported; however, the quantitative imaging features of EP remain insufficiently investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the clinical and imaging manifestations of EP, summarize its computed tomography (CT) features, quantitatively compare CT attenuation values between EP and native pancreas across different imaging phases, and explore phase-specific quantitative differences that may provide additional imaging information for EP characterization. Available magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings were also analyzed descriptively for supplementary illustration.MethodsThe clinical and imaging data of 17 patients with surgically confirmed EP were retrospectively reviewed. All patients underwent non-contrast CT, 13 underwent triphasic contrast-enhanced CT, and two underwent non-contrast MRI. The imaging characteristics of benign-appearing lesions representing EP tissue were evaluated, and exploratory qualitative and quantitative comparisons were performed between the EP lesions and the native pancreatic tissue.ResultsEP was most frequently located in the duodenum (52.9%, 9/17) and stomach (35.3%, 6/17), typically presenting as solitary ovoid lesions (52.9%, 9/17) with ill-defined margins (64.7%, 11/17) and a long diameter-to-short diameter ratio >1.4 in 64.7% (11/17) of cases. The following three imaging signs were observed in the 13 patients who underwent contrast-enhanced CT: the duct sign (5/13 cases, 38.5%), the umbilical sign (1/13 cases, 7.7%), and the perilesional fat line sign (1/13 cases, 7.7%). On the non-contrast CT and arterial-phase images, EP showed lower attenuation than the native pancreas [non-contrast: 45.18±2.91 vs. 48.59±2.90 Hounsfield units (HU), corrected P=0.002; arterial phase: 76.38±15.12 vs. 95.46±7.34 HU, corrected P=0.002]; however, no statistically significant differences were observed during the venous or delayed phases. In the exploratory analysis, arterial-phase CT attenuation showed the largest attenuation difference and the highest area under the curve (AUC) among the evaluated phases in this cohort.ConclusionsEP demonstrates characteristic morphological features and phase-specific quantitative CT attenuation patterns. In this small cohort, arterial-phase CT attenuation showed the greatest quantitative difference relative to the native pancreas among the evaluated phases and may provide supportive information for EP identification when interpreted alongside specific CT signs. These results should be interpreted with caution.Keywords: Ectopic pancreas (EP), X-ray computed tomography (X-ray CT), differential diagnosis

Introduction

Ectopic pancreas (EP) (also referred to as aberrant pancreas) was first described by Jean Schultz in 1727 in the ileal diverticulum of a neonate (1). It is defined as pancreatic tissue located outside the normal anatomical position of the pancreas and lacking anatomical, vascular, or ductal continuity with the orthotopic pancreatic gland (2). It should be clearly distinguished from the accessory pancreatic lobe, which maintains anatomical and/or ductal continuity with the main pancreas (3). EP is considered a congenital anomaly resulting from the abnormal migration of pancreatic tissue during embryonic development. EP has a reported incidence of 0.55–13.7% in autopsy series and approximately 0.2% in upper abdominal surgical procedures (4). However, due to its nonspecific clinical presentation, EP is rarely suspected preoperatively and is often detected incidentally in routine practice.

EP may demonstrate certain characteristic imaging features; however, its diagnosis is often challenging due to variable anatomical locations, nonspecific or absent clinical symptoms, and considerable overlap in imaging findings with other subepithelial or mesenchymal tumors, such as gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), leiomyomas, functional neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), and schwannomas (5). Therefore, this study sought to systematically and quantitatively evaluate the computed tomography (CT) imaging characteristics of pathologically confirmed EP. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to explore phase-specific differences in attenuation relative to the native pancreas. In addition, the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of EP were descriptively analyzed in a subset of cases to provide additional imaging insights into the characteristics of EP and to aid in its detection in clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0067/rc).

Methods

Study population

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at Sichuan University Affiliated Chengdu Second People’s Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pathologically confirmed EP diagnosed between August 2023 and August 2025; (II) CT and/or MRI performed prior to surgical or endoscopic intervention; and (III) availability of complete clinical and imaging data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) incomplete or poor-quality imaging data precluding reliable analysis; and/or (II) unclear or unconfirmed pathological diagnosis.

Initial screening identified 22 patients with pathologically suspected EP. Of these, two were excluded due to inadequate image quality precluding reliable analysis, and three were excluded due to unconfirmed pathological diagnoses. Ultimately, 17 patients were included in the final analysis. All patients underwent CT examinations, while MRI data were available in only a limited subset of cases. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan University Affiliated Chengdu Second People’s Hospital (No. [KY]PJ2026280). Informed consent was waived for this retrospective study.

Clinical data

Of the 17 enrolled patients, 13 were male and 4 were female. The age of the patients ranged from 33 to 80 years, with a median age of 54 years and a mean age of 54.59±13.78 years. Regarding clinical presentation, EP was incidentally detected during routine abdominal imaging examinations performed for unrelated conditions in seven patients, who had no specific gastrointestinal symptoms. Ten patients presented with chronic gastrointestinal symptoms, including abdominal pain and abdominal distension, with a maximum symptom duration of 30 years. In addition, three patients were admitted with acute abdominal pain and were postoperatively confirmed to have EP complicated by ectopic pancreatitis.

Imaging protocol

For the CT examinations, a CT scanner (Revolution CT, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) was used to perform non-contrast-enhanced scans of the upper or entire abdomen. In addition, triphasic contrast-enhanced CT, including arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases, was performed in 13 of the 17 patients using a fixed-delay protocol following contrast injection. All patients were scanned in the supine position. The CT acquisition parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 220 mA; slice thickness, 1.25 mm; slice interval, 1.25 mm; and display field of view, 40 cm × 40 cm. A nonionic iodinated contrast medium with an iodine concentration of 350 mgI/mL (ioversol; Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Lianyungang, China) was administered via the right antecubital vein at a dose of 1.5 mL/kg body weight and an injection rate of 2.5 mL/s. Contrast-enhanced images were obtained during the arterial phase (25 s after contrast injection), portal venous phase (40–65 s after injection), and delayed phase (3 min after injection).

A total of two patients underwent MRI examinations, all of which were non-contrast-enhanced. MRI was performed using a 1.5-T scanner (Avanto; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The imaging protocol included T1-weighted imaging (T1WI, with and without fat suppression), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI, with and without fat suppression), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with b values of 50, 400, and 800 s/mm2. The acquisition parameters were as follows: slice thickness, 6 mm; interslice gap, 1 mm; repetition time/echo time, 150/2.3 ms for T1WI and T1-weighted imaging with fat suppression (T1WI-FS), 1,400/90 ms for T2WI and T2-weighted imaging with fat suppression (T2WI-FS), and 4,500/65 ms for DWI.

Image analysis

The CT and MRI images were independently reviewed by two senior radiologists with experience in abdominal imaging, who were blinded to the pathological results and clinical information of the patients. In cases of disagreement, a third radiologist performed a consensus review to resolve discrepancies.

Quantitative measurements

CT attenuation measurements were obtained by region of interest (ROI) analysis. The ROIs were carefully placed to avoid the pancreatic ducts, blood vessels, necrotic areas, calcifications, and adjacent fat or bowel gas, and, in cases complicated by pancreatitis, obvious non-enhancing necrotic areas. For the benign-appearing EP lesions, circular ROIs with an area of approximately 10–15 mm2 were placed within the solid portion of the lesion on the slice showing the maximum cross-sectional area, and the measurements were repeated three times, with the mean value recorded. For the orthotopic pancreatic tissue, circular ROIs with an area of approximately 15–20 mm2 were placed in the pancreatic head, body, and tail at their respective maximal cross-sectional levels, and the average of these three measurements was calculated as the representative CT attenuation value.

The following imaging features were evaluated: (I) basic lesion characteristics, including lesion location, size (long diameter and short diameter), shape (ovoid, round, flat, or irregular), lesion margin, growth pattern, and the relationship between the lesion long axis and the orientation of the gastrointestinal wall; (II) imaging features, including lesion density or signal intensity, differences in density or signal intensity compared with the orthotopic pancreas, and the presence or absence of specific imaging signs, such as the “umbilical sign”, “duct sign”, and “perilesional fat line sign”; and (III) associated complications, including the presence or absence of ectopic pancreatitis, intussusception, intestinal perforation, and other related findings.

The long diameter and short diameter were measured on the plane (axial or multiplanar reconstruction) that best demonstrated the maximal lesion extent. The long diameter was defined as the maximum dimension of the lesion, and the short diameter as the maximum perpendicular diameter on the same plane.

The operational definitions of imaging descriptors and CT signs were as follows: (I) the duct sign was defined as a thin tubular or linear hypoattenuating structure within the lesion on contrast-enhanced CT, consistent with a duct-like morphology. Hypoattenuating areas that were irregular shape, had indistinct margins, or lacked a continuous tubular configuration were not considered positive for the duct sign. (II) The umbilical sign was defined as a central or near-central umbilicus-like indentation of the lesion surface protruding toward the gastrointestinal lumen, appearing as a focal inward depression continuous with the lesion on cross-sectional imaging. Superficial mucosal irregularities unrelated to the lesion were excluded. (III) The perilesional fat line sign was defined as a distinct linear or strand-like area of fat attenuation completely or partially outlining the lesion, with a clearly discernible interface between the lesion and adjacent normal fat. Nonspecific inflammatory fat stranding was excluded based on its distribution and lack of a well-defined linear configuration. (IV) Lesion margins were categorized as ill-defined when the interface between the lesion and adjacent tissue was indistinct or blurred, and as well-defined when a sharp, clearly demarcated boundary was present. (V) Growth patterns were categorized as intraluminal, extraluminal, or mixed intra-extraluminal based on the predominant direction of lesion extension relative to the gastrointestinal wall.

Pathological confirmation

All 17 cases were pathologically confirmed using surgical resection specimens. The diagnostic criteria for EP were defined as the presence of pancreatic tissue, including acini, ducts, and/or islet cells, located outside the normal anatomical position of the pancreas, without anatomical, vascular, or neural continuity with the native pancreas (2).

Statistical analysis

No data imputation was required, as all enrolled patients had complete clinical and imaging datasets for the primary analyses. The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 and Python version 3.10.9. The normality of the continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range). Differences in the CT attenuation values between EP and native pancreas across non-contrast and contrast-enhanced phases (arterial, venous, and delayed) were analyzed using paired t-tests; for non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. To account for multiple comparisons across phases, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with FDR controlled at 0.05.

A Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the linear association and enhancement-pattern similarity between the EP and native pancreas attenuation values, complementing paired comparisons of absolute attenuation differences. Additionally, interobserver agreement was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for quantitative CT attenuation measurements and Cohen’s kappa statistics for key categorical imaging features. Cohen’s kappa analysis was specifically applied to the duct sign, umbilical sign, and perilesional fat line sign, as these represent the most characteristic and observer-dependent CT features of EP.

Two subgroups were analyzed: (I) the overall cohort, comprising all 17 patients with confirmed EP, regardless of the presence of acute ectopic pancreatitis; and (II) the non-pancreatitis subgroup, excluding three patients with ectopic pancreatitis (n=14).

A ROC analysis was performed to explore the phase-specific discriminatory trends of CT attenuation for differentiating between EP and native pancreas. Given the small sample size, the ROC analysis was exploratory in nature, and the results should be interpreted with caution. To improve the robustness of the estimates, bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions was applied to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the area under the curve (AUC). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Interobserver agreement for the categorical imaging features (“duct sign”, “umbilical sign”, and “perilesional fat line sign”) was substantial (Cohen’s kappa =0.85–1.00), while agreement for the quantitative CT attenuation measurements across the four phases (arterial, venous, delayed, and non-contrast) was excellent (ICC =0.81–0.98).

Results

Lesion characteristics

The lesion characteristics are summarized as follows: (I) lesion location; (II) lesion size; (III) lesion shape; (IV) lesion boundary; (V) lesion growth pattern; (VI) relationship between the lesion long axis and the gastrointestinal wall; and (VII) imaging features.

Lesion location

All 17 ectopic pancreatic lesions included in this study were solitary. Six lesions (35.3%) were located in the stomach, all in the gastric antrum (four in the posterior wall and two in the anterior wall). Nine lesions (52.9%) were located in the duodenum, including five in the duodenal bulb and four in the descending duodenum. One lesion (5.9%) was located in the proximal jejunum, and one lesion (5.9%) was located in the ileal mesentery.

Lesion size

The long diameter of the lesions ranged from 1.2 to 3.5 cm, with a mean of 2.54±0.71 cm. The short diameter ranged from 0.7 to 2.3 cm, with a mean of 1.58±0.59 cm. The long diameter-to-short diameter ratio was 1.70±0.67, and 11 lesions had a ratio >1.4.

Lesion shape

Among the gastric lesions, three were ovoid, two were flat, and one was irregular. Among the duodenal lesions, four were ovoid, two were flat, two were irregular, and one was round. The lesions located in the jejunum and ileal mesentery were both ovoid. Overall, 9 lesions (52.9%) were ovoid, 4 (23.5%) were flat, 3 (17.6%) were irregular, and 1 (5.9%) was round.

Lesion boundary

Eleven lesions (64.7%) had ill-defined or slightly blurred boundaries, whereas 6 lesions (35.3%) were well circumscribed.

Lesion growth pattern

Excluding the lesion located in the ileal mesentery, 7 lesions (43.8%) showed mixed growth, 4 (25.0%) showed extraluminal growth, and 5 (31.3%) showed intraluminal growth.

Relationship between lesion long axis and gastrointestinal wall

Excluding the lesion located in the ileal mesentery, the long axis of 15 lesions (93.8%) was parallel to the direction of the gastrointestinal wall, whereas 1 lesion (6.3%) in the descending duodenum showed a non-parallel orientation.

Imaging features

Among the 13 patients who underwent contrast-enhanced CT, a duct-like hypoattenuating structure (“duct sign”) was observed in 5 lesions (38.5%). One lesion (7.7%) also exhibited a focal mucosal depression consistent with the “umbilical sign”, and one lesion (7.7%) exhibited a perilesional fat line sign (Figure 1).

Quantitative CT evaluation

The Shapiro-Wilk test results revealed that the CT attenuation values of both the EP and native pancreas across all imaging phases were normally distributed (all P>0.05). Paired t-test results (after correction for multiple comparisons) revealed that EP exhibited significantly lower attenuation than the native pancreas in the overall cohort [76.38±15.12 vs. 95.46±7.34 Hounsfield units (HU), P<0.001, corrected P=0.0020], with a large effect size (Cohen’s d=1.283). A similar and robust difference was observed in the subgroup excluding patients with ectopic pancreatitis (79.50±16.03 vs. 97.10±7.26 HU, P=0.009; Cohen’s d=1.050), which remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons (corrected P=0.0018). In non-contrast CT, EP showed slightly lower attenuation (45.18±2.91 HU) than the native pancreas (48.59±2.90 HU), and the difference was statistically significant in the overall cohort (P<0.001, Cohen’s d=0.972). A similar difference was observed in the non-pancreatitis subgroup (45.46±2.35 vs. 48.11±2.82 HU, P=0.004), which remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons (corrected P=0.016). Conversely, during the venous phase, although EP showed slightly lower attenuation (89.58±15.81 HU) compared to the native pancreas (91.73±11.08 HU) in the overall cohort, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.418, Cohen’s d=0.233). No significant attenuation differences between EP and the native pancreas were detected during the venous or delayed phases in either the overall cohort or in the non-pancreatitis subgroup (all P>0.05) (Table 1).

The Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong and significant correlation in CT attenuation values between EP and native pancreas during the venous phase. This association was observed both in the overall cohort (r=0.8197, P=0.0006) and in the non-pancreatitis subgroup (r=0.8112, P=0.0044).

A ROC analysis was conducted to explore the discriminative value of CT attenuation. The results revealed that arterial-phase CT attenuation had the highest discriminative ability among the evaluated phases, with an AUC of 0.858 (Bootstrap 95% CI: 0.654–1.0). A potential cutoff value of 94.0 HU was identified, yielding a sensitivity of 69.2% and a specificity of 92.3%. A similar trend was observed in the non-pancreatitis subgroup (AUC =0.850), with a slight increase in sensitivity from 69.2% to 80.0%. Non-contrast-enhanced CT showed moderate discriminative ability (AUC =0.808), while the venous and delayed phases showed lower AUC values (0.586 and 0.325, respectively), suggesting limited discriminative value in these phases. These findings should be considered preliminary, and their clinical applicability and stability require further validation in larger-scale studies.

MRI findings and associated imaging signs

The lesions of the two patients who underwent non-contrast-enhanced MRI were located in the gastric antrum and the descending duodenum, respectively. The gastric antral lesion exhibited high signal intensity on T1WI, with even more pronounced high signal intensity on T1WI-FS. It appeared isointense on T2WI, showing signal characteristics similar to the native pancreas (Figure 2A,2B). On DWI, the lesion exhibited high signal intensity, with an apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value comparable to that of the native pancreatic tissue (Figure 2C,2D). The lesion located in the descending duodenum showed signal intensity on T1WI comparable to that of the native pancreas; however, due to its small size, the lesion was poorly visualized on T2WI.

Complications associated with EP

Among the 17 patients, 3 (17.6%) developed complications related to EP, all of which were cases of acute ectopic pancreatitis. The lesions were located in the gastric antrum, proximal jejunum, and ileal mesentery, and all patients presented with acute abdominal pain upon admission. In one patient with ectopic pancreatitis in the proximal jejunum, inflammation of the EP occurred simultaneously with acute pancreatitis in the native pancreas. In contrast, in the other two patients, the inflammation was confined to the EP, with no signs of inflammation in the native pancreas. CT scans revealed blurred perilesional fat planes with mild exudation in all three cases, consistent with imaging features of acute inflammation (Figure 3). Laboratory tests were performed in only one of the three patients, revealing significantly elevated serum amylase and lipase levels. The absence of biochemical data for the remaining two cases is an inherent limitation of this retrospective study.

Discussion

Overview

Consistent with previous reports (6), EP in this cohort was predominantly located in the stomach and duodenum. Although the duodenum was the most common site in our series, this finding differs from previous studies and may reflect the small sample size.

Histopathologic subtypes were not analyzed in this study due to the retrospective study design and limited availability of tissue samples. While histologic classification may offer additional insights, the present study primarily focused on imaging characteristics and their potential relevance. The demographic and clinical features in this cohort were broadly consistent with previous reports (7), with males accounting for 76.5% (13/17) of cases, and the predominant age group being 40–69 years (10/17, 58.8%). Most patients with EP are asymptomatic, and abdominal pain is the most common symptom among symptomatic individuals (8). In our cohort, gastrointestinal bleeding and symptoms related to gastric outlet obstruction were not observed, which may reflect the small sample size and lesion distribution.

EP typically presents as a solitary, ovoid soft-tissue mass with ill-defined boundaries, often with a long diameter of <3 cm and a long diameter-to-short diameter ratio >1.4, although this ratio should be regarded as a descriptive morphological parameter rather than a strict diagnostic criterion (9). In the present study, 11 cases (64.7%) exhibited ill-defined or slightly blurred boundaries, 11 had a long diameter of <3 cm, and 11 (64.7%) had a long diameter-to-short diameter ratio >1.4. These findings are consistent with the typical imaging characteristics described in previous studies.

The duct sign and umbilical sign are relatively specific manifestations of EP. In the present study, the duct sign was observed in five cases, one of which also showed the umbilical sign. The duct sign was more frequently detected, while the umbilical sign was uncommon, which may be related to obstruction or masking of the ductal orifice by gastrointestinal contents, inflammation, or other factors.

The perilesional fat line sign refers to a linear fat density at the lesion margin and is relatively rare. In the present study, this sign was observed in only one case. Previous studies have hypothesized that its formation may be associated with fat infiltration into ectopic pancreatic tissue. However, this sign lacks specificity for EP and can only provide supplementary diagnostic information after excluding other fat-containing lesions such as lipomas and hamartomas (10). Notably, no internationally accepted quantitative criteria currently exist for these imaging signs, which are still largely evaluated qualitatively. Further investigations are warranted to establish standardized and quantitative diagnostic criteria.

Ectopic pancreatitis remains difficult to diagnose due to the lack of standardized criteria. It may also cause slight increases in serum amylase and lipase levels; thus, in clinical practice, the diagnosis is usually based on the criteria for acute pancreatitis together with lesion location and imaging findings (11).

In cases of ectopic pancreatitis, inflammatory changes such as perilesional fat stranding or localized exudation are typically observed around the ectopic tissue, while the native pancreas generally appears normal in size with preserved surrounding fat planes. Conversely, in acute pancreatitis involving the native pancreas with incidental EP, the native pancreas may show enlargement and peripancreatic exudation, whereas EP often lacks these inflammatory features. These imaging distinctions, combined with clinical symptoms and laboratory findings, can aid in differentiating between the two scenarios, although overlap may occur in rare cases.

Among the three patients with concurrent ectopic pancreatitis in this study, CT showed blurred perilesional fat spaces and effusion in all cases. Notably, one case of EP developed inflammation concurrently with the native pancreas, presumably due to the spread of inflammation from the native pancreas. In this patient, laboratory testing demonstrated markedly elevated serum amylase and lipase levels. However, pancreas‑specific laboratory tests were not performed in the other two patients, limiting the biochemical correlation in this cohort. Consequently, the diagnosis of ectopic pancreatitis herein relied primarily on integrated clinical and imaging criteria.

MRI findings were only available in two cases and are presented for illustrative purposes only. In these cases, EP showed T1WI hyperintensity similar to orthotopic pancreatic parenchyma. On T2WI, one gastric antral lesion was isointense relative to the native pancreas, whereas the duodenal lesion was poorly visualized. On DWI, the gastric antral lesion exhibited hyperintensity with an ADC value comparable to that of the native pancreas. This discrepancy may be explained by a T2 shine-through effect rather than true diffusion restriction, possibly related to the intrinsic tissue characteristics of ectopic pancreatic parenchyma.

Preliminary CT quantitative assessment

On non-contrast-enhanced CT, EP usually appears as a homogeneous soft-tissue lesion. Focal hypodense areas in the lesion may occur when the lesion is duct-dominant or complicated by secondary inflammation (9). To further investigate the potential effect of pancreatitis on the imaging characteristics, the patients were divided into two groups: one comprising all 17 patients with confirmed EP and another excluding the three patients with concurrent ectopic pancreatitis.

The CT attenuation values exhibited phase-specific differences, suggesting that these features may provide additional information for lesion characterization. In this cohort, EP demonstrated significantly lower arterial-phase attenuation than the native pancreas, which remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons, suggesting that there may be intrinsic enhancement differences rather than purely inflammatory effects. Although histopathologic subclassification was not available in this cohort, the observed attenuation differences may be related to variations in tissue composition, such as differences in acinar and ductal components, as well as the presence of inflammation and fibrosis, which may affect CT attenuation characteristics.

In the non-contrast CT phase, EP showed a modest but statistically significant lower attenuation than the native pancreas in the overall cohort (45.18±2.91 vs. 48.59±2.90 HU), with a mean difference of −3.41 HU. A similar pattern was observed in the non-pancreatitis subgroup. However, given the small absolute HU difference, this finding should be regarded as a supplementary quantitative observation rather than a primary discriminative feature. Conversely, the venous and delayed phases demonstrated limited diagnostic value, with no statistically significant attenuation differences observed.

A ROC analysis was conducted to explore phase-specific differences in CT attenuation between EP and native pancreatic tissue. The arterial phase exhibited the highest AUC (0.858, 95% CI: 0.654–1.0). A cutoff value of 94.0 HU was established for this cohort; however, this observation is preliminary and should not be considered a definitive diagnostic threshold. A similar pattern was observed in the non-pancreatitis subgroup (AUC =0.850), with improved sensitivity after excluding cases complicated by ectopic pancreatitis. In contrast, the venous and delayed phases exhibited lower AUC values (0.586 and 0.325, respectively), indicating less apparent separation between the EP and native pancreas in this dataset. These findings suggest the potential relevance of arterial-phase attenuation as a quantitative imaging feature, although the proposed cutoff should be interpreted cautiously.

Previous studies have suggested that the EP shows significant differences in CT attenuation values compared to the native pancreas in both the non-contrast and arterial phases, with no significant differences in the portal venous and delayed phases (12). The present study supports those findings, further supporting the potential role of the arterial phase as an informative phase for differentiation. Notably, our study provides a new perspective on the clinical application of these differences. Unlike previous studies, which reported the strongest correlation in the delayed phase (12), our Pearson correlation analysis showed the strongest concordance in the venous phase [overall cohort (r=0.8197, P=0.0006), non-pancreatitis subgroup (r=0.8112, P=0.0044)], implying similar enhancement behavior between EP and native pancreas at this phase. However, the ROC analysis revealed that the arterial phase yielded the highest discriminatory performance (AUC =0.858). This finding indicates that the quantitative attenuation differences in the arterial phase may provide additional information for lesion characterization compared with the enhancement similarity observed in later phases.

As EP originates from pancreatic tissue, its enhancement characteristics may share certain similarities with the native pancreas in terms of histologic composition and vascularity. When interpreted together with lesion morphology and anatomical location, these findings may provide supplementary information for the imaging recognition and characterization of EP. However, given the imaging overlap between EP and other gastrointestinal subepithelial lesions, comprehensive assessment integrating morphologic features, lesion location, and clinical context remains necessary in clinical practice.

Differential diagnosis

EP should be differentiated from other subepithelial lesions, particularly GISTs, leiomyomas, functional NETs, and schwannomas (4). In practice, differentiation relies mainly on a combination of lesion morphology, growth pattern, enhancement characteristics, and, when available, MRI signal features. Compared with EP, these lesions more often show well-defined margins, different growth patterns, or enhancement characteristics that are less similar to the native pancreas, although overlap may occur (9,13,14). The arterial-phase attenuation difference observed in this study may provide supportive information when EP is considered in the differential diagnosis. However, it should be emphasized that the proposed attenuation threshold was derived from comparisons with the native pancreas rather than direct comparisons with other subepithelial tumors. Therefore, it should not be applied as a standalone discriminator, and a comprehensive evaluation combining morphologic features and clinical context remains essential.

Limitations

The present study had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Larger, multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these results. Second, this was a retrospective study; thus, the available data were subject to inherent biases such as selection bias and missing data. In addition, a precise denominator population could not be defined, as EP is rarely suspected preoperatively and is often detected incidentally in different clinical settings. Third, the absence of data on histopathologic subclassification hindered the analysis of how enhancement patterns vary across different subtypes, thereby limiting a more comprehensive understanding of the imaging features.

Conclusions

The diagnosis of EP remains challenging. Quantitative CT analysis suggests that arterial-phase attenuation characteristics of EP may provide supplementary information for lesion characterization and may aid in the identification of EP when combined with morphologic imaging features and clinical context. Characteristic signs (including the duct sign, umbilical sign, and perilesional fat line sign) may provide additional clues. In cases of suspected ectopic pancreatitis, serum amylase and/or lipase may provide supportive information if available, although the laboratory data were limited in this study. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these preliminary findings and to further assess the potential role of quantitative CT parameters in the diagnosis of EP.
