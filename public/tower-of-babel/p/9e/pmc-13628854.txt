Deep anterior lamellar vs penetrating keratoplasty for macular and granular stromal dystrophies: clinical and refractive results
Authors: Cem Sari, Mehmet Ozgur Cubuk, Sibel Ahmet, Burcu Kemer Atik, Nilay Kandemir Besek, Ahmet Kirgiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate and compare visual, refractive, and keratometric outcomes and complications in patients who underwent Deep Anterior Lamellar Keratoplasty (DALK) and Penetrating Keratoplasty (PKP) surgery for stromal corneal dystrophies.MethodsThis retrospective study included 35 eyes that underwent keratoplasty for stromal corneal dystrophy. The eyes were divided into 4 groups according to dystrophy type and surgical procedure.ResultsIn the macular corneal dystrophy (MCD) group, 13 PKP, 6 DALK; in the granular corneal dystrophy (GCD) group, 10 PKP, 6 DALK surgeries were performed. Compared to the preoperative period, there was a significant increase in BCVA at 1 year postoperatively for all groups (p < 0.05). 1-year graft survival after PKP was 84.6% and 100% in the MCD and GCD groups, respectively. Whereas it was 83.3% after DALK in both groups. Two cases of endothelial graft rejection occurred in the MCD-PKP group, while one case of stromal graft rejection was observed in the MCD-DALK group. Additionally, one instance of disease recurrence was seen in each of the following groups: MCD-PKP, MCD-DALK, and GCD-DALK.ConclusionThese findings suggest that DALK may provide acceptable visual, refractive, and graft survival outcomes in selected patients with stromal corneal dystrophies. Given its closed-sky surgical nature and lower risk of endothelial rejection, DALK may be considered a suitable alternative to PKP in appropriately selected cases.Keywords: Cornea, Keratometry, Lamellar keratoplasty, Penetrating keratoplasty, Stromal dystrophy

Introduction

Corneal dystrophies are a group of non-inflammatory, genetically driven corneal disorders that typically present bilaterally. Diagnosis is primarily based on clinical examination, with histopathological analysis of biopsy specimens and genetic testing serving as supportive tools [1].

Corneal dystrophies are classified based on the affected corneal layer and are categorized into anterior corneal dystrophies, stromal dystrophies, and posterior corneal dystrophies [2]. Among stromal corneal dystrophies, macular corneal dystrophy (MCD) and granular corneal dystrophy (GCD) are two major subtypes frequently encountered in clinical practice and were the focus of the present study [3]. These disorders can result in corneal clouding which decreases the visual acuity. Treatment options include phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK) for superficial lesions, as well as penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) and deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) for more advanced cases [4]. While PTK is a less invasive procedure with favorable visual outcomes in patients with superficial corneal opacities, surgical interventions such as deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty and penetrating keratoplasty may be necessary for visual rehabilitation in cases with deeper and more severe corneal opacities [5].

DALK preserves the corneal endothelium and Descemet’s membrane (DM), thereby minimizing the risk of endothelial graft rejection and providing advantages over PKP. Additionally, the reduced duration of postoperative topical steroid use in DALK decreases the risk of complications such as secondary infections, cataract, and glaucoma. However, concerns regarding visual outcomes persist due to the lamellar nature of the surgery. Nonetheless, numerous studies indicate that DALK is comparable to PKP in terms of both efficacy and safety [6, 7].

The aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the visual, refractive, keratometric outcomes, and complications in patients with stromal corneal dystrophy (MCD and GCD) who underwent DALK and PKP.

Materials and methods

The medical records of patients who underwent keratoplasty surgery for corneal stromal dystrophy between 2015 and 2023 at Beyoglu Eye Training and Research Hospital were retrospectively reviewed. Approval for the study was obtained from the Health Sciences University Hamidiye Scientific Research Ethics Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. (Approval number: 23/622 Ethics committee approval date: 1.12.2023)

Patient population

The diagnosis of corneal dystrophy was made by two experienced corneal specialists based on detailed slit lamp examination and anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) imaging (Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Patients over 18 years of age who underwent PKP or DALK for corneal stromal dystrophy and had at least one year of regular postoperative follow-up were included in the study. Exclusion criteria included a history of intraocular surgery (excluding cataract surgery), systemic conditions that could impair wound healing like diabetes mellitus, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and retinal or uveitic pathologies.

Surgical technique

Keratoplasty was indicated for patients with a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) less than 0.3 (Snellen chart), corneal stromal opacities compromising the visual axis, and visual impairment not attributable to other ocular pathologies. DALK was preferred in patients with a central corneal thickness greater than 400 microns and superficial stromal opacities within the anterior 200 microns of the stroma (confirmed by AS-OCT). PKP was indicated for patients with a central corneal thickness less than 400 microns and deep stromal opacity. All surgeries were performed by experienced surgeons from the same corneal surgery team.

Penetrating keratoplasty

The horizontal and vertical diameters of the cornea were measured, and the central point of the cornea was marked. A trephine was used to create a full-thickness incision in the host cornea, while the donor cornea was punched out to a diameter 0.25 to 0.50 mm larger than the trephined area. The donor corneal tissue was secured to the host bed using sixteen interrupted 10.0 nylon sutures. The procedure was completed with a subconjunctival injection of dexamethasone.

Deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty

The horizontal and vertical diameters of the cornea were measured, and the central point was marked. A partial trepanation was performed to a depth of 60–70% of the total corneal thickness. A 30-gauge needle was used to inject air between the stroma and Descemet’s membrane at a 30–40° angle to create a ‘big bubble’ formation. If the anterior chamber pressure increased, a paracentesis was performed with a microvitreoretinal (MVR) blade to reduce the pressure, followed by the injection of air into the anterior chamber. The stroma was then perforated, and a viscoelastic substance was injected to detach the Descemet’s membrane complex. The stroma was excised using blunt scissors through the created opening. In cases where big bubble formation was unsuccessful, stromal excision was performed via manual dissection.

Regarding the donor cornea, firstly the endothelium was stained with 0.06% trypan blue for visualization during the endothelial peeling process of the donor tissue. After stripping the endothelium, the donor tissue was cut using a punch 0.25–0.50 mm larger than the trepanation size. The isolated donor corneal tissue was then secured to the recipient corneal bed using sixteen interrupted 10.0 nylon sutures. The procedure was concluded with a subconjunctival injection of dexamethasone.

Intraoperative complications, as well as the trepan and punch sizes, were recorded for each patient.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients were followed up in the 1st week, the 1st month and at 3 months intervals thereafter, postoperatively. At each visit, detailed ophthalmologic examination findings, including BCVA (Snellen lines), slit-lamp anterior and posterior segment assessment, and intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, were reviewed. Corneal topography was performed with Sirius (Sirius, Costruzioni Strumenti, Oftalmici, Italy) preoperatively and at 1-year postoperatively. Postoperatively, topical moxifloxacin was administered five times daily, while prednisolone acetate was given four times daily. Both groups continued prednisolone acetate at this frequency for approximately three months. In the DALK group, steroid treatment was gradually tapered and discontinued by the sixth month, whereas in the PKP group, it was extended for up to one year. Suture removal was guided by wound healing progress; in the DALK group, sutures were removed at 6th month or later, while in the PKP group, removal was performed at 10th month or beyond. Graft failure was defined as irreversible loss of graft clarity resulting in visually significant corneal opacity, including cases associated with irreversible graft rejection, recurrence causing significant visual deterioration, or persistent stromal edema/scarring during follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the NCSS (Number Cruncher Statistical System) 2007 Statistical Software (Utah, USA) and the SPSS 20.0 package. Descriptive statistical methods, including mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range were used to summarize the data. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of variable distributions.

For intergroup comparisons of normally distributed variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for subgroup analyses. The independent t-test was employed for comparisons between two groups.

For variables that were not normally distributed, Friedman’s test was used for time-based comparisons, Dunn’s multiple comparison test for subgroup analyses, the Kruskal-Wallis test for intergroup comparisons, and the Mann-Whitney U test for pairwise group comparisons. The chi-square test was applied for qualitative data comparisons.

The Kaplan-Meier method was used to analyze survival between diagnostic groups, while the log-rank test was employed to compare failure rates between surgical methods within groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study included 35 eyes of 35 (16 male, 19 female) patients. Only one eye per patient was included in the analysis to avoid inter-eye correlation bias; in patients eligible in both eyes, the first operated eye was selected. The mean age of 45.86 ± 14.34 years (range: 20–74) and a mean follow-up duration of 41.84 ± 25.74 months (range: 12–101). There was no statistically significant difference in mean age between the dystrophy groups (p = 0.604). However, a statistically significant difference was observed in gender distribution, with 31.5% of patients in the MCD group and 62.5% in the GCD group being male (p = 0.037). Among the 19 eyes with MCD, 13 underwent PKP, while 6 underwent DALK. In the 16 eyes with GCD, 10 were treated with PKP, and 6 with DALK. There was no statistically significant difference in the choice of surgical method between the groups (p = 0.934).

In the MCD group, the mean BCVA showed a statistically significant improvement at all postoperative all follow-up visits compared to the preoperative period (each p < 0.05). In the GCD group, the mean BCVA increased significantly at 6 and 12 months postoperatively compared to the preoperative period (p = 0.001, p = 0.003). Survival rates

The distribution of survival rates over time for patients in different stromal dystrophy groups is shown in Fig. 1. Graft survival at 1 year was 73.7% in the MCD group and 93.8% in the GCD group, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.120). The 1-year graft survival rate was 84.6% in the MCD-PKP group and 100% in the GCD-PKP group, with no statistically significant difference between them (p = 0.524). At 1 year, the graft survival rate was 83.3% in both the MCD-DALK and GCD-DALK groups, with no statistically significant difference between them (p = 1.000).

Complications

Suture revision was required in 3 eyes (23.08%) in the MCD-PKP group and 2 eyes (33.33%) in the MCD-DALK group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.634). Similarly, suture revision was required in 2 eyes (20%) in the GCD-PKP group and 2 eyes (33.33%) in the GCD-DALK group, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.551).

Among postoperative complications, glaucoma was observed in 1 patient (7.6%) in the DALK group and 2 patients (8.0%) in the PKP group, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.973). Cataract was observed in 1 patient (4.0%) in the PKP group, while no cases were reported in the DALK group. In terms of complications, there was no statistically significant difference between the MCD and GCD groups (p = 0.629).

While in the MCD-PKP group, 2 eyes had endothelial rejection and 1 eye had recurrence, in the MCD-DALK group, 1 eye failed due to stromal rejection and 1 eye failed due to recurrence, however there was no statistically significant difference (p = 0.637). There was no graft failure in the GCD-PKP group, whereas 1 eye in the GCD-DALK group failed due to recurrence. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.182). Discussion

Several reports worldwide indicate that keratoplasty is a relatively common procedure for patients with corneal dystrophy [8–13]. Numerous studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes in visual acuity and graft transparency for both PKP and DALK [14, 15]. However, the risks of endothelial graft rejection and disease recurrence remain important considerations when selecting a surgical procedure [7].

MCD is a recessively inherited condition known for its severity, making it the least common among the three classic stromal dystrophies. Most comparative studies between PKP and DALK primarily focus on MCD [16, 17]. The choice of transplantation method for MCD depends on the severity of the disease. Since MCD can affect the deeper layers of the cornea, some studies have suggested that visual outcomes after DALK may be inferior to those after PKP—a view supported by Cheng et al. [18], who found that patients in the PKP group had better BCVA at both 1 and 2 years postoperatively. However, if the deep stroma and Descemet’s membrane are unaffected, DALK remains a viable option. Notably, DALK surgery using the big bubble technique has demonstrated comparable or even superior visual outcomes compared to PKP in some studies [16, 18]. Similar to Cheng et al. [18] in our study, the 1-year postoperative mean BCVA was higher in the MCD-PKP group compared to the MCD-DALK group (p = 0.471). We believe that this was attributed to the fact that PKP removes the dystrophic cornea, providing rapid visual improvement in the short term, whereas recurrence and corneal opacification can occur after DALK.

Robin et al. concluded, through a histochemical study, that the recurrence of MCD in corneal grafts is due to the replacement of donor keratocytes with genetically defective host cells [19]. Since Descemet’s membrane, endothelial layer, and partial stroma tissue remain after DALK, the presence of more genetically defective tissue compared to PKP may explain the higher and earlier recurrence rate. As previously reported in a large retrospective series of PKP for MCD, the incidence of recurrence correlates with follow-up time and is inversely related to donor graft size and recipient trepanation [14]. In the study by Al Araj et al. [16] involving patients with MCD, the recurrence rate was reported as 2.9% following PKP and 4.5% following DALK (p = 0.69). In the present study, recurrence was observed in 1 (7%) patient after PKP and 1 (10%) patient after DALK in the MCD group, and this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.943). Similar findings regarding recurrence have been reported in the literature [7, 14, 16]. In this study, graft size was similar between PKP and DALK methods in the MCD group (p = 0.289). Although graft sizes were comparable in our study, the lack of a significant difference in recurrence rates could be explained by the small sample size.

GCD is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by progressive stromal opacities that gradually impair vision, typically leading to significant visual decline by the third decade of life [20]. Various surgical approaches, including PK and DALK, have been proposed for the management of corneal stromal dystrophies [21]. In a study by Lyons et al. [15] comparing DALK and PKP in patients with GCD, no significant difference in visual acuity was found [15]. Similarly, our study showed no statistically significant difference between the GCD-PKP and GCD-DALK groups (p = 0.088).

The literature includes case reports of recurrence following “big bubble” DALK surgery in patients with GCD [7, 21, 22]. Oke et al. [22] suggested that recurrence may occur due to keratocyte activation following stromal trauma during big bubble formation, leading to the development of hyaline deposits. In our study, recurrence was observed in one patient within the GCD-DALK group; however, no significant difference was found when compared to the PKP group (p = 0.182).

Corneal transplantation failure is primarily linked to graft rejection. Patel et al. found that irreversible rejection accounts for 25% of all failed grafts [23]. In a large series by Al-Swailem and colleagues, 20.5% of patients undergoing PKP for MCD experienced endothelial rejection, with 3.5% resulting in irreversible failure [14]. Another study reported that irreversible endothelial rejection accounted for 40% of failed corneal transplants, necessitating re-graft surgery in 3 out of 5 eyes (7.3%) in the PKP group due to graft failure [18]. While DALK eliminates the risk of endothelial rejection, the possibility of subepithelial and stromal graft rejection remains [24, 25]. Reports indicate that the incidence of immunological rejection following DALK ranges from 0% to 14.3%, with most cases being reversible with appropriate treatment [20, 26]. Ünal et al. reported stromal rejection in 3 patients (4.3%) with MCD following DALK, all of whom responded favorably to intensive corticosteroid therapy [7]. In our study, rejection was observed in 2 patients (8%) in the PKP group and 1 patient (7.6%) in the DALK group. Although DALK is generally considered to be more advantageous in terms of graft rejection, the similar rejection rates observed in our study may be attributed to the limited number of patients included.

Balıkçı et al. compared topographic parameters between PKP and DALK in patients with MCD, reporting an increase in postoperative Kmax, corneal thickness, and corneal astigmatism. Similar to our findings, no statistically significant postoperative changes were observed in the anterior corneal curvature (Kmean) values in their study. In the DALK group, lower postoperative anterior corneal Kmean and Q values were observed, while other parameters showed comparable results between the two groups [17].

In a study with comparable patient numbers, preoperative Kmean values were found to be similar between the MCD and GCD groups, while significantly lower values were observed in eyes with LCD (p < 0.001) [27]. In the current study, preoperative Kmean and K2 values were significantly lower in the MCD group compared to the GCD group (p = 0.023, p = 0.002 respectively). Furthermore, the postoperative K2 value in the MCD group was significantly higher at the one-year follow-up compared to preoperative values, with no significant difference observed between the DALK and PKP methods.

Long-term graft survival is strongly associated with a sufficiently high endothelial cell density. Studies have shown that PKP can lead to accelerated loss of endothelial cells, contributing to graft failure [28]. The average annual loss of endothelial cell density after PKP has been reported to range from 12% to 17% [29, 30]. In contrast, DALK replaces only the anterior cornea while preserving the host’s Descemet’s membrane and endothelium, resulting in a reduced immunological response and higher postoperative endothelial cell density. In our study, we observed one-year graft survival rates of 84.6% for the MCD-PKP group and 83.33% for the MCD-DALK group. For GCD, the one-year survival rate was 100% for the GCD-PKP group and 83.33% for the GCD-DALK group. Literature reports similar graft survival rates for MCD, with PKP survival ranging between 77% and 90%; [6, 14, 31] and DALK survival ranging between 80% and 85.7% [6, 24, 31]. The high graft survival rates in these disorders are attributed to their avascular nature, minimal ocular surface disease, and the young, compliant patient population with easy access to healthcare. Although we did not observe statistically significant differences, graft survival was superior in all PKP groups compared to DALK. This may be attributed to the shorter follow-up period in our study, and results could potentially differ with longer follow-up durations.

In the present study, no statistically significant difference in corneal astigmatism was observed among the MCD-PKP, MCD-DALK, GCD-PKP, and GCD-DALK groups at the one-year postoperative follow-up. Balıkçı et al. [17] also reported similar findings regarding corneal astigmatism in the MCD-PKP and MCD-DALK groups. Furthermore, several studies in the literature comparing DALK and PKP procedures have supported our results concerning corneal astigmatism data [27, 32–34].

DALK, although a challenging surgical procedure with a steep learning curve, significantly minimizes the risk of postoperative complications, including glaucoma, cataract formation, retinal detachment, cystoid macular edema, expulsive choroidal hemorrhage, and epithelial ingrowth, as it does not involve interference with intraocular structures. In contrast, although PKP is technically simpler, it increases the likelihood of postoperative complications such as cataracts and may compromise intraocular structures, potentially elevating intraocular pressure due to prolonged steroid use. In the current study, cataract complications were observed in 1 patient (4%) who underwent PKP. Supporting this, Al-Swailem et al. reported a 4.8% cataract incidence in their study on the outcomes of PKP in MCD patients [14]. Al-Araj et al. observed cataract rates of 11.1% after PKP and 4.5% after DALK [16]. Notably, no cataracts were found in the DALK group of our study. Reddy et al. also found a 3.6% cataract incidence in the PKP group, consistent with our findings, while they reported a 4.7% rate in the DALK group [6]. Their discrepancy may be attributed to the sample size imbalance, with 109 patients in the PKP group compared to 21 in the DALK group. Additionally, in the same study, glaucoma was observed in 11% of the PKP group and 9.5% of the DALK group. Our findings were similar, with glaucoma occurring in 8% of the PKP group and 7.6% of the DALK group. The higher incidence of secondary glaucoma in the PKP group is likely due to the longer duration of steroid use [35].

The present study has several limitations. First, the number of patients undergoing PKP was higher than that of patients undergoing DALK, mainly because many patients presented with advanced stromal involvement requiring full-thickness keratoplasty and because PKP had been performed more frequently in earlier years. Second, surgical selection was indication-based; DALK was generally preferred in eyes with more superficial stromal involvement and greater corneal thickness, whereas PKP was preferred in eyes with deeper stromal opacity and thinner corneas. Therefore, direct comparisons between the two procedures should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, the relatively small sample size and subgroup analyses may have limited the statistical power of the study. Finally, endothelial cell density analysis could not be included because of insufficient available data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both DALK and PKP provided favorable clinical outcomes in patients with stromal corneal dystrophies. The present findings suggest that DALK may offer acceptable visual, refractive, and graft survival outcomes in selected cases while avoiding the risk of endothelial rejection associated with PKP. However, due to the retrospective design, indication-based surgical selection, and relatively limited sample size, larger prospective comparative studies are required before definitive conclusions can be drawn.
