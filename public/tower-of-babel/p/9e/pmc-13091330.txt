The mediating role of obesity in the relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms: A population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Shaomin Diao, Weiwei Song, Xiaoming Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigates whether obesity mediates the relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms, aiming to provide evidence for obesity's mediating effect and inform public health interventions.MethodsData from 23,190 adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018 were analyzed. Generalized linear regression models assessed the associations between sleep duration, obesity indicators [body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and body roundness index (BRI)], and depressive symptoms. Mediation analysis explored the mediating role of obesity, with subgroup analysis across gender, age, and race.ResultsSleep duration was significantly negatively associated with BMI, WC, and BRI (all P < 0.001), and these obesity indicators were positively associated with depressive symptoms (all P < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that obesity partially mediated the relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms, with slightly stronger effects observed in individuals with diabetes.ConclusionObesity, assessed by BMI, WC, and BRI, showed a modest, partial mediating role in the association between sleep duration and depressive symptoms, with exploratory evidence of larger indirect effects among individuals with diabetes.Keywords: Sleep duration, Depressive symptoms, Obesity, Mediating effect

Introduction

Sleep is a key health indicator, and adequate sleep duration and quality are essential for maintaining physiological function and overall health [1,2]. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the Sleep Research Society (SRS) recommend that adults obtain at least 7 h of sleep per night [3]. Both insufficient and excessive sleep have been associated with adverse neuropsychological outcomes, including depressive symptoms [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Depressive symptoms are highly prevalent worldwide and impose a substantial burden on health and functioning [[11], [12], [13]], and individuals with depressive symptoms are more likely to experience multiple comorbid conditions and reduced quality of life [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]].

Obesity is another major public health concern. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines obesity as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that may impair health, commonly operationalized as BMI ≥30 kg/m2 [23]. Obesity is associated with increased mortality and a wide range of cardiometabolic and other adverse health outcomes [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]], and it has also been linked to depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment [32,33]. Importantly, obesity may plausibly connect sleep duration to depressive symptoms through several pathways. Sleep disruption may promote weight gain via hormonal changes (e.g., increased ghrelin and decreased leptin), reduced basal metabolic rate, and impaired insulin sensitivity [34]. In turn, obesity-related inflammation, endocrine dysregulation, and psychosocial factors (e.g., negative body image) may contribute to depressive symptoms [35]. The relationships may also be bidirectional: depressive symptoms can increase obesity risk through stress-system dysregulation and unhealthy behaviors, and some antidepressant medications may promote weight gain [36,37]. Prior studies have also emphasized the interrelationships among obesity, sleep disorders (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea), and sleep fragmentation [38].

Despite the growing literature linking sleep duration, obesity, and depressive symptoms, much of the evidence remains descriptive, and relatively few studies have quantified whether obesity indicators mediate the association between sleep duration and depressive symptoms [36,39,40]. Moreover, obesity is a multidimensional construct, and relying on a single indicator (e.g., BMI) may not adequately capture clinically relevant differences in overall versus central adiposity. Therefore, we examined three complementary obesity indicators: body mass index (BMI) as a measure of overall adiposity, waist circumference (WC) as a marker of central adiposity, and the body roundness index (BRI) as an anthropometric index intended to better characterize body shape and abdominal fat distribution.

Accordingly, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we investigated the association between sleep duration and depressive symptoms (assessed by PHQ-9) and evaluated whether BMI, WC, and BRI mediate this association. By quantifying these potential mediating effects in a large, nationally representative U.S. sample, we aim to provide evidence that may inform public health strategies targeting sleep and obesity to reduce depressive symptom burden.

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted a population-based cross-sectional study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018, a publicly available dataset administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). We initially considered 34,770 participants aged ≥20 years from six survey cycles (2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018).

Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥20 years and had information on depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), sleep duration, and the adiposity indicators (BMI, waist circumference, and BRI), as well as the covariates required for the adjusted models. Participants with missing data in these key variables were excluded. Information on antidepressant use was not consistently available across all cycles and was therefore not used as an exclusion criterion. The final analytic sample included 23,190 participants (Fig. 1).

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a nine-item screening instrument that asked questions about the frequency of symptoms of depression over the past 2 weeks [41,42]. The response categories for the nine items were ‘not at all,’ ‘several days,’ ‘more than half the days,’ and ‘nearly every day,’ with scores ranging from 0 to 3. Therefore, participants' total PHQ-9 score ranges from 0 to 27. Detailed items in this questionnaire can be found in supplementary (Table S1).

Assessment of sleep duration

Participants’ sleep duration was collected at home by trained interviewers using the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI) system. Between 2007 and 2014, participants in the NHANES surveys were asked: “How much sleep do you get (hours)?” In the 2015–2018 survey cycles, this question was modified to: “How much sleep do you usually get at night on weekdays or workdays (hours)?” Sleep duration categories were used mainly for descriptive and interpretability purposes, whereas the primary models treated sleep duration as a continuous variable.

Assessment of covariates

We considered the following covariates and included in this research: age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational level (College or above and below college), marital status, family poverty income ratio (PIR, with higher values indicating a higher economic status, ranging from 1 to 5) [38], serum cotinine (a marker for both active smoking, and as an index to Environmental Tobacco Smoke (ETS) exposure, or “passive smoking") [38], drinking status, diabetes (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no) and MET (metabolic equivalent) score. Although hypertension and diabetes status were consistently available and included as covariates, information on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was not collected in a standardized manner across the NHANES survey cycles analyzed. Therefore, OSA could not be included as a covariate in this study.

Diabetes was assessed by glycohemoglobin (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) in combination with self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥80 mmHg, together with self-reported physician diagnosis of hypertension.

The metabolic equivalent (MET) score was calculated from five categories of physical activity: vigorous work-related activity (8 points), moderate work-related activity (4 points), walking or bicycling for transportation (4 points), vigorous leisure-time physical activity (8 points), and moderate leisure-time physical activity (4 points). Statistical analysis

All participants were grouped by depression status diagnosed by PHQ-9 score (≥10) [34]. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median with Interquartile Range (IQR) were used to describe continuous variables as normally or non-normally distributed respectively, according to their histograms (Fig. S1). Frequency plus percentage were used to describe category variables. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance), Kruskal-Wallis test and chi square test were utilized to assess variables’ differences between different groups, respectively.

All analyses accounted for the NHANES complex survey design (weights, strata, and PSUs) to obtain design-based estimates and standard errors. Mediation was assessed using weighted models, with PSU cluster-robust sensitivity analyses to address variance estimation limitations in standard mediation routines, yielding consistent results.

The Generalized Linear Model (GLM) is a direct extension of classical linear regression, constructing the model through three components: first, the random component, which specifies the probability distribution of the response variable; second, the systematic component, forming the linear combination of predictors; and third, the link function, which establishes the relationship between the linear predictor η and the mean μ of the response variable, expressed as g(μ) = η. The core purpose of this model is to handle non-normally distributed response variables, making it widely applicable for modeling complex data types such as classification, count data, and continuous positive values. Generalized linear regression models (GLM) were employed to analyze the associations between sleep duration, obesity indicators (BMI, WC, BRI), and depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 score). GLMs were specified using a Negative Binomial distribution with a log link function to accommodate the dispersed count data distribution.

Three models were employed: Model 1 was a crude model without covariates; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, education level, race, marital status, and poverty income ratio (PIR); and Model 3 was further adjusted for alcohol consumption, serum cotinine, diabetes status, hypertension status, and MET score. Because BMI, WC, and BRI are correlated adiposity indicators, they were examined in separate models (one indicator at a time) to avoid multicollinearity. Potential non-linearity was evaluated using survey-weighted spline models. Covariates were selected a priori based on prior evidence and subject-matter knowledge as potential confounders, rather than data-driven selection. The adjusted set included demographic variables (age, sex, race/ethnicity), socioeconomic factors (education, marital status, and family income-to-poverty ratio), behavioral factors (alcohol use and smoking exposure), and major cardiometabolic comorbidities/health status indicators (e.g., diabetes, hypertension status, CVD, and physical activity). The same covariate set was applied consistently across all primary models, including (i) sleep duration–PHQ-9 models, (ii) sleep duration–adiposity models, and (iii) adiposity–PHQ-9 models used for mediation analysis.

In addition, a classic mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether obesity indicators mediated the association between sleep duration and PHQ-9 score. Path a examined the association between sleep duration and the three obesity indicators. Path b examined the association between the obesity indicators and PHQ-9 score. Path c examined the direct association between sleep duration and PHQ-9 score, and Path c′ represented this association after adjusting for obesity indicators. Because BMI, WC, and BRI are correlated adiposity indicators, mediation was evaluated in separate models (one mediator at a time) to avoid multicollinearity. Given the cross-sectional design, mediation estimates should be interpreted as associational and rely on strong assumptions (e.g., no unmeasured confounding and correct model specification), and thus are presented as exploratory evidence of potential pathways. Mediation analyses were performed using the R package mediation (version 4.5.0) [34].

Subsequently, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore obesity's mediating role between sleep duration and PHQ-9 among individuals in different sexes (male and female), age groups (<45, 45-59, >59), races (White, African American and Hispanic), diabetes status (yes and no) and hypertension status (yes and no).

Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach. We additionally performed a sensitivity analysis using inverse-probability weighting to account for differential inclusion due to missingness; results were consistent with the primary analyses. Sensitivity analyses using PHQ-9 above 10 and excluding extreme sleep durations yielded consistent results(Table S5).

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Compared with participants with PHQ-9 scores <10, those with scores ≥10 had significantly higher levels of obesity indicators and shorter sleep duration (P < 0.001). They were also more likely to be female and African American (P < 0.001). In addition, this group tended to have lower socioeconomic status, as reflected by fewer college attendees and lower poverty income ratios (PIR) (P < 0.001). Regarding health-related variables, participants with depression had higher cotinine levels and a higher prevalence of diabetes (P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed in alcohol consumption (P = 0.912) or hypertension status (P = 0.995).

GLM analyses were conducted to examine the linear associations between sleep duration, mediators, and PHQ-9 score. In contrast, these obesity indicators were positively associated with PHQ-9 score (BMI: β = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.07; WC: β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.03; BRI: β = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.17 to 0.22). The association between sleep duration and PHQ-9 score remained statistically significant even after additional adjustment for obesity indicators (Table 2).

Mediation analysis

Fig. 2 illustrates the mediating role of three obesity indicators in the association between sleep duration and depressive symptoms. The mediation analysis yielded the following results: the average causal mediation effect (ACME) was −0.01 (95% CI: −0.01 to −0.01), and the average direct effect (ADE) was −0.28 (95% CI: −0.32 to −0.25). These findings indicate that the influence of sleep duration on PHQ-9 score occurred primarily through direct pathways, with a relatively small mediation effect. The total effect was −0.30 (95% CI: −0.33 to −0.26), and the proportion mediated was 3.32% (95% CI: 2.29% to 4.00%). This suggests that most of the effect of sleep duration on depressive symptoms operated directly, with obesity accounting for only a small portion of the total effect. Similar patterns were observed for waist circumference and BRI, with mediation proportions of 2.61% (95% CI: 1.33% to 4.00%) and 2.19% (95% CI: 1.10% to 3.00%), respectively.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of mediation effects across different populations (Table S2). No significant differences were observed among subgroups defined by sex, age, race, or hypertension status. However, a slightly stronger mediation effect was observed among individuals with diabetes (BMI: 5%; WC: 4%; BRI: 4%).

Discussion

This study used NHANES 2007–2018 data to examine the relationships among sleep duration, adiposity indicators (BMI, waist circumference [WC], and body roundness index [BRI]), and depressive symptom burden measured by PHQ-9. In the primary analyses, sleep duration was inversely associated with adiposity indicators, and higher adiposity was associated with higher PHQ-9 scores. In additional survey-weighted spline analyses, we observed a non-linear association between sleep duration and PHQ-9: predicted PHQ-9 was lowest around ∼7 h of sleep and increased at both shorter and longer sleep durations (Fig. Sx; spline vs. linear, P < 0.001). In mediation analyses, BMI, WC, and BRI showed statistically detectable indirect effects; however, the mediated proportions were modest (approximately 2–4%), indicating that the sleep–PHQ-9 association operates primarily through direct (non-adiposity) pathways. These findings support a cautious interpretation: adiposity may represent a small, partial pathway linking sleep duration to depressive symptoms rather than a dominant mediating mechanism.

Comparison with other studies

Our results are broadly consistent with prior studies reporting that adequate sleep is associated with lower obesity-related measures [44] and that obesity is associated with depressive symptoms [45]. The observed non-linear pattern, with the lowest predicted PHQ-9 near ∼7 h and higher PHQ-9 at both shorter and longer sleep durations, aligns with epidemiologic evidence suggesting that non-optimal sleep duration is related to poorer mental health outcomes [3,8]. Importantly, the association between sleep duration and depressive symptoms remained statistically significant after adjusting for adiposity indicators, consistent with evidence in U.S. adults that sleep duration relates to depressive symptom burden independent of obesity [3,8]. Methodologically, leveraging NHANES provides nationally representative estimates and aligns with many population-based studies using similar data resources [[46], [47], [48]].

Compared with earlier work that often reported descriptive associations between sleep, obesity, and depressive symptoms [36], a key contribution of our study is the quantification of indirect effects using multiple adiposity indicators. Notably, although the indirect effects were statistically detectable, they accounted for only a small fraction of the total association (e.g., 3.32% for BMI, 2.61% for WC, and 2.19% for BRI), which emphasizes the importance of avoiding overstatement regarding mediation strength.

Interpretation of the modest mediation effects

The small mediated proportions suggest that factors beyond adiposity likely explain most of the sleep–PHQ-9 association. Even so, the consistency of indirect effects across BMI, WC, and BRI may still be informative because these indicators represent related but distinct dimensions of adiposity. BMI reflects overall adiposity, WC captures central adiposity closely linked to metabolic risk, and BRI is intended to characterize body shape and fat distribution via a geometric approach. Observing similar (though small) indirect effects across these indicators supports the interpretation that adiposity is a plausible—but limited—indirect pathway.

Given the modest effect sizes, we interpret the mediation findings as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. From a public health perspective, even small indirect effects could have population-level relevance when both suboptimal sleep and obesity are common; however, our results do not support the conclusion that weight status alone would substantially explain or eliminate the sleep–depressive symptom association.

Potential mechanism

Potential mechanisms linking adiposity, sleep, and depressive symptoms have been proposed in the literature, but they cannot be confirmed by our cross-sectional analyses and should be interpreted as plausible hypotheses rather than established explanations. Obesity is associated with a higher risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [49], which may contribute to sleep fragmentation and daytime symptoms. Adiposity is also linked to systemic inflammation [50], and inflammatory processes have been proposed to influence neurobiological pathways relevant to mood regulation [51]. In addition, sleep disruption and excess adiposity may relate to neuroendocrine dysregulation (e.g., cortisol and insulin pathways) [52] and to behavioral factors such as reduced physical activity and unhealthy diet patterns, which have been associated with both sleep problems and poorer mood [53]. Importantly, our findings provide statistical evidence of modest, partial mediation rather than proof of biological mechanism, and other non-adiposity pathways likely contribute to the sleep–PHQ-9 association.

Explanation for the subgroup analyses results

In subgroup analyses, the indirect effects appeared larger among participants with diabetes. However, subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory and may reflect residual confounding, differential measurement error, multiple comparisons, or limited power within strata. As such, we view this pattern as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of effect modification. One possible interpretation is that diabetes-related metabolic dysregulation (e.g., insulin resistance, chronic hyperglycemia, and lipid metabolism disturbances) [54] and chronic low-grade inflammation [55] could increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms and potentially interact with sleep and adiposity. In addition, diabetes commonly co-occurs with other cardiometabolic comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular disease, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease) [56], which may further complicate sleep–health relationships. Future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether diabetes meaningfully modifies the indirect pathway via adiposity.

Across other subgroups (sex, age, and race/ethnicity), we did not observe strong evidence of differential mediation. This suggests that the modest mediating role of adiposity may be relatively stable across major demographic strata, although limited power within some subgroups and residual confounding remain possible.

Clinical and public health implications

Clinically, our findings support considering sleep duration and adiposity as interconnected factors associated with depressive symptom burden, particularly in populations with metabolic disorders such as diabetes. However, given the modest effect sizes and small mediated proportions, implications should be framed conservatively: screening and counseling that address sleep and weight as part of comprehensive care may be reasonable, but the results do not justify strong causal claims.

At the public health level, strategies that promote healthy sleep and weight management may provide complementary benefits. Policies and programs that facilitate lifestyle modification (e.g., nutrition education, physical activity, and behavioral counseling) could be relevant for both metabolic and mental health outcomes. Prior evidence linking obesity-related measures to broader cardiometabolic risk further supports the interconnectedness of metabolic health with downstream health burdens [57].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of a large, nationally representative NHANES sample, standardized measures of adiposity, and the evaluation of multiple adiposity indicators (BMI, WC, BRI), which improves interpretability relative to relying on a single measure. We also quantified indirect effects and explored subgroup patterns relevant to diabetes, and we conducted additional survey-weighted spline analyses to assess non-linearity (Fig. S2).

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, sleep duration and depressive symptoms were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias, social desirability bias, and measurement error. Non-differential misclassification would likely attenuate the observed associations toward the null, whereas differential reporting related to health status or mood cannot be excluded and could bias estimates in either direction; therefore, our findings should be interpreted cautiously. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal ordering; therefore, the mediation results cannot be interpreted causally and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, antidepressant use could not be accounted for in the present analysis because medication information was not consistently available across all NHANES cycles included in this study. This is important because some antidepressant medications may contribute to weight gain and metabolic changes, and thus may confound the observed associations among sleep duration, adiposity indicators, and depressive symptoms, potentially affecting the estimated mediation effects. Third, information on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was not consistently available, limiting adjustment for this potentially important confounder. Finally, although NHANES weighting improves generalizability to the U.S. adult population, interpretation should distinguish between the analytic sample size and weighted population estimates.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, sleep duration was associated with depressive symptom burden, and adiposity indicators (BMI, WC, and BRI) accounted for a small, partial proportion of this association. Subgroup analyses suggested the indirect effects may be larger among individuals with diabetes; however, these findings are exploratory and require confirmation. Given the cross-sectional design and modest effect sizes, the results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a confirmed causal pathway. Future longitudinal and interventional studies using objective sleep measures are needed to clarify temporal ordering, confirm causality, and elucidate underlying mechanisms.
