Predictive value of HALP score for clinical outcomes in lung cancer patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yifan Wang, Jingguo Hu, Yu Sun, Kaixin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe hemoglobin-albumin-lymphocyte-platelet (HALP) index has emerged as a composite indicator reflecting nutritional and inflammatory status. Its prognostic relevance in lung cancer remains inconsistent across studies. This study quantitatively evaluated the association between pretreatment HALP levels and clinical outcomes in lung cancer.MethodsSeveral electronic databases were searched up to April 7, 2025. Primary endpoints included overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Secondary endpoints consisted of disease control rate (DCR), objective response rate (ORR), 90-day mortality, and cachexia. Hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs) were combined for the primary and secondary outcomes, respectively. Subgroup analyses based on tumor type and treatment were performed.ResultsSixteen studies were enrolled. Pooled results indicated that lower HALP score significantly predicted worse OS [HR =0.60; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.50–0.73; P<0.001] and PFS (HR =0.52; 95% CI: 0.39–0.69; P<0.001). Subgroup analysis manifested similar results. For the secondary outcomes, pretreatment HALP score was also related to 90-day mortality (OR =0.35; 95% CI: 0.24–0.51; P<0.001) and cachexia (OR =0.76; 95% CI: 0.61–0.95; P=0.02). However, non-significant association of pretreatment HALP score with DCR (OR =1.85; P=0.06) or ORR (OR =1.01; P=0.96) was observed.ConclusionsPretreatment HALP score might serve as a novel predictor for clinical outcomes in lung cancer and a lower HALP score predicts worse prognosis.Keywords: Hemoglobin-albumin-lymphocyte-platelet (HALP), lung cancer, clinical outcome, predictive, meta-analysis

Highlight box

Key findings

• This meta-analysis of 16 studies (10,379 patients) demonstrated that a lower pretreatment hemoglobin-albumin-lymphocyte-platelet (HALP) score significantly predicted worse overall survival [hazard ratio (HR) =0.60] and progression-free survival (HR =0.52) in lung cancer patients.

• Lower HALP score was also significantly associated with higher 90-day mortality [odds ratio (OR) =0.35] and greater risk of cachexia (OR =0.76), whereas no significant association with disease control rate or objective response rate was observed.

• Subgroup analyses across different tumor types (small cell lung cancer and non-small cell lung cancer) and treatment modalities (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy) consistently confirmed the prognostic value of HALP score.

What is known and what is new?

• Individual hematologic parameters (hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet) have previously shown prognostic relevance in lung cancer; however, their predictive reliability as isolated markers is limited.

• This is the first meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the predictive value of the composite HALP score across multiple clinical outcomes—including survival, mortality, and cachexia—in lung cancer patients receiving diverse treatment strategies.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The HALP score, derived from routine blood tests, represents a simple, cost-effective, and clinically feasible prognostic tool that integrates nutritional and immune-inflammatory status.

• Clinicians should consider incorporating pretreatment HALP score into routine prognostic assessment to facilitate risk stratification, optimize individualized treatment planning, and enable timely nutritional and supportive interventions for high-risk lung cancer patients.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a major global health burden and continues to account for a substantial proportion of cancer-related deaths worldwide (1). Despite improvements in diagnostic strategies and therapeutic approaches, long-term survival for many patients remains unsatisfactory (2,3). Reliable prognostic evaluation is essential for clinical decision-making, patient stratification, and individualized management. Nevertheless, due to the biological heterogeneity of lung cancer and the influence of various patient- and disease-associated factors, identifying dependable prognostic biomarkers remains challenging.

Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify and validate novel, convenient, and cost-effective biomarkers that can more accurately predict clinical outcomes. Accumulating evidence suggests that systemic inflammation and nutritional status play crucial roles in tumor progression and patient survival. Individual hematologic parameters such as hemoglobin concentration, serum albumin, lymphocyte count, and platelet count have each been linked to oncologic outcomes (4,5). However, reliance on single indicators may not adequately reflect the complex biological interactions underlying cancer progression.

The hemoglobin-albumin-lymphocyte-platelet (HALP) index was proposed as a composite measure integrating nutritional reserve and inflammatory activity (6). By combining these parameters, the index may provide a more comprehensive representation of host-tumor interactions (6,7). Although multiple studies have explored its prognostic value in lung cancer, reported findings have varied across populations and treatment settings.

To clarify these inconsistencies, we performed a meta-analysis and pooled analysis to examine the association between pretreatment HALP levels and survival outcomes, treatment response, and short-term clinical events in patients with lung cancer. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (8) (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0932/rc).

Methods

Literature searching

PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were systematically searched for eligible studies up to April 7, 2025. The following keywords were used: hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, platelet, HALP, lung, pulmonary, tumor, cancer, neoplasm, and carcinoma. The detailed search strategy was: (hemoglobin OR albumin OR lymphocyte OR platelet OR HALP) AND (lung OR pulmonary) AND (tumor OR cancer OR neoplasm OR carcinoma). Both medical subject headings (MeSH) terms and free-text words were applied during the search process.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible: (I) patients diagnosed with lung cancer; (II) HALP score calculated prior to anti-tumor treatment (9); (III) investigations evaluating the association between HALP score and clinical outcomes; and (IV) studies with available full texts published in English.

Reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, animal studies, and duplicated datasets were excluded.

Data extraction

The following information was extracted from eligible studies: first author, year of publication, sample size, country, tumor type [small cell lung cancer (SCLC) or non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)], tumor stage, treatment modality, cutoff value of HALP, study endpoints, hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Primary outcomes included overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Secondary outcomes comprised disease control rate (DCR), objective response rate (ORR), 90-day mortality after treatment, and cachexia.

Methodological quality evaluation

The quality was assessed by Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool and studies with NOS score >5 were defined as high-quality studies (10).

The literature search, selection, data extraction and quality assessment were all performed by two investigators independently.

Statistical analysis

All analysis was conducted using STATA version 17.0 software. Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. If significant heterogeneity was detected representing as I2>50% and/or P<0.10, the random effects model was applied; or the fixed effects model was applied (11). HRs and ORs with 95% CIs were combined to evaluate the relationship of HALP score with primary and secondary outcomes, respectively. Subgroup analysis based on the tumor type (SCLC vs. NSCLC) and treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy vs mixed strategies) was performed. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess stability of pooled analyses. Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test were conducted to detect publication bias. Obvious publication bias was defined as P<0.05 (12,13). If significant publication bias was detected, trim-and-fill method would be used to clarify potentially unpublished studies (14).

Predictive role of HALP score for primary outcomes in lung cancer

Thirteen and nine studies explored the association between HALP score and OS and PFS, respectively. Pooled analysis demonstrated that lower HALP score was related to worse OS (HR =0.60; 95% CI: 0.50–0.73; P<0.001; I2=78.2%, P<0.001) (Figure 2) and PFS (HR =0.52; 95% CI: 0.39–0.69; P<0.001; I2=75.5%, P<0.001) (Figure 3). Then, subgroup analyses based on the type of cancer (SCLC: HR =0.68, 95% CI: 0.47–0.99, P=0.047; NSCLC: HR =0.59, 95% CI: 0.48–0.73, P<0.001) (Figure S1A) and treatment (surgery: HR =0.63, 95% CI: 0.45–0.90, P=0.01; chemotherapy: HR =0.41, 95% CI: 0.24–0.69, P=0.001; immunotherapy: HR =0.62, 95% CI: 0.22–1.77, P=0.38; radiotherapy: HR =0.65, 95% CI: 0.48–0.88, P=0.005; mixed: HR =0.74, 95% CI: 0.66–0.82, P<0.001) (Figure S1B) for OS were performed, as well as the subgroup analysis for PFS (SCLC: HR =0.78, 95% CI: 0.54–1.11, P=0.17; NSCLC: HR =0.48, 95% CI: 0.35–0.67, P<0.001; surgery: HR =0.36, 95% CI: 0.15–0.88, P=0.03; chemotherapy: HR =0.65, 95% CI: 0.46–0.93, P=0.02; immunotherapy: HR =0.39, 95% CI: 0.10–1.54, P=0.18; radiotherapy: HR =0.71, 95% CI: 0.52–0.96, P=0.03; mixed: HR =0.54, 95% CI: 0.33–0.88, P=0.01) (Figure S2A,S2B). Discussion

Our meta-analysis manifested that pretreatment HALP score was significantly related to clinical outcomes in lung cancer and lower HALP score predicted worse survival. Subgroup analysis by tumor type and treatment identified above results. Therefore, pretreatment HALP score might serve as a novel predictor for clinical outcomes in lung cancer and contribute to the risk assessment and clinical management. Although substantial heterogeneity was observed, the consistency of the prognostic effect across different tumor types and treatment modalities supports the robustness and generalizability of HALP.

Importantly, our analysis demonstrated a significant association between low pretreatment HALP score and increased 90-day mortality. This finding is clinically relevant, particularly in the perioperative setting. It is well established that anemia and hypoalbuminemia are independent predictors of adverse short-term outcomes following lung resection. Given that HALP integrates hemoglobin and albumin with immune and inflammatory markers, it may provide a more comprehensive tool for identifying patients at high risk of early postoperative mortality. Therefore, HALP may have practical utility in preoperative risk stratification and perioperative optimization strategies.

The HALP score reflects a combination of nutritional and immunological status as well as systemic inflammation—three key factors known to influence cancer progression and patient survival. Hemoglobin and albumin are indicators of a patient’s nutritional and general health status; low levels are often associated with poor tolerance to therapy and worse outcomes (31). Lymphocyte count serves as a marker of immune competence, and reduced lymphocyte levels may reflect a compromised antitumor immune response (32,33). In contrast, elevated platelet counts are commonly linked to tumor-promoting inflammation and a pro-thrombotic state, both of which are associated with cancer progression and metastasis (34-36). By integrating these four parameters into a single score, HALP provides a more comprehensive evaluation of patients’ overall physiological condition. Compared to any individual marker, HALP demonstrates superior prognostic value by minimizing the limitations of isolated indices and enhancing predictive accuracy. Its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and reliance on routine blood tests also make it highly feasible for widespread clinical application.

While HALP score was strongly associated with survival outcomes and short-term mortality, its association with treatment response parameters such as DCR and ORR was not statistically significant and should be interpreted cautiously. The relationship between HALP and other clinical endpoints remains underexplored. Potentially, HALP may also be closely related to postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, and treatment-related toxicity. Several studies suggest that poor nutritional and immunological status—components reflected in the HALP score—are associated with increased surgical risks and slower postoperative recovery. For instance, a study by Aoyama et al. on gastric cancer patients undergoing gastrectomy reported that a lower HALP score was independently related to higher rates of postoperative complications, suggesting that HALP may serve as a useful tool in predicting perioperative risks in other cancer types (37). Moreover, systemic inflammation and immunosuppression, as indicated by low HALP scores, are known contributors to increased chemotherapy toxicity and reduced tolerance, implying a potential role of HALP in predicting treatment safety and compliance (38,39). Future studies are needed to further investigate these associations in lung cancer populations.

Cancer-associated cachexia is a complex metabolic condition marked by progressive body weight decline, depletion of skeletal muscle mass, and persistent inflammatory activation, which collectively contribute to diminished functional status and poorer survival outcomes. A low HALP score may signal a higher risk for cachexia, as it encompasses several parameters directly involved in its pathophysiology. Hypoalbuminemia reflects poor nutritional status and protein-energy wasting, while anemia (low hemoglobin) is a common feature of cachexia due to chronic inflammation and decreased erythropoiesis (40). Reduced lymphocyte counts suggest immune dysfunction, which may further contribute to catabolic imbalance and tumor-induced metabolic disturbances (41). Additionally, elevated platelet counts are indicative of systemic inflammation, which plays a central role in the development of cachexia through pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (42,43). Taken together, a low HALP score mirrors the systemic metabolic and inflammatory alterations seen in cachectic patients. Given the high prevalence of cachexia in advanced lung cancer, HALP may serve as an early warning indicator, aiding clinicians in timely nutritional and supportive interventions.

There are some limitations in this meta-analysis which should be noted and addressed in future research. First, most studies are from China, which may affect the generality of our conclusion. Second, it is unable to identify the predictive role of pretreatment HALP score for other outcomes such as postoperative complications and adverse events due to the lack of relevant data. Third, the cutoff values of HALP in included studies differ significantly, but it is unable to further determine the optimal cutoff value of HALP in this meta-analysis. Four, significant heterogeneity was observed in the analysis of primary outcomes. However, we failed to identify the source of heterogeneity by the subgroup analysis.

Conclusions

Pretreatment HALP score might serve as a novel predictor for clinical outcomes in lung cancer and a lower HALP predicts worse prognosis. However, more studies are still needed to verify above findings due to the limitations in the meta-analysis.
