Reconstruction of the Flexible IgM Fc Core Using Atomic Force Microscopy Topography and the AFM‐Assembly Pipeline
Authors: Harinderbir Kaur, Andrea J. Pinto, Jean‐Baptiste Reiser, Wai Li Ling, Jean‐Luc Pellequer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunoglobulin M (IgM) is the first class of antibody produced in response to pathogens. It also plays an important role in regulating the immune response, including in autoimmunity. IgM pentamers consist of an Fc core composed of Cμ2, Cμ3, and Cμ4 domains, as well as 10 Fab domains composed of Cμ1 and a variable domain. In this study, we use high‐resolution atomic force microscopy (AFM) topography to constrain the reconstruction of pentameric IgM Fc core pseudo‐atomic model (without Fabs). Reconstruction is performed in two stages using the AFM‐Assembly pipeline. First, we fit the known cryo‐EM structure of the inner core of the IgM Fc (Cμ3‐Cμ4) and the known x‐ray structure of Cμ2 beneath the AFM topographs using the DockAFM tool. Second, the two structural units are assembled to generate a complete pentameric IgM Fc core structure. The experimentally constrained reconstruction of the IgM Fc core reveals a divergence in the assembly of the five Cμ2 monomers: the first and last monomers (M1 and M5) are tightly attached to the inner core, while the remaining monomers (M2–M4) are more loosely bound. This reconstruction describes multiple alternative conformations for the attachment of the Cμ2 domains, suggesting that the reconstruction protocol integrates molecular flexibility present in molecules individually deposited on the AFM substrate.

Introduction

Type‐M immunoglobulins (IgMs) belong to the antibody class with the highest oligomeric states. They are the first antibodies to be produced during immune responses and play critical roles in both innate and adaptive immunity [1]. Beyond their recognition and defensive function against pathogens, secreted IgMs contribute to the regulation of immune responses and are implicated in autoimmune processes through their effector functions [2].

Like other antibodies, IgMs consist of the assembly of two identical heavy (H) chains and two identical light (L) chains covalently linked by disulfide bonds to form each IgM subunit (or “monomer”) (Figure 1A). These “monomers” (H2L2) assemble into pentamers or hexamers, depending on the presence of the accessory polypeptide, the Joining (J) chain [3]. The pentameric form predominates in vivo and forms when the J chain ((H2L2)5J) is present [4], whereas the hexameric form ((H2L2)6) can be found in certain physiological contexts (less than 5% of serum IgMs) or can be recombinantly produced [5].

The functional properties of IgMs in immunity remain an active research field. The secreted pentameric IgMs play important roles in protective immunity against parasitic and viral infections, in mucosal immunity, in certain auto‐immune disorders, in cancers, and in regulation of inflammation. These properties rely on the IgM peculiar structural features including their oligomeric state and their specific abilities to engage receptors such as the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR), the Fcμ receptor (FcμR) found on immune cell surfaces, and soluble C1 complex, the initiator of the complement classical pathway [2].

Historically, IgMs have been represented as a symmetric pentamer exhibiting a fivefold symmetry, as suggested by early electron microscopy (EM) images [6]. This model has long been used for interpreting single‐molecule imaging data, including atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis [7, 8]. However, it has been previously suggested by biochemical and structural evidence that pentameric IgMs likely adopt an asymmetric architecture, in which the J‐chain partially occupies the position of a sixth monomer found in the hexameric IgMs [9]. This asymmetric organization was later confirmed by negative‐stain EM (nsEM) of IgM Fc core [10]. Subsequent cryo‐EM reconstructions refined this view, revealing a relatively flat Fc‐core and confirming the asymmetric distribution of IgM domains, the J‐chain partially filling the gap left by the missing subunit [11].

Several recent cryo‐EM structures have further characterized, at high resolution, the pentameric IgM Fc core in complex with various receptor domains [12, 13, 14, 15, 16] or in a chimeric construct in which the Fab arms were replaced by nanobodies [17]. Although all Fc‐core constructs used for the structural determination included Cμ2 domains, none were able to observe the structured domain, certainly due to its inherent mobility. Indeed, the Cμ2 domain is suspected to replace the flexible hinge region found in other immunoglobulin classes [18]. It is now well established that full‐length IgM molecules can display a range of conformations in solution, from an extended and planar structure of about 30 nm diameter, in which Fab2s and Cμ2s stretched out from the Fc‐core, to a compact bell‐ or dome‐shape structure of 15–16 nm diameter [19]. The recent cryo‐EM structure of a full‐length IgM pentamer also revealed the increased flexibility from the inner core to the IgM peripheral regions, Cμ4 domains having the least degrees of freedom and the Fab highest one. More interestingly, the Cμ2 dimers from the five IgM “monomers” do not present equal mobility, one of them being clearly observable [20]. The dome‐shape structure of IgMs bound to an antigen was revealed by cryo‐electron tomography showing that it consists of the platform composed of Cμ3 and Cμ4 domains with Cμ2 dimers and the Fab arms bent down toward the antigen [21]. Such flexibility likely explains why the Cμ2 domains (and Fabs) of IgMs remain unresolved in most cryo‐EM structures, which rely on averaging large number of particles and therefore tend to blur the dynamic regions.

Our present study aims at obtaining a complete structure of the pentameric IgM Fc‐core, including Cμ2, Cμ3, Cμ4 domains and the C‐terminal piece (Tp), along with the J chain. We applied AFM, a single‐molecule approach that can directly obtain topographical data of molecules deposited on mica [22]. Although AFM lacks atomic resolution, it offers several advantages: imaging under near‐physiological buffer conditions, room temperature operation, and the capacity to visualize individual molecules rather than ensemble averages. These properties make AFM particularly suitable for probing the conformational variability and overall topology of the IgM Fc‐core. Note that in the field of structural biology, it is commonly accepted to use the term “atomic level” to indicate that experimental data led to x, y, z atomic coordinates. Usually, this does not imply that the structural biology technique used can resolve individual atoms in protein structures.

Two main strategies exist for reconstructing molecular assemblies at the atomic level using AFM data [23]: (1) fitting and assembling structural units [24, 25, 26] or (2) transforming three‐dimensional (3D) structures into 2D‐projection images followed by a cross‐correlation approach to compare them with experimental images [27, 28, 29, 30]. Additionally, novel strategies have been developed to improve AFM topography resolution, such as the Laplacian filter [31, 32], the localization‐AFM [33, 34]; and correcting for tip‐sample convolution [35]. In this work, we used the fitting and assembling approach implemented in the AFM‐Assembly pipeline [24, 36]. The process was carried out in two steps. First, using cropped AFM topographic maps of individual IgM Fc core (Cμ2‐Cμ3‐Cμ4‐Tp + J), we fitted the IgM Fc inner core unit (Cμ3‐Cμ4‐Tp), obtained from the cryo‐EM structure (6KXS [11]). Then, we fitted the structure of Cμ2 dimer, obtained by x‐ray crystallography (4JVU [37]) into the same individual cropped AFM topographic maps. Second, we performed a brute‐force combinatorial assembly of five Cμ2 dimers around the pentameric structure of the Fc inner core. The results indicate that the assembled pentameric IgM cores adopt highly dynamical conformations. These results suggest that the AFM topography and reconstruction protocol, such as AFM‐Assembly, can be used to characterize structural flexibility in multidomain, single, isolated protein molecules.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation

Protein samples were prepared according to Pinto et al. [38]. For AFM imaging, 2 μL of 0.12 g/L IgM‐Fc‐core were deposited for 3 min onto freshly cleaved mica (10 mm diameter grade v1 muscovite, NanoAndMore, ref. 50‐D‐10) glued on 12 mm steel puck (Ted Pella Inc., ref. 16,208) using Sader special expert glue (Vibrations and Hautes Températures, Bostik, France). The deposition was followed by two gentle washes (20 μL) with ultra‐pure Milli‐Q water (equipped with a biopak, Merk, Ref EQ 7008) to remove unbound material. Then, 40 μL of PBS buffer solution was added to maintain hydration throughout imaging.

AFM Imaging

AFM was performed in liquid mode using a Bruker Multimode 8 system equipped with a Nanoscope V controller and Nanoscope 9.2 software. Images were recorded in PeakForce Tapping (PFT) mode using silicon nitride cantilevers (ScanAsyst‐FLUID, Bruker AFM Probes, Camarillo, CA, USA), with the following nominal specifications: k = 0.7 N/m, Fq = 150 kHz, a tip radius of 20 nm inserted into the MTFML‐V2 fluid cell (Bruker AFM Probes). Square images were acquired at scan sizes of 500 and 1000 nm with a sampling of 512 pixels (px) with various acquisition conditions: from fully automated PFT to semi‐manual conditions with a maximum applied force of 120 or 200 pN.

Image Processing

The AFM images were corrected using Gwyddion software [39] and the previously described protocol [23]. Briefly, the raw images were corrected using mean‐plane subtraction and row alignment using the median method. The corrected images were then exported as Gwyddion's text files to apply the DeStripe filter [40]. Protein particles were manually cropped from the corrected images using expected geometrical characteristics such as diameter (about 30 nm) and roundness. All cropped particles were interpolated using Gwyddion's Schaum method to obtain an image of 192 × 192 pixels in size, then saved as Gwyddion text files for structural fitting. To enhance visualization of the AFM topography, a Laplacian weight filter was applied [32, 41].

Structural Fitting

The AFM‐Assembly pipeline was used to fit each AFM topography crop independently with the two structural units, as previously defined [23], obtained from the PDB [42]. The first unit was the Fc inner core (Cμ3‐Cμ4‐Tp) structure obtained by cryo‐EM (PDB: 6KXS [11]), which includes the J‐chain but excluded the bound polymeric immunoglobulin receptor P chain. The second unit is the Cμ2 crystal structure (PDB: 4JVU [37]). Both PDB entries contained missing regions that were reconstructed using the “Sequence‐ > Model Loops” tool from ChimeraX [43] with default parameters, modeling only internal missing residues. The missing residues from 6KXS are L445‐L448, K447‐K448, H445‐H446, F445‐F447. The missing residues from 4JVU are A290‐A295, B445‐B452, B490‐B497. Each molecular structure used in AFM Assembly was systematically recentered to the origin before fitting. While our past applications of AFM‐Assembly were performed using all‐atom structures [24, 25, 26, 36, 44], we used here only Cα atomic models for both structural units to facilitate fitting of the large assemblies.

To launch a fitting run, we used the following command: “DockAFM3.sh image.txt structure.pdb 192 2.543 1.0 25 6 15 04 1 200 150 18 > log.log”. “DockAFM3.sh” is the master script that executes computations on multiple processors (usually five), which includes embedded script files and various programs [36]. The image.txt and structure.pdb files are the AFM cropped image and the structural unit to be fitted, respectively. The computation was performed on a grid of 192 [3] cubes to maximize computational efficiency of the program. The grid spacing was 2.543 Å in the X and Y directions, and 1.0 Å in the Z direction. Only topography above a threshold of 25 Å was considered to compute a fitting score (the AFM substrate is at height equal to 0 Å). The favorable layer was 6 Å thick, and the forbidden layer 15 Å thick [36]. For each grid point, the fitting used 232,022 sets of Euler angles, equivalent to a 4° rotation increment. The last four parameters in the input line specify an erosion of the AFM topography, which was generated with a conical tip shape of 200 Å in height, a radius of 150 Å, and a tip‐opening angle of 18°. The output of the script file was saved as a text file named “log.log.” For each fitting, the top 99,999 poses were stored in a so‐called e6d file. Each pose includes a pseudo‐energy score proportional to the number of atoms located within the volume beneath the AFM topography, along with the grid translation and rotation Euler's angles of each fitted structural unit, as defined in the output of the underlying DOT search engine executing the computation [45]. It was thus possible to reconstruct the full atomic coordinates of each structural pose using the initial structure file (structure.pdb) with the information in the e6d file.

Note that the grid spacing in the X and Y directions was equal to the physical dimensions of the sample divided by the number of pixels (192 in this case). Among all crops used, the grid spacing in the X and Y directions ranged from 2.4 to 3.6 Å. The overall grid size must be large enough to contain the AFM topography, bearing in mind that the image is centered in the middle of the computation grid (half the maximum size in the Z direction). Using 192 cubes and a spacing of 1 Å provides a 9.6‐nm available space for AFM data in the z direction. Using five CPUs, the fitting run took about 7 h. Finally, the erosion process used a thinner tip (with a radius of 15 nm) because a previous study revealed degradation in erosion when the tip radius was overestimated [46]. Since the exact size and shape of the tips used in this study are unknown, we took a conservative approach and reduced the expected tip radius.

Structural Assembly of IgM Pentameric Fc Cores

To assemble a full pentameric structure, the “combine” program was used [24]. This efficient brute force approach has been designed to handle the combinatorial explosion in multiple structural units. However, testing all 99,999 poses of the e6d file obtained during the fitting procedure remains computationally unfeasible. The assembly was thus performed in two stages, referred to as “coarse” and “fine” runs. In the coarse run, 12,000 nonsimilar fitting poses were selected for each structural unit (Fc inner core and Cμ2). It has been determined that approximately 12,000 poses of 3497 XYZ coordinates can be simultaneously loaded within the memory of our computer system (approximately 7.6 GB per CPU). The results of the coarse run were stored in a text file named “topscore” that contained a quality score (in arbitrary pixel units) and the three translation and rotation parameters required for reconstructing the assembled structure. The fine run used the topscore output from the coarse run, as well as the initial e6d fitting file to scan all the initial e6d file and to identify poses in the vicinity of the coarse assembly poses among the top 10 coarse topscore. The “combine” program was then run again with all of these clustered fitted poses, storing a maximum of 10,000 assembled structures. As in the fitting procedure, the assembly relied on a master script file and was executed on eight parallel CPUs with the following command:“CombineDocksAFM.sh image.txt 1 6 10 10.0 0 ‐20 12000 5.0 8.0 10.0 8.0 molecule1.pdb molecule1.e6d … molecule6.pdb molecule6.e6d 1 2 4 12 25 34.777 63.834 38.049 ‐17.226 1.019 19.662 … 1 6 4 12 25 30.565 ‐65.985 51.997 ‐17.226 1.019 19.662 tip.tip”.

“CombineDocksAFM.sh image.txt 1 6 10 10.0 0 ‐20 12000 5.0 8.0 10.0 8.0 molecule1.pdb molecule1.e6d … molecule6.pdb molecule6.e6d 1 2 4 12 25 34.777 63.834 38.049 ‐17.226 1.019 19.662 … 1 6 4 12 25 30.565 ‐65.985 51.997 ‐17.226 1.019 19.662 tip.tip”.

The above command reads as follows: use the image.txt AFM topography (identical to that of the fitting procedure), use the erosion procedure as in the fitting procedure (1), there are six structural units (6) and 10 distance constraints (10), consider atom bumping distance of 10 Å (10.0) although a value of 7.0 or 3.0 have also been used, do not allow any bump (0), set the recording combine score threshold (−20), number of fitted poses to use after preprocessing (12,000), exclude similar fitted poses in the “coarse run” if they are within 5 Å in translation (5.0) and 8° in rotation (8.0), include fitted poses in the “fine run” among the top 10 “coarse run” results if they are within 10 Å in translation (10.0) and 8° in rotation (8.0), list all the couples molecule.pdb and molecule.e6d files (6 in total in our case), then list all the requested distance constraints that concern molecule1 (1), Cα of residue 345 of both Cμ3 chains, and molecule2 (2), Cα of residue 337 of Cμ2 of chain A, with a minimum distance of 4 Å (4), an average distance of 12 Å (12), and a maximum distance of 25 Å (25), then provide the XYZ coordinates of the atoms involved in the constraint (30.565–65.985 51.997–17.226 1.019 19.662), finally provide the file containing the shape of the tip used during the fitting procedure (tip. tip).

In the command line, repeated sequences of text for each molecule were omitted here for clarity “…”. In “combine,” distance constraints were used to avoid testing all atom‐atom distances between two units whenever any defined constraint was violated. This constraint has no effect on the final assembly but significantly reduces computational time by avoiding unnecessary mathematical calculations. Note that the XYZ coordinates for the distance constraints must come from the centered PDB structural unit file, not the original PDB. These centered PDB files are found in the output of the fitting procedure. Unlike the fitting procedure, the brute‐force “combine” algorithm loads all structural units into computer memory. Thus, for a complex assembly of six units, only one simultaneous run is possible with 8 CPUs on our in‐house computers. At the end of the “fine run,” a maximum of 10,000 assembled PDB structures was saved and a detailed analysis was performed on the top 1000 or less if 1000 solutions are not reached.

Structural Analysis of Reconstructed IgM Pentameric Fc Cores

Fitted and reconstructed structures are represented in two ways: (1) by their center and (2) by their Cα trace or spherical representation. The center corresponds to the X, Y, and Z translation of the molecular structure in grid coordinates during computation. A single sphere represents a molecular center. Therefore, displaying thousands of spheres provides a visualization of the spatial distribution of 1000 reconstructed molecules. Cα traces or van der Waals (VdW) representations display molecular structures in greater detail. All renderings were generated using VMD [47].

To assess the variability of reconstructed IgM pentamer, several geometric parameters, such as atom‐atom distances, angles between domains, and domain superposition were used. The pentameric Fc core consists of five “monomers” (M1‐M5), each one comprising two chains (1 and 2). The tail piece (Tp) was used to define a “global molecular” center of unitary mass (CoM) based on the single residue at position H566. Table 1 lists the reference residues used in each computation, along with their corresponding position in a consecutive renumbering of complete pentameric structures (renum). This renumbering starts at position 1, which is equivalent to position 345 in IgM sequence numbering. By construction of the “combine” algorithm, the ordering of Cμ2 structures is appended after the Cμ3‐Cμ4‐Tp unit.

To characterize the final reconstruction of pentameric IgM Fc cores, the center of mass (CoM) between the Cμ3‐Cμ4 and the Cμ2 units was computed. To estimate the co‐linearity between the Cμ3‐Cμ4 and Cμ2, the angle defined by the three CoM points (Tp, Cμ3‐Cμ4, Cμ2) was computed for each monomer. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of each monomer of the 1000 reconstructed Fc cores was determined by superimposing each reconstruction onto the top‐ranked reconstruction using the Cμ3‐Cμ4 unit as reference. Then, the RMSD values were computed for each monomer (M1–M5) based on the previous superposition. The linearity and flatness of the reconstructed IgM Fc core were calculated using the formula described previously [48]. A flatness of 0 corresponds to a planar geometry whereas a flatness of 1 indicates a more spherical geometry. A linearity of 0 denotes a straight line, whereas a linearity of 1 designates a circle. All other scripts and programs were developed in‐house and have been partially described elsewhere [49].

Results

Single‐Isolated AFM Topography of IgM Fc Cores With J Chain

Recombinant IgM Fc core pentamers with a J chain (IgM‐Fc‐J) were produced homogeneously [38] and deposited onto freshly cleaved mica for AFM imaging in a buffer solution using PFT mode. Topography was recorded at a sampling resolution of either 1.95 or 0.98 nm/px. High‐resolution topographs (0.98 nm/px) are shown in Figure 2A, others are shown in Figure S1A. A series of topographic cropped regions (“crops”) were extracted from these AFM images, as illustrated in Figures 2B and S1B. The cropped surfaces extracted from the high‐resolution topographs range from 48 × 48 to 64 × 64 pixels. To obtain a suitable grid spacing for the computation, each crop was interpolated to a final image size of 192 × 192 pixels, corresponding to an interpolation of less than four times. This level of interpolation has been previously found to be adequate in our AFM‐Assembly pipeline [23].

Molecular Structures for the Reconstruction

To reconstruct the full IgM Fc core, fitting the constituent structural units was first necessary. Following the definition described previously [23], two structural units were defined: (1) the inner core composed of Cμ3, Cμ4, and Tp, including the J‐chain, as found in the PDB entry 6KXS (Figures 1B and 2) Cμ2 as found in the PDB entry 4JVU (Figure 1C). For completeness, missing residues in both the inner core and Cμ2 were built using the loop‐building tool in ChimeraX, as described in the Methods section. For computational efficiency, only Cα atoms are kept in these structural units. The inner core and each Cμ2 contain 2382 and 223 atoms, respectively.

Fitting Structural Units

The two structural units were fitted within the experimental AFM topography of eight selected IgM Fc core crops (Figures 3A and S2A). The highest available resolution was used for fitting the units with 232,020 rotations, equivalent to a 4° rotation variation. Consequently, each fitting run evaluated approximately 1.6 × 1012 different orientations, requiring about 7 h of computational processing with five Xeon CPUs. Each fitting run generates output data for the top 99,999 poses, ranked based on a pseudo‐energy score. This score counts the number of atoms in each structural unit located in the favorable layer beneath the AFM topography. The pseudo‐energy scores of the top‐ranking fits are listed in Table S1. Figure 3B shows the distribution of the top 1000 poses for both structural units. The centers of the inner core unit are represented by purple spheres and those of Cμ2 in green. Interestingly, the top 1000 poses occupy the entire space at the center of the topography (red color) of the underlying grids in Figure 3B–D. Figure 3C shows the top‐ranked pose of the inner Fc core, with each monomer color‐coded as in Figure 1B. Reassuringly, the top‐ranked pose occupies the near center of the topograph for the four crops shown in Figure 3C.

Assembling Pentameric Fc Cores

Reconstructing a complete IgM Fc core requires connecting five Cμ2 to their respective Cμ3 of the inner core. This assembly was performed using the “combine” software, which applies a brute‐force approach to explore all possible combinations of the six structural units (one inner core and five Cμ2). The reconstruction process involved two steps. First, a filtering algorithm selected configurations (translation and rotation) that were dissimilar from one another, using a minimum translation distance of 5 Å and a rotation angle of 8°. Among these nonsimilar poses, only 12,000 were retained to produce the initial set of “coarse” reconstructions. Second, the top 10 reconstructions from the previous coarse run were used to select now, from the original 99,999 fitted poses, those with a nearby configuration using a maximum distance of 10 Å and a rotation angle of 8°. The reconstruction was repeated, storing the best 10,000 assemblies, in case more than 10,000 assemblies are produced. Ranking was performed using a pseudo‐root‐mean‐square deviation between the reconstructed and experimental topographies.

Figure 3D shows the centers of the best nonredundant assemblies obtained for all reconstructed IgM cores, and Figure 3E shows the top‐ranked reconstruction. The assembly centers revealed a limited number of selected inner cores (green) surrounded by Cμ2 (color‐coded spheres, following the IgM monomer color code in Figure 1B). The geometrical analysis of the reconstructed IgM Fc cores is summarized in Table 2. Surprisingly, the RMS values for all inner cores are relatively low, ranging from 0 to 2.4 Å. This agreement suggests a single predominant orientation of the inner core with marginal translation of a few grid points. It implies that among the top 1000 reconstructed IgM Fc cores, a single major orientation of the inner core dominates, highlighting the specificity of the AFM topographs. Geometrically, the collinear alignments of all Cμ2 along their respective IgM monomers showed minor variation, averaging 8.5° ± 3.4°. This is consistent with the global flatness of all reconstructed IgM Fc cores across the tested topographs, with an average flatness value of 0.13 ± 0.01 (Table 2). As expected, the linearity of the reconstructions averaged 0.75 ± 0.05, consistent with an incomplete circular shape of the reconstructed molecules.

The average distance between the CoM of all Cμ2 and the CoM of inner cores is about 58 Å. However, a variation was observed in the reconstructed IgM Fc cores. First, the distances to the inner core of Cμ2 located at IgM M1 and M5 are shorter (53–55 Å) compared to those located to the other IgM monomers M2, M3, and M4 (60–61 Å) (Table 2). Second, Cμ2 appeared to have differential variabilities in their positioning. Indeed, the standard deviation of their CoM distances to the inner core of reconstructed Cμ2 of M3 is the lowest (3 Å), while those of M1 and M5 the highest (6–8 Å) (Table 2). Analysis and superimposition of these reconstructions showed a limited but heterogeneous number of unique Cμ2 conformations, ranging from only 2 to 9 for M3, the least variable dimer, up to 48 or 62 for M1 and M5, which were the most mobile (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the feasibility of using the AFM‐Assembly reconstruction pipeline to model a pentameric IgM Fc core structure at the atomic level. AFM topography was used as an experimental constraint. The IgM Fc core comprises the immunoglobulin domains Cμ2‐Cμ3‐Cμ4 and Tp part, with the associated J chain included. The reconstruction required integrating two distinct structural components beneath the experimental topography: the inner Fc core, comprising Cμ3‐Cμ4‐Tp and the J‐chain, and the smaller Cμ2 domain. For each selected topography data set, the pipeline generated a series of reconstructions of the pentamer structure. This reconstruction of the pentameric IgM Fc cores presented numerous challenges and limitations, which are addressed in the following sections.

The first significant challenge in reconstructing macromolecular structures at the atomic level pertains to the resolution of experimental data such as AFM topography. Despite the absence of a prevailing consensus on the structural resolution of AFM topography, it is generally estimated to be approximately 1 nm in lateral and 1 Å in vertical dimensions [50]. However, experimental data are always acquired at a specified sampling resolution in the nanometer‐per‐pixel range. In this study, the pentameric IgM Fc core was imaged at two sampling resolutions: Crops labeled “a‐d” (see Figure 2B) were imaged at a resolution of 0.98 nm/px, while the crops designated “e‐h” (see Figure S1B) were imaged at a resolution of 1.95 nm/px. Geometrical analyses of the reconstructed structures revealed variability within both categories of topographs (see Table 2). Although the reconstruction of pentameric IgM Fc cores has been successful in all data, some reconstruction parameters were incompatible with pentameric assemblies. In particular, the atom bumping distance used to successfully obtain a pentameric assembly required significant adjustments (from 10 down to 3 Å). The reconstruction of crop “h” proved to be the most significant challenge, with a maximum atom bumping distance of only 3 Å. Nonetheless, the absence of a systematic trend indicates that a sampling resolution of up to 2 nm/px was sufficient to reconstruct IgM Fc pentamers for the eight cropped data sets that were tested.

A second notable challenge in reconstructing the pentameric IgM Fc molecule is the monobloc shape of the AFM topography, which lacks distinction between each monomer. The two structural units used in this study differ considerably in size. The inner Fc core comprises 2382 Cα atoms, whereas the Cμ2 comprises only 223. Consequently, for such a monobloc shape, it translates into a narrow range of fitting scores for the top‐ranked poses. This is particularly concerning for the majority of Cμ2 fitting poses spread across the entire volume located beneath the topography, as this is unrealistic based on the pentameric structure of the IgM Fc core.

Consequently, more fitting poses must be examined down the scoring list than is usually required for other systems, where 2000 poses were usually sufficient [24, 25, 26]. Indeed, most of the reconstructed pentameric structures are constructed using poses that are assigned low rankings during the initial fitting step as illustrated for the assembly of the IgM Fc core from crop “d” (see Table S2). It shows that the two inner core structures were ranked 6989 and 7662, respectively. Conversely, the top‐ranked Cμ2 attained a rank of 267 for monomer M5, while the lowest‐ranked Cμ2 was of 99,948, also for monomer M5. In this work, we stored a maximum of 99,999 poses per fitting step.

The analysis of thousands of reconstructed IgM Fc cores for each AFM topography provides insights into the structural positioning of Cμ2 dimers within the pentamer. Figure 4A illustrates the variability in the distribution of Cμ2 of the crop “d.” Variability is observed at multiple levels; for instance, the Cμ2 domains of M1 and M2 in crop “d” are colocalized near a single chain of Cμ3, whereas the others are most symmetrically positioned in the middle of the two chains of each monomer. Although these results concern a single crop, a similar variability pattern could be observed on all crops (Figure S3A–H); however, without any detectable consistencies. At a global level, we observe higher variability in monomers M1 and M5 (Table 3), illustrated by the higher number of alternative conformations of Cμ2 in all the computed assemblies.

It should be clear that variations in the orientation of Cμ2 dimers in this work represent a suboptimal distribution of IgM Fc core structures (Figure 4B). All the suboptimal distribution of Cμ2 is compatible with the experimental AFM topography. Although a scoring function ranks all reconstructions, this function is too crude (a RMSD in pixel units) to establish a probabilistic relationship between all computed assemblies. Further work awaits in this topic. This study used only eight AFM topography crops, or eight single IgM Fc core molecules, but considering their pentameric organization, this work counts 40 different monomer reconstructions.

As illustrated in Figure 4C, the superimposition of reconstructed Cμ2 domains, for the four high‐resolution topographies, forms a 3D probability distribution. The observed variability among the four crops is likely caused by the mobility of these domains when deposited on the mica surface. Although AFM imaging in buffer solution allows a certain molecular mobility on the mica surface, as illustrated by the presence of partially broken molecular surfaces (see Figures 2A and S1), mobility during imaging is unlikely the origin of the observed reconstruction variability. Nevertheless, the substantial structural variability of monomers M1 and M5 suggests that monomers M2‐4 located at the center of the IgM Fc core are more firmly bound to the mica surface compared to M1 and M5.

Although several IgM Fc core structures have been determined by cryo‐EM, none of these studies accurately resolved the position of all the Cμ2 domains [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. This observation supports the substantial conformational flexibility between Cμ2 and Cμ3. The sole exception is the structure reported by Chen et al. [20], in which a single Cμ2 of monomer 1 of a full‐length IgM molecule was reconstructed with reasonable confidence. We overlaid the final reconstruction of crop “c” onto the low‐threshold contoured structure of Chen et al. [20] to reveal the density of all Cμ2 regions. The result is shown in Figure 4D. At this contouring level, only blobs of density are observed. Nevertheless, there is reasonable agreement between the distribution of our assemblies and the blobs of cryoEM densities depicted in the cyan mesh in Figure 4D.

In summary, the AFM assembly reconstruction of IgM Fc cores provides an alternative set of conformations for this molecule, which is known for its flexibility. This approach will be particularly valuable for reconstructing more complex molecules, such as full pentameric IgM with their 10 Fabs, using AFM topography.

Conclusions

In this study, we used a combination of AFM topography and the AFM‐Assembly pipeline to reconstruct the pentameric structures of IgM Fc cores. These structures illustrate potential alternative orientations of the Cμ2‐Cμ3 connection. Of particular significance is the finding that these alternative conformations were deduced from the experimental constraints of the AFM data. The conformational variability observed in our reconstruction may result from favorable convolution between the low resolution of AFM topography (including the tip effect) and the resolution of Cα‐level atomic reconstruction in the assembly process. The dynamic view of the reconstructed IgM Fc core presented in this study contrasts with cryo‐EM structures, which infer conformational variability through the absence of clearly defined densities. This work presents a novel perspective on the AFM‐Assembly pipeline's capability to provide a pseudo‐dynamic representation of potential flexible molecular arrangements.
