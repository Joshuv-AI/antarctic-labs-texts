Case report - giant duodenal ulcer with Candida infection in a relatively healthy child

Introduction

Candida can theoretically infect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, the esophagus is by far the most commonly affected site, with infections of the stomach, small intestine, or large bowel being much rarer. Candida infections of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract are more commonly observed in immunocompromised individuals, such as those with Human Immunodeficiency Virus/Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (HIV/AIDS), cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplant recipients, or individuals taking immunosuppressive medications [1]. The diagnosis of gastrointestinal candidiasis can be challenging. Candida is part of the normal fungal flora in the GI tract, so its presence in samples like sputum or stool doesn’t necessarily indicate an infection. Instead, it might simply reflect colonization, meaning the yeast is present but not causing disease [2]. The confluent growth of Candida in cultures from gastric aspirates or ulcer edge biopsies can be more sensitive for diagnosing peptic ulcer-associated candidiasis than histological examination alone. However, histopathology remains crucial for confirming tissue invasion and the nature of the infection. The presence of specific features under the microscope—fungal spores, pseudo-hyphae, polymorphonuclear leukocytes, and an eosinophilic background—provides strong evidence of an active Candida infection in the GI tract, distinguishing it from simple colonization [3].

In contrast, Candida infections in immunocompetent individuals—those with a normally functioning immune system—are rare. When Candida infections do occur in immunocompetent individuals, they are often associated with specific risk factors, such as recent antibiotic use, diabetes, or other underlying conditions that may transiently weaken the body’s defenses. Duodenal candidiasis in an immunocompetent individual, especially in a child, is exceptionally uncommon [4]. We thus report a rare and noteworthy case of duodenal candidiasis to document and share with the medical community, as it can provide valuable insights into the potential factors or conditions that might predispose otherwise healthy individuals to such infections.

Case report

We report the case of a previously healthy 10-year-7-month-old boy who presents with a two-year history of intermittent, dull right upper abdominal pain, occasionally accompanied by nausea. The pain has recently begun to awaken him from sleep and is unrelated to meals. Over the preceding 3–6 months, he becomes noticeably pale, with reduced appetite and an unintentional weight loss of approximately 6 kg over three months. He denies fever, hematemesis, diarrhea, melena, or dark urine. His diet is unrestricted. Prior abdominal ultrasonography performed at another institution is unremarkable, and no further evaluation is undertaken until symptom progression prompts presentation to our hospital.

On physical examination, the patient exhibited pale conjunctivae and significant tenderness in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen, rated 9 out of 10 on the numerical pain scale. The Murphy’s sign was negative. The laboratory data were presented in Table 1. Immunoglobulins (G, A, M, E), C3, C4, and antinuclear antibody (ANA) were within normal ranges, and the HIV Ag/Ab test was negative. The stool occult blood test was negative.

This finding was corroborated by a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen (Fig. 2), which demonstrated a thickened duodenal wall measuring 5 cm in length and peri-duodenal fluid collection, consistent with acute duodenitis. Biopsy specimens collected from the margin of the duodenal ulcer showed the presence of Candida blastospores when stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Fig. 4a and b). An eosinophil count of 20–30 per high power field (Fig. 4c) and CD4-predominant lymphocytes (Fig. 4d and e) were observed in the submucosal layer of the duodenum. The histopathological image lacked the classic features of tuberculosis, such as caseation necrosis or granulomatous inflammation, making TB infection less likely. Immunohistochemistry staining for Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), as well as Giemsa staining for Helicobacter pylori, were all negative. Additionally, no fungal blastospores were found in the normal tissue of the duodenum, and blood culture did not show the growth of Candida albicans.

Treatment was initiated with a combination of 3 months of oral lansoprazole and a 6-week course of oral fluconazole at a dose of 50 milligrams twice daily. The patient responded well, showing significant improvement in symptoms. He reported a significant decrease in the frequency and intensity of abdominal pain, an improved appetite, and a gradual increase in body weight. The treatment was well-tolerated, with no adverse effects observed. The follow-up laboratory data were presented in Table 2.

A follow-up fiberoptic endoscopy at the end of treatment showed complete resolution of the duodenal lesion, and no Candida blastospores were detected in histological specimens at the 3-month follow-up. Evaluation of potential predisposing factors for the giant duodenal ulcer and fungal infection in this otherwise healthy patient is ongoing, with findings to be reported in a future case study.

Discussion

Candida species are normal inhabitants of the GI tract in many individuals, where they are part of the normal microbiota and typically coexist without causing harm. However, under certain conditions, Candida can become pathogenic and lead to disease. Contributing factors include immunocompromised status, chronic use of drugs that cause intestinal dysbiosis, mucosal damage, and chronic stress, among others [5]. Severe complications from Candida infections, such as spontaneous gastric perforation, though rare, can occur, including in vulnerable populations like small infants [6, 7]. Invasive candidiasis causing duodenal perforation has also been reported, though it is even rarer [8].

A giant duodenal ulcer (GDU) is a variant of peptic ulcer disease, characterized by an ulcer that is 2 cm or larger, extends through the full thickness of the duodenal wall, and occupies a substantial portion of the duodenal bulb. Historically, GDU is believed to require surgical intervention; however, the introduction of proton pump inhibitors has provided an effective medical treatment option. Fischer et al. concluded that omeprazole is effective in treating GDU. Most patients treated with a 12-week course of omeprazole have successfully healed their ulcers within 6 weeks and have experienced no recurrences [9]. Given the rarity of gastroduodenal candidiasis, standardized treatment guidelines have not been established. Therefore, our treatment strategy was extrapolated from adult treatment recommendations for esophageal candidiasis. Current clinical guidelines recommend systemic antifungal therapy with oral fluconazole at a dose of 100–200 mg daily (pediatric dose: 6–12 mg/kg/day) for 14–21 days. Treatment duration may be extended based on persistent clinical symptoms or pathological findings during follow-up [10, 11]. In our patient, fluconazole therapy was prolonged to a total of six weeks due to ongoing abdominal discomfort during outpatient follow-up, in conjunction with a three-month course of oral lansoprazole. No adverse effects were observed.

Ghosal et al. found that up to 47% of patients with peptic ulcers were colonized by Candida, compared to 15% of those with non-ulcer dyspepsia [12]. They also noted that larger ulcers greater than 2 cm were more frequently colonized by Candida (75%) compared to smaller ulcers (43%). They concluded that Candida colonization is significantly higher in peptic ulcers than in non-ulcer dyspepsia. Additionally, Wilcox et al. suggested that the majority of ulceration associated with Candida is likely due to viral coinfection, which may present histologic evidence of ulcerations, the extent of candidiasis, and viral cytopathic effects [13].

Accurate diagnosis of Candida infections often involves endoscopy and biopsy, with special stains or cultures used to confirm the presence of Candida. In cases of Candida infection in the small intestine, endoscopic findings can present a range of appearances, such as thrush, nodular formations, or ulcerations, which may complicate diagnosis [11]. Diagnosing candidiasis through histopathology can be challenging, particularly if the Candida filamentous forms (such as hyphae or pseudo-hyphae) are not visible or have been sloughed away. Specific histopathological features can aid in diagnosing Candida esophagitis, especially when distinguishing it from other types of esophagitis. Isabella et al. reported that the presence of certain immune cell patterns, such as superficial band-like neutrophils and increased peripapillary lymphocytes or CD4-predominant lymphocytes, may be indicative of Candida etiology [14].

In our case, a solitary ulcer measuring approximately 2 centimeters in diameter with irregular margins was identified in the second portion of the duodenum. Neither endoscopic brushing for fungal culture nor culture of the duodenal ulcer tissue was performed, as there was no strong initial clinical suspicion of fungal infection at the time of endoscopy. However, histopathological examination incidentally revealed abundant Candida blastospores within the necrotic debris of the ulcerated mucosa, accompanied by neutrophil and eosinophil infiltration. The predominance of CD4-positive lymphocytes further suggested an active immune response consistent with Candida infection, even in the absence of pseudo-hyphae formation. Importantly, Candida blastospores were not identified in the adjacent normal duodenal tissue (Fig. 4a), indicating that the organism was not merely colonizing the mucosa but was instead specifically associated with—and likely contributory to—the formation of the giant duodenal ulcer. Complete resolution of the duodenal lesion on follow-up endoscopy, together with the absence of Candida blastospores or pseudo-hyphae on post-treatment histology after completion of fluconazole therapy, further supports the diagnosis of Candida-associated duodenal ulceration.

Conclusion

In this case, we encountered a rare instance of a giant duodenal ulcer complicated by Candida infection in a relatively healthy individual. Although pathological images of fungal infection in the gut can be non-specific, it is important to consider fungal infection as a potential cause of chronic or unexplained duodenal ulcers in children, even in the absence of immunosuppression. Early detection and treatment of fungal infections are crucial to prevent invasive candidiasis, which can lead to high mortality rates, particularly in children.
