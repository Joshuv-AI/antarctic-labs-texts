Treatment adherence, social support and coping mechanisms among patients with drug-sensitive tuberculosis: a cross-sectional study in two directly-observed therapy centres in southwest Nigeria
Authors: Rasaq Adisa, Deborah Mafolayomi Durojaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate medication adherence, available social support and types of accessible support among patients with drug-sensitive tuberculosis (DS-TB). Coping mechanism(s) in TB management and associations between accessible support(s) and medication adherence were also explored.DesignProspective questionnaire-guided cross-sectional study.SettingsThe WHO-certified TB directly observed therapy (DOT) clinic of two tertiary hospitals in Ibadan, southwest Nigeria.ParticipantsAdult patients with DS-TB attending the TB-DOT clinic of the hospitals.Outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were level of adherence to TB regimens, available social support and type(s) of accessible support. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is currently considered the world’s leading cause of death from a single infectious agent, following the COVID-19 disease outbreak in 2020 to 2021, and caused almost twice as many deaths as HIV/AIDS.1,3 Globally, in 2023, an estimated 10.8 million people fell ill with TB, a further increase from 10.7 million in 2022 and 10.4 million in 2021.2 3 In addition, the TB incidence rate is estimated to have increased by 4.6% between 2020 and 20234 5 from 129 in 2020 to 134 in 2023 following a decline of 2% per year between 2010 and 2020.2 4 6 The net reduction in the global number of deaths caused by TB between 2015 and 2023 was 23%, with the biggest reduction recorded in the WHO African region, with a 42% decline by 2023.2 7 These reductions are still far from 2025 WHO targets of the End-TB strategy milestone of a 75% reduction in the number of deaths caused by TB, and a 55% reduction in the TB incidence rate.2 7 8 In the WHO African region, Nigeria and the Democratic Republic of Congo are among the eight countries that accounted for about two-thirds of the global number of people estimated to have developed TB in 2023.2 7 9 10 Generally, Nigeria has taken some significant steps towards meeting the End-TB targets and is aiming at reducing the TB incidence rate by 50% and TB mortality by 75% on or before 2030.11 12 However, there is still much to be done by concerned stakeholders in TB prevention and care in order to meet the targets.

Although TB is curable if treatment is commenced quickly, appropriately and uninterrupted throughout the 6–9 months course of treatment with the WHO recommended anti-TB medications.2 13 However, non-adherence to TB regimen is one of the major challenges in TB successful prevention and care, with varied factors preventing people from adhering to treatment recommendations and successfully completing treatment regimen.2 14 15 Among those factors are mental, socioeconomic, drug side effects and lack of access to quality healthcare facilities.16,20 Consequently, TB typically results in a major depreciation in patients’ health-related quality of life, including mental, physical, social, economic and other components, which are all integrated into the quality of life of an individual.21 It has therefore become universally recognised that the care and support required to live with TB is enormous, thus, services must be redesigned to allow patients to drive the care planning process.22,24

In TB management, the high number of persons lost to follow-up, treatment interruption and therapy non-adherence significantly contribute to poor treatment outcomes among the patients.13 25 26 The global TB treatment success rate for people with DS-TB treated with first-line regimens was 88% in 2022.2 7 In Nigeria, reports show that the TB treatment success rate is around 75.3%,13 25 and while there is an improvement from previous years, it still falls short of the WHO recommended target of 85%.13 25 Generally, adherence to treatment among people with TB is challenging given the complexity, modest tolerability and long duration of treatment regimens currently available for TB management.27 28 Thus, in TB prevention and care, an individualised and pragmatic approach is necessary to improve adherence to treatment regimen,29 with social support being identified as a factor in the overall management of patients with TB, especially in developing countries where there is a preponderance of socioeconomic problems.29,31

WHO has defined social support as the amount of perceived and practical care received from family, friends and/or the community.32 Social support may have a direct effect on a patient’s well-being, while knowledge of other opinions of one’s self may motivate one to recover and, as a result, improve their adherence to the treatment plan, with subsequent improvement in treatment outcomes.33,35 Studies have been conducted by WHO to evaluate the relationship between care and support in TB and possible treatment outcomes.2 14 15 However, those studies still left gaps that emphasise the need for continuous evaluation of impact of social support and coping mechanism(s) among patients with TB on medication adherence leading to better treatment outcomes.14 15 36

Although the directly observed therapy short-course (DOTs) strategy has largely contributed to improving TB prevention and care,37,39 continuous patient adherence to TB therapy, as well as full understanding of the factors that prevent patients from taking the prescribed medicines correctly and those that help them complete their treatment is highly essential for ensuring sustained improvement in TB treatment outcome.17 40 41 This study therefore, aimed to comprehensively assess adherence to TB regimens among DS-TB patients attending WHO-certified TB directly observed therapy (TB-DOT) clinic of two hospitals using a validated comprehensive medication adherence assessment scale 12-item (CMAAS-12).42 The social support system available for TB management, the specific type(s) of support received by the patients from the available source(s) and the preferred type(s) of support were explored. Coping mechanism(s) adopted by the patients in managing challenges encountered during TB management were also evaluated, while associations between accessible type(s) of support and medication adherence were subsequently investigated.

Method

Study design

This study was a prospective cross-sectional questionnaire-guided interaction with drug-sensitive tuberculosis (DS-TB) patients in two WHO-certified TB-DOT clinics for eight consecutive weeks (8 January 2024–8 March 2024).

Study site and description

The TB-DOT clinics of University College Hospital (UCH) and Adeoyo Maternity Teaching Hospital (AMTH) are both located in Ibadan, Oyo state. The UCH is a 1000-bed tertiary teaching hospital, while AMTH is a 500-bed tertiary healthcare institution that caters to the healthcare needs of ambulatory and institutionalised patients within and outside the region. Both hospitals are WHO-certified TB-DOT centres of excellence with fully equipped and functional DOT clinics for the management of different categories of TB, including DS-TB and multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) patients.

Study population

Adult outpatients with DS-TB attending the TB-DOT clinic of the two hospitals.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult outpatients with DS-TB who were on DOT short-course for not more than 6 months. Patients must have also given informed consent to participate in the study. Excluded were patients with DS-TB who were on DOTs for greater than 6 months and those who were booked for inpatient admission, as well as patients with MDR-TB.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was determined using Taro Yamane’s formula.43 The Yamane’s formula is used to determine the appropriate sample size when dealing with a finite population size (N), and a known margin of error (e) at a desired confidence level, that is, n=N ÷ (1+N(e)),2 where n=required sample size, N=estimated population size, e=margin of error (taken as 0.05).43 The formula takes into account a finite population correction, as well as assumptions of a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, and conservatively, the formula assumes that the proportion being estimated is 0.5. In our study, data collection was concurrently carried out in both hospitals for 3 days per week, specifically the major TB-DOT clinic on Mondays in AMTH, as well as the main TB-DOT clinic on Tuesdays and Thursdays in UCH. Available data from the health record unit of UCH indicated that eligible patients with DS-TB who regularly attended the TB-DOT clinic on the identified days per week were between 15 and 16, while an average of 6 to 7 attended the TB-DOT clinic on Mondays in AMTH. This gave a total estimated population size of 125 and 55 from UCH and AMTH, respectively, for the 8 weeks study period.

The proportionate sample size calculation to guide participants’ enrolment from each hospital TB-DOT centre was determined as follows:

For UCH, n=125÷(1+125 (0.05)2)=95, adjustment for 20% to account for the possible non-response rate translates to 95÷0.8=118.7 rounded to 110. For AMTH, n=55÷(1+55 (0.05)2)=48, adjustment for 20% non-response rate gave 48÷0.8=60. Thus, a total of 170 was obtained as target sample size for both hospitals.

Instrument for data collection

A pretested semi-structured questionnaire was the main instrument of the study. The questionnaire was designed and developed by the investigators after extensive review of relevant literature as well as their previous experience and expertise on adherence-related studies in chronic diseases.3142 44,46 The questionnaire was divided into five sections. Section A gathered sociodemographic and patient-specific data including, age, gender, educational level, marital status, occupation, average income per month, DOT clinic attended and duration on TB-DOT regimens. Section B assessed medication and disease-related information. Section C evaluated available and the most accessible social support for TB management as well as the preferred type(s) of social support by the patients. Section D assessed patients’ medication adherence using validated CMAAS-12.42 Section E evaluated the coping mechanisms adopted by the patients in TB management.

Validation of data collection instrument

The questionnaire was assessed for content validity by five lecturers from the department of Clinical Pharmacy and Pharmacy Administration, University of Ibadan, Nigeria, and a consultant pulmonologist from UCH, so as to ensure appropriateness and synergy between the question items and the study objectives. Subsequently, the questionnaire was pretested among ten patients with MDR-TB from UCH in order to ascertain the ease of comprehension of the item-statement in the questionnaire. Feedback from the content validation and pretest led to a few modifications to the questionnaire including the rephrasing of some ambiguous statements for clarity, as well as inclusion of relevant prompts for some question items so as to stimulate participants’ thoughts on the respective question. The final version was thereafter used for data collection.

Data collection procedure and sampling technique

In each hospital, eligible patients were courteously approached for participation in the study while they were waiting for their turn of directly observed TB medication intake from the attending health personnel. Prior to the administration of the questionnaire to individual participants, the objectives and significance of the study were clearly explained to each participant. They were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their response, while they were also informed that their participation is entirely voluntary. Both the questionnaire and informed consent form were translated into Yoruba, especially for those who did not understand English. An informed consent form was read to each participant before obtaining individual consent for participation. The consent form clearly stated the benefits and risks of involvement in the study. No infringement on patients’ privacy in the questionnaire and no burden was envisaged from participation in the study, except the 15 to 20 min taken to respond to the questions.

Participants were consecutively enrolled based on the study eligibility criteria, with the enrolment carried out on the identified major TB-DOT clinic day(s) in each hospital. An average of 7 to 8 eligible patients was usually enrolled and administered the questionnaire on the chosen data collection day in either of the hospitals. The data collection was continuous for the 8 weeks study period in the two hospitals until the target number of participants from each hospital was achieved. The questionnaire was interviewer-administered to each participant after verbal informed consent was individually obtained. Also, the first item-statement on the questionnaire duly captured consent information via a Yes/No response option. Our research was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement (STROBE).47 See online supplemental file 1. The questionnaire for each participant was appropriately coded to avoid duplication of data within the study period.

Patient and public involvement

Patients, and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting and dissemination plans of the study.

Data analysis

The data collected were sorted, coded and entered into SPSS V.27.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage, were used to evaluate the distribution of responses and summarise the data. In this study, binary categorisation of CMAAS-12 scores as adapted from one of our previous studies40 was used for assessment of medication adherence. A CMAAS-12 score ≥8 indicates ‘optimal adherence’ while a CMAAS-12 score <8 suggests ‘suboptimal adherence’.42 Pearson χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as may be appropriate, was used to evaluate associations between relevant sociodemographic variables, as well as the specific types of support received from the different source(s) and medication adherence. Analysis was done putting into consideration the relevant assumptions employed in the sample size determination, specifically with respect to adjusting to 95% CI and 5% margin of error, as well as a finite estimated sample size population of 170. The 2-sided p values were appropriately retrieved for the compared variables.

Results

Sociodemographic and patient-specific characteristics

A total of 170 patients with DS-TB participated in the study, comprising 110 (64.7%) from UCH and 60 (35.3%) from AMTH. All the 170 participants enrolled in the study consented to participation and responded to the questionnaire, giving a response rate of 100%. Twelve (7.1%) were within 18–24 years, 41 (24.1%) between 25 and 39 years, 48 (28.2%) within 40–54 years, 51 (30.0%) in the age range of 55–69 years, while 18 (10.5%) had ≥70 years. Male participants constituted 92 (54.1%), and females were 78 (45.9%). One hundred and eighteen (69.4%) were married, while 161 (94.7%) had at least a primary school education. A total of 103 (60.6%) had immediate family size >5. Most of the participants, 148 (87.1%) earned above ₦50 000 (32 to 129 USD) monthly. All the participants were on first-line anti-TB therapy. A total of 116 (68.2%) had been on DOTs regimen for ≤3 months, while 54 (31.8%) were on DOTs between 4 and 6 months. Assessment of medication adherence with comprehensive medication adherence assessment scale-12 (CMAAS-12)

All but one of the participants (169; 99.4%) knew the purpose for each prescribed medicine as anti-TB regimen. Most (168; 98.8%) reported to obtain the medication information from all the healthcare providers they were in contact with during clinic attendance. Twenty-nine (17.1%) occasionally skipped taking medication(s) for reasons other than forgetting, which largely included a tight work schedule (21; 72.4%) among others (table 2). A total of 167 (98.2%) could identify each of the prescribed TB regimens, mostly by name written on the dispensing label, 142 (52.4%). Thirty-nine (29.9%) reported taking other medications together with the prescribed TB medications. A total of 165 (95.9%) continue taking the prescribed TB regimen(s) irrespective of the medication side effects or any other discomfort with the TB regimen. Overall, evaluation of CMAAS-12 indicated that 152 (89.4%) had a score ≥8 suggesting optimal medication adherence, while 18 (10.6%) had a score <8,indicating suboptimal adherence. Challenges encountered with TB management and the adopted coping mechanism(s) by patients

Table 5 shows the summary of challenges encountered by patients in TB management and the adopted coping mechanisms. Difficulty in swallowing TB medication(s) was reported as the most common challenge (113; 66.5%), followed by too many medications to be taken at once (64; 37.6%). The most common coping mechanism typically employed by the patients in resolving challenges in TB management was self-encouragement to continue taking the medication(s) as prescribed (124; 72.9%). Seeking emotional support from family or friends (164; 96.5%) was also cited as a coping mechanism, and this was reported by a large number of participants (159; 93.5%) as the most effective to deal with the TB management challenges. A total of 141 (82.9%) engaged in some lifestyle changes as coping strategies for handling TB medication-taking challenge(s), out of which, setting alarm as medication reminder (108; 76.6%) and change in daily routines (72; 51.1%) were largely reported (table 5).

Challenge faced during tuberculosis management and the coping mechanism adopted by participants (n=170)

Challenge of transportation costs (118; 69.4%) was also reported as a major hindrance to regular attendance at the TB-DOT clinic, and as a coping mechanism, 81 (68.6%) each usually engaged in trekking a bit of distance before boarding a cab/taxi, as well as borrowed money from family or friends. All the participants reported that the TB medications are free in their respective DOT clinic. Ten (5.9%) of the participants engaged in concomitant herb usage, of which 4 (40.0%) took Vernonia amygdalina (Local name—Ewuro) extract, 3 (30.0%) used Ocimum gratissimum (Local name—Efinrin) extract, and 3 (30.0%) took herbal mixture of unidentified name.

There was no association between the patients’ educational qualification and medication adherence (p=0.21). Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively evaluate adherence to TB medication(s), available support system and the most accessible type(s) of support in TB management among patients with DS-TB. We also evaluate the coping mechanism(s) adopted by patients in managing the challenges of TB management. Exploring all these aspects in TB management is essential since TB is a chronic disease with enormous burden that affects the mental, physical and socioeconomic well-being of the patients.21,24 In our study, the use of CMAAS-12, a validated adherence scale, does not only assess medication adherence but simultaneously explores the different domains of non-adherence behaviours to reveal the intentional and unintentional barriers to adherence. With the patients’ response to CMAAS-12 question items, nearly 99% knew the purpose for the prescribed medications as a TB management regimen. This is highly commendable because knowing the purpose for the prescribed regimen is the first step toward ensuring optimal commitment to medication-taking by patients. Healthcare providers, especially pharmacists, should therefore take cognisance of this preliminary domain of non-adherence behaviour during medication adherence evaluation among patients with chronic diseases generally.

Overall, more than three-quarters (precisely 89.4%) of the DS-TB patients had optimal TB medication adherence. This is largely encouraging and this adherence rate may perhaps be considered in line with previous adherence assessment studies among TB patients. For instance, previous adherence assessment to TB medication was estimated to be as low as 40% in some developing countries, including Nigeria.36 48 Also, the overall medication adherence rate to anti-TB treatment was reported as 50% in India,49 84.5% in Thailand17 and an average of 75.6% from the studies in South Ethiopia.18 19 However, it should be noted that the reported medication adherence rates across the different studies are largely dependent on factors such as the study settings and participants’ characteristics, type and content of adherence measuring scales, as well as differences in the study scope and design. The recorded higher number of patients with optimal TB medication adherence in our study may partly be envisaged on account of the fact that the two study sites are well-known WHO certified TB-DOT centres of excellence in the region, with regular direct supervision and focused counselling for patients initiated on TB-DOT regimens, thus, a greater likelihood of increased patients’ commitment to medication-taking. Nonetheless, an adherence rate of 100% should be expected of the patients on DOTs, which is specifically one of the fundamental reasons for the widespread adoption and implementation of TB-DOTs strategy in many developed and developing countries.14 15

Interestingly, in our study, all the participants acknowledged that the TB medications are provided free in the respective TB-DOT clinic, while medication-related information is usually provided by all healthcare providers they were in contact with during TB-DOT clinic attendance. This is quite commendable, and healthcare providers should continue to engage the patients more in this regard. Patient-provider’s communication in a non-threatening and non-judgemental manner could improve patients’ understanding and commitment to prescribed TB regimens.31 45 46 50

It is worthy of note to mention that all the participants had access to one form of social support or the other. Above 90% mentioned family and healthcare providers’ support as the most available source of support for TB management and care, approximately 70% cited government and NGO, while about one-half and slightly above one-third, respectively, cited religious association and friends. Overall, the most accessible type(s) of support from each of the available sources of support varies. One hundred, and approximately eighty-two per cent respectively cited emotional and financial supports as the most accessible types of support from family members. Similarly, one hundred per cent each cited health information support as the major support received from the healthcare providers and government, while drug support was mostly received from the NGO, mainly the DAMIEN foundation. Consequent on this, we noted that the patients’ priority for the desired type(s) of support from the source seemed to differ on some occasions from the accessible support. For instance, health information and financial supports were largely preferred from government and the NGO, while health information and emotional supports were mostly desired from healthcare providers. However, emotional and financial supports were still the most preferred types of support from family and friends. Studies in different chronic diseases have shown that patients have varying preferences for different sources and types of social support, with the amount of support and manner of delivery contributing to perception of support,3651,54 while accessible support(s) also vary in their effectiveness in enhancing adherence.3648 51,54

Generally, in our study, there was no clear-cut association between the type(s) of support received by the patients from different source(s) and medication adherence. Though the lack of evidence of an association may not strictly translate to evidence of absence of a difference between the variables compared. There is generally a need to consistently put into consideration the varied factors including mental, socioeconomic, drug side effects and lack of access to quality healthcare facilities16,20 that may hinder adherence to treatment recommendations and successfully completing treatment regimens.2 14 15 Specifically in our study, it is noted that a substantial number of patients who received health information as well as drug support from the NGO had a considerably higher medication adherence. Also, patients who received emotional support from family had slightly higher TB medication adherence compared with those without such kind of support. A review of four intervention studies showed that emotional social support from family members, friends or healthcare providers improved medication adherence either through educating the patients, supporting their behaviour or supervising and reminding them to take medications.3655,58 Typically, aside from the availability of free TB medication(s), TB as a chronic disease impacts enormously on the well-being of the patients and may substantially affect their quality of life including psychosocial functioning55 and, as such, patients may not be able to sustain or meet the individual and family financial commitment and obligations.55 59 Previous studies have advocated for public health programmes incorporating a variety of strategies to improve adherence at the health system level including financial incentives or enablers to at least offset the costs of accessing treatment, as well as improving coordination and logistics around TB management burdens.3133 60,62

In our study, among the varying coping mechanisms mentioned by the patients in dealing with challenges of TB management, a substantial number, precisely three-quarters, cited emotional support from family or friends as the most effective coping mechanism. Literature indicated that emotional focus type of coping typically helps to reduce the negative feelings connected to a problem.63 Also, more than three-quarters engaged in lifestyle changes as coping mechanisms, including setting alarm for medication reminders, as well as change in daily routines. The WHO guidelines for treatment of DS-TB patients have recommended the use of digital health interventions as part of integrated patient care, especially in improving medication adherence and TB outcomes.13 39 64 Difficulty in swallowing TB medication(s) was reported as the most commonly encountered medication-taking challenge, and as part of coping mechanisms, patients largely resorted to self-encouragement to continue taking the TB regimen(s). Generally, proactive engagement of individual patient to partake in chronic disease management should always be encouraged, as this will further enhance their commitment as well as allow them to drive the medicine-use and TB care planning process.

Worthy of note is the fact that more than two-thirds reported the challenge of transportation costs as a major hindrance for regular TB-DOT clinic attendance. As a result, a substantial number of patients do engage in some forms of catastrophic coping mechanisms, including trekking a bit of distance before boarding a cab/taxi in order to reduce transport costs, while some engage in borrowing money from family or friends to offset TB management expenses. Government and other relevant stakeholders in TB management in low- and middle-income countries are therefore required to adopt measures to domestically mobilise resources for TB patients care and support. This is critical in achieving the WHO End-TB strategy milestone target by the year 2030.79 65,67

Strengths and limitations of this study

Despite the useful information provided in our study, it has some limitations worthy of mention. For instance, our study was carried out using a self-report measure of adherence which has its inherent drawbacks including memory or recall bias. Also, the data collection carried out only on the selected TB-DOT clinic days of the hospitals might have led to exclusion of some eligible walk-in patients with DS-TB on any other clinic day in the hospitals. Therefore, the relatively small sample size may not completely guarantee sample representativeness, as well as allow for absolute generalisation of our study findings to the entire drug-sensitive TB population in the region. In addition, the cross-sectional nature of the study does not pointedly support an establishment of cause-effect relationship between the variables compared. Though our study strictly followed the STROBE guideline47 for reporting findings in observational research studies. Furthermore, the possibility of selection bias may not be totally excluded considering the non-probability sampling technique used for participants’ enrolment.

Nonetheless, the use of CMAAS-12, with a non-threatening and non-judgemental question-item for evaluation of TB medication adherence, has helped in exploring the different domains of non-adherence behaviours to reveal barriers to medication adherence among the patients. Also, extensive inquiries and the resultant findings on the available sources of support system for TB management, specific type(s) of support accessible by patients, as well as the preferred type(s) of support might have provided useful information to TB stakeholders on new policy direction for TB management and care. Nevertheless, future research should take into consideration the identified gaps, especially a larger sample size in order to ensure a far-reaching conclusion.

Conclusion

In our study, nearly ninety per cent of the patients with DS-TB had optimal medication adherence. Above ninety per cent each mentioned support from family and healthcare providers as the most commonly available source of support for TB management and care, while about seventy per cent cited government and NGO. Overall, the most accessible type(s) of support from each of the available source(s) of support varies, with one hundred per cent and more than three quarters, respectively, citing emotional and financial support as most accessible from family. Health information support was mostly accessible from the healthcare providers and government, while drug support was largely received from NGOs. Though health information and financial supports were largely desired by the patients from the government and NGOs, emotional and financial supports were the most preferred types of support from family, as well as from friends.

There was generally no considerable association between the type(s) of support received by the patients and medication adherence. However, medication adherence was higher among patients who received health information as well as drug support from NGOs, while patients who received emotional support from family also had slightly higher TB medication adherence compared with counterparts without such support. Seeking emotional support from family or friends, self-encouragement to continue taking the TB regimens, as well as intentional engagement in some lifestyle changes was found as effective coping mechanisms for dealing with challenges in TB management.

There is generally a need for initiation and implementation of financial assistance and other expanded social support systems by government, especially, and other relevant stakeholders in TB management and care. This will serve as a critical step towards relieving the socioeconomic burdens of the disease on the patients, while subsequently helping in achieving the WHO End-TB strategy milestone target by the year 2030.
