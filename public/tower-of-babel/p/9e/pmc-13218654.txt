Continuous Opioid Infusion Among Patients Who Receive Mechanical Ventilation and Who Transition to Comfort-Focused Care
Authors: Justin M Rucci, Tenzin Dechen, Ashley O’Donoghue, Emma Lee, Nicholas A. Bosch, Allan J. Walkey, Jennifer P. Stevens
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Limitation or withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies is common within ICUs.1,2 When comfort measures only (CMO) care is initiated for patients who are receiving mechanical ventilation, guidelines support treating dyspnea and pain with opioids and acknowledge that clinicians should balance benefits of treatment against potential adverse effects (eg, somnolence, ileus).3,4 Given this deference to clinical judgment, we hypothesized that opioid infusion use among patients who received mechanical ventilation who transition to CMO care would vary across hospitals. We sought to identify factors associated with opioid infusion during CMO care and to evaluate the association between opioid administration and time to death after CMO transition.

Methods

This study was designated exempt by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center institutional review board (Protocol #2020P000672).

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the electronic ICU Collaborative Research Database (> 200,000 admissions across 208 United States hospitals participated in the Phillips electronic ICU program 2014 to 2015).5 To our knowledge, this is the only available multisite ICU data set with the requisite time-varying data (ie, to the minute time stamps of transitions to comfort-focused care and opioid infusion rates) to conduct our analysis. We identified adult patients who received mechanical ventilation for ≥24 hours and who were transitioned to CMO care. Patients were excluded if they had CMO status prior to ICU admission, experienced cardiac arrest (after which opioids are typically minimized to monitor neurologic status), or were admitted to an ICU type where opioid administration is likely impacted by the patient population (eg, surgical ICU, neurologic ICU). We identified opioid (morphine, hydromorphone, fentanyl) administration from opioid infusion rates listed in nursing flow-sheets and removed patients (n = 47) with erroneous opioid administration records (infusion documented as administered after discharge/death). We evaluated variables that may impact opioid administration during CMO care, including patient (demographics, primary diagnosis category, hospital admission source, ICU type, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE)-IV score, Charlson Comorbidity Index score,6 opioid administration [yes/no] before CMO, opioid dose, sedative [benzodiazepines, propofol, dexmedetomidine, ketamine] infusion before CMO [yes/no]) and facility (region, teaching status, hospital bed count) factors. Opioid dosing was defined in both the before and after CMO period with the use of a value of median daily fentanyl equivalents, which we calculated for each patient as follows: (1) converted all opioid infusion doses to fentanyl equivalents, (2) calculated the total fentanyl equivalent dose administered in each 24-hour period, and (3) determined the median value across these daily amounts for the period of interest (before or after CMO). There were no missing data for outcomes of interest. Data were missing for some categoric covariates and were accounted for by the creation of missing data categories (Table 1).

Covariates were described as counts and proportions or medians and interquartile ranges. We assessed a binary outcome of ever or never receiving an opioid infusion after CMO transition in a mixed-effects, multivariable logistic regression model (admitting hospital as a random intercept). We evaluated factors associated with opioid administration and reported the intraclass correlation coefficient. To evaluate different opioid treatment approaches, we conducted a multivariable survival analysis assessing time to death from CMO status across 4 opioid infusion treatment patterns: infusion before and after CMO, infusion after CMO only, infusion before CMO only, or no infusion. Analyses were conducted with SAS software (v9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary NC).

Results

We identified 1,635 adults who received mechanical ventilation, initiated CMO care, and met all inclusion/exclusion criteria. Most patients (75%) were ≥ 61 years old; 45% of them were female, and 78% of them identified as White race (additional factors in Table 1). Overall, 138 patients (8%) received an opioid infusion after CMO transition. Factors associated with opioid infusion after CMO included a before CMO opioid dose (adjusted OR [adjOR], 1.04; 95% CI, 1.01 to 1.06, per 100 μg/hr change in opioid infusion), a Charlson Comorbidity Index score value in the fourth quartile of the cohort compared with a value in the first quartile (adjOR, 5.43; 95% CI, 1.12 to 26.22; other quartiles not statistically significant), and Hispanic race compared with White race (adjOR, 3.00; 95% CI, 1.27 to 7.10). Figure 1A provides a visual representation of hospital variation in opioid use after CMO by plotting the difference between the log odds of receiving opioids after CMO at a given hospital vs the overall population log odds, while accounting for fixed-effect covariates. We found an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.37 (95% CI, 0.21 to 0.57), which indicated that 37% of the variation in opioid administration was attributable to the admitting hospital. Figure 1B shows the number of patients in each opioid treatment pattern. Median (interquartile range) time to death within each administration pattern was 50 hours (23 to 93 hours) for opioid infusions before and after CMO, 36 hours (23 to 110 hours) for infusions after CMO only, 5 hours (2 to 11 hours) for infusions before CMO only, and 8 hours (3 to 28 hours) for no infusions. Compared with patients who received opioid infusions before and after CMO, there was no difference in hazards of death among those who received infusions only after CMO (adjHRafter-CMO only, 1.49; 95% CI, 0.94 to 2.35) and increased hazards of death for those who received no infusions after CMO (adjHRbefore-CMO only, 2.12; 95% CI, 1.48 to 3.05; adjHRnever, 1.75; 95% CI, 1.22 to 2.52).

Discussion

In this first, to our knowledge, national description of opioid administration patterns among critically ill, mechanically ventilated patients who transitioned to CMO, only 8% of patients received opioid infusions during CMO care, and hospital opioid practices during CMO varied. Use of opioid infusions during CMO care did not hasten time to death.

Our study aligns with prior work that demonstrated hospital-variation in analgesic use during CMO care in the ICU.7 Effective palliative care, including opioid administration, has been shown to prolong survival8,9; our work extends this finding to patients who receive mechanical ventilation who transition to CMO care. Use of opioid infusions did not hasten time to death, which could alleviate clinician and family concerns about end-of-life opioid administration in ICUs. Despite this reassuring finding, a minority of patients received opioid infusions during CMO care. Limited opioid administration during CMO care may reflect undertreated pain/dyspnea in critically ill patients,10 may align with shortcomings in palliative care for patients with chronic lung disease who die in the ICU,11 or may reflect facility-driven variation in practice (eg, variation caused by geographic region, ICU size, or local processes/structures). Alternatively, patients may have received opioids via noninfusion routes or lacked symptoms warranting opioid administration.

Published guidelines note that the use of evidence-based tools to assess pain and dyspnea is critical to guide end-of-life opioid administration and that clinicians should make patient-specific opioid treatment decisions (including the option to forego opioids when not indicated or when harms outweigh benefits).2,3 This individualized decision-making may be reflected by our data. Higher opioid doses before CMO were associated with greater likelihood of infusion use after CMO; it is possible that patients who required more opioids to tolerate mechanical ventilation during critical illness also have more pain and dyspnea during CMO care. Higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores were also associated with greater likelihood of opioid infusion after CMO, possibly indicating clinician consideration of patient comorbidities when making opioid treatment decisions. At the same time, patient factors such as acuity of illness, receipt of sedative infusions, and primary diagnosis did not associate with opioid infusion after CMO. Future research should identify the specific characteristics that predict benefit from opioids during ICU CMO care to further improve patient-centered decision-making.

Our study has important strengths. We used a multicenter ICU database that provided granular data to assess timing of transition to CMO and use of opioid infusions before and after CMO and allowed evaluation of practice variation. We accounted for numerous factors that may influence opioid administration. Limitations were that the data were from 2014 to 2015, which may not reflect current practices (but would still reflect 2008 guidelines4). We were unable to evaluate noninfusion opioid administration, bedside assessments of pain/dyspnea/delirium, cause of death or whether patients remained ventilated until death, all of which could impact findings. The substantive proportion of data (ie, >10%) missing from some covariates (primary diagnosis, APACHE-IV score, number of hospital beds, region) limited our ability to fully assess their association with outcomes and could affect results. Immortal time bias could affect results if death after CMO limited opioid eligibility; this appeared unlikely because median time to death in patients who received no opioids after CMO was 5 hours.

Among patients who were mechanically ventilated who transitioned to CMO, the use of opioid infusions varied across hospitals. Although opioid infusions during CMO care did not hasten time to death, there was limited after CMO infusion use. To achieve guideline-recommended, patient-specific decision-making, additional research should define which patient factors predict benefit from opioids during ICU CMO care.
