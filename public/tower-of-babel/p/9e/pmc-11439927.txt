In vivo, contactless, cellular resolution imaging of the human cornea with Powell lens based line field OCT
Authors: Keyu Chen, Nima Abbasi, Alexander Wong, Kostadinka Bizheva
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Potentially blinding corneal diseases alter the morphology of the human cornea. At the early stages of disease development, these changes occur at the cellular level. The ability to visualize and quantify such changes can lead to early diagnostics of corneal pathologies, which is pivotal for the long-term preservation of vision. Here we present a Powell Lens-based Line-Field Optical Coherence Tomography system that combines high spatial resolution (2.4 μm × 2.2 μm × 1.7 μm (x × y × z)) in biological tissue, sufficient to resolve individual cells, high sensitivity (90.5 dB), sufficient to image the semi-transparent human cornea, and fast image acquisition rate (~ 2,400 fps), sufficient to suppress most involuntary eye motion artifacts and allow for contactless, in-vivo imaging of the cellular structure of the human cornea. Volumetric images acquired in-vivo from corneas of healthy subjects show epithelial, endothelial and keratocytes cells, as well as sub-basal and stromal nerves. The system’s high axial resolution also allows for volumetric morphometry of the corneal endothelium, Descemet’s membrane and the pre-Descemet’s (Dua) layer.

Introduction

The cornea is the transparent multi-layered tissue at the front part of the eye that accounts for approximately two-thirds of the eye’s total refractive power1. Degenerative, infectious and inherited corneal pathologies such as keratoconus, Fuch’s dystrophy, etc. constitute the 4th leading cause of blindness worldwide after cataract, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration2. At the early stages of their development, these diseases alter the corneal morphology at the cellular level. Therefore, the early diagnostics of corneal pathologies and the effective planning of therapeutic approaches are strongly dependent on the ability of imaging technologies to visualize, identify, and characterize the cellular structure of the cornea in-vivo.

Currently, the only clinically available technology capable of imaging the cellular structure of the human cornea in-vivo is In-vivo Confocal Microscopy (IVCM)3. While IVCM can generate high-quality enface images of the cornea, it has a number of limitations: (a) requires physical contact with the corneal epithelium that necessitates corneal anesthesia and significantly increases the risk of corneal infections and abrasions if the imaging probe is adjusted in the XY plane while making contact with the corneal surface; (b) limited field of view (maximal FOV ~ 400 μm × 400 μm); and (c) difficulty generating high resolution volumetric images due to depth location ambiguity associated with the slow axial scanning compared to fast axial eye motion.

In terms of in-vivo imaging of the cornea, optical coherence tomography (OCT)4,5 offers numerous advantages over IVCM such as larger FOV, volumetric imaging, faster volumetric imaging rate and spatial resolution comparable to that of IVCM that allows for visualization of the cellular structure of the cornea. Over the past decade, OCT technology with different optical designs has been used to image in-vivo the cellular structure of the human and animal cornea. Point-scanning spectral-domain (SD) OCT can achieve axial resolution of ~ 1 μm in corneal tissue by using broadband lasers6–12. Although in-vivo volumetric SD-OCT images of the corneal cellular structure have been reported in the past from anesthetized animals9,11, where anesthesia was used to reduce image artifacts induced by the involuntary eye motion, in-vivo imaging of the human cornea remained challenging due to the limited image acquisition speed (< 100,000 A-scans/s). Our research group utilized a linear-array camera with 250 kHz image data acquisition rate to generate in a contactless way the first, in-vivo, volumetric SD-OCT images of the healthy human cornea with spatial resolution sufficient to visualize the cellular structure of all corneal layers12. However, eye motion artifacts were still noticeable in the 3D images and the Field-of-View (FOV) was restricted to less than 200 μm × 200 μm. Furthermore, fast involuntary eye motion in the axial direction prevented generation of enface images of the corneal endothelium in-vivo.

Full-field (FF) time-domain (TD) OCT combines very high transverse resolution (~ 1 μm) and relative insensitivity of the images to optical aberrations13–15, which allows FF-TD-OCT to image the cellular structure of the human corneal stroma and endothelium both ex-vivo16–18 and in-vivo19,20. However, FF-TD-OCT was not able to resolve cells in the corneal epithelium due to the limited dynamic range of the area camera, resulting in limited Signal-to-Nosie Ratio (SNR)20. FF swept source (SS) OCT utilizes a rapidly tunable light source to generate high resolution, volumetric images of the human cornea. This technology offers better sensitivity and faster volumetric acquisition rate compared to FF-TD-OCT, as well as high phase stability of the enface OCT images, necessary for digital correction of aberrations21. While FF-SS-OCT can generate enface images of the cellular structure of the human cornea, its axial resolution is limited to ~ 4 μm in corneal tissue due to the fairly narrow tunable range of the light source. This axial resolution is insufficient for visualization and precise morphometry of the thin corneal layers such as the endothelium and the pre-Descemet’s layer, whose thickness is comparable to the FF-SS-OCT axial resolution.

Line-Field OCT (LF-OCT) offers a much faster data acquisition rate compared to point-scanning SD-OCT and in most occasions, even SS-OCT designs, by projecting a line-shaped beam onto the imaged object, scanning the beam only in one direction and utilizing an ultrafast area camera at the detection end of the system. Although the concept of LF-OCT was published more than two decades ago22, the use of LF-SD-OCT and LF-SS-OCT technology for in-vivo imaging of the human retinal structure and blood flow23,24 and the retinal response to visual stimulation25 was only reported recently. Our research group pioneered the use of LF-SD-OCT for in-vivo human corneal imaging and demonstrated that the combination of high spatial resolution and fast imaging allows for volumetric visualization of the corneal cellular structure26. However, the use of a cylindrical lens as the line generator resulted in a Gaussian distribution of the optical power along the length and width of the line-shaped beam, which contributed to > 10 dB sensitivity loss between the center and the edges of the LF-SD-OCT corneal images. This restricted the FOV over which corneal cells can be visualized from ~ 1,400 μm × 1,400 μm to ~ 400 μm × 400 μm. Recently, our research group explored the use of a Powell lens as the line generator in an LF-SD-OCT system that offers a “Top-Hat” optical power distribution along the length of the line beam27. The use of the Powell lens reduced the sensitivity roll off from the center to the edges of the FOV in LF-SD-OCT images to < 3 dB and allowed for significant expansion of the FOV. However, technical issues with the original optical design of the system (the depth-of-focus (DOF) was too short and axial saccades prevented generation of 3D OCT images over a large enough FOV that were properly focused) and the large RIN noise of the NKT laser which resulted in lower system’s sensitivity, prevented the use the first-generation Powell lens-based LF-SD-OCT (PL-LF-SD-OCT) system for in-vivo human corneal imaging.

Here we present a 2nd generation design of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system. The new design offers ~ 2.4 μm × 2.2 μm × 1.7 μm (x × y × z) resolution in biological tissue, assuming an average refractive index of 1.38, maximum sensitivity of 90.5 dB, measured for data acquisition rate of 2,400 frames per second (fps) and 2.6 mW optical power incident on the corneal surface, and 2.6 dB sensitivity loss from the center to the edges of the FOV in the line direction.

Methods

PL-LF-SD-OCT system design

The first-generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system published recently by our research group27 had some limitations in terms of sensitivity, sensitivity roll-off, balance between transverse resolution and DOF and camera triggering issues that limited the FOV in the scanning direction and prevented the safe, in-vivo, contactless imaging of the human cornea. In the second-generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system design presented here, the light source and camera were replaced with alternate options to improve the system’s sensitivity and resolve the camera triggering issues to allow for in-vivo imaging over wider FOV. The system’s optical design was also revised for better control of the sensitivity roll-off, finer digital resolution both in illumination line direction and spectral direction, and optimal trade-off between transverse resolution and DOF. The DOF of the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system was expanded from ~ 1.7 μm to ~ 31 μm, which is important for in-vivo cellular resolution corneal imaging where involuntary eye motion in the axial direction can cause significant defocus and distortions of the OCT images. The DOF expansion resulted is slight loss of transverse resolution (from ~ 1.7 μm in the 1st generation system to 2.4 μm in the 2nd generation system). A schematic diagram of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system is presented in Fig. 1, where the major components that were replaced relative to the original design are marked with red dashed lines.

Light source

A custom broadband light source (cBLMD-T-850-HP, Superlum) comprised of three superluminescent diodes (SLDs) whose emissions that can be controlled individually, was utilized. The default version of this light source has an almost “Top-Hat” emission spectrum with some intensity modulations. For our study, the light source was customized to increase the spectral power of the emission spectrum at longer wavelengths in order to compensate for the significant wavelength-dependent decrease of the camera’s quantum efficiency (QE). Figure 2A shows the emission spectrum of the customized broadband light source as measured with a spectrum analyzer, as well as the QE response curve of the new camera (PCO, Germany).

Figure 2B shows the sample and reference arms spectra of the interferometer, measured using silver-protected mirrors as targets. The two spectra have almost identical 3dB bandwidths of ~ 170 nm, centered at ~ 840 nm, resulting in axial point-spread function (PSF) of ~ 2.3 μm in free space, corresponding to ~ 1.7 μm in biological tissue assuming an average refractive index of 1.38 (Fig. 2C). The axial PSF displayed in Fig. 2C was generated by zero-padding the raw data before applying a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Both hardware (HDC) and software dispersion compensation (SDC) were applied to generate a symmetric axial PSF.

Illumination and detection arms of the system

The illumination arm of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system is similar to the previously published version27 with one major modification: the achromat doublets lens pair (Thorlabs) following the Powell lens was replaced with an achromat cylindrical lens pair(Thorlabs) with individual focal length of 75 mm and 100 mm. This modification removed the magnification power applied in the X direction of the optical beam by the original achromat doublets pair. This modification resulted in ~ 1.8 times expansion of the system’s DOF with no change to the optical beam size in the line direction. The expansion of the DOF while preserving the system’s transverse resolution facilitated the in-vivo acquisition of volumetric images of the human cornea, as it reduced the system’s susceptibility to defocus due to axial eye motion.

The system’s depth of focus was also influenced by the choice of the microscope objective (Mitutoyo, NIR Infinity corrected series, 10x magnification and 30.5 mm working distance). Also, slits were added at several locations in the system to reduce unwanted reflections from the optical components of the system.

In LF-OCT technology, the lens that generates the line-beam also decouples light propagation in the X and Y directions. The digital OCT resolution in the scanning direction, referred to as the X direction in this study, mirrors that of point-scanning SD-OCT systems and is dependent on the density of the scanning. However, the digital resolution in the line direction, or the Y direction in this study, is dependent on the total magnification power of the system in the line direction of the detection arm, as well as the camera pixel size. To adhere to the Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem for signal processing, a high line-direction magnification power is needed to achieve cellular resolution. This, however, results in reduced magnification power in the X direction, or equivalently, a smaller incident light spot diameter before the camera due to the inverse conjugation between the XY magnification powers. This results in a larger focusing spot projected onto the camera sensor, introducing crosstalk in the spectral direction within an A-scan and between consecutive A-scans. In this PL-LF-SD-OCT system, we implemented an anamorphic cylindrical lens pair design in a new location in the detection arm to control the system’s magnification in the X and Y directions independently25,27. This modification allows for a much more flexible optical design of the entire system.

The spectrometer at the detection end of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system was redesigned to include a new ultrahigh-speed area camera (pco.dimax cs4, PCO), featuring a full pixel range of 2016 × 2016 and a pixel size of 11 × 11 µm2. The larger pixel size in combination with new optical design of the spectrometer contributed to the overall higher SNR of the new PL-LF-SD-OCT system. Note that the new system design utilized only 1680 pixels in the spectral direction since the SLD light source has somewhat smaller spectral bandwidth compared to the SuperK supercontinuum laser used in the original design of the system27, and 600 pixels in the line direction. The camera acquisition speed (B-scan rate) was set at ~ 2,380 fps, which is equivalent to an A-scan rate of ~ 1.43 MHz. With the current selection of the active pixel region (1680 × 600), the maximum achievable frame rate is ~ 4,400 fps (equivalent to 2.64 MHz A-scan rate), though increasing the camera rate to maximum while keeping all other factors the same would result in ~ 2.5 dB sensitivity drop.

System’s performance

Figure 2 summarizes the performance test results for the new PL-LF-SD-OCT system, that were conducted with ~ 2.6 mW optical imaging power incident on the imaged target and with image acquisition rate of 2,380 fps. Figure 2D shows the sensitivity roll-off of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system. The maximum sensitivity of 90.4 dB was measured at a depth of 100 μm, with 18 dB sensitivity roll off over a scanning range of 1.5 mm. Figure 2E shows the axial PSF width as a function of scanning depth. The axial PSF width remains at approximately 2.2 μm in free space for the initial 1.1 mm depth, then diverges to roughly 2.6 μm up to a scanning depth of 1.4 mm. This trend correlates with an almost 10 dB sensitivity drop for depth locations between 1.1 mm and 1.4 mm. The most likely cause for this trend is the divergence of the optical beam in the reference arm of the system caused by the Powell lens.

Figure 2F shows the B-scan sensitivity as a function of pixel, measured across all 600 pixels at an imaging depth of 100 μm. The novel design of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system results in only ~ 2.6 dB sensitivity difference between the center and the edges of the B-scan. To accurately evaluate the system’s lateral resolution, the coherence line spread function (cLSF), which is the intensity distribution function along a selected line, and the coherence edge spread function (cESF), which is the spatial derivative of the cLSF, were utilized. In a region of the cLSF where a significant intensity change can be observed, such as at the edge of a reflective mirror, the corresponding cESF forms a local Gaussian shape. The Full-Width-at-Half-Maximum (FWHM) of this Gaussian-shaped cESF is equivalent to half of the lateral resolution. As shown in Fig. 2G, two orthogonal lines are selected from a square target region on the resolution target to measure the lateral resolution in both the line direction and scanning direction; their corresponding cLSF and cESF functions are plotted in Fig. 2H,I, respectively. Using this methodology, the system’s lateral resolution was measured to be 2.4 μm in the X direction and 2.2 μm in the Y direction.

Imaging protocol and image processing

In-vivo human cornea imaging with the PL-LF-SD-OCT system has received full ethics clearance from the Research Ethics Office at the University of Waterloo for a pilot study on healthy volunteers. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines/regulations of the University of Waterloo. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the LF-LF-SD-OCT imaging sessions. The goal of the pilot study was to test and optimize the performance of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system and gather sufficient imaging data from healthy normal subjects to apply for funding in the future for clinical studies of different corneal diseases.

The corneal images presented in this paper were acquired from a 26-year-old healthy male volunteer who has not been image prior with any other OCT technology. The subject’s head was placed in a custom head support frame attached to XYZ translation stages to allow for alignment of the imaged eye with respect to the optical imaging beam. The imaging optical power incident on the corneal surface was 2.6 mW, which is lower than the maximum permissible exposure for the human cornea and retina imaging, as stipulated by the ANSI standard28. Volumetric datasets comprising of 600 A-scans and 600 B-scans were acquired over a FOV of 570 μm × 620 μm at a rate of 2,380 fps. While the image acquisition time for each volumetric OCT data set was very fast (~ 0.25 s), alignment of cornea relative to the OCT imaging probe can take a few minutes. The time for alignment is dependent both on the skills of the operator of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system and the ability of the imaged subject to fixate their gaze on a fixation target and keep their head as still as possible.

The raw PL-LF-SD-OCT images were dispersion compensated numerically, followed by cross-correlation of consecutive B-scans to compensate for any involuntary eye motion. Afterwards, the images were flattened to compensate for the natural curvature of the cornea and allow for generation of flat enface images from different depths inside the cornea. For the flattening procedure, the corneal curvature was mapped separately for the XZ and YZ B-scans using a maximum intensity projection (MIP) method, by using the anterior surface of the corneal epithelium or the posterior surface of the corneal endothelium. A mask filter was applied to the MIP data to eliminate erroneously counted data from other regions, followed by a high-order polynomial fit applied to the reference curve. The volumetric dataset was flattened separately based on the XZ and YZ fits.

A novel algorithm was developed for sharpening and suppressing speckle noise in the volumetric PL-LF-SD-OCT images. Briefly, the algorithm is based on the following principle: First, speckle in log-scale images is modelled as a stationary correlated noise. Subsequently, the derived model is fed into a self-similarity-based model alongside a log-scale intensity image to derive non-local structural priors. Moreover, the priors are integrated into a maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) reconstruction framework with additional regularization terms such as total variation (TV). The MAP framework generates a speckle-free and aberration-reduced image. More detailed description of the novel algorithm and its performance can be found in a recent publication29.

Results

Considering that the DOF of the current design of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system was 31 μm, while the typical total human cornea thickness is in the range of 500 to 600 μm, volumetric images of the anterior, middle and posterior regions of the cornea were acquired separately by translating the image focal at the desired depth within the cornea.

Anterior cornea

Figure 3 summarizes PL-LF-SD-OCT images acquired from the anterior cornea. Figure 3A shows a volumetric image of the corneal epithelium and anterior stroma. The green arrow marks highly reflective features in the tear film layer that most likely correspond to cellular debris. A representative B-scan from the volumetric data set is presented in Fig. 3B. The cellular structure of the corneal epithelium (EPI) and the boundaries of the Bowman’s membrane (BM) are clearly visible. Keratocytes in the stroma (STR) appear white reflective dashes. Since the focal plane was located close to the EPI/BM boundary and the DOF was about 2 time smaller than the thickness of the corneal EPI, both the superficial EPI cells and keratocytes located farther away from the BM surface appear blurred. The small reflective feature at the EPI/BM boundary, marked with the red arrow corresponds to a cross-section of a sub-basal cornea nerve. An enface image of the EPI/BM interface generated from the volumetric data set shows a group of sub-basal corneal nerves (Fig. 3C). The dark horizontal lines in the image correspond to fast axial eye motion that causes defocus in the OCT images and resulted in lower image contrast.

Figure 3D–F show enface images of the wing cells of the corneal EPI for different magnifications. Individual epithelial cells are resolved over a FOV of ~ 550 μm × 550 μm (Fig. 3D), while individual cellular nuclei are resolved under higher magnification from the same data set (Fig. 3F, red arrows).

Posterior stroma

Figure 5 shows PL-LF-SD-OCT images acquired from the posterior cornea. A volumetric image is shown in Fig. 5A. Residual eye motion artefacts are visible in the scan direction. A representative B-scan is shown in Fig. 5B, where the imaging plane was focused close to the posterior surface of the endothelial layer (END). The image clearly shows the multi-layered structure of the posterior cornea. Figure 5C shown the same image with boundaries of the END, the Descemet’s membrane (DM) and the pre-Descemet’s layer (PDL) are marked with red and blue dashed lines respectively. The PDL layer was first identified in corneal histological images30 and our research group was the first to image it in-vivo and characterize its thickness in healthy subjects using cross-sectional OCT scans generated with a point-scanning OCT7. The system’s digital axial resolution is high enough not only to visualize these 3 corneal layers, but also to measure their average thickness: END – 5.2 μm, DM – 7.4 μm and PDL – 4.7 μm. These values agree well with data from anatomical30 and previous OCT studies7. Figure 5D shows a B-scan from the scanning direction, generated from the volumetric data set. While the 3 posterior corneal layers are clearly identified in the image, residual eye motion artefact was present that caused challenges in generating enface images of the END that were in-focus over the entire FOV. Enface images of the corneal END that were generated using MIP over the thickness of the END layer, are shown in Fig. 5E,F under different magnification. While the cellular structure of the END is clearly visible in both images, the eye-motion induced blur is visible in some locations of the image in Fig. 5E. The system’s transverse resolution was sufficient to visualize not only individual END cells, but also reflections from their nuclei (dark spots inside the cells), which appear as highly reflective spots in Fig. 5G. Figure 5H was generated by 2D FFT of the data presented in Fig. 5G and shows distribution density map for the cellular nuclei. The green circle marks the highest distribution density, while the radius of the circle corresponds to the mean distance between neighbouring nuclei (~ 20 μm). This number agrees well with the mean size of corneal endothelial cells from anatomical studies31–33.

Discussion

The sub-sections below provide an in-depth comparison between the 1st and 2nd generations of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system, and discuss the quality of the corneal images in context with images generated by other OCT technologies such as point-scanning OCT, FF-OCT, FF-SS-OCT and LF-OCT.

SNR and SNR roll-off improvement

There are several design factors that contributed to the improved SNR and SNR roll-off of the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system compared to the 1st generation system design27:

Larger pixel size of the camera in combination with optimized optical design: For a SD-OCT system to reach optimum sensitivity and SNR roll-off, the spectrometer has to be designed in such a way that the spot size for each pixel of the camera line along the spectral direction has to be smaller than or equal to the pixel area to prevent loss of light. This requirement is very difficult to satisfy especially in cases when cameras with small pixel size are used in ultrahigh resolution OCT systems that utilise very broad band light sources. In the 1st generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system we used an AOS camera with 7.8 μm × 7.8 μm pixels and the optical design of the system ensured that the diffraction limited spot at the centre of the camera pixel row is smaller than the pixel size. However, aberrations toward the edges of the pixel row caused light leakage, which resulted in strong SNR roll-off (~ 13 dB over 1 mm scanning range). In the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system we used a PCO camera with 11 μm × 11 μm pixels. We also changed the optical design of the system to ensure that all pixels in the spectral direction are properly filled and potential light leakage due to aberrations is minimized. These changes contributed significantly to the improvement of the system’s sensitivity near the zero-delay line (90.5 dB compared to 87 dB) and SNR roll-off (< 7dB compared to 13 dB over 1 mm scanning range).Camera Quantum Efficiency (QE): While the PCO camera has overall better quantum efficiency than the AOS camera (QE curves are available from the camera manufacturers), since the 2nd generation system was designed for operation in a longer wavelength spectral region compared to the 1st generation system, the camera QE did not contribute significantly to the SNR and SNR roll-off improvement.RIN of the light source: The 1st generation system utilized a supercontinuum source (NKT SuperK, 320 MHz), while the 2nd generation utilized an SLD based broadlighter (Superlum, Ltd). We have measured the RIN noise of the 2 light sources with the same camera by blocking the sample arm of the OCT system and recording intensity data over a time period of 5 s with the spectrometer’s camera. The intensity data was processed to calculate the STD of the intensity fluctuations. It was determined that the noise STD ratio (NKT/SLD) is 1.22, which corresponds to a difference of 1.74 dB in the system’s SNR, under the assumption that all other system parameters remain unchanged. Therefore, about 50% of the 3.5 dB SNR improvement in the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system measured near the zero-delay line can be attributed to replacing the light source with a lower RIN one, while the other 50% is due to the camera replacement and the changes of the optical design to ensure minimal loss of light due to optical aberrations in the spectrometer. The nearly 2x improvement of the SNR roll-off is due to the camera replacement and the changes of the optical design that ensured minimal light loss along the pixel row in the spectral direction of the system.

We also changed the optical design of the system to ensure that all pixels in the spectral direction are properly filled and potential light leakage due to aberrations is minimized. These changes contributed significantly to the improvement of the system’s sensitivity near the zero-delay line (90.5 dB compared to 87 dB) and SNR roll-off (< 7dB compared to 13 dB over 1 mm scanning range).

Larger pixel size of the camera in combination with optimized optical design: For a SD-OCT system to reach optimum sensitivity and SNR roll-off, the spectrometer has to be designed in such a way that the spot size for each pixel of the camera line along the spectral direction has to be smaller than or equal to the pixel area to prevent loss of light. This requirement is very difficult to satisfy especially in cases when cameras with small pixel size are used in ultrahigh resolution OCT systems that utilise very broad band light sources. In the 1st generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system we used an AOS camera with 7.8 μm × 7.8 μm pixels and the optical design of the system ensured that the diffraction limited spot at the centre of the camera pixel row is smaller than the pixel size. However, aberrations toward the edges of the pixel row caused light leakage, which resulted in strong SNR roll-off (~ 13 dB over 1 mm scanning range). RIN of the light source: The 1st generation system utilized a supercontinuum source (NKT SuperK, 320 MHz), while the 2nd generation utilized an SLD based broadlighter (Superlum, Ltd). We have measured the RIN noise of the 2 light sources with the same camera by blocking the sample arm of the OCT system and recording intensity data over a time period of 5 s with the spectrometer’s camera. The intensity data was processed to calculate the STD of the intensity fluctuations. It was determined that the noise STD ratio (NKT/SLD) is 1.22, which corresponds to a difference of 1.74 dB in the system’s SNR, under the assumption that all other system parameters remain unchanged. Therefore, about 50% of the 3.5 dB SNR improvement in the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system measured near the zero-delay line can be attributed to replacing the light source with a lower RIN one, while the other 50% is due to the camera replacement and the changes of the optical design to ensure minimal loss of light due to optical aberrations in the spectrometer. The nearly 2x improvement of the SNR roll-off is due to the camera replacement and the changes of the optical design that ensured minimal light loss along the pixel row in the spectral direction of the system.

DOF and axial eye motion

A point-scanning OCT is inherently confocal and the confocality is symmetric in the XY plane. A Line Scan OCT system such as the PL-LF-SD-OCT system presented here, is only confocal in the X direction (across the width of the line beam), while it is non-confocal in the Y direction (along the line length of the imaging beam). The short DOF in the X direction results in defocus in the images outside the focal plane in a range +/- DOF/2. Note that due to the short OCT coherence gate (corresponding to very high OCT axial resolution of ~ 2 μm in the cornea), and the high optical transparency of the human corneal tissue, the PL-LF-SD-OCT corneal images are primarily generated from back-scattered or single scattered light.

LS-OCT technology can generate high resolution enface images from locations in the volumetric stack that correspond to the focal plane +/- DOF/2. Outside of this region, the images will show progressive blur with distance away from the focal plane, as in a regular CM. In our case, the thickness of the human cornea is ~ 500 μm, while the DOF of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system is ~ 31 μm, which accounts for the significant blur and loss of image contrast in the corneal images for locations far away from the focal plane. Recently, we have developed a computational approach to correcting defocus in LS-OCT corneal images. Detailed description of the algorithm and test results are summarized in a recent publication29.

The flattening algorithm discussed can enhance enface visualization by compensating for the inherent curvature of the cornea as well as eye movements. However, it does not aid in correcting defocusing caused by axial eye motion, nor the shift in focus due to the curvature of the eye. As illustrated in Figs. 3C,D and 5D,F, even at image acquisition rates of ~ 2,400 fps, it is still likely to capture fast axial motion in the range of 50–70 μm, which causes defocusing of the corneal images and reduces the visibility of epithelial and endothelial cells. To address this issue, three improvements can be considered: (a) Further reducing the lateral resolution to increase the DOF. Currently, the system has a DOF of ~ 30 μm for a scanning lateral resolution of ~ 2.4 μm. Lowering it to ~ 3 μm could provide an additional 18 μm DOF. However, this could result in the inability to resolve cellular structures when in focus. (b) Further increasing the acquisition speed for an even shorter volumetric data acquisition period. Doubling the acquisition speed will reduce eye motion artefacts. However, this approach will result in ~ 3 dB sensitivity loss and affect the system’s ability to resolve low contrast structures such as epithelial cells.

Powell lens and sensitivity loss in the line direction

In our previous study27, we reported a line direction sensitivity change of ~ 1.6 dB between the cent and the edges of the line, while the 2nd generation design of the PL-LF-SD-OCT system resulted in ~ 2.6 dB change. The decrease in sensitivity uniformity stems from two primary factors: (a) The Powell lens used in this study is designed for visible wavelength monochromatic light. The emission spectrum we are working with in this study has a similar full spectral bandwidth compared to the previous study27, though it is shifted towards the IR region, which degraded the lens performance. It is possible to design the Powell lens achromatically with a secondary surface added for chromatic aberration correction, similar to achromatic doublet lenses. However, to our best knowledge, no such product has been released yet. (b) The digital resolution in the line direction is determined by the ratio of pixel size to line direction magnification power. While the camera pixel size in this study is larger (11 μm compared to 7.8 μm), the digital resolution was higher (1.05 μm).

Quality of the corneal images

The human cornea images acquired with the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system (Figs. 3, 4 and 5) are of similarly high quality as the images published by our research group ~ 2 years earlier, that were acquired with an LF-SD-OCT system using a cylindrical lens as line generator26. This is not surprising since the 2 imaging systems have very similar spatial resolution, SNR and SNR roll-off. However, a major limitation of the LF-SD-OCT system was the nearly 10 dB SNR loss between the center of the line and its edges due to the Gaussian intensity profile of the line generated by the cylindrical lens. This resulted in severe contrast loss (> 10 dB SNR difference between the center and edges of the FOV) in the LF-SD-OCT images and reduced the FOV over which different types of corneal cells can be resolved from ~ 1,400 μm × 1,400 μm to ~ 400 μm × 400 μm. This issue was resolved in the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system where the SNR loss along the line was reduced to ~ 2.6 dB. This allowed for visualization of individual corneal cells in the epithelium, endothelium and stroma over a FOV of ~ 600 μm × 600 μm with high contrast. Furthermore, the high axial resolution allowed for volumetric visualization, as well as precise morphometry of the thin layers of the posterior cornea (PDL, DM and END). The PDL was first visualized in-vivo by our research group only in B-scans acquired with a point-scanning SD-OCT system and morphometrically characterized in healthy, normal human cornea7. However, since the upper boundary of the PDL is defined as the last layer of keratocytes, there are spatial variations in the PDL thickness that can only be characterized from volumetric OCT images such as the ones acquired with the 2nd generation PL-LF-SD-OCT system presented here. While a lower cost CL-based LF-OCT system was published recently34, the lower axial resolution (6.3 μm) did not allow for visualization of the posterior layers of the human cornea. Furthermore, the low SNR (70 dB for 5,000 fps and 2.7 mW imaging power) and strong back-reflections from the cornea and the imaging system’s optics prevented clear, enface visualization of corneal epithelial and endothelial cells.

While other OCT modalities have been used in the recent past to image the cellular structure of the human cornea in-vivo, each of them has advantages and limitations. Point-scanning SD-OCT offers a combination of compact, fiber optics design, 1.5 μm axial resolution, 98 dB SNR and data acquisition rate of 250,000 A-scans/s10. While this system allowed for acquisition of volumetric corneal images where epithelial cells and keratocytes can be visualized, the slow data acquisition rate relative to ocular saccades prevented enface visualization of endothelial cells and restricted the FOV to ~ 250 μm × 250 μm. FF-OCT utilizes parallel detection in the XY plane and offers both very high transverse resolution and insensitivity to aberrations in the transverse direction20,21. As such, FF-OCT has the capacity to generate high resolution enface images of the cornea’s cellular structure. However, this technology has some limitations: the short dynamic range of the camera prevents imaging of epithelial cells, while the slow scanning in the axial direction prevents in-vivo, volumetric imaging of the human cornea. The original version of the FF-TD-OCT also had difficulties generating enface images of the cornea over large enough FOV due to the corneal curvature20,21. More recent development of this technology used a curved reference mirror to image curved objects such as the human cornea over a larger FOV35. However, it will be challenging to implement this technology for clinical imaging as the curvature of the human cornea varies with age and with ocular conditions such as myopia, hyperopia, keratoconus, etc. FF-SS-OCT overcome most of the limitations of FF-TD-OCT and is able to generate volumetric images of the human cornea in-vivo and with cellular resolution in the transverse direction over a wide FOV with an impressive data acquisition rate of 60,000 fps22. However, the low SNR associated with the high imaging speed necessitates averaging of 30 volumes in order to generate images of sufficient contrast. Another advantage of the FF-SS-OCT technology is the phase stability of the OCT fringe pattern at such high acquisition rates, which allows for digital aberration correction of the corneal images. Currently one limitation of the FF-SS-OCT is the short tunable range of the light source which limits the axial resolution to ~ 5 μm. The image quality offered by the FF-SS-OCT technology is fairly comparable to the one achieved by the CL-based LF-SD-OCT26 and the PL-LF-SD-OCT system discussed here. Future development of novel low cost, fast area cameras, broad band, low RIN CW light sources and broad sweep range tunable lasers can boost the development of clinical versions of both the LF-SD-OCT and FF-SS-OCT / FF-TD/SD-OCT technologies.

Conclusion

We have developed a new design for a PL-LF-SD-OCT system with improved sensitivity, larger FOV and demonstrate the suitability of the system for in-vivo, contactless, volumetric imaging of the human cornea. Healthy corneal images acquired with this system showed that the combination of high spatial resolution, fast image acquisition rate and improves sensitivity are sufficient to visualize individual cells and cellular nuclei in the high contrast PL-LF-SD-OCT images, as well as carry out morphometric analysis on the thin corneal layers (END, DM, PDL and BM). Future applications of this novel technology will include clinical studies of various corneal pathologies.
