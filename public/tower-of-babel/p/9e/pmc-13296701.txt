Activation of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2 (SERCA2) reduces brain injury and improves cognitive function following ischemic stroke
Authors: Mohd Salman, Abdul Majid, Syamal K Bhattacharya, Djamel Lebeche, Tauheed Ishrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke is a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, driven in part by disrupted intracellular calcium (Ca2+) homeostasis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative damage, and neuroinflammation. The sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2 (SERCA2) pump is essential for maintaining intracellular Ca2+ balance, and its dysfunction contributes to ischemic neuronal injury. Here, we investigated the neuroprotective effects of CDN1163, a small-molecule allosteric activator of SERCA2, in a murine model of photothrombotic stroke (pt-MCAO). Male C57BL/6 J mice received CDN1163 (50 mg/kg, i.p.) at 1, 24, 48, and 72 h after stroke induction. Neurological and cognitive outcomes were assessed using CatWalk gait analysis, novel object recognition (NOR), and the Morris water maze. Infarct volume, blood-brain barrier integrity, oxidative stress, apoptosis, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, intracellular Ca2+ dynamics, and SERCA ATPase activity were evaluated by TTC staining, immunoblotting, TUNEL assay, intracellular calciumimaging, and ATPase assay, respectively. CDN1163 significantly reduced infarct volume, brain oedema, and IgG extravasation, while restoring SERCA2 expression and preserving blood-brain barrier integrity. CDN1163 treatment also improved intracellular Ca2+ handling and enhanced SERCA2 ATPase activity following pt-MCAO stroke. Mechanistically, CDN1163 suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, reduced oxidative stress markers (4-HNE and nitrotyrosine) and apoptotic markers (Bax and cleaved PARP-1), decreased TUNEL-positive cells, and increased oxidative phosphorylation protein expression. These molecular changes were accompanied by improved gait performance and cognitive recovery. Collectively, these findings identify pharmacological activation of SERCA2 by CDN1163 as a promising therapeutic strategy for ischemic stroke by restoring Ca2+ homeostasis and attenuating neuroinflammatory and oxidative injury.Keywords: CDN1163, SERCA2 activation, Stroke, Oxidative stress, Cognitive Impairment, NLRP3, Neuroprotection

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a complex neurological disorder caused by an interruption of cerebral blood flow, leading to substantial mortality and long-term disability. The World Health Organization reported that over 15 million people a year, approximately one in every 400 people, suffer from ischemic stroke worldwide [1]. The pathophysiology of ischemic stroke is complex and involves activating a series of pathological signaling cascades and cellular interactions that ultimately result in neuronal dysfunction and cell death [2]. During ischemic stroke, cerebral blood flow is interrupted or markedly reduced, resulting in hypoxia, ischemic injury, and neurological dysfunction. This process involves a variety of cascade mechanisms, including excitotoxicity, excessive oxidative stress, inflammatory response, organelle dysfunction, macro-molecule damage, intracellular Ca2 + overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular structural damage, disrupted bioenergetics (low glucose-oxygen supply, decreased ATP production), neurovascular damage, cytotoxic edema, and neuronal cell degeneration [3,4]. At the cellular level, disruption of blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, whose core structural component is the cerebral vascular endothelium, represents a fundamental pathological mechanism underlying ischemic stroke [5]. Disruption of BBB integrity following ischemic insult permits the uncontrolled influx of intravascular substances, including ions, fluids, and immune cells, into the brain parenchyma. This breach initiates a cascade of detrimental events, such as neuroinflammation, cerebral edema, and progressive expansion of neuronal injury, ultimately leading to worsened neurological and functional outcomes [6–8]. Recent studies have demonstrated that ischemic stroke can also cause endoplasmic reticulum (ER) damage and dysfunction [9], and activate the ER stress response, which likely contributes to brain damage [10, 11]. It has been reported that Ca2+ homeostasis disruption in the ER plays a decisive role in various neurological disorders, including ischemic stroke [12,13]. Ca2+ depletion in the ER induces ER stress and contributes to excessive intracellular Ca2+ overload, which can activate diverse enzymes, such as phospholipases, proteases, and nucleases, triggering cell death [14]. Also, excessive intracellular Ca2+ accumulation has been closely linked to the pathophysiology of neurovascular and neurologic functional disorders under ischemic conditions and outcomes [15–17]. A major regulator of intracellular Ca2+ is the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ pump (SERCA2), as SERCA2 dysfunction leads to elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels and the induction of ER stress, resulting in toxicity and cell death. A recent study showed that SERCA2 is regarded as a gatekeeper of neuronal Ca2+ homeostasis in the central nervous system [18]. The low ATP levels observed in ischemic stroke and the Ca2+-induced production of NO reduce SERCA2 activity, further exacerbating ER stress [19]

There are limited treatment options for ischemic stroke, with thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy being the only currently approved therapies [20]. However, due to medical constraints and the complexity of these procedures, only a limited number of patients undergo these therapies. Thus, the primary goal of ischemic stroke research is to develop cost-effective and promising treatments that enhance brain function following ischemic stroke injury through a better understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms. There is an urgent need to identify effective therapeutic targets that can limit ischemic brain injury. We have recently demonstrated that CDN1163, a small-molecule allosteric activator of sarcoendoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2b (SERCA2b), is effective in treating diabetes and its associated metabolic disorders [21]. CDN1163 selectively enhances SERCA2b activity, promoting intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, and is capable of efficiently crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB) when administered orally [21]. Preclinical studies have established its safety and therapeutic efficacy across a range of conditions, including diabetic, cardiac, neurological, and metabolic disorders [22–24]. As a member of a novel class of positive allosteric modulators, CDN1163 does not alter the basal activity of SERCA2b but significantly enhances Ca2+ sequestration into the endoplasmic reticulum, thereby reducing cytosolic Ca2+ levels [21]. While a recent report has already addressed the protective effect of CDN1163 in the subacute phase of an intraluminal rat model of stroke [25], the therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms of SERCA2b activation in ischemic stroke remain unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to establish SERCA2b-targeted pharmacotherapy as a novel and effective strategy for mitigating stroke-related brain injury and long-term cognitive recovery in a clinically relevant photothrombotic mouse model of stroke.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment regimen

Adult male C57BL/6 J mice (14–16 weeks old) were acquired from Jackson Laboratory. The mice were housed in a regulated environment (22°C ± 3°C, 60% ± 5% humidity, 12 h light/12 h dark cycle). All experimental procedures and protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Tennessee Health Science Center, Memphis, TN, in accordance with guidelines established by the United States Public Health Service Policy on the Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and relevant protocols. The functional outcomes were assessed using validated, reproducible tests and reported in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. The mice were assigned to three experimental groups: i) Sham + vehicle, ii) stroke + vehicle, and iii) stroke + CDN1163, n = 7/group. The animals were evaluated at two endpoints: (a) 72 h, to study the sub-acute effect of CDN1163 modulation following stroke; and (b) 30 days following stroke, to evaluate the long-term functional outcome. The experimental design is depicted in Fig. 1 A.

(B) TTC Images, (C) Infarct volume, (D) Ipsilateral brain edema, (E, F) Images of IgG extravasation and ZO-1 Immunoblots (G–I) bar graphs. Drug treatment

The compound, CDN1163, was administered intraperitoneally (50 mg/kg body weight) at 1, 24, 48, and 72 h post-stroke. CDN1163 was dissolved in a solution containing 10% DMSO and 10% Tween-80, with the final volume adjusted to 10 mL using 0.9% sterile normal saline [21]. Mice were euthanized at two different time points: in the sub-acute study, euthanasia was performed at 72 h post-stroke, while in the long-term study, CDN1163 was injected twice a week, and mice were euthanized on day 30 post-stroke. Body weight was recorded for each group of mice before stroke induction and on the final day of the experiment.

Induction of pt-MCAO stroke in mice

Ischemic stroke was induced by middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) using the photothrombosis method, as established in our laboratory [4], due to its high accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility, in infarct volume, with no associated mortality rate and visual confirmation of artery occlusion. The procedure was performed with minor modifications. In brief, mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, and their heads were shaved. An incision was made along the left side of the scalp between the ear and eye to expose the proximal artery. A photothrombotic dye (Rose Bengal, 15 mg/kg in PBS) was injected into the retroorbital sinus (maximum 30 μL/mouse), followed by the exposure of a photo beam at the proximal middle cerebral artery location for 4 min. After surgery, mice were housed in groups and monitored for signs of pain or distress until the end of the study.

Assessment of neurological function

Catwalk gait analysis test

Catwalk tests were performed for Gait analysis at 72 h post-stroke using the CatWalk XT automated analysis system (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, The Netherlands) to assess specific gait parameters, as previously reported [4]. Briefly, mice were trained to traverse the gated walkway for five consecutive days, each lasting 30 min, and baseline measurements were documented before ischemic stroke surgery. The CatWalk XT system captures footprint images and foot-intensity profiles using a digital camera positioned beneath a glass walkway, with its software processing the data. As the mice crossed the walkway, their paw prints were illuminated by green light, while the instrument’s ceiling light enhanced contrast to define the mice’s body contour. Three trials were recorded from each mouse, and the results were represented as SEM

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The Novel Object Recognition (NOR) task was performed to assess non-spatial short-term functional memory 20 days post-stroke and after CDN1163 treatment. Based on the spontaneous tendency of mice to explore and interact with a new object, a standard test method was used; the mice were allowed to examine two similar objects during the acquisition trial, with one changed during the final trial. The habituation period was conducted one day before the final test; mice were allowed to acclimate to an empty arena for 15 min. On the final test day, mice were first presented with two identical objects and allowed to explore them for 5 min. Then, they returned to their home cage for a 60-minute retention time. On the final test, a similar object was swapped for a novel object [26]. The time spent by mice exploring familiar and novel objects was recorded to calculate the recognition index (RI) and discrimination index as an indicator of working memory, using the formula:

Recognition index = TN / (TN + TF), where TF and TN are the time spent interacting with the familiar and novel object.

Discrimination index = (TN − TF) / (TN + TF), where TF and TN are the time spent interacting with the familiar and novel object.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The Morris water maze (MWM) test was performed to evaluate spatial learning and memory, as reported previously [26]. The training phase comprised four trials per day, each lasting 60 s, conducted over seven consecutive days following two days of cued test training. Briefly, mice were trained to locate a hidden escape platform submerged 2 cm below the water surface within a circular maze, measuring 132 cm in diameter and 60 cm in height, divided into four virtual quadrants (South, North, West, and East). Each day, mice completed four trials, starting from different dropping spots, with distinct spatial cues placed around the maze to assist navigation. If a mouse failed to locate the platform within the given time, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to remain there for 15–20 s. The time taken to reach the hidden platform was recorded as the escape latency in seconds. Spatial reference memory and memory consolidation were assessed using a probe trial on the final day. During the trial, the platform was removed, and mice were allowed to swim freely. Memory performance was assessed based on escape latency to the first entry, time spent, and the number of entries into the target quadrant where the platform was placed, indicating the degree of memory consolidation.

Western blot analysis

Ipsilateral brain tissue samples were collected at 72 h and 30 days post-stroke, homogenized, and processed for Western blot analysis. Briefly, mice were euthanized by an overdose of isoflurane and perfused with ice-cold PBS. The brain was carefully isolated, and the tissue was homogenized in 10% (w/v) lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. The final homogenate was then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and 30 μg of total protein was resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST for 90 min at room temperature before being probed with primary antibodies against Bax, β-Actin (1:500; sc-7480; sc–47778; Sant Cruz Biotechnology), NLRP3, caspase-1, ASC (1:1000; AG–20B–0014; AG–20B–0042; AG–25B–0006, Adipogen Life Sciences), cleaved IL-1β (1:1000; ab9722 Abcam, UK). The following day, membranes were washed three times with TBST for 5 min each, then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, including anti-mouse IgG and anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; A9044; A9169; Sigma, USA), for 60 min at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescent substrate solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The optical density of the bands was quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to the respective loading controls.

Microsome isolation and ATPase assay

Brain microsomes were isolated as previously described in our published protocol [21]. SERCA ATPase activity was measured in the isolated microsomal fraction using the QuantiChrom™ ATPase/GTPase Assay Kit (DATG-200; BioAssay Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

OGD/reoxygenation and Ca2+ imaging

To mimic ischemic-like conditions in vitro, cell cultures were exposed to oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) for 4 h and then returned to 95% air/5% CO2 and glucose-containing medium for the indicated recovery period [27]. CDN1163 (10 μM) was added 24 h prior to OGD where indicated. Briefly, mouse N2a neuroblastoma cells were transferred into a temperature-controlled (37 °C) hypoxia chamber containing a gas mixture of 5% CO2 and 95% N2. The culture medium was replaced with deoxygenated, glucose-free Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Invitrogen), and cells were maintained in the hypoxic chamber for 4 h. After OGD, cells were returned to normoxic culture conditions (95% air/5% CO2, 37 °C) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 4 h.

For intracellular Ca2+ measurements following OGD and reoxygenation, cells were rinsed with PBS and briefly stained with Hoechst (5 μg/mL) for 5–15 min at 37 °C, followed by PBS washing. Cells were then loaded with Fura-2 AM (Invitrogen, F1221; 5 μM) and 0.01% Pluronic F-127 (Invitrogen, P3000MP) in physiological buffer (mM): 125 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1.2 MgSO4, 1.8 CaCl2, 1.2 KH2PO4, 23 NaHCO3, 3 HEPES, and 10 D-glucose, for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by a 10 min de-esterification period. Fluorescence images were acquired using a BioTek Cytation C10 Imaging Reader with the instrument’s configured excitation/emission settings for Ca2+-dependent Fura-2 fluorescence. Intracellular Ca2+ was quantified on a per-cell basis after background subtraction using ImageJ (NIH; v1.51)

Slot blot for nitrotyrosine and 4-HNE

The expression levels of oxidative stress markers, such as 4-HNE and nitrotyrosine (NO2-Tyr), indicators of lipid peroxidation and superoxide-dependent peroxynitrite formation, were determined by slot blot. Ipsilateral brain tissue samples were homogenized as described in the Western blot analysis, and 25 μg of protein was immobilized onto a nitrocellulose membrane using a slot blot microfiltration unit. The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 60 min at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies against nitrotyrosine (1:1000; 05–233; Millipore) and 4-HNE (1:1000; SMC-511; StressMarq Biosciences) overnight. The next day, membranes were probed with a secondary antibody, and protein detection was performed with an enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The optical density of each group was quantified using densitometry in ImageJ.

TUNEL assay

Animals were deeply anesthetized with an overdose of isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice-cold phosphate buffer saline after 72 h post-stroke. The mouse brain was then stored in 10% buffered formalin (Fischer Scientific). The next day, brain samples were transferred to a 30% sucrose gradient in PBS until they sank for cryoprotection. The fixed brain samples were embedded in OCT solution, sectioned in the coronal plane at 10–12 μm, and subjected to a TUNEL assay to detect cell death following stroke and CDN1163 treatment, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All results were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (San Diego, CA, USA). The escape latency in the MWM test was analyzed using mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA). Independent-samples t-tests were used when the data were segregated into two groups. When the grouping is greater than or equal to three groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Significant value was selected by a P < 0.05.

Results

CDN1163 treatment reduced the infarct volume and brain edema following stroke

Infarct volume was calculated after TTC staining, as shown in Fig. 1B. Ischemic stroke mice exhibited visible infarction, associated with increased brain edema and dead tissue, demonstrating brain damage and cell death. Post-treatment with CDN1163 significantly reduced ischemic lesion volume and brain edema compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 1C–D). Furthermore, we examined the effect of CDN1163 on IgG extravasation and ZO-1, established markers of BBB disruption at 72 h post-stroke. We found a significant increase in IgG extravasation and downregulation of ZO-1 in the ipsilateral hemisphere in the vehicle group compared to the sham group (Fig. 1E and F). However, CDN1163 treatment markedly reduced the expression of IgG heavy and light chains while upregulating ZO-1 levels (Fig. 1E and F), indicating a preservation of BBB integrity. However, we recognize that IgG extravasation and ZO-1 immunoblotting provide static and indirect measures of BBB integrity rather than a comprehensive functional assessment, and therefore, future studies using dynamic permeability assays and cell-specific vascular analyses will be important to further define the effects of CDN1163 on BBB function.

CDN1163 treatment restored SERCA2b expression and ATPase activity following stroke

We demonstrated for the first time that ischemic stroke injury significantly downregulates SERCA2b protein expression and its ATPase activity in the ipsilateral brain region (Fig. 2). This finding strongly suggests that reduced SERCA2b expression may play a critical role in stroke pathogenesis. Under normal physiological conditions, SERCA2b is essential for maintaining ER function, and its downregulation is known to trigger ER stress in various disease conditions [28]. We administered multiple doses of CDN1163 at different time points. We found that SERCA2b expression and its activity were significantly reduced in vehicle-treated mice compared with sham-treated mice. However, CDN1163 treatment significantly restored SERCA2b protein expression and its activity compared to vehicle, as shown in Fig. 2A–C. These findings suggest that CDN1163 not only restores SERCA ATPase activity but may also preserve or enhance SERCA2b expression after stroke.

Consistent with SERCA dysfunction leading to impaired ER Ca2+ sequestration and cytosolic Ca2+ overload, hypoxic/ischemic-like conditions markedly increased intracellular Ca2+ levels in N2a cells, as indicated by elevated Ca2+-dependent fluorescence compared with normoxia (Fig. 2D, E). Importantly, CDN1163 significantly reduced the hypoxia-induced Ca2+ elevation, restoring intracellular Ca2+ signals towards normoxic levels (Fig. 2D, E). Together, these data support the conclusion that CDN1163-mediated SERCA activation preserves Ca2+ homeostasis under ischemic stress, which may contribute to its protective effects following stroke.

CDN1163 treatment inhibited NLRP3-inflammasome activation following stroke

The role of NLRP3 inflammasome activation in ischemic stroke condition has been well-documented, with prior studies highlighting its contribution in aggravating neuroinflammation and neuronal injury through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the execution of pyroptotic and apoptotic cell death mechanisms [29,30]. The activated NLRP3-inflammasome complex consists of NLRP3, the adaptor protein ASC, and caspase-1, which facilitates the maturation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [31]. To assess the impact of SERCA2 activation on NLRP3-inflammasome stimulation in the ipsilateral brain region following ischemic stroke, we investigated the key components of the inflammasome and the subsequent synthesis of caspase-1 and IL-1β. Our findings show a significant increase in NLRP3, ASC, cleaved caspase-1, and cleaved IL-1β expression at 72 h post-stroke compared with sham mice (Fig. 3A–E), indicating activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Further, pharmacological treatment with CDN1163 attenuated stroke-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as indicated by reduced cleaved caspase-1, ASC, and cleaved IL-1β (Fig. 3A–E). However, CDN1163 did not significantly affect NLRP3 protein levels, suggesting that CDN1163 may act downstream of NLRP3. Collectively, these findings indicate that CDN1163 ameliorates brain damage by inhibiting NLRP3-inflammasome signaling in post-stroke mice.

CDN1163 treatment attenuated oxidative damage and ameliorated neuronal cell death following stroke

We then sought to examine the effect of CDN1163 on oxidative stress. Our findings support the well-established view that lipid peroxidation, impaired antioxidant defence, and excessive generation of reactive species are key contributors to oxidative damage after ischemic stroke [32]. 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) (a lipid peroxidation product) and nitrotyrosine (reactive nitrogen species) markers are implicated in oxidative stress. Slot blot analysis showed a significant increase in the expression levels of 4-HNE and NT in the vehicle-treated group compared to the sham group following stroke injury (Fig. 4A–C). In contrast, post-treatment with CDN1163 markedly suppressed the expression of both oxidative stress markers relative to the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 4A–C). Furthermore, oxidative damage is a key contributor to neuronal dysfunction and cell death. To assess apoptosis, we analyzed the expression of apoptosis-related proteins by western blotting. The vehicle-treated group exhibited elevated levels of the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved PARP-1. In contrast, treatment with CDN1163 significantly downregulated the expression of these markers compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 4D–F). To assess late-stage apoptosis, we performed a TUNEL assay 72 h post-stroke. Mice treated with CDN1163 showed a significantly lower number of TUNEL-positive cells than the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 4G, H). These findings suggest that post-stroke administration of CDN1163 effectively mitigates oxidative stress and protects against neuronal cell death.

CDN1163 treatment restores oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) protein expression following stroke

We evaluated the expression levels of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complexes, which are responsible for ATP production and support mitochondrial bioenergetic function [33]. Mitochondrial dysfunction led to altered oxidative stress and cellular functions in the affected tissue [34]. A significant reduction in complexes I and IV was observed in the vehicle group compared to the sham group, indicating impaired mitochondrial function. In contrast, treatment with CDN1163 restored the expression levels of these complexes, suggesting a potential improvement in mitochondrial activity (Fig. 5B). Overall, these findings suggest that CDN1163 partially restores mitochondrial OXPHOS protein expression after stroke, particularly in complexes I and IV. (Fig. 5A, B).

CDN1163 treatment attenuated gait impairments following stroke

Ischemic stroke resulted in gait abnormalities and sensorimotor impairments in experimental animals [35]. To evaluate the impact of CDN1163 therapy on motor functional recovery, we performed catwalk tests. We conducted a comprehensive gait analysis to quantitatively assess footfalls and locomotion at 72 h post-stroke. The vehicle-treated mice showed worse footfall and locomotor activities compared to the sham mice. However, post-stroke administration of CDN1163 markedly improved gait performance (Fig. 6A–C), strongly indicating that it alleviates stroke-induced behavioral deficits.

CDN1163 improved non-spatial working memory and alleviated cognitive impairment following stroke

We then used the novel object recognition test to examine short-term recognition memory of experimental mice. We calculated the recognition index (RI) and discrimination index (DI) for each group and then compared the scores among groups. The RI was calculated from the ratio of time spent on a novel object to total exploration time. Vehicle-treated mice showed a significant decrease in RI and DI compared with sham mice (Fig. 7A–C). Interestingly, CDN1163 treatment improved non-spatial memory preference, as indicated by novel object recognition indices (Fig. 7A–C). To evaluate spatial learning and memory, we conducted the Morris water maze (MWM) test. Mice underwent six consecutive days of training to locate a hidden platform, averaging 4 trials per day. Vehicle-treated mice exhibited significantly prolonged escape latency compared to sham controls, indicating impaired spatial learning and memory following stroke (Fig. 7D, E). In contrast, CDN1163-treated mice demonstrated improved performance during the hidden platform trials, suggesting that CDN1163 enhances spatial learning in post-stroke conditions (Fig. 7D, E). The next day after hidden training, a probe trial was executed to evaluate reference memory consolidation. The average number of entries in the target quadrant was used to test different aspects of spatial memory. Vehicle-treated mice showed a decrease in the average number (frequency) of target quadrant entries compared with the sham group. However, the CDN1163-treated mice showed significant retention of spatial memory, as evidenced by a higher number of entries in the target quadrant (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

The major findings of this study reveal that CDN1163, a small-molecule activator of SERCA2, exerts robust neuroprotective effects in a preclinical model of ischemic stroke. Specifically, CDN1163 significantly suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and its downstream signaling, leading to reduced neuronal cell death. To our knowledge, this is the first study to show that targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway with CDN1163 improves both neuroprotection and behavioral outcomes following photothrombotic ischemic stroke in mice. Recent work by our group and others has demonstrated that although tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) remains the only FDA-approved pharmacological treatment for acute ischemic stroke, its clinical use is limited by risks of neurotoxicity and hemorrhagic transformation [36]. This underscores the urgent need for safer, more effective, and economically viable therapeutic alternatives. In this context, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of CDN1163 in mitigating post-stroke complications, including BBB dysfunction, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, neuronal loss, and deficits in gait and cognition. Our results reveal that CDN1163 significantly improved stroke outcomes by alleviating behavioral impairments, reducing oxidative damage and inflammation, and preserving neuronal viability, supporting its potential as a promising therapeutic modality for ischemic stroke. Additionally, CDN1163 enhanced mitochondrial function by upregulating OXPHOS protein expression after stroke. Importantly, these protective effects were accompanied by restoration of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, as demonstrated by calcium imaging, and by a significant improvement in SERCA ATPase activity, indicating that CDN1163 exerts its neuroprotective actions, at least in part, through recovery of SERCA-dependent calcium handling. Collectively, these findings support SERCA2 activation as a viable target in stroke management and its activator CDN1163 as a promising candidate for adjunctive or alternative therapy in ischemic stroke, offering a novel mechanism of action that addresses key limitations of current treatment strategies.

Behavioral deficits, including gait abnormalities and long-term cognitive impairments, are common long-term consequences of ischemic stroke in both clinical and preclinical settings [37]. CDN1163 treatment resulted in significant improvements in gait performance, as evidenced by enhanced CatWalk outcomes. Additionally, CDN1163 treatment led to substantial improvements in cognitive function, as demonstrated by performance in both the NOR and MWM tests. CDN1163 treatment significantly improved non-spatial memory, as measured by the recognition index (RI) and discrimination index (DI). It also showed a trend toward improved performance in the MWM, suggesting meaningful attenuation of cognitive impairments. Overall, the observed benefits in both spatial reference memory and non-spatial working memory align with prior findings that SERCA activators exert neuroprotective effects and improve cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease mouse models [38,39]. These behavioral improvements may also be linked to the restoration of calcium dynamics observed in our calcium imaging studies, suggesting that normalization of intracellular Ca2+ signaling contributes to preservation of neuronal function and cognitive performance following stroke.

Ischemic stroke is a primary driver of oxidative stress and cellular organelle dysfunction, which are central to NLRP3 inflammasome activation [40,41]. The NLRP3 inflammasome is a multiprotein complex that functions as a key innate immune sensor, activated in response to cellular stress signals such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial dysfunction, and ion flux disturbances. Once activated, NLRP3 recruits the adaptor protein ASC and pro-caspase-1, leading to the cleavage of caspase-1, which in turn transforms pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β into their active forms, initiating cell death and dysfunction [42,43]. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has been well-documented in cardiovascular and neurovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke and neurodegenerative diseases. In ischemic stroke, the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), along with oxidative damage and excitotoxicity, contributes to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. This activation amplifies neuroinflammation, promotes neuronal cell death, disrupts BBB integrity, and ultimately exacerbates neurological deficits [44]. Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that NLRP3 activation not only promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine release but also mediates cellular injury through caspase-1–dependent pyroptosis [45], further aggravating brain tissue damage. Notably, both pharmacological inhibition and genetic deletion of NLRP3 signaling have been shown to protect the brain from ischemic injury. The evaluation of NLRP3 inhibitors, including CY-09, MCC950, and OLT1177, in preclinical models of cerebral ischemia demonstrated neuroprotective effects through suppression of neuroinflammatory cascades, preservation of BBB integrity, and reduction of infarct volume and neurological disorders [46–48]. These inhibitors primarily act by blocking NLRP3 activation, inhibiting inflammasome assembly, and decreasing downstream caspase-1 expression/activity [49]. In the current study, we demonstrate that CDN1163 significantly inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation following stroke, as evidenced by downregulation of ASC, cleaved IL-1β, and cleaved caspase-1, despite no significant change in NLRP3 protein levels. This suggests that CDN1163 primarily targets downstream triggers of NLRP3 activation rather than directly disrupting inflammasome assembly. Because disturbed Ca2+ homeostasis is a known upstream trigger of inflammasome activation, our calcium imaging findings provide additional mechanistic support for the observed anti-inflammatory effects of CDN1163. Restoration of SERCA2 ATPase activity by CDN1163 may reduce ER stress, mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, and ROS generation, thereby limiting the intracellular signals that promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation. These findings are consistent with previously reported protective effects of CDN1163 against metabolic disorders [21,24] and highlight its therapeutic potential in targeting brain health signaling pathways to mitigate neurological damage [50,51]. However, it is conceivable to argue that given that CDN1163 significantly reduces overall infarct volume and cell death, the downregulation of NLRP3, 4-HNE, and NT could merely be secondary artifacts of having more surviving, healthy tissue rather than a direct pharmacological inhibition of these pathways. Nonetheless, we believe that CDN1163 has pleiotropic actions, and that its effects on post-stroke inflammation and oxidative stress likely reflect a combination of indirect consequences of reduced tissue injury and direct pharmacological actions. Our work also supports direct anti-inflammatory effects of CDN1163, independent of neuroprotection (unpublished). We demonstrated that CDN1163 directly suppresses inflammatory signaling in microglial cells, including inhibition of NLRP3 activation, even in the absence of neuronal injury (unpublished). Thus, while reduced infarct burden likely contributes to the attenuated inflammatory and oxidative markers observed at 72 h post-stroke, the direct pharmacological effects of CDN1163 on microglia and inflammatory signaling provide a mechanistic basis for interpreting these changes as more than secondary artifacts. While the evidence presented is compelling, further investigation is warranted to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which CDN1163 modulates inflammasome activation.

In the present study, ischemic stroke mice exhibited a significant increase in 4-HNE, a key biomarker of lipid peroxidation, along with elevated nitrotyrosine, a well-established marker of nitrosative stress [32]. CDN1163 treatment effectively diminished these markers of oxidative damage, consistent with previous reports highlighting its antioxidative properties [21,24,52]. Oxidative stress is a major pathological condition of ischemic stroke, resulting from excessive production of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) due to mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired OXPHOS following ischemic insult in the brain [4]. Disruptions in OXPHOS impair ATP generation, leading to energy dyshomeostasis, ion imbalance, excitotoxicity, and initiation of multiple downstream cell death pathways [53]. Furthermore, oxidative stress following ischemic stroke activates complex cellular and molecular cascades, including the upregulation of pro-apoptotic protein Bax and cleaved PARP-1, which are central mediators of apoptosis. In the present study, ischemic mice demonstrated elevated expression of pro-apoptotic markers Bax and cleaved PARP-1, indicating activation of apoptotic signaling pathways [54]. Treatment with CDN1163 effectively downregulated the expression of these proteins, thereby attenuating apoptosis and promoting neuronal survival. This neuroprotective effect was further supported by a significant reduction in TUNEL-positive cells in CDN1163-treated mice, confirming its ability to prevent neuronal cell death. Beyond apoptosis, ischemic stroke pathogenesis involves a broad array of degenerative processes, including mitochondrial dysfunction, OXPHOS impairment, calcium overload, inflammation, excitotoxicity, and BBB disruption [55]. Dysfunctional mitochondria not only compromise energy metabolism but also generate excessive ROS, amplifying oxidative damage and further impairing neuronal function [56]. In this context, calcium dysregulation is a key pathogenic event linking ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal injury. Impaired SERCA2 function after ischemic insult limits Ca2+ reuptake into the ER, leading to sustained cytosolic Ca2+ elevation and pathological mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation. Our calcium imaging results support this concept by showing disrupted intracellular Ca2+ dynamics after stroke, whereas CDN1163 treatment restored a more physiological calcium pattern. In parallel, SERCA2 ATPase activity was significantly reduced in ischemic stroke and was improved by CDN1163 treatment, demonstrating that the compound functionally enhances SERCA2 activity in the injured brain. CDN1163 treatment enhanced OXPHOS protein expression, thereby improving mitochondrial bioenergetics. Thus, activation of SERCA2 by CDN1163 helps maintain rhythmic Ca2+ signaling to mitochondria, supporting efficient ATP production while preventing sustained Ca2+ overload that can lead to bioenergetic failure and neuronal cell death. The current findings suggest that CDN1163 confers multifaceted neuroprotective effects in the ischemic brain by mitigating oxidative and nitrosative stresses and suppressing apoptotic pathways. Importantly, these effects are likely coupled to restoration of calcium homeostasis and improved SERCA2 enzymatic function, which together may preserve ER-mitochondrial communication and neuronal bioenergetic stability. These neuronal effects are consistent with our previous findings showing that restoration of SERCA2a activity through administration of the small-molecule activator CDN1163 enhances mitochondrial efficiency and inhibits ROS-driven cell death in diabetes and heart failure models [21,24]. Future studies should focus on delineating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying CDN1163’s therapeutic effects, particularly its impact on mitochondrial function and other neuroinflammatory signaling pathways. Further work should also define how CDN1163 modulates Ca2+ flux kinetics and SERCA2 ATPase function in different neural cell populations following ischemic injury. Such insights will be critical for advancing our understanding of CDN1163’s translational potential as a viable treatment strategy for ischemic stroke.

This study also has some limitations that should be addressed in future studies. First, there is a lack of long-term follow-up data concerning the sex-specific effects of CDN1163 treatment during ischemic stroke recovery since this study used only male C57BL/6 J mice.

Secondly, the time-dependent molecular mechanisms by which CDN1163 affects downstream inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, including OXPHOS expression, require further investigation to elucidate their roles in protection against ischemic stroke and to establish fundamental associations. We previously demonstrated that CDN1163 may confer protective metabolic benefits through SERCA2b activation of AMPK and PGC1α elevation, leading to improvements in ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in liver tissue [21]. Whether these molecular pathways are also activated in the setting of ischemic stroke remains to be determined. Thirdly, while our results offer valuable insights into the effects of CDN1163 in a well-established stroke model, additional work is needed to validate its efficacy across different ischemic stroke models, including embolic and MCAO models, as well as aging-related ischemic stroke.

Fourth, the clinical translation of the neuroprotective effects of CDN1163 requires careful consideration of its optimal dosing (dose-response curve), route of administration, and potential side effects, which were not addressed in this study, although our previous study in diabetes showed the compound had no apparent toxicity or CDN1163-related pathology [21].

Fifth, we acknowledge that administering the first dose of CDN1163 at 1 h post-stroke represents an earlier intervention than is typically achievable in clinical settings. In this study, however, the 1-hour time point was intentionally selected as an initial proof-of-concept for two key reasons. First, early post-ischemic treatment is a standard starting point in preclinical neuroprotection research, particularly when evaluating a novel therapeutic mechanism such as SERCA2 activation and its influence on acute inflammatory signaling, Ca2+ dysregulation, and apoptotic pathways. Using this early window allowed us to directly test whether modulating SERCA2 during the initial phase of ischemia–reperfusion injury is sufficient to produce robust neuroprotection and to clearly delineate downstream molecular effects under conditions where the intervention is most likely to exert its full biological impact. Second, the primary objective of the present work was mechanistic rather than translational - specifically, to establish that pharmacological SERCA2 activation with CDN1163 can modulate inflammatory stress, oxidative stress, and apoptosis after stroke. Defining the therapeutic time window was beyond the scope of this initial study. To address this important translational question, future work will systematically evaluate delayed dosing paradigms (e.g., 3–12 h post-occlusion) to determine how long after stroke SERCA2 activation remains effective and to identify the latest time point at which CDN1163 can still confer neuroprotection.

Finally, we acknowledge that the use of whole ipsilateral brain homogenates - which contain a mixture of neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and endothelial cells - does not permit cell type-specific resolution of the responses to CDN1163. Therefore, the present study cannot definitively identify whether neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, or other components of the neurovascular unit are the principal responders to SERCA2b activation. Rather, our tissue-level analyses were designed to determine whether pharmacological activation of SERCA2 confers overall neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects after stroke. Given that SERCA2b is broadly involved in intracellular Ca2+ regulation across multiple neural and vascular cell populations, the protective effects of CDN1163 are likely mediated through coordinated multicellular responses. Future studies employing cell-specific markers and mechanistic approaches will be required to define the dominant cellular targets of CDN1163 in the post-stroke brain.

In summary, we show that CDN1163, an allosteric activator of SERCA2, offers considerable neuroprotective benefits in a well-established stroke model. It alleviates cognitive and gait impairments, reduces neuroinflammation, and attenuates neuronal cell death and dysfunction. Furthermore, CDN1163 regulates inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby decreasing oxidative damage and lessening apoptosis, and improving mitochondrial efficiency. In addition, CDN1163 restores intracellular calcium homeostasis and improves SERCA ATPase activity, further supporting the conclusion that correction of calcium dyshomeostasis is a central mechanism underlying its neuroprotective effects. Our observation strongly suggests that CDN1163 could be a promising therapeutic target for improving ischemic stroke cellular and functional damage by addressing both inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways. Future work should focus on optimizing these strategies for clinical translation.
