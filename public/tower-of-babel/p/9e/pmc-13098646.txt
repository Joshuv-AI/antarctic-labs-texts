Matrin-3 forms spherical and wormlike assemblies that are modulated by RNA binding and ALS/FTD-associated mutations
Authors: Macy L Sprunger, Min Kyung Shinn, Sabrina K Talir, Ken Lee, Rohit V Pappu, Meredith E Jackrel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMatrin-3 (MATR3) is an RNA-binding protein (RBP) that is associated with familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). MATR3 features two RNA recognition motifs, two zinc-finger motifs, and four intrinsically disordered regions. Here, we report that human MATR3 associates with itself to form nanoscale spherical assemblies at ultralow protein concentrations. Through concentration-dependent associations, the spheres, which are 20–30 nm in diameter, transition into wormlike assemblies. These observations are reminiscent of sphere-to-worm transitions and micellization of amphiphilic molecules. Using computations and experiments, we discovered that the pattern of inter-domain attractions and repulsions gives MATR3 an inverse bolaamphiphile-like architecture that explains the concentration-dependent assembly characteristics. RNA binding causes shortening of wormlike assemblies of MATR3, whereas ALS/FTD-associated mutations render MATR3 assemblies less responsive to modulation by RNA. Overall, our findings highlight the unique assemblies formed by MATR3 while also showing how RNA-dependent interactions and ALS/FTD-associated mutations modulate the assemblies.

INTRODUCTION

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is defined by the degeneration of motor neurons.1-3 ALS exists on a disease spectrum with frontotemporal dementia (FTD), which affects the frontal and temporal cortices.1-3 Mutations in over 40 different genes have been implicated in ALS/FTD, among which numerous RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) have been identified. These include TDP-43,4 FUS,5,6 hnRNPA1,7 hnRNPA2B1,7 EWSR1,8,9 TAF15,9,10 ATXN2,11 TIA-1,12 and Matrin-3 (MATR3).13,14 Homotypic interactions, which are intermolecular interactions between the same molecules, of IDRs in RBPs are known to drive phase separation. One hypothesis is that aberrant phase transitions might be associated with ALS/FTD pathology and dysregulated RNA metabolism.2,15 These aberrant phase transitions are thought to be driven by unregulated homotypic interactions of the RBP and loss of heterotypic interactions between the RBP and RNA.1,2,15-18

MATR3 is a DNA- and RNA-binding nuclear matrix protein encompassing two folded tandem RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) and two zinc-finger motifs (ZFs). The remaining 70% of the protein is intrinsically disordered.19 MATR3 is implicated in a range of processes, including DNA damage response, transcription, splicing, RNA degradation, mRNA export, X chromosome inactivation, and chromatin attachment to the nuclear matrix.19,20 There is conflicting evidence regarding the localization of MATR3 to sodium arsenite-induced stress granules, although a truncated construct of MATR3 lacking the C-terminal RRM and IDR localizes to cytoplasmic mRNA processing bodies.20-24 However, pathological inclusions of wild-type (WT) and mutant MATR3 have been observed in sporadic ALS/FTD and a distal myopathy.13,25 While many ALS/FTD-linked RBPs, including TDP-43 and FUS, contain prion-like low complexity domains (PLCDs) that drive phase separation, MATR3 does not harbor a PLCD.14,16,26,27 However, MATR3 constructs with RRM deletions coalesce into spherical puncta with apparent liquid-like properties.23,26,28-31 While MATR3 pathology is directly linked to fewer ALS/FTD cases than TDP-43 or FUS, MATR3 cytoplasmic inclusions are also positive for TDP-43. Further, MATR3 and TDP-43 have an RNA-dependent interaction.1,2,13,32

The prevalence of MATR3 inclusions in cells highlights the need to better understand the types of assemblies MATR3 can form and if these assemblies are consistent with phase separation. In previous studies, we found that full-length MATR3 forms shell-like, micron-scale puncta in yeast.26 Disease-associated mutations altered the dynamics and morphology of these puncta.26 Shell-like structures on the micron scale are suggestive of clustered micellar or nanoscale assemblies driven by competing contributions from inter-domain and intermolecular attractions and repulsions.33-35 MATR3 also displays strong nuclear staining in mammalian cells, with a granular rather than punctate pattern.13,22,36 Further, studies have shown key differences in MATR3 localization upon disruption of RNA binding.23,26,28-31 Both RRMs contribute to RNA binding, favoring single-stranded pyrimidine-rich RNA sequences in polypyrimidine tracts and introns.19,37,38 Additionally, MATR3 RRMs were found to drive toxicity in yeast and Drosophila models.26,39 Despite the importance of MATR3 RRMs in driving toxicity and the implications of RNA binding on phase separation, the specific residues driving its RNA binding have not been identified, resulting in many MATR3 studies utilizing RRM deletion constructs.

In this study, we developed methods for purification of recombinant full-length MATR3 and elucidated its intrinsic and RNA-dependent phase behaviors. We discovered that MATR3 forms nanoscale spherical and wormlike assemblies that are size-limited. These assemblies are distinct from those formed via macrophase separation.40 We identified key residues of MATR3 mediating RNA binding and delineated the key features of RNA sequences influencing binding-mediated changes to nanoscale assemblies or their relative stabilities. Finally, we report that ALS/FTD-associated mutations alter MATR3 assemblies and the driving forces for forming assemblies.

RESULTS

MATR3 forms higher-order assemblies in vitro

Beyond the canonical DNA- and RNA-binding domains of MATR3, we have defined four IDRs (Figures 1A and 1B).19,26 Sixteen patient-derived mutations have been identified in MATR3. These include two RRM mutations associated with childhood neurological disorders, while the remaining mutations, localized to IDRs, are ALS-associated (Figure 1A).13,19,41-48 First, we investigated whether recombinant MATR3 undergoes canonical macrophase separation in vitro. To overcome the poor solubility of MATR3 in vitro,29 we employed an established strategy used successfully for TDP-43 and FUS49,50 whereby we fused maltose-binding protein (MBP) to the N terminus of full-length human MATR3 to promote MATR3 solubility. We incorporated a tobacco etch virus protease (TEV) cleavage site between MBP and MATR3 to generate a construct referred to hereafter as MBP-MATR3. We expressed MBP-MATR3 in mammalian cells and purified it under native conditions (Figure 1C). Following TEV protease cleavage of MBP-MATR3, solutions became turbid in less than 1 h. In the absence of TEV, the solution became only slightly turbid over 12 h (Figure 1D). Sedimentation analysis also showed that MATR3 accumulates in the insoluble fraction in a TEV-dependent fashion (Figures 1E and 1F). Accumulation of MATR3 in the pellet was detected within 30 min and was nearly complete by 4 h.

We next employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to characterize the higher-order assemblies. Following the addition of TEV, MATR3 forms wormlike assemblies with a cross-sectional diameter of ~20–25 nm and a length of ~100–400 nm (Figure 1G). These assemblies formed within 30 min, while in the absence of TEV, assemblies were only noted after 4 h. The wormlike species formed by MATR3 are reminiscent of assemblies formed by amphiphilic molecules such as surfactants and block copolymers.52

MATR3 forms micron-scale assemblies in the presence of macromolecular crowders

It has been proposed that MATR3 can undergo macrophase separation in cells.24,26,29,30 Macrophase separation refers to the separation of a system into coexisting, homogeneous, solvent-rich, and protein-rich phases that coarsen to form micron-scale macrophases. These macroscopic phases can be liquids or solids.53-55

Microscopy did not reveal the presence of micron-scale assemblies over a 24-h period (Figure S1A). In other systems, macrophase separation can be induced by crowding agents.56-58 Upon addition of dextran, we observed ~1.0–1.5 μm assemblies of MBP-MATR3 (Figures 1H, S1B, and S1C). Assemblies formed with dextran but without TEV showed a dispersed and rounded appearance, while those formed with dextran and TEV adopted irregular shapes (Figure 1H). We examined the same conditions by TEM and observed wormlike assemblies under all conditions (Figure 1I). Wormlike assemblies formed in all conditions regardless of micron-scale assembly formation, while the presence of both dextran and TEV resulted in the formation of additional spherical assemblies at 120 min (Figure 1I, white arrows).

Next, we investigated the concentration-dependence of MATR3 assemblies. Bright-field microscopy revealed distinct spherical species with diameters up to ~1.0–1.5 μm that increased in abundance with protein concentration (Figures 1J and S1D). At higher concentrations or longer time points, MATR3 assemblies clustered (Figures 1J, S1E, and S1F) and did not coalesce as would be expected for macrophase separation.58-68 Further, as concentration increased, the number of micron-scale assemblies increased without a notable increase in assembly size. Importantly, TEM revealed that wormlike assemblies were present at all concentrations (Figure 1K). To reconcile the observation of nanoscale wormlike assemblies by TEM with micron-scale assemblies observed by bright-field microscopy, we investigated the ultrastructural features of MATR3 by embedding and sectioning the assemblies for TEM (Figure 1L). This revealed that the micron-scale assemblies associate to form clusters lacking distinct boundaries. Fragments consistent with wormlike assemblies were also present. Clusters of wormlike assemblies are distinct from macrophases. For MATR3, we propose that micron-scale clusters of wormlike assemblies form via crowder-induced, depletion-mediated attractions.56,69

MATR3 forms size-limited spherical assemblies that transition to wormlike microphases

Micellization or microphase separation is defined by the formation of size-limited assemblies.70-72 To test if MATR3 forms size-limited assemblies, we measured assembly size by microfluidic resistive pulse sensing (MRPS). MRPS helps quantify particle size distributions, irrespective of particle shape, by measuring transit times and electrical currents of particles as they pass through a nanoscale channel.73 Currents are converted into species counts and the effective volume, accounting for non-spherical morphologies of wormlike assemblies. We report sizes as occupancy diameters, which correspond to the diameter of a sphere of equivalent volume as the transiting species. The counts per particle size quantify abundance, and this was monitored at a series of concentrations (Figure 2A) and plotted as a function of MATR3 concentration (Figures 2B, 2C, and S2A). The sizes measured were limited to ~295–335 nm over a 16-fold range of MATR3 concentration (Figure 2B). While the sizes span a limited range, abundance increases with MATR3 concentration (Figure 2C).

We next performed TEM over a 500-fold concentration range (Figure 2D). At lower concentrations (0.01–0.08 μM MATR3), we observed sparse wormlike assemblies (~20 nm diameter, ~50–100 nm length). However, at these concentrations we noted spherical assemblies of ~20–30 nm diameter. At intermediate concentrations (0.02–1.0 μM MATR3), a mixture of spherical and wormlike assemblies formed. At 5.0 μM MATR3, only wormlike assemblies were observed (Figures 1G and 2D). Importantly, the ratio of spherical-to-wormlike assemblies changed across this range. Spheres were more abundant below 0.20 μM MATR3, the two species were of similar abundances at 0.20 μM MATR3, and wormlike assemblies were more abundant above 0.20 μM MATR3.

Microphase separation and/or micellization are characterized by the presence of one or more threshold concentrations.52,74,75 TEM analysis suggested an apparent threshold concentration for spherical assembly formation at ~0.02 μM. We also observe a second transition to wormlike assemblies. We measured this second threshold, designated csw, using right-angle light scattering (RALS). We observed a discontinuity in scattering intensity, indicating a transition, at sub-micromolar concentrations of MATR3 (~0.2 μM) (Figures 2E and 2F). At this concentration, both spherical and wormlike assemblies are present (Figure 2D). We designated the discontinuity as a signature of the transition from spherical to wormlike assemblies. Above csw, we observed increased abundance of wormlike assemblies and decreased abundance of spherical assemblies (Figures 2D and 2F). Similar trends are observed in the absence of TEV, although here csw is significantly increased (Figures 2E, 2F, and 2H).

Next, we applied a Rayleigh analysis (Equation 1 in STAR Methods) to estimate the apparent interaction coefficient, A2, between pairs of spherical assemblies. Positive A2 values signify repulsive interactions, whereas negative values signify attractions.76,77 The estimated A2 values in the absence versus the presence of TEV are −1.0 × 10−5 mol·mL/g2 and −4.6×10−5 mol·mL/g2, respectively (Figure 2G). Cleaving the MBP tag enhances associations between spherical assemblies, thereby enhancing the drive for forming wormlike assemblies and lowering csw.

To investigate the mesoscale properties of MATR3 assemblies, we generated relatively homogenous populations of spherical and wormlike assemblies and subjected them to centrifugation to generate soluble and pellet fractions. By TEM, both fractions maintained their morphologies and sizes following centrifugation (Figure 2I). Both the spherical and wormlike assemblies increased in abundance in the pellet, though they did not fuse under these high-force conditions. MATR3 showed negligible Thioflavin T staining, and the assemblies were readily disrupted in 1% SDS (Figures S2B-S2E). Furthermore, by RALS, we find that SDS ablates the discontinuity observed in the absence of SDS (Figures S2E and 2E). These observations indicate that the assemblies are not amyloid fibrils or other types of irreversible aggregates.

Overall, our data show that MATR3 forms spherical assemblies at low concentrations. These assemblies transition to wormlike structures above a threshold concentration. The assemblies are solid-like given that they do not fuse when forced together via centrifugation. Our data rule out macrophase separation as the mode of assembly. Additionally, the nanoscale assemblies and micron-scale clusters of the assemblies are distinct from amyloid fibers or amorphous aggregates. Instead, these assemblies are consistent with a process akin to micellization or microphase separation, giving rise to spherical and wormlike assemblies that can associate to form micron-scale clusters. Microphase separation has recently been demonstrated for other RRM-containing proteins.75

MATR3 forms elongated nanoscale assemblies under physiological conditions

To investigate the physiological assemblies of MATR3, we visualized endogenous MATR3 in HEK293T cells with super-resolution stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy (Figure 2J). MATR3 is reported to display granular or speckled nuclear staining by confocal microscopy.13,22,36 Endogenous MATR3 displayed granular nucleoplasmic staining using antibodies specific to either the N or C terminus (Figures 2J and S2F). Yet at this higher magnification, MATR3 particles were elongated and formed an interconnected network, with the short diameter measuring ~50 nm (Figure 2K), consistent with previous work.31 Importantly, even with the different environment in cells, where MATR3 likely interacts with various nucleic acids and proteins, we still observed nanoscale assemblies.

Next, we investigated whether endogenous MATR3 assemblies showed concentration-dependent morphological changes by generating a concentration gradient. At low levels (~80% decrease) following small interfering RNA (siRNA) treatment, MATR3 assemblies form similar nanoscale elongated particles with lower abundance but a consistent ~45–60 nm short diameter between control and treated cells (Figures 2L-2N). At higher levels upon plasmid overexpression (~4.5× increase), MATR3 assemblies still formed similarly shaped particles with higher abundance and a consistent ~45–60 nm short diameter (Figures 2O-2Q). In sum, cellular MATR3 forms elongated assemblies of consistent size and shape over a wide concentration range, indicating that the MATR3 concentrations investigated were above the csw threshold.

Simulations suggest that MATR3 is akin to a flexible inverse bolaamphiphile

Based on the modular sequence architecture of MATR3 (Figure 1A), we analyzed the compositional biases and binary sequence patterns within each of the four IDRs using the NARDINI+ algorithm.78-81 The statistically significant features are as follows: in IDR1, there is enrichment of leucine and aliphatic residues combined with depletion of acid and residues that promote chain solvation (Figure 3A). IDR2 is enriched in aromatic residues, especially tyrosine, and these residues are uniformly distributed with respect to acidic residues. IDR3 is defined by clustering of polar residues, specifically glycine patches, and depletion of glutamate versus aspartate. Finally, IDR4 is enriched in aspartate residues and lysine patches. IDR4 is also significantly depleted of arginine and enriched in residues that drive chain solvation.

To explore how the IDRs and folded domains interact with one another, we performed atomistic simulations using the ABSINTH implicit solvation model and forcefield paradigm.82,83 First, we computed the radius of gyration (Rg) distribution for MATR3. There are four distinct modes in the Rg distributions located at ~45Å, ~52Å, ~62Å, and ~70Å (Figure 3B). Conformations of smaller Rg are observed more often than extended conformations of larger Rg. We also computed distances between all pairs of residues and normalized them against intramolecular distances for an ideal, non-interactive system (Figure 3C). For a pair of residues i and j, we define sij as the scaled distance between residues i and j, which is computed as (rij/rij,ideal), where rij is the average distance between residues i and j computed using the ABSINTH model, and rij,ideal is the distance obtained from the ideal, non-interacting model. Using inter-residue distances, we computed average scaled distances SAB for pairs of domains A and B. SAB was then used to compute an excess inter-domain interaction parameter as ΔAB = (SAB–1). If ΔAB is less than zero, the inter-domain interactions are attractive. Conversely, if ΔAB is greater than zero, the inter-domain interactions are repulsive. Finally, if ΔAB = 0, attractions counterbalance repulsions.

The intra-domain interactions are essentially ideal, as evidenced by excess interaction coefficient values (ΔAB) along the diagonal being zero or near zero with the exception of IDR3 (Figure 3C). IDR1 and ZF2, which are located at the N and C terminus, respectively, are defined by negative ΔAB values for all inter-domain interactions. Interactions involving IDR1 and ZF2 are the strongest inter-domain attractions. This architecture, defined by attractions mediated by the terminal domains and repulsions involving domains through the middle, is reminiscent of inverse bolaamphiphiles.84 These molecules have hydrophobic termini and hydrophilic middle regions.84-87 Thus, we classify MATR3 as a flexible inverse bolaamphiphile (Figure 3C). Amphiphiles come in different types and can form a range of supramolecular assemblies.74,84 Theories propose the existence of an energy gap whereby spherical assemblies form at low concentrations.74 Above a threshold concentration, where spherical assemblies are saturated, the amphiphilic molecules are predicted to be incorporated into wormlike assemblies governed by packing considerations. Theory also predicts that the threshold concentration for the formation of spherical assemblies should be lower than that for the formation of wormlike assemblies.74

As protein concentrations increase, inter-domain interactions also occur between molecules. To assess the nature of these intermolecular, inter-domain interactions, we performed simulations of pairs of MATR3 domains unconstrained by chain connectivity (Figure 3D). Here, many intramolecular, inter-domain attractions are weakened or even repulsive. For example, the inter-domain interactions between IDR4 and IDR2 as well as IDR4 with itself become strongly repulsive (Figure 3D). Additionally, while homotypic associations between IDR1 become stronger between unconstrained domains, all other inter-domain attractions between IDR1 are weakened by the lack of constraints imposed by chain connectivity. There is also a gain of homotypic attractions, such as those between RRM1 and IDR3. Inter-domain attractions that are stronger at the level of an individual molecule when compared with pairs of molecules will oppose assembly, whereas the converse will be true for inter-domain attractions that are stronger between molecules as opposed to the single molecule. The presence of strong inter-domain repulsions will cap the sizes of assemblies as a consequence of intermolecular associations. The collection of computations provides a molecular-scale rationalization for the experimentally observed assemblies.

Truncation of the C terminus of MATR3 disrupts the inverse bolaamphiphile and gives rise to flower-shaped assemblies

To test hypotheses generated by the simulations, we perturbed the inverse bolaamphiphile-like character of MATR3. Specifically, we generated MBP-MATR3(1–575), which lacks IDR4 and ZF2, a key region interacting with the N-terminal portion of MATR3 (Figures 3C-3E). Single-chain simulations revealed that the interaction patterns for MATR3(1–575) become more akin to those of canonical amphiphiles (Figure 3E). RALS reveals two discontinuities at 0.04 and 0.40 μM, which we term c1 and c2, respectively (Figures 3F and 3G). Both transitions have apparent A2 values with small magnitudes, although above c1 the A2 is positive (repulsive), while above c2 the A2 is negative (attractive) (Figure 3H). These patterns suggest that assemblies become capped in size at the first threshold and grow directionally to accommodate repulsions on one scale and attractions on a different scale.88

By TEM, we observed spherical assemblies (~30–70 nm diameter) at the lowest concentration (0.02 μM), while wormlike assemblies (~25–40 nm along the short axis, ~50–100 nm along the long axis) were observed near c1 (0.05 μM) and above (Figure 3I). The assemblies formed by MBP-MATR3(1–575) appear similar to those formed by full-length MBP-MATR3 (Figure 3J). Strikingly, above 0.08 μM, we observe new types of assemblies that are donut-shaped and smaller than the spherical assemblies (Figure 3I, pink arrowheads). Higher magnification imaging reveals a flower shape, with six petals (16 nm diameter) and a columnar arrangement on the side view (16 nm length) (Figure 3K). MRPS measurements correspond to results with full-length protein at intermediate concentrations where wormlike assemblies form (Figures 3L and 3M). As concentrations increase, we observe a rightward shift to the cumulative distribution functions, consistent with increased likelihoods of forming larger assemblies. Extrapolating the top and side views suggests a probable orientation of six cylindrical micelles into a flower shape (Figure 3N).

MATR3 displays RNA-binding preferences that are distinct from those of TDP-43

We next dissected the RNA-binding preferences of MATR3. First, we determined surface electrostatic potentials (SEPs) for MATR3 RRMs and RRMs of paraspeckle RBPs that share an affinity for the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) NEAT1 (Figure 4A).89,90 The RRMs of essential paraspeckle RBPs (category 1) have a positive median SEP, the RRMs of RBPs that regulate paraspeckle size and number (category 2) have a median negative SEP, and the RRMs of nonessential paraspeckle RBPs (category 3)90 have a median SEP near zero. While MATR3 is not included in these categories, RRM1 and RRM2 of MATR3 display median SEPs near zero or positive, similar to categories 1 and 3. Furthermore, MATR3 RRM surfaces are both amphoteric and amphiphilic with oppositely charged faces and hydrophobic patches (Figures 4B and 4C). Notably, TDP-43 falls in category 2, suggesting that TDP-43 has a different binding mechanism than MATR3, even with shared RNA substrates like NEAT1.

We next performed fluorescence polarization binding assays using different RNA sequences to probe MATR3 and TDP-43 binding (Figures 4D and S3A; Table S1). The GU-rich consensus sequence, A(GU)6AA, binds TDP-4392 and is being evaluated as a therapeutic for ALS/FTD.93,94 TDP-43 binds A(GU)6AA tightly (Kdapp = 36 ± 6 nM), while binding to MATR3 is weaker (Kdapp = 116 ± 19 nM). Neither TDP-43 nor MATR3 binds the negative control, A(CA)6AA.92,93 A MATR3 binding motif, (UCUUUC)2AA, identified from eCLIP data39 tightly binds only MATR3 (Kdapp = 26 ± 4 nM). However, with additional repeats, this sequence bound both TDP-43 (Kdapp = 42 ± 11 nM) and MATR3 (Kdapp < 20 nM). Our results indicate that MATR3 displays distinct binding preferences dependent on RNA sequence, composition, and length. Importantly, at the low concentrations used in the binding assays, MATR3 is in either a spherical or wormlike assembly state.

RNA oligonucleotides regulate MATR3 phase behaviors in a sequence-specific manner

Next, we sought to define the role of RNA in modulating MATR3 assemblies. Each of the RNA sequences tested increased the turbidity of MATR3 solutions, suggesting enhanced assembly (Figure 4E). Addition of (UCUUUC)6AA resulted in the greatest increase, while (UCUUUC)2AA showed the smallest increase. Even A(CA)6AA increased turbidity, suggesting that these changes are driven by the increased length and/or number of RNA-binding sites and not by affinity alone.

We next measured occupancy diameters and abundance of clusters of assemblies using MRPS at a 1:1 MATR3:RNA molar ratio (Figures 4F, 4G, and S3B). The clusters were size-limited, similar to MATR3 alone, although the sizes decreased in the presence of RNA. Importantly, the abundances reach RNA-sequence-specific plateaus, indicating that cluster abundance is limited by the number of available binding sites on the RNA. We performed RALS assays but found that the addition of RNA did not statistically alter csw (Figures 4H and S3C). However, the RNA substrates significantly decreased the magnitudes of A2 (Figure 4I). This indicates a weaker attraction between spherical assemblies, as noted by smaller diameters in the presence of RNA (Figure 4F). The smaller magnitude of A2 also indicates enhanced stability of the spherical assemblies.

By TEM, we observed sphere-to-worm transitions at csw with all RNA substrates (Figure 4J). Notably, as the affinity of the RNA–MATR3 interaction increased, the diameters of the wormlike assemblies remained unchanged, but the lengths changed in an RNA-specific manner (no RNA: ~50–150 nm, A(CA)6AA: ~70–170 nm, A(GU)6AA: ~50–140 nm, (UCUUUC)2AA: ~40– 100 nm, (UCUUUC)6AA: ~30–55 nm), consistent with the MRPS occupancy diameters (Figure 4F). These differences persisted even at 5 μM MATR3 (Figures S3D and S3E). These data indicate that MATR3-RNA ribonucleoprotein complexes also form wormlike assemblies, and the driving forces for assembly weaken in an RNA-dependent manner, with higher affinity interactions decreasing assembly length.

To determine whether RNA can modulate cellular MATR3 phases, we transfected RNA into HEK-293T cells and imaged endogenous MATR3 by STED microscopy (Figure 4K). Treatment with A(CA)6AA, A(GU)6AA, or (UCUUUC)2AA yielded nanoscale elongated assemblies of MATR3 in the nucleoplasm similar to the lipofectamine control. Treatment with (UCUUUC)6AA RNA drove formation of multiple shell-like nuclear foci of MATR3 with diameters greater than 500 nm (Figures 4L and 4M). Similar shell-like assemblies of MATR3 were observed in our yeast model.26 Our results indicate that MATR3 assemblies are modulated by RNA both in vitro and in cells, and we postulate that MATR3 forms vesicular structures in cells through lateral, RNA-mediated associations of wormlike assemblies.

MATR3 RRM2 utilizes noncanonical residues to mediate RNA binding

To understand how RRMs contribute to RNA binding and RNA-mediated assembly, we deployed a more sophisticated approach that differs drastically from deleting RRMs. We employed our MATR3 yeast model to identify point mutations that ablate RNA binding while maintaining overall MATR3 organization.26 Residues H401 and F438 of RRM1 and H499 and F536 of RRM2 are in conserved β-sheet RNP positions, which typically engage RNA (Figures 5A and S4A).95-97 Screening over 30 missense mutations identified Y526 and Q534 as positions of interest in RRM2. Adapting strategies used with TDP-43,11,92 we identified the variant H401L-F438L-Y526L-Q534L (hereafter referred to as MATR3:mRRM or mRRM), which showed the most similar toxicity and staining compared with RRM deletion controls (Figures S4B-S4E).26 Analogous mutational studies of key chelating residues within the MATR3 ZFs did not significantly alter toxicity or staining (Figure S5).

MATR3:mRRM displayed approximately 5-fold weaker binding against (UCUUUC)2AA in an isolated MATR3-RRM background (MATR3(398-575)-6xHis) compared with MATR3:WT (Figures S4F and S4G; Table S2). Mutations in MATR3 that impair RNA binding disrupt the formation of wormlike assemblies

Having identified MATR3:mRRM, we investigated how altered RNA-binding modulates assembly. While TEV cleavage and/or addition of (UCUUUC)6AA increased MATR3:WT turbidity, neither had a large effect on MATR3:mRRM, which overall showed less turbidity than WT (Figure 5C). We observed size-limited behavior for MATR3:mRRM similar to MATR3:WT and decreased aggregate abundance compared with MATR3:WT (Figures 5D, 5E, and S6C). Furthermore, changes were independent of the presence of (UCUUUC)6AA (Figures 5D and 5E). RALS measurements of MATR3:mRRM indicated a csw of ≈0.2 μM, which is identical to MATR3:WT (Figures 5F and S6D). However, we noted a statistically significant, ~40% decrease in the magnitude of A2 compared with MATR3:WT (Figure 5G). Conversely, MATR3:mRRM with (UCUUUC)6AA displayed a non-significant increase in csw to ≈0.3 μM compared with MATR3:WT and further decreased the magnitude of A2 compared with MATR3:mRRM alone (Figures 5F and 5G). These results indicate that MATR3:mRRM is a weaker driver of wormlike assembly, manifested in the abundance and interactions of assemblies. The smaller magnitude of A2 also indicates that RRM mutations enhance the stability of spherical assemblies.

Examining MATR3:mRRM by TEM revealed small spherical assemblies with few wormlike assemblies below csw (Figure 5H). A transition from spherical to wormlike assemblies occurs at csw, although the wormlike structures of mRRM were morphologically distinct from those of MATR3:WT. Above csw, we observed a mixed population of short wormlike structures (~25–35 nm diameter, ~60–140 nm long) and irregular assemblies (~30–40 nm diameter), independent of RNA. Well above csw, we note short wormlike assemblies (~20–30 nm diameter, ~50–100 nm long) and irregular spherical assemblies (~30–50 nm diameter) for MATR3:mRRM (Figure S6E). Further, MATR3:mRRM does not form crowder-induced micron-scale aggregates (Figure S6F). Comparative assessments of interactions within individual MATR3 molecules and across MATR3 domains show that patterns of intra- and intermolecular inter-domain interactions are altered by RRM mutations (Figures S6G and S6H). While the inverse bolaamphiphile nature is preserved, there are stronger intramolecular repulsions accompanying the RRM mutations. Together, these data show that the key residues modulating RNA binding contribute to the formation of elongated wormlike assemblies and are central to assembly accumulation in the presence of crowding-induced, depletion-mediated attractions.

Disease-associated mutations in MATR3 IDRs modulate MATR3 phase behaviors

We previously observed that disease-associated MATR3 mutations disrupt condensate dynamics and morphology in yeast.26 We expressed and purified the disease-associated mutants: MBP-MATR3:P154S, MBP-MATR3:T622A, and MBP-MATR3:F115C13 (Figure 6A). We were unable to purify MBP-MATR3:S85C, likely due to its decreased solubility (Figure 6A).29 MATR3:F115C, MATR3:P154S, and MATR3:T622A showed similar solubility and slightly increased turbidity as compared with MATR3:WT (Figures 6B-6D). Addition of (UCUUUC)6AA RNA further increased turbidity, with all the variants showing a similar profile to MATR3:WT.

Next, we assessed these variants using MRPS, RALS, and TEM. Here, the variants sampled limited ranges of diameters and showed increases in particle abundance independent of (UCUUUC)6AA (Figures 6E, 6F, and S7A). By RALS, we measured a csw of ~0.5 μM for MATR3:T622A, which was 2.5-fold higher than for MATR3:WT, indicating that this mutation hinders the transition to wormlike assemblies. The csw values were not significantly higher for MATR3:F115C and MATR3:P154S as compared with MATR3:WT (Figures 6G and S7B). By contrast, the magnitudes of apparent A2 were significantly more negative by ~2.7-fold (MATR3:T622A) and ~4.8-fold (MATR3:F115C) or significantly less negative by ~2.5-fold (MATR3:P154S) (Figure 6H). This indicates that T622A and F115C enhance associations among the assemblies, whereas associations are weakened by P154S. Interestingly, addition of (UCUUUC)6AA makes the csw values equivalent to MATR3:WT, showing that addition of RNA restores the sphere-to-wormlike transition of MATR3 mutants (Figure 6G). However, this happens without statistically significant differences in Kdapp values for (UCUUUC)2AA and (UCUUUC)6AA as compared with MATR3:WT (Figures 6I and S7C).

Visualization of the assemblies confirmed that the disease-associated mutants transition from spherical to wormlike assemblies independent of (UCUUUC)6AA (Figures 6J and 6K). In the absence of RNA, the variants form longer assemblies (~120–250 nm), and the spherical assemblies are more persistent above csw. Addition of RNA to the disease-associated mutant assemblies does not alter their length, which still appears elongated relative to MATR3:WT (Figure 6K). At a concentration of 5 μM, the wormlike assemblies and micron-scale assemblies of the mutants and of MATR3:WT are indistinguishable (Figures S7D and S7E). While the inverse bolaamphiphile architecture is maintained by all three disease-associated mutants, the intramolecular inter-domain interactions within neighboring domains are affected, and the overall intramolecular inter-domain attractions are weakened (Figures S7F-S7H). These changes appear to explain why either csw changes and/or why A2 values are affected by the disease-associated point mutations. In sum, these data suggest that the ALS-associated mutants are less responsive to morphological modulation by RNA, although their affinity for RNA does not change as compared with MATR3:WT.

DISCUSSION

Here, we report that MATR3 forms nanoscale spherical and wormlike assemblies, which appear to originate from its inverse bolaamphiphile-like domain organization and interaction patterns (Figure 7). These features generate spherical assemblies ~20–30 nm in diameter. Spherical assemblies can transition to wormlike assemblies via well-established interaction modes that become favorable as concentration increases.74,98 In contrast to macrophase condensates that typically have varying sizes and can fuse with other condensates, MATR3 undergoes microphase separation, akin to micellization, to form size-limited species of two distinct morphologies and two size scales. These nanoscale assemblies are also evident in HEK293T cells, suggesting that physiological MATR3 forms similar assemblies.

Proposed model of MATR3 assembly and the effects of RNA and ALS-associated mutations

(A) The domain architecture of MATR3 is accompanied by a schematic showing how attractive and repulsive interactions (see color bar on the right) are driven by different regions that give MATR3 an inverse bolaamphiphile character.

(B) MATR3 forms concentration-dependent spherical and wormlike assemblies. The color-coding uses the color bar from (A), highlighting how the interplay of repulsive versus attractive interactions drives assembly.

(C) In the presence of crowders, the nanoscale spherical and wormlike assemblies associate via depletion-mediated attractions to form micron-scale clusters.

(D) Spherical assemblies attract one another, and these attractions are weakened by RNA binding.

(E) Attractions between spherical assemblies drive the concentration-dependent sphere-to-worm transitions. ALS-associated mutations weaken this transition.

(F) RNA-binding modulates the driving forces for sphere-to-worm transitions, shortening the wormlike assemblies.

We also discovered that the driving forces for MATR3 assembly and assembly morphologies are modulated by RNA binding. Our studies also provide insights into RNA binding by MATR3, especially when compared with TDP-43. TDP-43 is known to bind GU-rich RNA, with (GU)6 being investigated as a potential therapeutic to counter TDP-43 aggregation in ALS/FTD.93,94 We showed that MATR3 also binds (GU)6, albeit with weaker affinity than TDP-43. By contrast, MATR3 binds tightly to (UCUUUC)2, though TDP-43 does not bind this sequence. Thus, TDP-43 and MATR3 have distinct RNA-binding preferences. Our findings show that RNA can regulate interactions between spherical assemblies and influence the sizes of wormlike MATR3 assemblies. We have also identified the key MATR3 residues mediating RNA binding. Impairment of RNA binding in MATR3:mRRM disrupts formation of wormlike assemblies and inter-sphere interactions, indicating that native RRM functionality is important not only for RNA binding but also for assembly (Figure 7D).

The spherical and wormlike MATR3 assemblies form in different concentration regimes, with a concentration-dependent transition between the two. Our investigation of disease-associated MATR3 mutations revealed that these mutations do not significantly alter MATR3 solubility or its RNA-binding affinity, yet they weaken the transition from spherical to wormlike assemblies (Figure 7E). Significantly, while RNA-binding shortens MATR3:WT wormlike assemblies, RNA does not modulate the lengths of wormlike assemblies formed by mutant MATR3 (Figure 7F). This suggests that the internal interactions of wormlike assemblies are perturbed by the disease-associated mutations, altering the ability of RNA to regulate their formation and length.

There are distinct considerations that could drive the transition from spherical to wormlike assemblies.74 Wormlike assemblies are expected to feature non-interacting spherical caps, along with a cylindrical middle characterized by internal interactions. The diameter of the caps and the lengths and cross-sectional diameters of the wormlike regions would be governed by the interplay of the free energy of the caps, internal interactions, and surface free energy. These considerations determine the values of csw. Conversely, the A2 values are primarily governed by interactions between pairs of spherical assemblies. Our data suggest that the sphere-to-worm transition is weakened by ALS-associated mutations, whereas the strengths of sphere-sphere interactions are mutation-dependent. In general, binding to (UCUUUC)6AA weakens the associations between pairs of spherical assemblies, thereby weakening the transition to wormlike structures.

A question that remains unanswered pertains to the functional roles of spherical and wormlike assemblies of MATR3 in cells. Recently, Zhang et al. performed super-resolution microscopy, which displayed distinctly nanoscale wormlike structures in AML12 nuclei, suggested to be part of the nuclear matrix.31 We hypothesize that the assemblies formed by MATR3 might underlie its involvement in the nuclear matrix, which is essential for chromatin attachment and nuclear organization.36,99-102 Indeed, we observed that RNA drives the formation of nuclear shell-like foci of MATR3. Further, disease-associated mutations have altered sensitivity to RNA-induced assembly modulation, suggesting that the interactions of MATR3 with other components, such as chromatin or other RBPs may reflect the importance of assembly on key physiological processes. Importantly, similar assemblies to those of MATR3 have been identified in a range of other systems, perhaps most notably DNA, histones, and lamins.31,103-105 Similar nuclear granular staining for a range of chromatin organization components and RBPs has also been reported.106-109 Thus, we suggest that these assemblies reflect an alternative mode of phase separation that is not yet fully appreciated but plays key roles across biology.

Limitations of the study

In future work, it will be important to investigate the presence and role of micellar assemblies of MATR3 in cells. Even with super-resolution STED imaging, with a ~35 nm resolution limit, it is difficult to conclusively validate the species we observe in vitro are present in cells, although our cellular imaging remains consistent with the trends we observe in vitro. Likely many other proteins and systems form spherical and wormlike assemblies similar to MATR3, but these assemblies are not apparent when imaging the cells at lower resolutions.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Yeast strains

Yeast strains used were WT W303aΔhsp104 (MATa; can1-100; his3-11,15; leu2-3,112; trp1-1; ura3-1; ade2-1).113 Yeast were grown in rich medium (YPD) or in synthetic media without amino acids used for selection. 2% sugar (dextrose, raffinose, or galactose) was added to synthetic media.

METHOD DETAILS

Plasmids

Human MATR3 cDNA was obtained from Dharmacon (MHS6278-202757255) in the pCMV-Sport6 backbone. Sequence encoding the MBP tag and TEV cleavage site was inserted on 5’ end of MATR3 sequence using AgeI (5’) and SalI (3’) restriction enzymes (New England Bio Labs). MATR3 point mutations were generated by Quikchange site-directed mutagenesis (Agilent) and confirmed by DNA sequencing. pcDNA3.1-MATR3-WT-Myc was obtained from GenScript. MATR3 RRMs fragment (amino acid residues 397-576) was amplified with a C-terminal 6xHis tag and inserted into pET21a(+) using StyI-HF (5’) and XhoI (3’) restriction enzymes (New England Bio Labs). All missense mutations were generated by Quikchange site-directed mutagenesis (Agilent) and confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Oligonucleotides

Oligonucleotides synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) were modified with a 2’OMe group and a phosphorothioate backbone. RNA substrates for fluorescence polarization assays were modified with a 3’ fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) tag while all other RNA substrates were not fluorescently labeled. Oligonucleotides for siRNA knockdown experiments in HEK-293T cells were synthesized by Thermo.

Turbidity Assays

Reactions were prepared at 5 μM MBP-MATR3 in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM DTT) in a 384-well black flat optical bottom plate (Thermo). When included, RNA was equimolar to the MATR3 concentration. Reactions were initiated by the addition of TEV protease and/or 50 mg/ml (5%) dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, Mr 450,000-650,000). Reactions were monitored for turbidity by continuously measuring absorbance at 395 nm at 25°C without agitation in a BioTek Epoch plate reader. Raw data was buffer subtracted and start point set to zero.

Sedimentation Assays

Reactions of 5 μM MBP-MATR3 were prepared in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM DTT), initiated with TEV protease or buffer control addition, and incubated at 25°C in an Eppendorf Thermomixer without agitation. Samples were harvested at the indicated time point and centrifuged at 21,000 g for 30 min at 4°C to separate the soluble and insoluble fractions. Following centrifugation, the total, soluble, and insoluble (2x) fractions were resuspended in sample buffer (60 mM Tris, pH 6.8, 5% glycerol, 2% SDS, 4% β-mercaptoethanol). The total (T), soluble (S), and insoluble (P) fractions were then resolved by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. The amount of protein in each fraction was determined by densitometry using the Image Lab software on a Bio-Rad Gel Doc EZ Imaging system.

For sedimentation fractionation and electron microscopy imaging (Figure 2I), samples were prepared as above until the harvesting of each total, soluble, and pellet fractions. Then each fraction was prepared for electron microscopy (see Electron Microscopy below) rather than SDS-PAGE analysis.

Electron Microscopy

Embedded samples were prepared as described,121 with some modifications. Here, 5 μM MBP-MATR3 was prepared in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM DTT), mixed with assembly buffer to 5% dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, Mr 450,000-650,000), and incubated at 25°C in an Eppendorf Thermomixer without agitation for 30 min. Droplets were then centrifuged at 21,000 g for 5 min at 25 °C, supernatant was replaced with HEPES buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol), samples were incubated for 10 min at 25°C with agitation, and repeated for three total rinses. After rinsing, the samples were submerged in 4% Osmium Tetroxide (EMS) in HEPES buffer for 1 h. The samples were then rinsed three times and dehydrated through a graded series of ethyl alcohols, 25-100%, for 5-10 min intervals at each step. After two changes of 100% EtOH the alcohol was replaced with propylene oxide (EMS). The series of infiltration using PO is as follows: two incubations in 100% PO for 15 min each followed by a 3:1 mixture of PO/epoxy (EMbed 812 Embedding Kit, EMS), 1:1 PO/epoxy, and 1:3 PO/epoxy, for 30 min each. Samples were then submerged two times in 100% epoxy for 30 min followed by a change in epoxy and then overnight incubation at room temperature. Finally, the samples are embedded in fresh epoxy and placed in an oven at 62°C for 24 h. Once samples had hardened, they were trimmed using a glass knife to expose the cutting surface for loading onto grids (200 mesh copper grids, EMS). A UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica) was used to section the samples at 70 nm thickness using a diamond knife (Diatome). Grids were allowed to dry overnight and then stained using a secondary staining protocol with 4% uranyl acetate for 10 min, rinsed with DI H20 and Reynolds lead for 2 min, and then thoroughly rinsed, blotted, and placed in a grid box for drying. After 24 h of drying, the grids were then imaged using a JEOL JEM-1400 120 kV transmission electron microscope.

For immunogold labeled electron microscopy, samples were applied to 200 mesh, pure carbon, copper grids (Ted Pella #0184-F). Grids were then sequentially washed five times in PBS + 0.1% BSA, then three times in PBS, then five times in water. Finally, grids were stained in 2% uranyl acetate and imaged as above.

Microfluidic Resistive Pulse Sensing (MRPS)

Microfluidic resistive pulse sensing (MRPS) measurements were performed on an nCS1 instrument (Spectradyne LLC, Signal Hill, CA, USA). MBP-MATR3 samples were prepared in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM DTT) and incubated at 25°C in an Eppendorf Thermomixer without agitation for 24 h in the presence of TEV protease. When included, RNA was equimolar to the MATR3 concentration. 5 μL of each sample was measured using C-2000 cartridges, with a measurement range of 250–2000 nm. At least n > 200 particles were recorded per sample. At least two biological replicates per sample were analyzed. False-positive signals were excluded during data analysis (Data Analysis software, Spectradyne, Torrance, CA) by applying filters based on transit time, signal-to-noise ratio, peak symmetry, and/or diameter. The resulting species distributions were best fit to Lorentzian distributions, as determined by the fitter package used in a custom Python script. The particle abundance in the raw MRPS traces (for example, Figure 2A) are shown as a species distribution in the unit of particles per ml, which translates to the count of the detected particle of a particular size (in nanometers) per milliliter of solution that is flowed through the microfluidic channel.

Then, the species distribution was best fit to a Lorentzian distribution, and the center of the distribution peak was plotted as the representative occupancy diameter and ordinate of the peak was plotted as the representative particle abundance, each of which were plotted against protein concentration (for example, see Figures 2B and 2C). The passage of assemblies through microfluidic channels and nanoconstrictions at high forces in this system may result in some assembly fragmentation. Comparing particle sizes with our complementary techniques suggests that these effects are not substantial.

NARDINI+ analysis of IDRs

Non-random sequence patterning features of MATR3 IDR sequences were calculated using the NARDINI algorithm.78 The 20 canonical amino acids are grouped into eight categories: pol = polar (C, H, N, Q, S, T); hyd = hydrophobic (I, L, M, V); pos = positively charged (K, R); neg = negatively charged (D, E); aro = aromatic (F, W, Y); A = alanine; P = proline; and G = glycine. The z-scores are calculated with respect to the null model of 105 randomly scrambled sequences with fixed amino acid composition. The sequence composition features were calculated from the primary sequence features using localCIDER115 and the z-scores were calculated with respect to the null model of all ortholog sequences or the IDRs extracted from the human proteome.79 Z-scores > 0 indicate nonrandom clustering of residues or enrichment of indicated features and z-scores < 0 indicate nonrandom mixing of residues or depletion of indicated features.

All-Atom Simulations of MATR3

All-atom Monte Carlo simulations were performed using the ABSINTH implicit solvent model and forcefield paradigm as made available in the CAMPARI simulation package (http://campari.sourceforge.net).82,117 Simulations utilized the abs_3.2_opls.prm parameter set in conjunction with optimized parameters for neutralizing and excess Na+ and Cl− ions.122 Each of the simulations were performed using spherical droplets with a diameter for 235 Å for isolated IDR4 and 250 csw for full-length proteins with explicit ions to mimic a concentration of 10 mM NaCl. Ensembles corresponding to a temperature of 340 K were used in the analysis reported in this work. For full length MATR3, we used the predicted structures of folded RRMs from Alphafold (Uniprot: P43243 95,97). The RRMs were initially equilibrated to make sure bond lengths were consistent with the ABSINTH model. Following the initial equilibration of the RRMs, a single simulation step was performed at 20 K to build in the IDRs. Then the end PDB structure from this step was used to generate ten distinct full length starting structures. Specifically, the RRMs were frozen such that only side chain moves were allowed and then 20,000 simulation steps at 500 K were performed. The end PDBs from the ten simulations were then used as the input structures for the ten independent full simulations ran at 340 K. For each full simulation a total of 6.15 x 107 steps were performed with 107 discarded as equilibration. Simulation results were analyzed to extract the Rg values and the distance maps using the MDTraj118 and the SOURSOP package.119 Reference simulations were also performed by imposing restraints on the folded domains, fixing the bond lengths, bond angles, and peptide bond geometries. All non-bonded interactions were modeled using purely repulsive potentials inverse power potentials.123 The value of the exponent, set to be 6, was chosen to ensure that the scaling of internal distances within IDRs matches that of the Flory random coil.124 Snapshots of the molecule from the simulations were generated by VMD125 and color-coded from the N-terminus to the C-terminus, as blue to white to red.

For the simulations that extract intermolecular, inter-domain interactions, we followed the protocol introduced by Shinn et al.75 Briefly, each simulation comprises two domains X and Y that were either a pair of RRMs, a pair of IDRs, or an RRM and an IDR. From the ensemble of equilibrated configurations for each of the distinct pairs of domains, extracted from ten independent simulations for each pair, we quantified statistics for the inter-residue distances using MDTraj118 and SOURSOP.119 These were used to compute the ensemble-averaged distances 〈RXY(ij)〉 between pairs of residues i and j, where the former is located on domain X and the latter on domain Y. To calibrate the observed pattern of inter-domain, inter-residue distances against a suitable prior, we performed reference simulations to model the expectations for an ideal, non-interacting pair of the domains of interest that freely samples the same simulation volume. These simulations were used to extract ensemble-averaged distances denoted as 〈RXY,ideal(ij)〉. The two sets of distances were then used to compute the elements of the Δ-matrix as described by Shinn et al.75

Surface Electrostatic Potentials

Poisson-Boltzmann electrostatics analysis were performed using UCSF Chimera120 to determine the surface electrostatic potentials for paraspeckle proteins that have RRM structures available on the PDB (RCSB.org126). When the PDB files included additional residues to the RRM, they were removed before calculating the surface electrostatic potential. In the case that a protein contains more than one RRM, each RRM was treated separately and indicated by a number after an underscore. For hnRNPK, the calculation was performed on the KH3 domain. The surface electrostatic potential was calculated for each surface vertex. The PDB files used are PDB: 3SDE (PSPC1/NONO), PDB: 2LA6 (FUS), PDB: 1HA1 (hnRNPA1), PDB: 1WF0 (TDP43), PDB: 1J5K (hnRNPK), PDB: 1X4D (MATR3:RRM1), and PDB: 1X4F (MATR3:RRM2). MATR3 structures are annotated by hydrophobicity values calculated using the Kyte-Doolittle scale by UCSF Chimera based on the amino acid sequence.127

Fluorescence Polarization Assays

Cleared proteins were serially diluted in a 1:1 ratio from 1000 nM in MBP-MATR3 assay buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.3 M NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5% sucrose, 2 mM DTT] or MATR3:RRMs-6xHis assay buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 10% glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 25 mM KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT]. The indicated oligonucleotide substrates were spiked into protein serial dilutions for a final concentration of 5 nM (<15 nucleotides) or 1.8 nM (>36 nucleotides). After a 20 min incubation at 25°C while protected from the light, reactions were measured in a 384-well black flat optical bottom plate (Thermo Fisher) in a Tecan Spark plate reader with excitation and emission wavelengths of 470 and 520 nm, respectively. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 where single curve samples were fit with a nonlinear regression to calculate Kdapp for each trial. Each data point is the average of 5 technical and 3 biological replicates, ± SEM. Solid lines represent best nonlinear fit to calculate apparent dissociation constant, Kdapp. For global fit analysis, curves were generated at four concentrations of RNA and fit simultaneously to Equation 4 in GraphPad Prism 10 to obtain an apparent dissociation constant (Kdapp) and stoichiometric ratio of RNA:protein (N).

Here, F is fluorescence, ΔFmax is the total change in F at saturation, R is the total concentration of RNA, and P is the concentration of protein. Equation 4 is derived from Li et al.128 Values within 20 nM > Kdapp > 500 nM were reported, values outside of this range are not reliable. Importantly, the low concentrations, short equilibration time, and lack of TEV protease in these binding assays were chosen to minimize the presence of MATR3 spherical or wormlike assemblies. We analyzed the fluorescence polarization data by fitting the data to Equation 4 and ignoring the contributions of assemblies, because we do not have information regarding the partitioning of molecules between the assemblies and dispersed molecules. However, the assemblies form at very low concentrations, and the assemblies are small. This implies that more than 90% of the molecules would be in the dilute phase, so no corrections to the concentrations used in the analysis via Equation 4 are needed to analyze the binding isotherms. To guard against over interpretation, we refer to the dissociation constants as apparent Kd values, which are essentially “lumped” constants. This acknowledges the reality that we do not have information regarding the partitioning of molecules across different species, which would be needed to extract microscopic dissociation constants.
