Network connectivity underlying information processing speed in children: Application of a pediatric brain tumor survivor injury model
Authors: Noor Z Al Dahhan, Arthur S Powanwe, Minarose Ismail, Elizabeth Cox, Julie Tseng, Cynthia de Medeiros, Suzanne Laughlin, Eric Bouffet, Jérémie Lefebvre, Donald J Mabbott
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The influence of white matter on neural communication and processing speed is unclear.•This study uncovers these links in the default mode and executive control networks.•Structural connectivity directly influences neural communication & processing speed.•Structural compromise indirectly impacts processing speed via aberrant neural synchrony.•Our findings further our understanding of the neural underpinnings of processing speed.Keywords: Structure-function coupling, Brain network connectivity, Cognitive performance

Introduction

The dynamic features of the brain’s functional networks and the structural networks that support them are vital for higher-order brain functions, such as cognition (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009, Fields, 2010). Despite the intuitive relationship between the structure–function coupling of brain networks, it is unclear how structural networks give rise to emergent cognitive functions and how structural compromise leads to cognitive impairments (Litwińczuk et al., 2022, Zimmermann et al., 2018). The structural and conductive properties of white matter have been proposed to impact cognition through their influence on functional network dynamics (Bells et al., 2017, Noori et al., 2020, Park and Lefebvre, 2020). Subsequently, cognitive performance is hypothesized to be subserved by coherently oscillating neuronal populations that produce temporal windows for communication (Fries, 2015, Pajevic et al., 2014, Uhlhaas et al., 2010). However, a critical missing element to these fundamental theories is elucidating how adaptive or maladaptive changes to the structure and conductive properties of white matter influence and modulate the synchrony and coordination of brain networks, and how this coupling ultimately impacts cognitive performance.

Building upon these fundamental theories, we focus on information processing speed as our measurement of cognition. Information processing speed is the time required to perform a cognitive task (Kail and Salthouse, 1994, Salthouse, 2005, Turken et al., 2008), and is driven by the speed of neuronal transmissions among multiple brain networks, including the default mode (DMN) and executive control (ECN) networks (Breukelaar et al., 2017, Breukelaar et al., 2020, Raichle, 2015, Supekar et al., 2010). We combine human connectome data with simulated neural activity to investigate how structural connectivity facilitates neural synchronization and impacts information processing speed in the developing brain and in those with acquired brain injuries. Pediatric brain tumor survivors (PBTS) were included as an injury model to examine how damage to tissue structure and function manifests as cognitive impairments as they are known to exhibit significantly slow information processing speed, white matter microstructure compromise, and perturbations in neural communication (Al Dahhan et al., 2022, Bells et al., 2017, Gauvreau et al., 2019, Mabbott et al., 2008, Moxon-Emre et al., 2016, Oyefiade et al., 2021). Despite the well documented impact of pediatric brain tumors and their treatments on network communication and information processing speed, it is unclear how structural and functional network connectivity combine and impact behavioral performance. Therefore, exploring how pediatric brain tumors and their treatments lead to structural and functional neural network compromise will importantly further our understanding of the complex network vulnerabilities associated with acquired brain injuries that commonly lead to impairments in information processing speed.

To understand the influence of the structure–function coupling of brain networks on information processing speed, we examine this relationship during the absence of specific task demands (i.e., a resting state). In addition to this fundamental and more general insight, we also examine how this coupling is associated with performance during a cognitive task. During the absence and presence of task demands we examine the structural and functional brain networks that support information processing speed. Structural connectivity has been found to predict the information processing speed in typically developing children and adults, and in neurological disorders (Bethune et al., 2011, Mabbott et al., 2006, Madden et al., 2004, Scantlebury et al., 2016). Furthermore, compromised structural and functional connectivity have independently been found to predict slow information processing speed (Bells et al., 2017, Dockstader et al., 2014, Gauvreau et al., 2019, Scantlebury et al., 2016). However, the underlying neural basis for how structural compromise of specific regions of the DMN and ECN leads to slow information processing speed is unknown.

Building upon these fundamental studies, we assessed whether structural compromise of specific regions of the DMN and ECN was associated with slower information processing speed, and whether neural synchronization between these regions mediated this relationship in a sample of healthy controls and PBTS from the ages of 5–18 years. We hypothesize that faster reaction times in children are associated with the temporal coordination of neural activity between brain networks that is supported by white matter connections (Fig. 1). Investigating this hypothesis has extensive implications for not only understanding the underlying neural basis of typical performance, but also for uncovering the etiology of impairments that occur in neurological and psychiatric disorders due to white matter compromise. Tractography of diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data was conducted to examine network structural connectivity (Beaulieu, 2002, Le Bihan and van Zijl, 2002). Magnetencephalography (MEG) and measures of neural communication was conducted to examine network functional connectivity during a resting state and during a visual-motor task. To index functional connectivity, weighted phase-lag index (wPLI), a measure of neural synchrony, was computed among regions of the DMN and ECN. wPLI reliably measures the asymmetry of the distribution of phase differences between two signals and is highly sensitive to changes in phase synchronization (Stam et al., 2007, Vinck et al., 2011). To ensure that our assessment of information processing speed was not impacted by the MEG environment, the Choice Reaction Time task from the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) was administered outside of the MEG as a measurement of information processing speed (Cambridge Cognition, 2006). Using computational modeling based on empirical data has the unique potential to provide key insights into how white matter connectivity damage affects neural network dynamics, and consequently impairs cognition. Hence, we computationally explored how the structural circuity of white matter shapes functional coupling, and how brain connectivity compromise effects neural synchronization. Specifically, structural connectomes (fractional anisotropy; FA) were used to constrain a stochastic coupled Stuart-Landau model of network connectivity and simulated wPLI was computed for each group (Cabral et al., 2022, Deco et al., 2017, Moon et al., 2015, Schöll, 2016).

Hypothesizing the Impact of Structural Connectivity on Network Neural Communication and Information Processing Speed Performance. Focusing on information processing speed to assess cognitive performance, we predicted that increases in structural connectivity among regions of the default mode (DMN) and executive control (ECN) networks would decrease reaction times and thus improve information processing speed performance both directly and indirectly via increases in functional connectivity.

Our findings contribute to the growing body of literature demonstrating the importance of human structural connectomes in shaping the coordination of network neural communication and, consequently, information processing speed (Bassett et al., 2009, Imms et al., 2021, Kuceyeski et al., 2016, Seguin et al., 2023, Van Den Heuvel and Sporns, 2019). This body of work highlights that while structural and functional connectivity can independently predict cognitive performance, their combined influence offers a more nuanced understanding of how brain network communication underlies cognitive functions, particularly information processing speed. Specifically, our study underscores the role of white matter in facilitating efficient neural communication, which is crucial for optimal information processing speed performance. By integrating insights from both structural connectomes and functional connectivity, we advance our comprehension of how disruptions in these networks can lead to slower information processing speed, which is often observed in various neurological and psychiatric conditions. Our findings emphasize that effective communication between specific regions of the DMN and ECN—two critical brain networks involved in information processing speed—is significantly influenced by the organization of white matter pathways. Thus, our work not only reinforces the importance of structural and functional connectivity on information processing speed but also provides new avenues for exploring how impairments in these networks contribute to cognitive deficits. Future research that further elucidates the interplay between structural and functional connectivity could reveal novel targets for interventions aimed at improving cognitive outcomes in populations with compromised brain networks.

Materials and methods

Participants

Ninety-three participants between the ages of 5 and 18 completed this study at The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids; Toronto, ON): 41 healthy controls (12.67 ± 3.26 years; 20 males and 21 females) and 52 pediatric brain tumor survivors (PBTS) (13.68 ± 3.08 years; 31 males and 21 females) treated for a posterior fossa (PF) brain tumor. PBTS were treated with surgery with or without chemotherapy (n = 22) or with surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation (n = 30). Participant demographics and medical variables for PBTS are summarized in Table 1. Prior to participation in this study, parents provided written informed consent and children provided assent. When deemed capable, participants provided written informed consent. This study was approved by SickKids’ Research Ethics Board.

Eligible PBTS were identified through a database review, which included those who were diagnosed with a PF tumor, were between the ages of 5 and 18 years old and were native English speakers or had at least two years of English studies. PBTS were excluded if they had metal implants, required sedation for MRI testing, or if they had a prior diagnosis of a developmental delay, learning disability, neurological disorder, or traumatic brain injury. All patients were seen 12 or more months following treatment. Patients were not recruited if they had a diffuse brainstem glioma, were receiving palliative care, or had a premorbid history of neurological/learning disability. Healthy controls were self-described as being free from all clinical or neurological disorders. Healthy controls were either recruited from the community, were siblings of PBTS, or were family members of SickKids staff. From the total recruited sample of ninety-three participants: MRI data was collected from 36 healthy controls and 40 PBTS, and MEG data was collected from 38 healthy controls and 44 PBTS.

MR image acquisition and preprocessing

MR image acquisition

MR images were acquired with a GE Signa HDxt 1.5 T scanner with an 8-channel head coil (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin) (3 healthy controls and 5 PBTS) using a 3D-T1 FSPGR protocol (TR/TE = 10.1/4.2 ms, 124 contiguous axial slices, slice thickness = 1.5 mm, matrix = 256x192 mm interpolated to 256x256) and a single-shot spin echo diffusion-weighted (DW) sequence (31 directions, 1 b0 image, b value = 1000 s/mm2, TE/TR = 95.4/15000, flip angle = 90°, slice thickness = 3 mm, matrix = 128x128 interpolated to 256x256mm, encoding direction = A/P) or a Siemens 3 Tim Trio 3 T scanner with a 12-channel head coil (Siemens Canada Ltd., Mississauga, Ontario) (38 healthy controls and 47 PBTS) using a 3D-T1 MPRAGE Grappa 2 protocol (TE/TR = 3.91/2300 ms, 160 contiguous axial slices, flip angle = 90°, 256 × 224 matrix, FOV = 256 × 224 mm, voxel size = 1 mm ISO), and a DW sequence using a single shot spin echo (30 directions, 1 b0 image, b value = 1000 s/mm2, TE/TR = 90/9000 ms, flip angle = 90°, 70 contiguous axial slices, 122 × 122 matrix interpolated to 244 × 244, FOV = 244 × 244 mm, voxel size = 2 mm ISO, encoding direction = A/P). When possible, 3 T images were acquired due to their superior resolution. However, 1.5 T images were acquired due to scheduling conflicts with the 3 T scanner, or when MRI-incompatible devices, including shunts, metal plates, or implants had been inserted in the head and prevented the safe and effective use of the stronger 3 T magnet. Specifically, one healthy control was scanned with the 1.5 T due to a dental cap; two healthy controls were scanned with the 1.5 T due to scheduling conflicts with the 3 T scanner; four PBTS had an intraventricular shunt, and one PBTS had a programmable shunt. Each of these participant’s scans were checked to ensure that there were no visible artifacts before proceeding with image analyses (described below). There were no significant group differences between healthy controls and PBTS on scanner type used for the MRI (χ2(1, n=93) = 0.69, p = 0.95). For coregistration between MRI and MEG information, MRI-visible markers were placed in the same location as the three MEG fiducial head coils.

MRI preprocessing

Cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation of anatomical T1 images was conducted using the FreeSurfer image analysis suite (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). T1 anatomical images were motion-corrected prior to the automated removal of non-brain tissue and segmentation of subcortical white matter and deep gray matter structures. Intensity normalization, automated topology correction, and surface deformation was performed prior to cortical parcellation using the HCP MMP 1.0 atlas (Glasser et al., 2016). Diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were denoised, eddy current corrected for current distortions, motion corrected, and bias corrected to correct B0 field inhomogeneities using MRtrix3 package software (v3.0.2, https://www.mrtrix.org). DWI from all participants passed quality assurance. All DWI were non-linearly registered to T1 (Talairach) space using Advanced Normalization Tools (v2.3.5., ANTs) (Avants et al., 2011).

Probabilistic tractography

Fiber orientation distributions (FOD) were estimated using a single-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) model (Tournier et al., 2013). The CSD model was used as it has been shown to address confounds that stem from crossing white matter fibers (Farquharson et al., 2013, Jeurissen et al., 2014). Before streamlines were generated, T1 anatomical images were segmented into tissue types (white matter, cortical grey matter, subcortical grey matter, CSF, and pathological tissue) in order to perform anatomically constrained tractography (Smith et al., 2012). This step increases the biological plausibility of streamlines that are generated by filtering out any streamlines that end in regions that are anatomically implausible, such as streamlines terminating in the CSF.

Whole-brain white matter connectivity was mapped by probabilistic tractography using default parameters of the iFOD2 algorithm (Tournier et al., 2010). Specifically, streamlines were drawn along paths with large FOD amplitudes, and terminated at sub-cortical grey matter, grey-white matter interface, or at the interior edge of the image. Streamline propagation was stopped if the number of required streamlines had been met, or if the maximum number of seeds had been exceeded. Termination of streamlines was completed through a radial search outward from the termination point to a maximum radius of 4 mm, and the streamline endpoint was assigned to the first none-zero node index. Streamlines were restricted to a minimum length of 2.5 mm and a maximum length of 250 mm, with a tensor FA cut-off of 0.1. Ten million streamlines were generated for each participant, which were then filtered to one million streamlines using the spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (SIFT) algorithm (Smith et al., 2013) in order to improve the fit between the FOD and number of streamlines in every voxel.

For our analyses, streamlines connecting regions within the DMN (Posterior Cingulate Cortex, Medial Prefrontal Cortex, Medial Temporal Lobe, and Angular Gyrus) and ECN (Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex, Anterior Cingulate Cortex, and Posterior Parietal Cortex), or between regions of the DMN and ECN were selected with the following procedure. Inclusion masks were generated for each region of the DMN and ECN, and exclusion masks were generated for all remaining regions of the HCP MMP 1.0 atlas, the cerebellum, and the brainstem in order to prevent the inclusion of streamlines from these regions (Glasser et al., 2016). Using these inclusion and exclusion masks derived from the whole-brain structural connectome, we ensured that only streamlines connecting specific regions of the DMN and ECN were extracted for our empirical analyses. This approach allowed us to specifically examine the connectivity within these critical networks. For each participant, DTI metric maps (FA, mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD)) were calculated using the FOD maps for streamlines among regions of the DMN and ECN, and mean FA, MD, RD, and AD across streamlines was calculated.

MEG data acquisition and preprocessing

MEG recording

Neuromagnetic activity was recorded using a whole-head 151-channel MEG system (CTF Systems Inc., Port Coquitlam, British Columbia, Canada) located in a magnetically shielded room to filter out electromagnetic artifacts originating from the environment. Third-order synthetic gradiometers were used to correct for noise. Continuous MEG data was recorded while participants lay supine in the MEG during a resting state for which participants were instructed to maintain fixation on a black cross in the center of a semitransparent screen placed 50 cm from them for the duration of 3 min. Continuous MEG data was also collected during a visual-motor task assessing information processing speed (described below). For both the resting state and visual-motor task, MEG data was collected at a sampling rate of 1200 or 600 Hz (samples/second) and band-pass filtered from 1-150 Hz. Before MEG data acquisition, each participant was fitted with three fiducial head coils placed on the nasion as well as the left and right preauricular areas to localize the position of the participant’s head relative to the MEG sensors. Before and after each recording, head position relative to the coordinate system of the MEG helmet was recorded by sending a small alternating current through these fiducial coils. Head position relative to the MEG sensors was continuously recorded and monitored using the three fiducial coils so that data with greater than 5 mm of head motion could be removed from subsequent analyses. Eye movements were also monitored with electro-oculograms (EOGs) that were applied laterally to the left and right temple regions of the head, as well as the mastoid process, which acted as a ground against which all movement was compared. After MEG data acquisition was complete, MEG fiducial coils were replaced with MRI-visible fiducial markers to accurately co-register MEG and MRI information.

MEG visual-motor reaction time task

During the visual-motor task administered in the MEG, participants fixated on a black cross with their dominant hand resting at their side and their index finger resting between two buttons on a button box (Fig. 2A). Every 1.5–3.0 s, the black fixation cross was replaced with a green arrow pointing towards the left or right with a static visual contrast grating presented in the lower-left visual field. The dimensions and location of this contrast grating has been shown to elicit a strong visual evoked field ∼ 75 ms following stimulus onset in the right hemisphere (Koelewijn et al., 2011). In response to the presentation of the green arrow, participants were instructed to press the corresponding left or right button as quickly as possible. Once participants pushed the button, the black fixation cross reappeared within 1 s. There were 100 left arrow and 100 right arrow trials presented pseudo-randomly. Presentation order was counterbalanced across participants within each group. For each participant, information processing speed was measured as the reaction time between the presentation of the green arrow and the button press during correct trials. Response accuracy was measured as the number of correct trials participants performed on the task.

MEG data preprocessing

MEG data collected during resting state and the visual-motor task was preprocessed using a custom-built MEG pipeline (https://github.com/MabbottLab/MEGneto) in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., USA) using the Fieldtrip Toolbox (Oostenveld et al., 2011). Continuous raw MEG data was first band-pass filtered at 1–150 Hz. Data collected at a sampling rate of 1200 samples/s was down-sampled to a rate of 600 samples/s. A notch filter at 60 Hz and a third-order spatial gradient environmental noise-cancellation was applied to the recording.

For both the resting state and visual-motor task data, MEG trials were segmented into epochs two seconds before and after the stimulus presentation. Trials were removed prior to analysis if they contained excessive head motion (>10 mm), head motion velocity (>15 mm/s), electromagnetic artifacts, and muscle movements. Participants were removed from further analyses if they had greater than 15 channels or 20 % of trials removed. For the remaining trials, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was conducted to identify and remove heartbeats or eye-blinks contaminating the MEG signal (Vigario et al., 2000). Given that noise was reduced prior to ICA, a conservative approach was applied whereby components were removed if they contained a clear signature of eye-blink and heartbeat artifacts. Rhythmic signals with magnetic smearing around the outside of the brain are known to be heartbeat artifacts, and large, random amplitude changes in a signal accompanied with a strong magnetic dipole in the frontal regions of the brain represent eye blinks (Vigáro et al., 1998). Participants were removed from further analyses if they had more than 5 % of channels removed. On average, during the visual-motor task, participants had 5.90 of channels and 2.71 % of components removed during preprocessing. Following quality assurance to remove noise in the MEG signal during the visual-motor task, a sub-sample of 24 healthy controls and 28 PBTS were included in the final task analyses. During resting state, participants had 3.73 of channels and 1.97 % of components removed during preprocessing. Following quality assurance to remove noise in the MEG signal during resting state, a sub-sample of 35 healthy controls and 35 PBTS were included in the final resting state analyses. Sample characteristics of the 28 PBTS included in the final task analyses, and 35 PBTS included in the final resting state analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Individual time-series sources were reconstructed for the 360 regions of the HCP MMP 1.0 atlas (Glasser et al., 2016) using a single-shell linear constraint minimum variance (LCMV) beamforming approach (Van Veen et al., 1997). The LCMV approach was selected due to its ability to suppress signals from extraneous regions and attribute signals from the cortex and deep brain regions (Van Veen et al., 1997). To ensure that source locations corresponded across participants, the source model of each participant was normalized to a template source model in MNI space (Collins et al., 1998). MEG data was filtered into canonical frequency bands: theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–29 Hz), low gamma (30–59 Hz), and high gamma (60–100 Hz). Weighted phase-lag index (wPLI) values were computed within and between regions of the of the DMN (Posterior Cingulate Cortex, Medial Prefrontal Cortex, Medial Temporal Lobe, and Angular Gyrus) and ECN (Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex, Anterior Cingulate Cortex, and Posterior Parietal Cortex) to index functional connectivity. These specific regions were selected based on their established roles in the cognitive processes related to reaction time and information processing speed in typical development (Breukelaar et al., 2017, Breukelaar et al., 2020, Raichle, 2015, Supekar et al., 2010). While other regions within the DMN and ECN are also relevant, the selected ROIs were prioritized based on their direct association with the processes we aimed to investigate. Including these key regions ensures a focused analysis on areas most likely to reflect the targeted cognitive functioning. For the visual-motor task and resting state, this generated a 7x7 adjacency matrix for each participant for each frequency band. wPLI represents the level of synchrony between the neural oscillations of two regions. A wPLI of 1 represents complete synchronization and a wPLI of 0 represents complete asynchronization (Vinck et al., 2011).

Assessment of information processing speed outside of the MEG

The choice reaction time subtest from CANTAB (Cambridge Cognition, 2006) was administered outside of the MEG. CANTAB consists of a collection of well-validated and sensitive touchscreen tests measuring cognitive function. Similar to the visual-motor task conducted in the MEG (Fig. 2A), participants were presented with a central fixation cross, followed by the appearance of an arrow pointing either to the left or right. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing the corresponding left or right button on the touchscreen. The task measured both the reaction time, which is the interval between the appearance of the arrow and the participant's button press, and the accuracy of responses.

CANTAB tasks have been found to be reliable across cultures and ages (4–90 years), and have been found to be useful for assessing both healthy and patient populations, including PBTS (Cambridge Cognition, 2006, Gauvreau et al., 2019, Riggs et al., 2016). In addition, CANTAB is reported to have adequate to high internal consistency in children, particularly for reaction time tasks (Syväoja et al., 2015). Furthermore, CANTAB battery correlations have been reported to fall within or above the accepted level of test–retest reliability of 0.75–0.80 (Lowe & Rabbitt, 1998). In particular, reaction time tasks have test–retest reliability correlations > 0.76 (Lowe & Rabbitt, 1998).

Computationally generating neural synchrony from structural connectomes

Structural connectomes (FA) for both PBTS and healthy controls was used to constrain a stochastic coupled Stuart-Landau model and compute wPLI for each group through numerical simulations (Deco et al., 2017). The simple nature of the Stuart-Landau model makes it an ideal model to mimic oscillatory fluctuations observed in neural activity (Cabral et al., 2022, Moon et al., 2015, Schöll, 2016). Specifically, the features of the Stuart-Landau model rely on two parameters, frequency and amplitude, which makes this model appropriate for data fitting and appropriately captures amplitude fluctuations observed in our data.

For our computational model, whole-brain structural connectomes were used to build the network model. To maintain consistency with our empirical analyses, the model was constrained to focus on the same region nodes of the DMN and ECN. This ensures that both our empirical and simulated analyses are aligned in terms of the regions of interest. Based on the HCP MMP 1.0 atlas (Glasser et al., 2016), the network model consisted of 360 regions and resulted in a set of two stochastic differential equations:

(1)dxk(t)dt=akxk-ωkyk-xkxk2+yk2+G∑p=1NCkpxk-xp+σξkxt

dxk(t)dt=akxk-ωkyk-xkxk2+yk2+G∑p=1NCkpxk-xp+σξkxt

(2)dyk(t)dt=akyk+ωkxk-ykxk2+yk2+G∑p=1NCkpyk-yp+σξkyt

dyk(t)dt=akyk+ωkxk-ykxk2+yk2+G∑p=1NCkpyk-yp+σξkyt

For these equations, the variables xk,yk depict the dynamics of each node; ak controls the transition to the oscillatory regime; and ωk depicts the intrinsic frequency of a node. Structural connectivity is given by the elements of the matrices C and G and is a coefficient that controls the strength of structural connectivity couplings. The quantities ξkx and ξky are white Gaussian noises with the following properties:

(3)〈ξkx(t)〉=〈ξky(t)〉=0;〈ξkx(t)ξky(t′)〉=0;〈ξkx(t)ξkx(t′)〉=〈ξky(t)ξky(t′)〉=δ(t-t′)

〈ξkx(t)〉=〈ξky(t)〉=0;〈ξkx(t)ξky(t′)〉=0;〈ξkx(t)ξkx(t′)〉=〈ξky(t)ξky(t′)〉=δ(t-t′)

We consider the variables xk as measures of brain fluctuations or time series at each node. For the model to generate brain-like fluctuations, the parameters ak must be chosen to be less than zero (ak<0). In this regime, rhythms are noise-induced and the synchronization amongst the nodes (xk,yk) (i.e., the brain’s functional connectivity) is caused by a combination of the network intrinsic variability and the structural connectivity coefficients Ckp. The values of ωk are chosen to belong to the frequency bands of interest. Precisely, we chose ωk to be Gaussian distributed within each frequency band. The system of coupled Stuart-Landau equations (1) and (2) was simulated using the Euler-Maruyama scheme for a total duration of T = 200 s (we excluded 5 s transients at the beginning of each simulation). The integration timestep was dt = 0.0001 s, with a sampling rate of 200 Hz. To obtain the values of the parameters (a, G, and σ) in our model, we performed a parameter search and identified those that qualitatively gave similar wPLI group differences than those obtained from the data analysis. In line with results from previous studies(Deco et al., 2017, Lombardi et al., 2023, Poil et al., 2012), we found that values less than zero (a < 0) and at the Hopf bifurcation (a = 0) were necessary to obtain results close to those obtained from our empirical data. The noise intensity and the connectivity strengths were kept constant at σ=0.02 and G=15 for all the simulations performed. The values of ak were chosen to be equal across nodes but changed for each frequency band. We used ak=a=0,-10,-20, for the theta, beta, and high gamma frequency bands, respectively. We performed the simulations for each participant in each group. For a given participant, we identified the nodes which corresponded to regions of the DMN and ECN in the right and left hemispheres. From these nodes, we built the time series of the regions in the DMN and ECN and computed wPLI coefficients between each region pair. We repeated this procedure for all the participants in the left and right hemispheres separately, and computed the averages over the participant in each of the two groups to obtain the final values of the simulated wPLI. We did not consider a time delay between the nodes since our results did not give a better correspondence with the experimental time series when they were included in the simulation(see Noori et al., 2020). This also reduced the amount of free parameters and helped focus more on the role of the structural connectivity matrices.

Statistical analyses

We aimed to determine the relationship between DMN and ECN structural connectivity, functional connectivity, and information processing speed performance. We used partial least squares (PLS) path modeling to test the relationship between structural (FA/AD/MD/RD) and functional (wPLI) network connectivity in predicting behavioral task performance. To streamline our analyses, only variables with significant group differences were carried forward to the path model. The following sections outline analyses to determine group differences in structure, function, and behavior before addition to the path model. Demographic variables were assessed for group differences with robust regressions in R (p < 0.05) using the following contrasts: PBTS/healthy controls. Variables tested for PBTS/healthy controls differences included age at testing, sex, handedness, and average parental education.

Information processing speed task performance

We assessed group differences in reaction time and accuracy separately for the MEG and CANTAB tasks using constrained and unconstrained robust regression models in R (Hoaglin et al., 1985, Maechler et al., 2021). The constrained model included group as the predictor (PBTS, healthy controls), and the unconstrained model incorporated demographic variables (age at assessment, sex, parental education) and clinical covariates (e.g., tumor type, hydrocephalus, neurological complications).

Variables that significantly predicted the task outcome (accuracy, reaction time) with a p-value less than 0.05 were kept in the unconstrained model. If both models adequately fit the data, we conducted model comparison using Adjusted R2. If Adjusted R2 was similar for both models, the constrained model was selected in the interest of parsimony. All tests were adjusted for the family-wise error rate via FDR correction with a significance threshold of 0.05.

To assess the effect of age, participants were binned into younger (5–12 years) or older age groups (13 + years). These age groups were selected around the mean age of participants (PBTS = 13.68 years, healthy controls = 12.67 years). Based on this split, the healthy controls group included 22 younger and 19 older participants, while the PBTS group included 20 younger and 32 older participants. We conducted secondary comparisons to examine the influence of age on task performance with the following contrasts: younger PBTS and older PBTS, younger healthy controls and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, older PBTS and older healthy controls, older PBTS and younger healthy controls, and older healthy controls and younger PBTS. Processes for model development and testing were the same as for the primary regression models.

A paired-sample t-test was conducted to assess differences in reaction time and accuracy between the MEG visual-motor task and CANTAB choice reaction time task.

Default mode and executive control network structural connectivity

FA, MD, AD, and RD values were extracted and averaged across all streamlines per region connection per participant. These means were then inputted into Network-Based Statistics (NBS) to determine region connections with significantly different metric values between PBTS and healthy controls (Zalesky et al., 2010). NBS controls for family-wise error rate via false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Zalesky et al., 2010). NBS test parameters included: t-test, 5000 permutations, intensity component sizes, threshold of 3, and 0.05 significance level (Manual R, Connectome NBS, 2012). A threshold of 3 was selected as it was the highest value that yielded significant results for tests of both structural and functional connectivity (Zalesky et al., 2010). T-tests were conducted for comparison of two groups, with contrasts of [1, −1] (PBTS > healthy controls) and [−1, 1] (PBTS < healthy controls). We then calculated the means and standard deviations of FA, MD, AD, and RD values for region connections with significant group differences. A MANCOVA was conducted in R to examine the effect of group (healthy controls vs. PBTS) on our diffusion metrics while controlling for tumor type, hydrocephalus, and neurological complications.

As a secondary analysis, to explore the effects of age, diffusion metrics with PBTS/healthy controls differences were also compared using age groups (5–12, 13 + ). NBS T-tests were conducted on diffusion metrics using the following contrasts: younger PBTS and older PBTS, younger healthy controls and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, older PBTS and older healthy controls, older PBTS and younger healthy controls, and older healthy controls and younger PBTS. Parameters were the same as for earlier NBS analyses. Comparisons of younger PBTS and healthy controls and older PBTS and healthy controls were particularly of interest to delineate which age group were driving significant results of primary analyses.

Default mode and executive control network functional connectivity

Similar to our analyses of structural connectivity, NBS was conducted to determine region connections with significantly different wPLI values between PBTS and healthy controls for each frequency band (theta, alpha, beta, low gamma, and high gamma) (Zalesky et al., 2010). We then calculated the means and standard deviations of wPLI values for region connections with significant group differences. A MANCOVA was conducted in R to examine the effect of group (healthy controls vs. PBTS) on wPLI while controlling for tumor type, hydrocephalus, and neurological complications. To explore the effects of age, frequency bands with PBTS/healthy controls differences were also compared using age groups (5–12, 13 + ).

PLS path model analyses

PLS path model was conducted to determine the relationship between structural and functional connectivity and behavioral task performance in PBTS and healthy controls (R software, bootstraps = 5000) (Sánchez, 2013).

The inner model contained the latent variables: participant, structural connectivity, functional connectivity, and task performance. Latent variables were indirectly measured through reflective manifest variables in the outer model. Manifest variables for participant were sex, age, and any other demographic variables with significant PBTS/control differences. Manifest variables for structural and functional connectivity were diffusion (FA, MD, AD, RD) and wPLI metrics, respectively, for network connections with significant PBTS/heathy controls differences. The model hypothesized that structural connectivity had a direct effect on task performance and an indirect effect via its effect on functional connectivity.

The accuracy of each statistical model was first assessed by examining the relationship between the latent constructs with their associated measures, referred to as loadings. Cronbach’s alpha and Dillon-Goldstein's rho were both used to examine how well each measure corresponded to their latent construct. The discriminant validity of our measurement model was also assessed by examining whether the cross-loading for each measure compared to the other latent constructs were larger than the loadings obtained from measures belonging to other latent constructs.

The quality of each statistical model was then assessed by examining: 1) the significance of the regression fit (t-test); 2) the R2 determination coefficients of the endogenous variables, with values lower than 0.2 considered low and values between 0.2 and 0.5 considered moderate; 3) the average variance extracted (AVE); and 4) the Goodness-of-Fit (GoF), which is the geometric mean of the average R2 and the average communality. A good fit of our measurement model was identified if: 1) R2 did not contain zero; 2) bootstrap percentile confidence intervals for path weights (β; 95 % percentile); and 3) the GoF was greater than 0.36 (Tenenhaus et al., 2005).

Three separate PLS path models were built with either resting state, simulated, or task-evoked neural synchrony. PLS path modeling cannot accommodate missing data, thus only participants who had usable MRI and MEG data were included in our model (resting state: 35 healthy controls and 35 PBTS; computational: 35 healthy controls and 35 PBTS; visual-motor task: 22 healthy controls and 25 PBTS).

Reaction time is significantly slower in pediatric brain tumor survivors

The MEG visual-motor task and CANTAB choice reaction time task performance was evaluated using both constrained (group as a sole predictor) and unconstrained models. This approach aimed to assess whether PBTS or healthy controls predicts task performance, and whether clinical and demographic characteristics could improve this prediction. The unconstrained model included predictors such as group (PBTS, healthy controls), average parental education, and clinical covariates (e.g., tumor type, hydrocephalus, neurological complications). Family-wise FDR correction was conducted by accounting for the two regressions conducted for each task: reaction time and accuracy. Model comparison was performed to compare adjusted R2 values for both models.

For the MEG visual-motor task, the constrained model significantly predicted faster reaction times for healthy controls compared to PBTS (β = 0.89, Adjusted p = 0.019, Adjusted R2 = 0.65, CI95: 0.60, 1.17) (Fig. 2B, Supp. Fig. 1A). The unconstrained model did not improve prediction beyond the constrained model (β = 0.75, Adjusted p = 0.025, Adjusted R2 = 0.24, CI95: 0.55, 1.05). Neither model yielded significant predictions for accuracy (β = -0.004, Adjusted p = 0.067, Adjusted R2 = 0.02, CI95: −0.02, 0.03) (Fig. 2C, Supp. Fig. 1A). Reaction time and accuracy were assessed for age group differences with the following contrasts: younger PBTS and older PBTS, younger healthy controls and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, older PBTS and older healthy controls, older PBTS and younger healthy controls, and older healthy controls and younger PBTS. Regression analysis showed that older PBTS had longer reaction times compared to older healthy controls (β = -0.63, t-value = -1.61, q-value = 0.020), with no significant differences in accuracy between these two groups (p > 0.05). In addition, younger PBTS were significantly slower than older healthy controls (β = -0.78, t-value = -2.61, q-value = 0.03), with no significant differences in accuracy between these two groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, older PBTS were significantly slower than younger healthy controls (β = -0.45, t-value = -3.12, q-value = 0.008), with no significant differences in accuracy between these two groups (p > 0.05). There were no significant reaction time or accuracy differences between younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, younger and older healthy controls, or younger and older PBTS (p > 0.05).

Similarly, for the CANTAB choice reaction time task, the constrained model significantly predicted faster reaction times for healthy controls compared to PBTS (β = 0.74, Adjusted p = 0.03, Adjusted R2 = 0.44, CI95: 0.91, 1.05) (Fig. 2B, Supp. Fig. 1B). The unconstrained model did not improve the constrained model’s prediction (β = 0.65, Adjusted p = 0.04, Adjusted R2 = 0.17, CI95: 0.85, 1.11). Neither model yielded significant predictions for accuracy (β = -0.04, Adjusted p = 0.04, Adjusted R2 = 0.004, CI95: −0.01, 0.04) (Fig. 2C, Supp. Fig. 1B). Reaction time and accuracy were assessed for age group differences. Regression analysis showed that participant group significantly predicted reaction time when testing within the older age groups (β = -0.98, t-value = -2.99, q-value = 0.027), indicating that older PBTS had longer reaction times compared to older healthy controls. Younger healthy controls were found to be significantly slower (β = -0.09, t-value = -2.17, q-value = 0.036, Adjusted R2 = 0.34, CI95: −0.12, −0.06) and less accurate (β = -0.01, t-value = -2.87, q-value = 0.013, Adjusted R2 = 0.01, CI95: −0.02, −0.003) than older healthy controls on the CANTAB. In addition, younger PBTS were found to be significantly slower (β = −0.09, t-value = -2.05, q-value = 0.048, Adjusted R2 = 0.18, CI95: −0.18, −0.09) and less accurate (β = -0.02, t-value = −2.47, q-value = 0.04, Adjusted R2 = 0.04, CI95: −0.03, −0.02) than older healthy controls on the CANTAB. There were no significant reaction time or accuracy group differences between the younger and older PBTS, younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, or older PBTS and younger healthy controls (p > 0.05).

Paired-samples t-tests indicated that there were no significant differences in mean reaction time between the MEG visual-motor task and the CANTAB choice reaction time task (t(51) = −3.35, p = 0.55), indicating that our findings are not impacted by the different information processing speed tasks that were used or by the MEG environment.

Structural connectivity influences reaction time both directly and indirectly via effects on resting state neural synchrony

The accuracy of our statistical measurement model was first assessed by examining the relationship between the latent constructs with their associated measures, referred to as loadings. The latent constructs included: 1) participant, indicated by group status (healthy controls or PBTS), age and sex (age and sex were included as they are known to influence reaction time (Dykiert et al., 2012)); 2) structural connectivity, indicated by bilateral FA, MD, RD, and AD (FA loadings on the latent variable was recoded to be similar to the dimensionality of MD, RD, and AD); 3) resting state functional connectivity, indicated by left hemisphere beta wPLI, right hemisphere high gamma wPLI, and bilateral theta wPLI; and 4) CANTAB choice reaction time, which was the outcome variable (Fig. 5A). To test our hypothesis, we initially constrained our PLS path model to only include connections that survived corrections for multiple comparisons between regions of interest (ROI) with significant group differences in both structural and resting state functional connectivity. All indicators had a loading > 0.8 on their respective latent constructs, except for age, gender, and AD; thus, they were removed from the model (<0.7). All remaining measures corresponded well with their latent constructs (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.8 and Dillon-Goldstein's rho > 0.7) and satisfied communality (>0.5) (Sanchez, 2013). Discriminant validity was also satisfied as manifest variables had higher cross-loadings (>0.1) with their latent variable blocks compared to other latent variable blocks.

Computationally modeling structure-function coupling

Thus far, we have demonstrated that in the absence of task demands, increased MD and RD, and decreased FA, predicts increased reaction time and decreased resting state wPLI; and in turn decreased wPLI predicts increased reaction time. These observations provide compelling evidence that structural connectivity may directly influence neural communication and information processing speed, and white matter compromise has an indirect adverse impact on reaction time via perturbed neural synchrony.

We attempted to corroborate these empirical findings by computationally building and analyzing a participant-specific large-scale brain network model in which neural activity within each region followed noise-driven Stuart-Landau equations. Stuart-Landau equations are widely used to mimic oscillatory fluctuations observed in neural activity across frequency bands (Cabral et al., 2022, Moon et al., 2015, Schöll, 2016). The connectivity of this network model was constrained by our experimentally acquired structural connectomes for both healthy controls and PBTS. Specifically, FA measures were used to weight inter-region connectivity through white matter and assess its organization. To facilitate the comparison with our experimental data, functional connectivity among regions of the simulated DMN and ECN were also quantified using wPLI. Numerical simulations were performed for each participant to generate simulated wPLI for all pairwise regions of the DMN and ECN (Deco et al., 2017).

Pearson correlations revealed that there were no significant correlations between our empirical resting state wPLI and our computationally generated wPLI on any DMN and ECN region pairs (all p’s > 0.05). Despite the lack of a 1-to-1 relationship between our empirical and simulated neural activity, NBS and follow up two-sample t-test analyses revealed that for DMN and ECN networks built using PBTS structural connectomes, simulated wPLI was decreased in left hemisphere beta (NBS t-test; p = 0.037) and right hemisphere high gamma bands compared to those networks built using the healthy controls structural connectomes (NBS t-test; p = 0.034) (Table 2; Fig. 4B; Supp. Fig. 6 & 7). In addition, PBTS networks showed increased simulated DMN and ECN wPLI in bilateral theta (NBS t-test; left hemisphere: p = 0.016; right hemisphere: p = 0.003) compared to healthy control networks. When examining the influence of age, NBS revealed that there were no significant group differences in simulated left hemisphere beta, right hemisphere high gamma, and bilateral theta wPLI between younger PBTS and older PBTS, younger healthy controls and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, older PBTS and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and older healthy controls, or older PBTS and younger healthy controls (p > 0.05).

To examine the generality and robustness of our empirical findings, we statistically tested our resting state PLS path model by substituting our empirical resting state wPLI with our simulated wPLI measures. Results remained qualitatively unchanged: all ROI connections with significant group differences in both structural and simulated functional connectivity corresponded well with their latent constructs (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.8 and Dillon-Goldstein's rho > 0.7), satisfied communality (>0.5), and satisfied discriminant validity as manifest variables had higher cross-loadings (>0.1) with their latent variable blocks compared to other latent variable blocks(Sanchez, 2013). Age, sex, and AD were removed from the model due to inadequate loading (<0.7).

Our PLS path model informed by our simulated functional connectivity supports the hypothesis that DMN and ECN structural connectivity directly influences neural communication and information processing speed, and white matter compromise has an indirect adverse impact on reaction time via perturbed neural synchrony (Fig. 5B). Specifically, participant significantly predicted structural connectivity (β = 0.53 (CI95: 0.38, 0.65), t = 6.21, p < 0.001), structural connectivity predicted simulated functional connectivity (β = -0.58 (CI95: −0.68, −0.43), t = 5.71, p < 0.001) and CANTAB choice reaction time (β = 0.32 (CI95: 0.23, 0.66), t = 1.09, p < 0.001), and simulated functional connectivity predicted reaction time (β = -0.53 (CI95: −0.79, −0.35), t = -5.03, p < 0.001). R2 for each latent variable were: structural connectivity = 0.28 (CI95: 0.14, 0.43), functional connectivity = 0.35 (CI95: 0.24, 0.46), and reaction time 0.42 (CI95: 0.23, 0.67). The indirect effect of structural connectivity on reaction time through simulated neural synchrony was significant (β = 0.38(CI95: 0.23, 0.54)): increased white matter organization predicted increased simulated neural synchrony and decreased reaction time. The GoF measure for our model was 0.49 (Tenenhaus et al., 2005). Average variance extracted for structural and simulated functional connectivity were 0.77 and 0.62, respectively (Sanchez, 2013). Importantly, similar results were found after including all ROI connections with significant group differences in white matter organization and simulated neural synchronization of the DMN and ECN; the GoF for our model with all significant ROI connections was 0.43. The synergy of findings between our empirical and simulated data collectively confirms the importance of white matter organization on network neural synchronization across frequency bands. In addition, these results indicate that efficient cognitive processing is due to the consistent temporal coordination of neural activity of brain networks that is subserved by white matter connections.

Generalizability of resting state structure-function coupling to a visual-motor task

NBS and follow up two-sample t-test analyses indicated that during the visual-motor task (Table 2; Fig. 2A; Fig. 4C; Supp. Figs. 8 & 9), PBTS showed decreased wPLI in left hemisphere beta (NBS t-test; p = 0.017) and right hemisphere high gamma (NBS t-test; p = 0.037) bands in the DMN and ECN compared to healthy controls. Similar group differences in DMN and ECN functional connectivity between the visual-motor task and resting state indicates that our findings are not impacted by the presence of task conditions. When assessing the influence of age, NBS revealed that there were no significant group differences in visual-motor task left hemisphere beta or right hemisphere high gamma wPLI between younger PBTS and older PBTS, younger healthy controls and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and younger healthy controls, older PBTS and older healthy controls, younger PBTS and older healthy controls, or older PBTS and younger healthy controls (p > 0.05).

A MANCOVA was conducted to examine the effect of group on wPLI while controlling for tumor type, hydrocephalus, and neurological complications. Using Wilks' Lambda, there was a significant effect of group on left hemisphere beta (λ = 0.194, F(21, 27) = 5.347, p = < 0.001, q (FDR-corrected) = < 0.001.), and right hemisphere high gamma (λ = 0.257, F(21, 27) = 3.711, p = < 0.001, q (FDR-corrected) = 0.003) after controlling for our clinical characteristics.

During the presence of task demands, PLS path modelling indicated that DMN and ECN structural connectivity directly influences neural communication and information processing speed, and white matter compromise has an indirect adverse impact on reaction time via perturbed neural synchrony (Fig. 5C). Specifically, participant significantly predicted structural connectivity (β = 0.62 (CI95: 0.41, 0.80), t = 5.28, p < 0.001), structural connectivity predicted task-based functional connectivity (β = -0.47 (CI95: −0.65, −0.39), t = -3.61, p < 0.001) and reaction time (β = 0.51 (CI95: 0.32, 0.70), t = 4.30, p < 0.001), and task-based functional connectivity predicted reaction time (β = -0.46 (CI95: −0.64, −0.28), t = -3.90, p < 0.001). R2 for each latent variable were: structural connectivity = 0.38 (CI95: 0.17, 0.64), task-based functional connectivity = 0.35 (CI95: 0.22, 0.52), and reaction time = 0.37 (CI95: 0.25, 0.53). The indirect effect of structural connectivity on reaction time through task-based network neural synchrony was significant (β = 0.42 (CI95: 0.32, 0.69)): increased white matter organization predicted increased task-based neural synchrony and decreased reaction time. The GoF for our model was 0.67 (Tenenhaus et al., 2005). Average variance extracted for structural and task-based functional connectivity were 0.62 and 0.67, respectively (Sanchez, 2013). Bootstrapping with 5000 permutations validated the accuracy and quality of our measurement model (Sanchez, 2013). Importantly, similar results were found after including all ROI connections with significant group differences in white matter organization and task-based neural synchronization of the DMN and ECN; the GoF measure for our hypothesized PLS path model with all significant ROI connections was 0.51.

Discussion

This study empirically explores the impact of structural and functional connectivity of specific regions of the DMN and ECN on information processing speed performance during resting state, a visual-motor task, and through computational modeling of human connectome data. In line with our hypothesis, our results indicate that efficient information processing speed performance is due to the coordinated synchrony of the neural activity of specific regions of the DMN and ECN that is modulated by their white matter networks. These results are supported by findings from our data-constrained computational model which illustrates that when our experimentally acquired structural connectomes are used to simulate functional neural activity, not only do the resulting functional simulations align with our empirical functional results, but they also accurately predict group differences in information processing speed performance. These findings showcase the critical role that adaptive and maladaptive changes to the structural properties of white matter have on influencing and modulating the synchrony of the DMN and ECN, and consequently impacting information processing speed performance. Our findings build upon a growing body of literature showcasing the importance of structural connectomes in shaping the coordination of network neural activity and, consequently, information processing speed (Bassett et al., 2009, Imms et al., 2021, Kuceyeski et al., 2016, Seguin et al., 2023, Van Den Heuvel and Sporns, 2019).

Despite the well documented impact of pediatric brain tumors and their treatments on structural and functional network connectivity and cognition, it is unclear how this coupling in the DMN and ECN impacts information processing speed performance. Our findings indicate that damage to white matter structures supporting efficient signal transmission between neurons may disrupt neural synchrony and lead to decreased functional connectivity (Bartolomei et al., 2006, Bells et al., 2017, Nunez et al., 2015, Oyefiade et al., 2022). Interestingly, we found that maladaptive changes to structural organization of the DMN and ECN lead to frequency-specific aberrant functional connectivity that was associated with slower information processing speed. The frequency of network oscillations have been proposed to support specific cognitive functions (Ward, 2003). Specifically, beta oscillations have been associated with sensory and motor processing, and the detection of salient stimuli during cognitive tasks (Kisley and Cornwell, 2006, Uhlhaas et al., 2009). Loss of task-dependent beta connectivity for PBTS may highlight impairments in attentional control and central executive processing that is required during the task. Furthermore, gamma oscillations have been found to support multiple cognitive processes, such as attention and executive control (Bosman et al., 2014, Ward, 2003). Thus, reduced functional connectivity of these fast oscillations found for PBTS relative to healthy controls may suggest that neural information processing may be compromised in PBTS which leads to longer reaction times. In addition, during resting state PBTS exhibited greater bilateral theta connectivity than healthy controls which was associated with slower information processing speed. Increases in theta oscillations have been thought to be a signature of injury, and have been found to characterize decreases in network segregation and global efficiency of functional networks (Dunkley et al., 2015, Modarres et al., 2017). Our findings may reflect that in PBTS, regions of the DMN and ECN are not integrated to form functional networks compared to healthy controls, which leads to slower information processing speed. Slower information processing speed may be due to broad network damage and the maladaptive network reorganization due to that damage, rather than due to isolated damage to specific region pairs in which we observed functional connectivity group differences. Furthermore, the generalizability of our resting state finding to our visual-motor task indicates that aberrant connectivity of the DMN and ECN may be a promising indicator of cognitive impairments. Further establishing alterations in structural and functional connectivity of the DMN and ECN as biomarkers of information processing speed impairments could facilitate early intervention and monitoring of these deficits following brain tumor treatment.

Overall, our findings contribute to the growing body of literature demonstrating the importance of human structural connectomes in impacting the coordination of network neural communication, and consequently impacting cognitive functioning (Bassett et al., 2009, Imms et al., 2021, Kuceyeski et al., 2016, Seguin et al., 2023, Van Den Heuvel and Sporns, 2019). Our simulation approach aimed to model structural connectivity and explore how structural alterations might impact network-level functional dynamics of the DMN and ECN. This method provides insights into the mechanistic links between structural connectivity changes and network dynamics, complementing our empirical observations. We focused on understanding these mechanistic relationships rather than exact replication of observed functional connectivity patterns. In this study, DTI metrics, including FA, were used to infer white matter microstructure (Basser, 1995). Our computational model highlights that myelin plays a crucial role in driving and shaping network-level dynamics by influencing the speed and efficiency of neural signal transmission. This is particularly important as myelin facilitates the coordinated communication between different brain regions, which is essential for maintaining functional connectivity and supporting cognitive processes. By focusing on myelin's impact, our model provides valuable insights into how structural changes in white matter can alter network interactions and potentially contribute to cognitive impairments observed in conditions such as brain tumors. This emphasizes the need for further investigation into myelin's role in neural network dynamics and its broader implications for understanding brain function and dysfunction.

In considering our analyses and results, it is worth noting some considerations related to white matter microstructure and brain development that requires further investigation. Although we observed no significant differences between healthy controls and PBTS in the distribution of scanner types used for MRI, suggesting that variation in scanner types did not bias our findings, future studies would benefit from using harmonized imaging data. Furthermore, while our findings are a critical starting point for understanding how white matter connectivity damage impacts neural network dynamics and consequently impairs information processing speed, a causal relationship cannot be concluded and more detailed biophysical investigation is required for a complete characterization. Furthermore, white matter is composed of axons, dendrites, and glial cells for which their analyses were outside the scope of this study. In particular, glial cells play a critical role in myelinating axons and facilitating the speed and coordination of action potential propagation along neuronal fibers and among neurons (Forrest et al., 2018). The potential differential impact of these different cell types on neural synchronization is currently unknown. Thus, greater characterization of the role of these different cell types on network neural synchronization requires further elucidation. These types of studies have the potential to elucidate the degree to which synaptic plasticity, myelination, and the development and refinement of neural circuits ultimately impact cognitive performance. The characterization of white matter will importantly further the understanding of the driving force behind individual differences in cognitive abilities, above and beyond those explained by age and/or diagnostic status. Furthermore, this will be key in understanding why two individuals who are under similar conditions may emerge with vastly different cognitive outcomes. In addition, it is important to examine how our network connectome-based results of the DMN and ECN relate to white matter tracts, such the superior longitudinal fasciculus, that are known to play key roles in cognitive functioning in children (Urger et al., 2015). Addressing these and related questions will require new directions of empirical research that bridge the gap between cognitive performance and the complex neurobiological architectures that are required to support them. These questions would benefit from translational and cross-translational animal and human longitudinal empirical studies as a key step towards characterizing developmental changes in cognitive functioning and how this is associated with adaptive and maladaptive structure–function coupling.

The results of this work are primarily limited by the characteristics of the clinical population studied. Although our sample was large for an imaging study with a clinical population, the group sample sizes limited our ability to examine individual differences that may impact links in structure–function coupling. Furthermore, there was heterogeneity in our patient demographics. For example, our sample had a wide age range, and even though we statistically examined the influence of age on network connectivity and information processing speed performance, there are maturational changes that occur in the brain during childhood and adolescence (Lebel & Beaulieu, 2011). For example, the DMN and ECN may potentially be in different developmental stages. Thus, additional research is necessary to further explore the influence of brain development on neural network connectivity and information processing speed using longitudinal and within-subject experimental designs. Furthermore, the PBTS group had heterogeneous clinical characteristics which may be related to varying patterns of network connectivity. Controlling these medical variables in future studies with larger patient samples is important to examine the role that different tumor and treatment regimens may play on neural network connectivity and cognitive performance. Despite these limitations, results from this study provide key insights into the impact of DMN and ECN white matter connectivity on neural communication and information processing speed performance.

Conclusion

Examining how adaptive and maladaptive changes to the structure and conductive properties of white matter influences and modulates the synchrony and coordination of brain networks, and how this coupling impacts cognitive performance is an important question in human neuroscience. We combined human connectome data, computational modeling, and cognitive measures of information processing speed performance to explore these links in both the developing brain and in acquired brain injuries. Our findings suggest that during the presence and absence of task demands, DMN and ECN structural connectivity influences neural communication and information processing speed, and that white matter compromise indirectly impacts behavior by disrupting neural synchrony. The ability to accurately recapitulate function from structure through our computational model is pivotal to deciphering the underlying link between DMN and ECN connectivity and information processing speed. Taken together, these results suggest that efficient information processing speed is due to the coordinated synchrony of neural activity of the DMN and ECN that is modulated by their white matter networks.
