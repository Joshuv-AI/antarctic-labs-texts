Phase II randomised placebo-controlled trial of sodium selenate as a disease-modifying treatment in chronic drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy: the SeLECT study protocol
Authors: Lucy Vivash, Hannah Johns, Leonid Churilov, Sara MacPhail, Pablo Casillas-Espinosa, Charles Malpas, Sandy R Shultz, Chris Tailby, Manori Wijayath, David Reutens, Lisa Gillinder, Piero Perucca, Patrick Carney, John-Paul Nicolo, Nicholas Lawn, Patrick Kwan, Dennis Velakoulis, Christopher M Hovens, Terence J O’Brien

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEpilepsy is one of the most common neurological conditions worldwide. Despite many antiseizure medications (ASMs) being available, up to one-third of patients do not achieve seizure control. Preclinical studies have shown treatment with sodium selenate to have a disease-modifying effect in a rat model of chronic temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE).AimThis randomised placebo-controlled trial aims to evaluate the antiseizure and disease-modifying effects of sodium selenate in people with drug-resistant TLE.MethodsThis will be a randomised placebo-controlled trial of sodium selenate. One hundred and twenty-four adults with drug-resistant TLE and ≥4 countable seizures/month will be recruited. Outcomes of interest will be measured at baseline, week 26 and week 52 and include an 8-week seizure diary, 24-hour electroencephalogram and cognitive, neuropsychiatric and quality of life measures. Participants will then be randomised to receive a sustained release formulation of sodium selenate (initially 10 mg three times a day, increasing to 15 mg three times a day at week 4 if tolerated) or a matching placebo for 26 weeks.OutcomesThe primary outcome will be a consumer codesigned epilepsy-Desirability of Outcome Rank (DOOR), combining change in seizure frequency, adverse events, quality of life and ASM burden measures into a single outcome measure, compared between treatment arms over the whole 52-week period. Secondary outcomes will compare baseline measures to week 26 (antiseizure) and week 52 (disease modification). Exploratory measures will include biomarkers of treatment response.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the lead site, Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (594/20). Each participant will provide written informed consent prior to any trial procedures. The results of the study will be presented at national and international conferences, published in peer-reviewed journals and disseminated through consumer organisations.ConclusionThis study will be the first disease-modification randomised controlled trial in patients with drug-resistant TLE.Trial registration numberANZCTR; ACTRN12623000446662.Keywords: epilepsy, clinical trial, randomized controlled trial

Background

Epilepsy, a common and serious disabling brain disorder affecting over 50 million people worldwide,1 is characterised by an enduring predisposition to seizures and has numerous neurobiological, cognitive and psychosocial consequences.2 In about one-third of patients, the currently available symptomatic treatment, antiseizure medications (ASMs), do not effectively control seizures and often have significant adverse effects.3 Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is the most prevalent cause of drug-resistant epilepsy in adults.4 In recent times there have been significant advances in the understanding of epilepsy and this has contributed to the development and marketing of more than 15 new ASMs. However, these new ASMs have contributed only minor benefits in terms of improved outcomes, and have not reduced the proportion of people with drug-resistant epilepsy.5 Most importantly, it is believed that all standard ASMs are merely symptomatic, suppressing seizures while the patient is taking them regularly, but do not prevent the development of epilepsy (ie, antiepileptogenesis), nor in established epilepsy mitigate its severity, drug resistance or associated common cognitive and neuropsychiatric comorbidities in a sustained manner (ie, disease modification).6

One randomised clinical trial, EPISTOP, reported an antiepileptogenic effect of treating infants (<2 years of age) with tuberous sclerosis preventatively with vigabatrin after epileptiform discharges were detected on video-electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings compared with conventional treatment, where the initiation of vigabatrin was delayed until after the occurrence of clinical or electrographic seizure.7 Patients who received preventive treatment were reported to have a longer time to onset and reduced incidence of clinical seizures and were less likely to develop drug-resistant epilepsy and infantile spasms by 24 months of age. This study provides ‘proof-of-concept’ data that medical antiepileptogenesis/disease modification is possible, however, the final assessment in this study at 24 months of age was while the children were still being treated with vigabatrin, so whether the beneficial effects are sustained after the treatment was ceased is still to be established. No randomised controlled trial (RCT) has been published that has assessed a disease-modifying effect of a medical treatment for patients with established epilepsy, in adults or children.

Drug-resistant epilepsy is associated with significant morbidity, including decreased quality of life, increased injury and hospitalisation rates, higher medical and psychiatric comorbidities,8 elevated death rates9 and increased economic costs to society.10 The most effective way currently to render patients with drug-resistant epilepsy seizure-free is by resective epilepsy surgery, with only small probabilities of seizure freedom with dietary therapies or neurostimulation. For successful epilepsy surgery, a sufficient amount of the epileptogenic zone is excised or destroyed ideally resulting in seizure freedom without need for ASM (ie, a cure) or rendering the epilepsy drug-responsive (ie, seizure free on medication).11 These outcomes are achieved in approximately 70%–80% of patients who are ‘ideal’ surgical candidates, in whom all of the aforementioned adverse accompaniments of drug-resistant epilepsy, including psychiatric comorbidities and death rates, are significantly improved.11–13 Unfortunately, <5% of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy are candidates for epilepsy surgery because the epileptogenic zone cannot be sufficiently localised to a single excisable or destroyable region.14 Therefore, the challenge for the field of translational therapy development research for epilepsy and related disorders is to devise effective disease-modifying therapies for drug-resistant epilepsy with medical or cellular approaches. Such a treatment, unlike current ASMs, has the potential to have an enduring effect on drug-resistant epilepsy, and improve the quality of life, safety and risk of death for millions of people who suffer with drug-resistant epilepsy.15

Hyperphosphorylated tau (h-tau) has been implicated as a key pathological driver of a variety of neurodegenerative and neurological diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, progressive supranuclear palsy and chronic traumatic encephalopathy.16 17 There is building preclinical and clinical evidence that h-tau is also implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE).17–23 h-tau is present in the brains of rodents with MTLE.18 Deposits of h-tau have been identified in surgically resected tissue from patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, including MTLE,17 19 20 22 and have been shown to correlate with cognitive decline.21 22 Sodium selenate specifically activates protein phosphatase 2 (PP2A), the enzyme responsible for over 70% of brain phosphatase activity.24 Reducing h-tau through activation of PP2A can be antiepileptogenic/disease modifying for the epilepsy and associated neuropsychiatric comorbidities.23 25Previous preclinical work by our group has demonstrated that sodium selenate has not only antiseizure,23 but also antiepileptogenic25 and disease-modifying effects in rodents.26 Using the post-kainic acid status epilepticus rat model of chronic drug-resistant TLE, we have demonstrated that 4 weeks of treatment with sodium selenate reduced seizures recorded on a 1 week continuous EEG 8 weeks after the end of treatment, with 4/12 of the rats having no seizures in the post-treatment recording period, and also a significant mitigation of measures of cognitive and sensorimotor comorbidities and molecular biomarkers of tau hyperphosphorylation and senesce.26

Previous clinical trials of sodium selenate in neurodegenerative diseases by our group have shown excellent safety and tolerability with chronic treatment.27–29 The aim of the present study is to perform a double-blind RCT of sodium selenate as a symptomatic and disease-modifying treatment for chronic drug-resistant TLE.

Aims/outcomes

The primary aim of this trial is to investigate if 26 weeks of treatment with sodium selenate (15 mg, taken three times a day) improves clinical epilepsy outcomes at 52 weeks in patients with chronic drug-resistant TLE when compared with 26 weeks of treatment with placebo. The primary outcome measure will be Desirability of Outcome Rank (DOOR). This epilepsy-DOOR framework has been specifically adapted by people with epilepsy for use in this study combining change in seizure frequency, adverse events, quality of life and medication burden measures into a single outcome measure.30

The secondary outcome measures will be change in seizure and epileptiform discharge frequency, measured by 8-week seizure diaries and 24 hour EEG recordings; ASM burden, measured using the Liverpool Adverse Event Profile (LAEP); psychiatric comorbidities measured using the Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory for Epilepsy (NDDI-E) and General Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7); cognitive deficits as measured by the Australian Epilepsy Project (AEP) cognitive battery; and quality of life as measured by the Quality of Life in Epilepsy Inventory 31 (QOLIE-31). Secondary outcome measures will compare baseline measures to both 26 weeks (end of treatment) and 52 weeks (end of study).

Exploratory measures will include blood biomarkers, such as tau, phosphorylated-tau and neurofilament light chain levels, as well as levels of ASMs, and sodium selenate pharmacokinetics to investigate the impact of treatment on biomarkers as well as identifying predictive biomarkers of treatment response. Changes to seizure severity (ie, changing from bilateral tonic–clonic to focal impaired awareness seizures) and other measures of changes to epilepsy severity will also be investigated as exploratory endpoints. Moreover, subgroup analyses of dominant/non-dominant TLE and bilateral TLE may also be investigated if sufficient group sizes are available.

Methods and analysis

This is a multisite, phase II, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial of sodium selenate as a treatment for drug-resistant TLE. Participants will be randomised to receive 26 weeks of treatment with either a sustained release formulation of sodium selenate (15 mg three times a day31 32) or placebo and then assessments originally performed at baseline repeated. At the end of the treatment period, participants will remain in the study for another 26 weeks and then assessments performed at baseline again repeated. One hundred and twenty-four patients will be recruited into the study from nine specialist epilepsy centres in Melbourne, Brisbane, Sydney and Perth who are members of the Australian Epilepsy Clinical Trials Network (AECTN). The study is funded by the Australian Medical Research Future Fund (GNT2023250). The multisite study was approved for all sites by the Alfred Health Human Research Ethics Committee, Melbourne (319/23) under the national mutual acceptance scheme. The trial is prospectively registered with the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Register, ANZCTR (ACTRN12623000446662). The study will commence recruitment in October 2023 and is anticipated to complete (last patient last visit) in December 2026.

Patient and public involvement

Consumers have been involved in the design of this study and will continue to be involved in the ongoing conduct of the trial through the study advisory board. Consumers informed the original adaptation of the epilepsy-DOOR scale, and further refinement of the scale was made at the protocol development forum, where eight consumers finalised the epilepsy-DOOR scale and provided input into other aspects of the study design/protocol.30

The study advisory board have reviewed and provided feedback on the participant information and consent forms and other patient facing documents. The study advisory board will meet with the core investigator team every 6 months to review the progress of the study and provide their input into the ongoing conduct of the trial. They will also be invited to the annual investigator meeting to discuss the study progress with the wider study team.

As is required by Australian ethics committees, and detailed in the study consent form, a plain English summary of the study will be provided to all study participants at the conclusion of the study. Wider dissemination of the trial results to the community will be done via media, patient support groups such as the epilepsy foundation and epilepsy action and open events at hospitals and universities.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure will be the epilepsy-DOOR outcome over the 52-week study period in both sodium selenate and placebo-treated participants. The epilepsy-DOOR measure is a consumer codesigned outcome measure which combines seizure frequency, adverse events and quality of life measures.

The secondary outcome measures will be the epilepsy-DOOR outcome at 26 weeks (the end of treatment period). Other secondary outcome measures will be change in seizure frequency and interictal epileptiform discharge number, ASM burden measured by the LAEP, psychiatric comorbidities measured by the NDDI-E and GAD-7, cognitive deficits measured by the AEP cognitive battery and quality of life measured by the QOLIE-31. All secondary measures will compare baseline to week 26 to measure anti-seizure effects and baseline to week 52 to measure disease modification effects.

Exploratory outcomes will measure protein biomarker levels in plasma as well as drug levels (both sodium selenate and ASMs), and other exploratory blood measures (including DNA telomere length). Advanced statistical modelling will be investigated to identify baseline predictors of treatment response and non-response.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants will be aged 18–75 years at the time of enrolment and have a diagnosis of drug-resistant TLE for at least 2 years at the time of screening. Participants must have ≥4 countable seizures per month (any combination of focal impaired awareness seizures, focal to bilateral tonic clonic seizures or focal aware seizures with motor symptoms) and be on ≥1 stable ASM for a period of >8 weeks. Participants with vagal nerve stimulators (>6 months since implantation surgery, stable settings >8 weeks) are eligible, as are participants who have had prior epilepsy surgery (>12 months prior to screening). Participants must have interictal epileptiform discharges (average >1 spike or sharp wave per hour) on baseline 24-hour EEG localising to one or both temporal lobes without extratemporal discharges or seizures. Participants need to have had an epilepsy protocol MRI which identified no potentially epileptogenic lesion other than hippocampal/mesial temporal sclerosis. Participants must be able to provide written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Participants with extratemporal or genetic generalised epilepsy are excluded, as are those with a current or historic substance use disorder (as per diagnostic and statistical manual version 5). Women who are pregnant or lactating and persons of reproductive potential unwilling/unable to take adequate contraceptive precaution for the duration of the study are excluded. Participants who have recently (within 3 months of screening) participated in another interventional clinical trial are excluded. Participants who have a recent (within 5 years) history of psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, or who have significant medical or neurological disease other than epilepsy that is not adequately controlled by therapy are excluded. Likely non-compliance with the study procedures or study medication is exclusionary as is a clinically significant abnormality on safety blood tests, physical examination, ECG or EEG, known sensitivity to sodium selenate, selenium, related selenoproteins or lactose. Participants who have commenced medications with a known effect on mood, cognition or seizures within 4 weeks of screening are also excluded.

Intervention, randomisation and blinding

Each participant will be assigned a unique screening number at signing of the consent form. On completion of all screening assessments and confirmation of participant eligibility the participant will be assigned a randomisation number generated within the REDCap electronic cases report form (eCRF).33 Participants will be stratified according to seizure frequency during the screening period (greater or fewer than eight seizures/month) and presence or absence of hippocampal/mesial temporal sclerosis. The randomisation number will be provided to the site pharmacist who will then dispense the drug/placebo in accordance with the randomisation schedule.

Participants will be randomised to receive 26 weeks of either sodium selenate or placebo (1:1). Each tablet contains 5 mg of drug/placebo in a sustained release formulation (Recipharm Pharmaservices Pvt. Ltd, Nelamangala Taluk, Bengaluru, India) as for our ongoing RCTs for the treatment of Frontotemporal Dementia32 (ACTRN12620000236998) and Progressive Supranuclear Palsy31 (ACTRN12620001254987). The initial dose will be 10 mg three times a day, increasing to 15 mg three times a day at week 4, subject to tolerability. One within-subject down titration to 10 mg three times a day will be allowed at the investigator’s discretion in the event of adverse events. A further down titration to 5 mg three times a day may be enacted after consultation with the medical monitor.

Participants will cease the study treatment after 26 weeks but will remain in the study until week 52 to monitor for the long-term sustained disease-modifying effects of sodium selenate treatment.

Adjustment to concomitant ASMs will be allowed throughout the study. These changes will be carried out by the site investigator and detailed in the eCRF.

The study participant and all site staff (with the exception of the pharmacy team) will be blind to study treatment for the duration of the study.

Procedures and assessments

The study procedures and assessments will be carried out as outlined in table 1 and figure 1. At the screening assessment the participant will be assessed against all inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria to ensure compliance. Participants will be asked to complete an 8-week seizure diary (paper or app-based), as well as undergo a baseline 24-hour EEG to confirm the patient has sufficiently frequent seizures and/or interictal epileptiform discharges (ie, an average of at least one epileptiform discharge per hour). At the baseline visit the inclusion/exclusion criteria will be reviewed and eligibility confirmed. They will undergo a range of baseline safety assessments: physical and neurological examinations, safety blood and urinalysis, ECG and blood sample for future exploratory analyses. The participant will complete a battery of cognitive, neuropsychiatric and quality of life scales. Participants will be administered their first dose (10 mg) of drug in the clinic and monitored for 2 hours for adverse events. Participants will be given diaries to record both seizures and adverse events between clinical visits.

Measures

Seizure diaries

Participants will be given seizure diaries to record the number and type of seizures occurring between clinical visits. The seizure type (focal aware with motor, focal impaired awareness, focal to bilateral tonic clonic seizures) and frequency will be recorded by patients on paper or app-based diaries. Patients will be instructed by the study trial team regarding how to classify their different seizure types into Type A, Type B, Type C and so on.

24-Hour ambulatory EEG

24-Hour ambulatory EEGs will measure seizures and epileptiform activity (spikes). Baseline EEGs will be reviewed locally to ensure sufficient epileptiform activity (>1 interictal epileptiform discharge or electrographic seizure/hour) and temporal lobe localisation. For the secondary endpoint analysis EEG seizures and interictal epileptiform discharge frequency on all 24-hour EEGs will be centrally read by a specialist epileptologist who is an ANZAN Level 3 certified EEG reader and blinded to the patients identity, treatment group and week that the EEG was acquired, to ensure consistency and objectivity of the EEG analysis. 24-hour ambulatory EEGs will be acquired at prior to baseline, Week 26 and week 52.

Cognitive battery and other participant scales

The cognitive battery and participant scales will be administered at baseline, week 26 and week 52. The administration of cognitive measures, including training of study staff, will be overseen by a board-certified clinical neuropsychologist.

AEP cognitive battery

The Australian Epilepsy Project cognitive battery will be delivered remotely the day before participants’ clinic visits. It is a computerised battery of cognitive tests that are specifically designed to assess cognitive deficits that are common in patients with epilepsy. The battery takes approximately 35 mins to complete.

Quality of life in epilepsy inventory (QOLIE-31)

The Quality of Life in Epilepsy Inventory (QOLIE-31) contains 31 items that make up seven scales that measure health concepts associated with epilepsy: emotional well-being, seizure worry, energy/fatigue, social function, cognitive function, medication effects and overall quality of life. Each concept is comprised of multiple individual items and scores out of 100 calculated for each concept as well as an overall QOLIE-31 score.34 The scale takes approximately 10 min to complete.

Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory in Epilepsy (NDDI-E)

The Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory in Epilepsy (NDDI-E) is a 6-item questionnaire that is used to screen for depression (and depression severity) in people with epilepsy. For each item, participants score between 1 and 4 and resulting in a total score of 6–24. A score of>15 is considered a positive screening outcome for depression.35 The scale takes <5 min to complete.

Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7)

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7) is a 7-item questionnaire that is used for the screening of anxiety. For each item, participants score between 0 and 3 resulting in a total score of 0–21. A score of >7 is considered a positive screening outcome for anxiety.36 The scale takes <5 min to complete.

Liverpool Adverse Events Profile (LAEP)

The Liverpool Adverse Events Profile is a 19-item questionnaire assessing the presence and severity of common adverse effects associated with ASMs. Items are scored between 1 and 4, resulting in a total score of 19–76.37 The scale takes approximately 5 min to complete.

Safety assessments

Safety assessments including physical and neurological examinations, vitals, 12-lead ECG and urine pregnancy test will be conducted at screening, baseline, week 10, 26 and 52. Safety bloods and urinalysis will be completed at screening, baseline, week 10, 26, 34 and 52. Adverse event and concomitant medication reviews, urine pregnancy test and Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale will be completed at each visit.

Exploratory blood biomarkers

Blood samples will be collected for measures of exploratory biomarkers and pharmacokinetics of sodium selenate and trough ASM levels. Exploratory samples will include whole blood for DNA and RNA, serum and plasma for measures of tau, phospho-tau, neurofilament light chain and other related proteins. Additional hypothesis-driven testing may be performed. Samples for DNA/RNA will be at baseline only, other samples will be collected at baseline, week 26 and 52.

Outcomes and statistical overview

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint measure is the epilepsy-DOOR from baseline to week 52 compared between treatment and placebo. At the end of the study each participant will be given an epilepsy-DOOR based on combinations of three outcomes: change in seizure frequency (from baseline to week 52, 4 different categories of outcome), adverse events (presence and severity throughout the 52 weeks, 5 categories of outcome) and change in quality of life (from baseline to week 52, 3 categories of outcome).30 A summary of the outcomes can be found in table 2. Categories of outcome for each of these measures will be combined into a total of 60 possible outcomes. The primary analysis will compare a randomly selected active treatment participant to a randomly selected placebo-treated participant. Whomever has the higher rank ‘wins’. Change in ASMs will be used to separate any ties (reduction in ASM burden preferable over no change/increase in ASM burden). More ‘wins’ in the treatment group will demonstrate a positive outcome for the treatment. All analyses will be completed on both the intention to treat and per-protocol populations, further details will be provided in the statistical analysis plan.

Power and sample size

The study is powered to detect a difference in the primary outcome and will therefore be declared positive or negative based on the primary outcome measure. The sample has been estimated based on sodium selenate treatment having a win:draw:loss proportions of 60:10:30 resulting in a win ratio = (0.6+0.1/2)/(0.3+0.1/2) = 0.65/0.35=1.86.

Recruiting 108 participants would yield power of 0.8 to observe such hypothesised treatment effect against the null hypothesis of no treatment effect (Win Ratio=1) assuming two-sided alpha level of 0.05.38 In order to account for 10–15% potential losses due to screen failures and non-evaluable data the final sample size for this study is set as 124 participants.

Secondary endpoints

Secondary endpoints will use generalised linear models to assess group-level differences between baseline and week 26 to assess antiseizure effects, and baseline and week 52 to assess antiepileptogenesis effects. Differences in seizure frequency on seizure diaries, epileptiform discharge frequency on 24-hour EEGs, NDDI-E score, GAD-7 score, QOLIE-31 score, AEP cognitive composite score, LAEP score will all be compared between treatment and placebo groups. Baseline measures will be included in the models as covariates and the model will estimate the adjusted mean change (with 95% CIs) as a marker of treatment.

Safety and tolerability assessments

Adverse events will be presented as frequency tables (number of participants and percentage). Tables will demonstrate both the number of participants (N) and the number of incidences (n). Determination of safety and tolerability will be the frequency of serious adverse events (SAEs; common terminology criteria for adverse events score ≥3)

Monitoring and data quality

All data collected will be verified by review of source data by the project manager at regular intervals throughout the trial to ensure the eCRF remains up to date and accurate, and that no major protocol deviations that could affect study integrity are occurring. The rate of subject recruitment at each site will determine the frequency of monitoring visits.

An independent medical monitor will provide safety oversight. They will oversee the study conduct and regularly review all safety-related events.

A Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) will be made up of an independent clinician, an independent biostatistician and the medical monitor. The first DSMB meeting will occur within 2 weeks of the week 10 visit for the third randomised patient or within 1 week of the second SAE occurring, whichever is earlier. Meetings will then occur every 6 months. Additional DSMB meetings may be requested by a site principal investigator or the medical monitor if there are any urgent safety concerns. Following each DSMB meeting the medical monitor (as chair of the DSMB) will make recommendations to the principal investigator on the ongoing conduct of trial based on safety and tolerability issues.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been reviewed and approved by Alfred Hospital Human Ethics Committee (319/23). All participants will provide written informed consent prior to the commencement of study procedures. The results of the trial will be disseminated through presentations at national and international scientific conferences, consumer association meetings and published in peer-reviewed journals. The study is prospectively registered with the ANZCTR, and any protocol amendments will be approved by the Human Ethics Committee prior to implementation and the study registration updated on ANZCTR.
