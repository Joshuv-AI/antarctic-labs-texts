The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Dynamically Downscaled Ensemble (EDDE) version 2: A 3D physics-based dataset for projections of future extreme weather events
Authors: Megan S Mallard, Tanya L Spero, Jared H Bowden, Jeff Willison, Kathy Brehme, Lara J Reynolds
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Dynamically Downscaled Ensemble version 2 (EDDEv2) contains 3D physics-based projections of future conditions and extreme events over a model domain with 12-km grid spacing covering most of North America and focusing on the contiguous U.S. (CONUS). The Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model is used to downscale global projections from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) over a 30-year historical period (1985–2014) and a 75-year future period (2025–2099) under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathways. The output from WRF is further processed to create self-describing netCDF files conforming to the Climate and Forecasting System (CF) standards. A subset of EDDEv2 data is available with free egress via Amazon Web Services (AWS) Open Data Project at temporal frequencies ranging from 5 min to monthly. In addition to key variables like precipitation and 2-m temperature, EDDEv2 also contains other dynamically consistent atmospheric and soil fields that can support subsequent modeling applications, including humidity, winds, radiative variables, heat fluxes, and soil temperature and moisture, among others. The continuous, high-frequency data through the end of the century make EDDEv2 well-suited to explore potential changes to localized extreme events that can occur over a range of timescales, including heat extremes and flooding. The use of a large suite of variables facilitates modeling potential impacts on agriculture, infrastructure, and ecosystems, among other applications.Keywords: Regional climate modeling, WRF, CMIP6, Resilience, Adaptation, Hydrological, Air quality, Agricultural

Value of the Data

•The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Dynamically Downscaled Ensemble version 2 (EDDEv2) dataset is a comprehensive suite of dynamically downscaled, 3D projections of future conditions under multiple scenarios through the end of the 21st century at high temporal frequencies (i.e., 5-minute to monthly) over most of North America and at 12-km horizontal grid spacing. Each future ensemble member is accompanied by a 30-year simulated historical period to explore potential changes to weather extremes.

Background

Global climate models (GCMs) simulate the evolution of atmospheric, oceanic, terrestrial, and biogeochemical processes across multiple centuries. These data are typically provided at spatial scales of ∼1° × 1°. Local decision makers require data that have greater spatial and temporal granularity for adaptation and resilience planning. “Downscaling” is used to project changes to atmospheric conditions at smaller scales that can inform decision making.

EDDEv2 [1] was created using “dynamical downscaling”, a physics-based modeling process that generates holistic and dynamically consistent 3D data that can inform adaptation and support resilience. EDDEv2 uses the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model version 4.5.1 (WRFv4.5.1) [2] to downscale GCM simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). EDDEv2 includes hourly and 5-minute data at 12-km grid spacing for most of North America, including the U.S. Caribbean.

EDDEv2 incorporates new science for prescribing climatology-informed lake and sea surface temperatures, spectral nudging toward moisture, lengthened spin-up periods, and corrections to WRF’s tracking of accumulated fields over very long simulations. The transition from EDDEv1 to EDDEv2 is described by Spero et al. [3]. EDDEv2 can be used for modeling that requires data beyond temperature and precipitation, including air quality, hydrology, ecology, agriculture, and infrastructure applications.

Data Description

EDDEv2 is composed of WRF output files, which have prescribed frequencies of 5 min (for five variables) and 1 hour (for 164 variables). The complete EDDEv2 dataset is too large to publicly host, so a subset of EDDEv2 fields is available via Amazon Web Services (AWS); see Table 1. This subset of EDDEv2 is available in netCDF for select variables with free egress. Selected time-invariant fields are also provided to describe the model grid cells and aid analysis. For most fields in the publicly hosted EDDEv2 data, the variable names and units conform to Climate and Forecasting System (CF) conventions (https://cfconventions.org/) version 1.8. The complete EDDEv2 dataset, including all WRF input and output files, is 3.9 terabytes per simulation year, and it is archived on the EPA High-End Scientific Computing system.

To build the publicly hosted subset of EDDEv2, the WRF output was postprocessed to extract and prepare the variables shown in Table 1. The time-invariant (i.e., “static”) fields were extracted from the output from the WRF Preprocessing System program, Geogrid, which establishes the locations and properties of the individual grid cells in the WRF modeling domain (i.e., spatial footprint; see Fig. 1). The time-varying data were extracted from WRF model output. Temperature, specific humidity, precipitation, and surface pressure from WRF were saved at both 5-minute and 1-hour frequencies. A subset of variables (e.g., geopotential height and soil variables) are only available on AWS on a 6-hourly frequency. All variables provided on AWS are made available for the entirety of the historical and future periods, only excluding the initial model spin-up time. The availability of sub-daily data throughout the historical and future periods can support a variety of modeling applications and the computation of indices which require sub-daily data. Spero et al. [3] (Section 3) provides a summary and review of applications already undertaken utilizing EDDEv1. Within EDDEv2, key variables have also been temporally aggregated to daily and monthly timescales for the convenience of users who do not require higher temporal frequency data.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Global data and scenarios

The MPI-ESM1–2-HR [4] (hereafter, “MPI”) model was identified as among the top-performing GCMs over the CONUS within an unpublished literature review performed by the authors. The initial EDDEv2 simulations are driven by MPI for two of the four Tier 1 CMIP6 Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) [5]: SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0. The MPI variant, r1i1p1f1, is chosen for all scenarios, and data are acquired at ∼100 km for atmospheric and land surface components and ∼50 km for ocean temperatures. The upper-atmospheric data from MPI were acquired on 95 native model levels to minimize the loss of information from multiple vertical interpolations. The atmospheric data from MPI were available at 6-hourly increments for temperature, humidity, horizontal wind components, and pressure, as well as soil temperature and moisture. Ocean temperatures and surface temperatures were provided from MPI as monthly data. For WRF, water temperatures were assigned to be valid at the 15th day of each month and linearly interpolated to 6-hourly intervals to allow gradual transitions in those fields.

Dynamical downscaling configuration

WRF physics setup

EDDEv2 was run using WRFv4.5.1 with modifications to WRF that were tailored for EDDEv2, as described further below. WRF is maintained by the National Science Foundation (NSF) National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR), and intermittent updates to WRF are publicly released to include new science, software corrections, and advanced capabilities. EDDEv2 utilizes a 12-km domain with 577 × 433 grid points covering the CONUS and the U.S. Caribbean, as well as parts of Canada and Mexico (Fig. 1). In the vertical, 35 hybrid sigma-pressure levels extend from the surface to a model top set at 50 hPa, with thinner layers (i.e., higher vertical resolution) near the surface and a gradual transition to thicker layers aloft. Spectral nudging [6] is employed to constrain the WRF simulation toward the GCM solution. Spectral nudging is applied between the top of the planetary boundary layer (PBL) and the tropopause for temperature and water vapor mixing ratio, and it is applied above the PBL for geopotential and horizontal winds [7].

In EDDEv2, the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) 21-category land use (LU) classification—where category 21 generally distinguishes freshwater (lake) cells from saltwater (ocean) cells—defines the properties of each grid cell. The Noah land surface model [8] is used with a mosaic option to represent land-surface processes with properties of up to 6 LU classifications within each grid cell [9]. These options were evaluated by Mallard et al. [10], who demonstrated improvements over the CONUS for dynamical downscaling using reanalysis data. The Yonsei University PBL scheme [11] simulates near-surface air movement and interactions with the land-surface. The Multi-Scale Kain Fritsch scheme [12] parameterizes subgrid scale convection, and cloud microphysics is modeled with the WRF single-moment 6-class scheme [13]. The Rapid Radiative Transfer Model for Global Models [14] is used for both longwave and shortwave radiation, and the atmospheric greenhouse gases evolve in WRF following the scenarios. The advection time step is set to 60 s, representing a 5:1 ratio of the time step to the horizontal grid spacing that is recommended for WRF by NCAR. Intermittently, the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability condition was violated with this time step, so a handful of simulation days were run with a 30- or 40-second time step to overcome CFL violations.

EDDEv2 simulations are initialized 1 October 1983 for the 1985–2014 “historical” period and 1 October 2023 for the 2025–2099 future period. Each simulation includes a 15-month spin-up to allow both atmospheric and upper-level soil conditions to approach equilibrium, acknowledging that the length of the spin-up period is a pragmatic compromise for these resource-intensive simulations [15]. The spin-up periods are excluded from analysis. The historical period is deliberately set to a 30-year period to meet the conventional definition of a climatological period. The historical period ends at the transition to the SSPs in CMIP6 simulations. Each WRF simulation is run continuously to avoid physical discontinuities from reinitialization that could affect subsequent complex modeling applications.

EDDEv2 simulations are conducted on the EPA High-End Scientific Computing system. WRF was compiled with Intel Fortran 21.4 for the initial EDDEv2 simulations, and those simulations used 528 Intel Cascade Lake processors. Each year was simulated within the same execution of WRF, except as noted above for intermittent (average of 1 day per decade) CFL stability violations. WRF restart files were used at 00 UTC 1 January of each year after initialization to chronologically continue each scenario with the same physical and dynamical conditions while managing disk space and wall clock time limits.

Climatology-informed sea surface temperature gradients

In dynamical downscaling, WRF typically depends on the driving GCM to provide sea surface temperature (SST) data. As in most dynamical downscaling simulations, EDDEv2 does not use an explicit ocean model, and the ocean temperatures are only defined at the air-sea interface. Within MPI, the ocean model is run at ∼40-km grid spacing, and archived MPI SSTs are available at ∼50-km grid spacing. Although SSTs from MPI are available at finer horizontal grid spacing relative to the MPI atmospheric fields, those SSTs are coarser than the WRF domain. In general, the coarseness of the SST fields from GCMs (relative to the downscaling models) cannot capture offshore SST gradients that reflect major ocean currents that are important for cyclogenesis, storm intensification, and storm tracking that can produce extreme weather events.

To improve the SST fields obtained from GCMs for EDDEv2, a procedure was developed to leverage high-resolution ocean temperature records to create climatologically influenced temperature gradients that are at spatial scales used in the WRF simulations. To develop this pseudo-SST dataset (PSST), scaling factors are used to establish a climatologically derived relationship between observed SST and MPI SST that are interpolated bilinearly to the observed SST grid. First, a monthly climatology of observation-based SSTs is derived from the 1-km satellite-derived Group for High-Resolution SST (GHRSST; [16]) dataset over a 20-year period (2003–2022) for each month. A 30-year climatology of monthly bilinear interpolated MPI SSTs is also computed; then the scaling factor is taken as the ratio between the observed and interpolated GCM SSTs for each month and each grid cell. Subsequently, this set of spatiotemporal scaling factors is applied to scale monthly MPI SSTs over the historical and future periods. Therefore, this approach allows the PSST to incorporate temporal variability (e.g., interannual variability and future long-term warming over the basin) while including a type of bias correction that superimposes near-coastal gradients that are resolved by the observation-based SSTs. This procedure to preprocess the SSTs for WRF created gradients that were overlaid on the GCM SSTs, reflecting the likely characteristics of currents, such as the Gulf Stream off the eastern coast of the U.S. In EDDEv2, the resultant fields were incorporated into WRF via the standard lower-boundary (surface) forcing.

Although this process should result in physically plausible gradients in SSTs, it is imperfect. For example, diurnal SST cycles are not developed for shallow near-shore saltwater bodies, such as sounds and bays. In addition, transient eddies within the near-coastal water temperatures that are associated with intensification of synoptic storms are also not reflected in these data.

Subgrid lake surface temperatures

Sea surface temperatures provided by GCMs often have limited representation of lakes because most lakes are not explicitly resolved by GCMs. Even when large lakes, i.e., the Laurentian Great Lakes in North America, are partially or fully resolved by a GCM, the lake surface temperatures (LSTs) are frequently omitted from the ocean temperature data (de facto SSTs) that are publicly available in the GCM datasets. The absence of LST data in the GCM files can lead to erroneous lake temperatures in dynamically downscaling. For example, when no water temperature data are available from the driving data for WRF, WRF by default adopts a “nearest neighbor” outward radial search to assign water temperatures from the nearest point with a defined water temperature in the driving dataset [17]. This approach tends to work well along jagged coastlines, but it is often detrimental when applied to water bodies that are far inland and have no climatological association with the nearest ocean [18].

In the 12-km WRF domain used in EDDEv2, numerous grid cells are defined as predominantly isolated inland water bodies where no water temperatures are represented in the driving GCM, so no freeze-thaw cycles are associated with the water in those cells for WRF. Without intervention, those lakes in WRF would acquire water temperatures that would be mapped indiscriminately from the closest large water body, such as the Pacific Ocean or Hudson Bay.

For EDDEv2, a procedure was developed to set the LSTs, and subsequently the freezing and thawing times, for inland water bodies using climatology. Like the development of PSSTs described above, a pseudo-LST (PLST) is developed using scaling factors generated with a monthly climatology at each grid cell from the GHRSST product, which includes LST with potential improvements noted for atmospheric modeling studies [19].

For this application, in contrast to the PSST approach, model-simulated water temperatures are unavailable for many lake grid cells within the WRF target domain, as MPI lacks the spatial resolution to represent them. Therefore, scaling factors are computed as the ratio of the monthly observed GHRSST climatology and the monthly bilinear interpolated surface temperatures from MPI over those grid cells. Over the historical and future projected periods, these monthly scaling factors are then applied to surface temperatures to generate PLST fields. A minimum of 273.14 K is imposed on the resulting PLST product to prevent spuriously low PLSTs while also allowing freezing at the represented grid cells. All inland water bodies are assumed to be freshwater, so the freezing point for inland water bodies is set to 273.15 K (as discussed further below).

This methodology relies on the stationarity of the historical relationship between GCM surface temperature and the observation-based climatology used to compute the scaling factors. Similar dependencies should be considered when using WRF’s alternative lake setting, which became available in WRFv3.3 and allows the use of monthly-averaged 2-m temperatures to set LSTs, as discussed further by Mallard et al. [17] (see Section 2.1). However, these PLSTs are preferred over water temperatures that are arbitrarily assigned and climatologically dissociated from inland water bodies.

Distinguishing freshwater and saltwater freezing points

Historically, the water body freezing point in WRF was set for saltwater, i.e., 271.15 K, because LU classification options did not distinguish between freshwater and saltwater bodies until recently. Several LU classification options in WRF—including the MODIS 21 category LU classification used in EDDEv2—can now distinguish lakes from other water points with a supplemental LU category. This identification of lakes can be used as a simple proxy to distinguish freshwater and saltwater bodies.

Within WRF, ice on water bodies can be represented by either prescribing ice coverage through an input field or by invoking a runtime option that allows ice to be assigned based on water temperature. When using the latter option, ice forms with 100 % coverage over the grid cell when water temperature drops below a user-specified runtime threshold (seaice_threshold). When the WRF runtime option assigns ice in water bodies, the freezing point is applied indiscriminately to both saltwater and freshwater, which previously could not be distinguished. Applying this threshold to all water points with the originally implemented 271.15 K threshold would underrepresent frozen lakes which appear at a freezing point of 273.15 K. However, setting it to 273.15 K over the domain (Fig. 1) would tend to overrepresent sea ice development.

For EDDEv2, WRFv4.5.1 was modified to use the MODIS LU categories for lake and ocean points (in the “lake mask”) to apply different freezing points for freshwater and saltwater. The saltwater freezing point was set to 271.15 K through the WRF runtime variable, seaice_threshold. In addition, the authors added logic in the Real preprocessor in WRFv4.5.1 to use the lake mask to apply the freezing point of 273.15 K at grid cells that were identified as lakes.

Publicly released improvements to WRF

The authors developed three additional groups of changes to WRF for EDDEv2 simulations. These changes represent modifications to WRFv4.5.1 used in EDDEv2, and each of the changes was subsequently incorporated by NCAR into public releases of WRF. First, spectral nudging was expanded to include both an option to nudge toward water vapor mixing ratio and an option to restrict the nudging of thermodynamic fields (i.e., temperature and water vapor mixing ratio) to below the tropopause [20]. Those changes were targeted at improving the simulation of clouds, radiation, and precipitation, all of which are important for capturing physical processes in dynamical downscaling. These capabilities were submitted in pull request (PR) 378 to the WRF GitHub repository (https://github.com/wrf-model/WRF/releases) and released in WRFv4.0.

Second, a separate WRF output file was created for EDDEv2 that contains five 2D variables at 5-minute frequency: 2-m temperature (T2), 2-m water vapor mixing ratio (Q2), surface pressure (PSFC), and accumulations of precipitation generated by convective and non-convective physical processes (RAINC and RAINNC). These files were generated using an auxiliary (i.e., extra) output option that was enabled in WRF at runtime. After generating the new files, the authors discovered that the metadata in the auxiliary output files were incomplete, including the netCDF global attribute for the “tipping bucket” depth for precipitation (BUCKET_MM), which is required for isolating the precipitation in each grid cell within temporal increments. Hence, the authors introduced changes to WRF to fully populate the metadata in all auxiliary files. This capability was submitted to NCAR in PR2044 and subsequently released in WRFv4.6.1.

Finally, through aggregation, analysis, and postprocessing of the WRF output, the author team discovered that isolating the precipitation from a running total into shorter time increments resulted in spurious negative precipitation values that appeared about 32 years into a continuous WRF simulation. Upon closer inspection, the author team found that the condition occurred immediately after exceeding 224 (16,777,216) simulation minutes. The WRF timekeeping variable, XTIME, is a single-precision floating point variable that tracks the minutes of the simulation that have elapsed since initialization. As mentioned earlier, the integration time step for the EDDEv2 simulations is 60 seconds (1 minute), which means that values in the WRF log files should reflect that XTIME has increased by 1 for each time step. When XTIME exceeds 224, the single-precision floating point values in XTIME cannot be resolved correctly, and they become imprecise such that odd values of XTIME are incorrectly stored in memory as even numbers that are ±1 removed from the correct value.

Consequently, when XTIME is stored incorrectly in memory, XTIME is inappropriately evaluated at thresholds for periodic operations in WRF. One of those operations is emptying the filled precipitation buckets every 6 hours (360 minutes). After XTIME becomes imprecise in a sufficiently long simulation, the periodic operations in WRF for EDDEv2 were conducted one time step early, on time, and/or one time step late, sometimes repeating the operations on subsequent 60-second (1-minute) time steps and adversely affecting the WRF solution. In the case of the precipitation, the tipping buckets were unpredictably emptied prematurely (one time step early), leading to spurious negative precipitation values.

In addition, the imprecise values of XTIME adversely affected the update times for bracketing analyses used in spectral nudging. The frequency of the analyses for spectral nudging matches the frequency of the upper-atmospheric fields that are provided from the GCM, and it is 6-hourly for MPI (like most GCMs). In spectral nudging in WRF, XTIME is scaled within an algorithm such that the evaluation of that expression becomes imprecise at ∼23.25 years into the simulation. Accordingly, the bounding analyses for spectral nudging are inappropriately updated, sometimes in three successive time steps.

The comprehensive solution for WRF for very long simulations would be to move XTIME to a double-precision floating point variable. However, XTIME pervades the WRF system—including in subroutines that are shared among other NCAR models—so implementing the comprehensive solution would be onerous. Therefore, the author team developed pragmatic solutions in WRF to accurately reference XTIME in the precipitation tipping bucket and spectral nudging operations. These improvements to WRF were submitted to NCAR in PR2063, and they were publicly released in WRFv4.7.0.

Postprocessing WRF for public hosting

Some of the EDDEv2 variables are available in the same form (i.e., values and units) as they were simulated by WRF (e.g., 2-m temperature, surface radiative fluxes, surface pressure). Those variables were extracted and passed forward through postprocessing without modification. Other variables were derived from prognostic fields in WRF (e.g., wind direction, wind speed, relative humidity, dew point temperature) (Table 1).

Calculating precipitation totals at each grid cell over a given time increment involves several steps and an understanding of the precipitation management in WRF. By convention, precipitation in WRF is saved as a cumulative sum through the entire simulation, partitioned by the products of convective and non-convective atmospheric processes. WRF includes an optional “tipping bucket” that can be invoked to manage the precision of the simulation-total precipitation in long (i.e., multi-decadal) simulations. Consequently, precipitation in incremental (e.g., one-hour) blocks is computed at each grid cell from four fields and one global attribute: accumulated convective precipitation (RAINC), accumulated non-convective precipitation (RAINNC), the number of bucket tips for convective precipitation (I_RAINC), the number of bucket tips for non-convective precipitation (I_RAINNC), and the capacity of the bucket (BUCKET_MM, set to 1000 mm in EDDEv2 simulations). In WRF, the precipitation buckets in each grid cell are checked and emptied every 6 h of model time, so the accumulated totals can temporarily exceed BUCKET_MM. If either RAINC or RAINNC exceeds BUCKET_MM at the 6-hourly checkpoint, then the corresponding bucket counter (I_RAINC or I_RAINNC) is incremented by 1, and the total in RAINC or RAINNC is reduced by BUCKET_MM. This process is like trading pennies for dollars. The incremental precipitation totals at each grid cell are computed by subtracting values at the bracketing model time steps (e.g., the current hour and the previous hour) to calculate the convective and non-convective components, and then adding the convective and non-convective total over the period (e.g., hour) to compute total precipitation.

The hosted EDDEv2 files are in netCDF, a format that is commonly used for data from atmospheric models and is supported by readers in a variety of programming languages, including C, Fortran, R, and Python. Files in netCDF can be aggregated, subset, and visualized using several open-source codes, which enhances the portability of the data. Further, the hosted EDDEv2 files meet CF compliance—which prescribes conventions for variable names, units, timekeeping, and metadata—to enhance interoperability with other dynamically downscaled datasets.

Quality assurance of the data was provided through several automated and manual methods. After each year of WRF simulation, both the raw WRF output files and postprocessed files were examined by visualizing a subset of fields and using Fortran programs that scanned for “not a number” (i.e., NaN) values and ensured that the ranges of near-surface values were within realistic tolerances. The WRF output was visually spot-checked to ensure that the WRF simulation produced results that were meteorologically plausible for given regions and seasons. Files that were postprocessed for hosting on AWS were evaluated to confirm CF compliance using a code acquired from https://github.com/cedadev/cf-checker; that code only supports CF conventions through version 1.8, which is reflected in the metadata in the hosted EDDEv2 files.

In the AWS hosting of EDDEv2, all fields are split into individual files by variable using software programs written in Fortran. Each file reflects data for one time-varying field for one month and year at a prescribed frequency. In addition, each file includes latitude and longitude associated with each grid cell in the WRF simulation domain. The names of the EDDEv2 files on AWS follow this convention:

varname.scenario.GCM.EDDE-WRF.frequency.domain-resolution.YYYY-MM.raw.nc where “varname” refers to the abbreviated variable name in the CF conventions (where applicable); “scenario” is the GCM scenario; “GCM” is the driving model; “EDDE-WRF” identifies that the data are part of EDDE and the downscaling model is WRF; “frequency” is the temporal frequency of the data in the file; “domain-resolution” indicates the spatial coverage for the dataset and the horizontal grid spacing in kilometers; “YYYY-MM” is the four-digit year and two-digit month associated with the data, as taken from the GCM; and “.raw.nc” indicates that these data are directly from WRF without bias correction and that the file format is netCDF.

An example file from EDDEv2 is named “hur.hist.mpi.EDDE-WRF.1hr.NA-12.1990-04.raw.nc”. In this example, the variable is “hur”, which is the abbreviation assigned to relative humidity. The scenario, “hist”, indicates that these data follow the GCM historical period. The driving data, “mpi” indicate that MPI was used to provide large-scale forcing for WRF. The frequency, “1hr”, shows that there are hourly data in the file. The notation, “NA-12″ indicates that the data are for a domain over North America at 12- km horizontal grid spacing. The notation, “1990-04”, indicates that the data are associated with April 1990 in the GCM simulation.

The metadata in the EDDEv2 files include the standard suite of categories in netCDF files: dimensions, variables, and global attributes. There are four dimensions in each time-varying EDDEv2 file: time (unlimited, by netCDF convention), timestr (string length for an intuitive variable that represents time, mtime), and x and y (the number of grid cells in the grid-relative west-east and south-north dimensions, respectively).

The variables in each file are time, mtime, lon, lat, y, x, and the WRF variable listed in the file name. The variable, time, is a double-precision floating-point counter of the number of hours elapsed since WRF initialization; this style and representation of time is required to conform with CF conventions. The variable, mtime (model time), is a secondary representation of time, but it is in a user-readable and intuitive format that is used by WRF (YYYY-MO-DD_HH:MI:SS, where YYYY is the four-digit year, MO is the two-digit month, DD is the two-digit day of the month, HH is the two-digit hour from 00 to 23, MI is the two-digit minute, and SS is the two-digit second). The variables, lon and lat, are longitude and latitude associated with each grid cell, respectively, where positive values indicate east and north, and negative values indicate west and south. The variables, y and x, are double-precision floating-point representations of the coordinates of the WRF simulation domain in the Cartesian system; those coordinates are distances in grid-relative meters from a reference point in the simulation domain.

The global attributes in the EDDEv2 files on AWS broadly fall into four categories: tracking information for the code that postprocessed the data, domain description, data description, and data attribution and restrictions. Specifically, the global attributes, PROGNAME, VERSION, and CODE_DATE, refer to the Fortran code that generated the file.

In addition, several global attributes define the location and projection of the WRF simulation domain: NX, NY, MAP_PROJ, CEN_LAT, CEN_LON, TRUELAT1, TRUELAT2, MOAD_CEN_LAT, STAND_LON, DX, DY, and CRS. From that list of attributes, NX and NY are the numbers of grid cells in the west-east and south-north dimensions; these are identical to the WRF global attributes: WEST-EAST_PATCH_END_UNSTAG and SOUTH-NORTH_PATCH_END_UNSTAG, and one fewer than WEST-EAST_GRID_DIMENSION and SOUTH-NORTH_GRID_DIMENSION, respectively. The global attribute CRS (coordinate reference system) defines the definition of the projected space using the conventions required in GIS. The remaining global attributes in this cluster define parameters for the WRF domain in projected space, and their names and definitions are as in WRF.

The global attributes that describe the data include Conventions (the CF conventions that were applied to the data), title (the dataset title; here, “EDDE”), institution and institute_id (both refer to U.S. EPA), source (includes WRF, the driving GCM, and the scenario), model_id (“EDDE-WRF”, as in the file names), rcm_version_id (the WRF version that was used in the simulations), region (“North America”), resolution_id (the horizontal grid spacing used), and history (direct origin of the data). Some of these attributes were required by U.S. EPA to be included in metadata of publicly hosted files.

The final group of global attributes cover attribution and restrictions. For attribution, references (with a digital object identifier) and contact (names with email addresses) are provided for users to cite the data and to request additional information about the data. The attribute, useconstraints, provides important caveats that should be considered. The attribute, comment, includes other information about interpreting the data in the files. Finally, disclaimer releases the developers and their employers from warranty claims of accuracy and suitability of the data for any given application, as well as from liability for misuse of the data in the EDDE datasets.

Examining historical extremes

Here, daily maximum 2-m temperatures from the historical EDDEv2 MPI-driven simulation are compared with observational data from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) dataset [21]. A complete analysis of historical EDDEv2 is ongoing and best suited for subsequent publication. However, a single extremes metric is presented here for 2-m temperature—a variable of high interest for a variety of applications—to provide a brief assessment of the dataset’s performance for capturing extreme events.

Here, the 95th percentile of daily maximum 2-m temperature is computed for each year over the historical period of 1985–2014, then all days exceeding this threshold are identified (producing ∼18 days per each year at each grid cell). Therefore, not just the annual maximum but all events within the top 5th percentile are considered. The 95th percentile is computed using the “timpctl” operator within the Climate Data Operators (CDO) version 2.2.1. Here, the number of bins used within the algorithm to compute the 95th percentile is increased from the default of 101 to 1000, as the estimation of this threshold value and the resulting number of exceedances per year was found to be sensitive to the number of bins at smaller bin numbers. These top 5th percentile events are then averaged over the 30-year historical period at each grid point for the PRISM data, the MPI-driven EDDEv2 simulation using WRF, and the MPI GCM outputs (Fig. 2). To compute bias for both EDDEv2 and the MPI GCM, Earth System Modeling Framework (ESMF) bilinear remapping is used to transform PRISM’s values onto each respective simulation’s model grid. Comparison of these extreme temperatures between MPI GCM and the downscaled EDDEv2 simulation demonstrates added value from downscaling.

Average of the top 5th percentile daily maximum 2-m temperatures (K) for each year over 1985–2014 for PRISM (left), EDDEv2 (top center), and the MPI GCM (top right). Biases for EDDEv2 and the MPI GCM are shown on the lower panels.

The average top 5th percentile daily maximum temperatures from EDDEv2 show a mix of warm and cool biases of approximately ±1–3 K over much of the CONUS (Fig. 2). Generally, cool biases in these maxima are evident over the northeastern CONUS, as well as in the Pacific Northwest. Meanwhile warm biases occur over the south-central U.S. and in California’s Central Valley. When compared with biases from the MPI GCM, there are improvements in representing complex topography over portions of the western CONUS and the Appalachian Mountains in the eastern CONUS. Improvements are also evident over much of the southwestern U.S., as the area impacted by warm bias is reduced in EDDEv2 relative to the MPI GCM. Complex terrain and arid areas of the southwestern U.S. can be presumed to be better represented in the finer-resolution mosaic LU and topography present in the regional climate model (RCM). Biases in EDDEv2 compared against PRISM generally resemble those of the driving GCM but are mitigated by downscaling, which is consistent with Nolte et al. [22] when examining seasonally-averaged biases in 2-m temperature from EDDEv1 historical simulations. In both EDDEv1 and EDDEv2, spectral nudging constrains the RCM towards the driving GCM at wavelengths resolved by the global model while leaving smaller-scale features free to develop consistent with the RCM’s physics [7].

Spero et al. [3] (their Fig. 6) illustrated added value in EDDEv2 (compared with MPI) in representing the summer monsoon in the southwestern CONUS. Further evaluation of historical results is ongoing for temperature and precipitation extremes. Inclusion of additional GCMs and SSPs is also planned to expand the EDDEv2 ensemble to further address uncertainty associated with GCM and scenario selection. Depending on the needs of the application, uncertainty associated with the chosen downscaling methodology, e.g., statistical versus dynamical, should also be considered, when possible, when including EDDEv2 with other ensembles [23].

Limitations

As with all downscaled datasets driven by GCMs, neither the “historical” nor the future data specifically correspond to the labelled dates. Instead, the data are representative of and plausible over an aggregated climatological period.

EDDEv2 did not use bias correction. In EDDEv2, data from the GCMs was used to constrain the downscaling using spectral nudging in WRF, so biases in the GCM driving fields will also be reflected regionally in the EDDEv2 data. At the time of writing, there was not a robust scientific consensus on if or how to apply bias correction for dynamical downscaling applications like EDDEv2. In its most common application, bias correction uses the observational record to adjust downscaled simulation output a posteriori based on deviations between the model and observations over a climatological period. Some research suggests that bias correction will inappropriately constrain the extremes in projected data because bias correction is developed with the assumption that the biases in the historical record will have the same characteristics (i.e., “stationarity”) in the projected period.

The collective size of the EDDEv2 files prohibits the hosting of the complete dataset. Only selected fields are publicly available. Additional data can be made available upon request.
