Hierarchical bound states in heat transport
Authors: Shuihua Yang, Guoqiang Xu, Xue Zhou, Jiaxin Li, Xianghong Kong, Chenglong Zhou, Haiyan Fan, Jianfeng Chen, Cheng-Wei Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTopological phases have set waves in the photonic mode modulations. However, the lattice symmetry of photonic crystal confines the robust transport only at explicit system boundaries and thus fundamentally restricts the flexible reconfiguration in conventional topological insulators. This work breaks this intrinsic restriction, and now, we show complex eigenmodes and domain walls can be reconfigurable. This further creates significant hierarchies of topological bound states at arbitrary positions within the trivial bulk states in heat transport.

The understanding of topological phase of matter has transcended its origins in condensed matter physics and expanded to include classical wave systems of optics, acoustics, and mechanics, unveiling a plethora of counterintuitive phenomena (1–6). Recent discoveries in heat transport have broadened the territory of topological physics to dissipative diffusion following continuous model (7–10). Their property of thermal energy localization originates from topological properties, offering unique advancements in enhancing thermal management and actively modulating heat transport (11). Upon whether introducing advection, topological phases can emerge in either skew-Hermitian systems characterized by purely thermal diffusion or non-Hermitian systems involving convective heat transport (12–14).

High-order topological insulators (HOTIs) have emerged as a class of materials that exhibit bulk topological invariants with protective symmetries, featuring a hierarchy of bound states with lower dimensions than the overall system (15–17). One remarkable feature is the presence of topologically protected corner states in two classes of HOTIs, including the topological multipole systems with negative couplings described by Benalcazar–Bernevig–Hughes (BBH) model and the HOTIs derived from Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model (18, 19). These characteristics have been extensively utilized in photonic applications like low-loss waveguides (20), resonance (21), and lasing (22). Pioneering attempts have also revealed higher-order band topology in purely thermal conduction systems based on 2D SSH models, as well as in non-Hermitian quadrupole systems that rely on directional convection and complex bands (13, 23). However, state-of-the-art HOTIs solely confine the eigenstates to definite system boundaries, thereby restricting the reconfigurations of states and localization of energy under topological protection to arbitrary positions in both Hermitian and non-Hermitian systems (24–27). These monotonous states enabled by conventional topological insulators cannot satisfy the demands of in situ tunability in practical applications, thus leading to the significant challenge of synergizing flexibly topological guiding independent of a static structure. Such an open problem sheds dim light on creating arbitrarily reconfigurable bound states via both system couplings and on-site gain and loss, particularly in generalized topological systems featuring multidomain walls (28–31).

Here, we resort to judicious modulated Hermiticity and non-Hermiticity of a thermofluid system to provide a generalized diffusive-advective HOTI for demonstrating the reconfigurable bound states at arbitrary positions. We incorporate spatially modulated thermal coupling strength and on-site energy to construct interfaces in the non-Hermitian thermofluid system, which further enable configurable domain walls, respectively, in the real and imaginary bands following the continuum model. Depending on the localizations of these configurable interfaces, the bound states significantly occur at the tailored positions and exhibit hierarchical features of robust corner and nontrivial edge states within trivial bulk system. Our recipes provide a feasible way of actively reconfigurable localized states in non-Hermitian thermofluid systems and unlock rich topological phase transitions in extensively dissipative diffusions (32–34).

Due to the diffusive nature of thermofluid, non-Hermiticity is available to facilitate topological mode switching and transport of distinct modes within the lattice. Here, we explore the reconfigurable properties of thermal-bound states. Upon the foundation of topologically nontrivial thermal lattices, we separate the lattice into several domains along the constructed interface. The coupling strength along different domain boundaries can be flexibly tuned, thus partitioning the thermally higher-order lattice into distinct domains. By modulating the interface coupling strength or the advection in different regions, topological corner and edge modes emerge along the interfaces of these different domains as shown in Fig. 1 B and C.

We first focus on the hierarchical bound states enabled by the non-Hermitian thermal coupling. The manipulations of the thermal coupling strength at the interface result in shifts of the eigenmodes on both sides of the interface. Interestingly, the presence of anomalous energy dissipation along the interface leads to a breakdown of the insulating interior, resulting in the generation of internal topological modes. From the perspective of non-Hermitian eigenvalues and eigenstates, this occurs because the manipulation of local coupling or gain-loss terms can shift eigenstates originally in the bulk bands into the bandgap. This shift results in temperature field evolution characteristics distinct from those of the bulk, enabling controlled localization of energy and the formation of tunable bound states. This implementation mechanism does not rely on global symmetries; however, it is equally applicable to systems that possess global symmetries and topological protection. In such scenarios, our approach imparts additional robustness to the underlying system. To illustrate this phenomenon, we consider a 2D thermofluid lattice with two mirror-symmetric rectilinear interfaces, and the coupling strength can be tuned by the width of the coupling channels as shown in Fig. 2A. As we have configured the intercell coupling to be five times the intracell coupling, the system exhibits a large bandgap, as depicted in Fig. 2C. This significant bandgap helps in engineering the eigenstates. We impose weak advection with equal magnitudes but opposite directions on the diagonal and off-diagonal sites to maintain the non-Hermiticity of the 9 × 9 lattice (v1 = −v2 = 0.1Qc). Along the interface, we adjust its thermal coupling strength to 0.5 Qc. The imaginary part of the eigenenergy of the finite-sized thermal lattice is shown in Fig. 2B. By manipulating the interface thermal coupling, a larger number of bulk states are shifted from the gapless bulk region to gapped edge states and inner corner states. For 0D corner state, the eigenfrequencies remained in the mid-plane of the energy spectrum even after engineering the eigenvectors. In that case, the eigenenergy of the inner corner states emerge in the vicinity of the outer corner states. The numerical calculated eigenmodes and simulated temperature distributions are shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4. We then experimentally demonstrate the field distributions of the outer and inner corner modes in two recipes (see Materials and Methods for details on sample fabrication and measurements). The captured temperature profiles at specific moments are presented in Fig. 2 F and G. When the imaginary part of the eigenfrequency tunes to 12.17Qc, the energy localization exhibits outer corner states. Moreover, the energy is localized in the inner corner state, when the imaginary eigenfrequency is in the vicinity of 12.17Qc with slight deviations. It is noteworthy that the intensity of the outer corner state still maintains a certain magnitude even when the system is in the eigenmode of the inner corner state, since the eigenfrequencies of the two types of the corner states are very close. The measured temperature field intensities calculated by normalized temperatures T∗-T¯aveTmax-Tmin are shown in Fig. 2 J–M, where T*, T¯ave, Tmax and Tmin respectively represent the temperature at specific measured points, the average temperature of the system, and the maximum and minimum temperature of the system.

To explore the reconfigurable nontrivial edge modes in addition to 0D corner states, we calculated the projective band structure of a 9 × 1 supercell with two rectilinear interfaces, as illustrated in Fig. 2D. Notably, three pairs of edge states emerge within the bulk band gap, in contrast to a single pair of edge modes in the absence of inner bound states. The eigenfunctions of two eigenstates (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) remarkably present the characteristics of outer and inner edge states. Since both types of 1D modes coexist within the bandgap, we choose to excite two eigenmodes that are far apart. The eigenfrequency corresponds to Im f ~ 5.71Qc as indicated by the blue arrow in Fig. 2B. Under the aforementioned coupling strategy, we observe the inner edge states as depicted in Fig. 2I. Similarly, by further increasing the interface coupling strength to Qc, the eigenmode of the system transitions from the inner edge mode to the outer edge mode (Fig. 2H). The distribution diagrams of the intensity field offer a more intuitive visualization of the controllable energy localization (Fig. 2 I and M). Such features agree well with the numerical results (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

We further construct a closed square interface within the 2D thermofluid lattice. The thermal coupling strengths along the configured interface are modified to induce a shift in the eigenmodes near the interface, and the corresponding spectrum is presented in Fig. 3B. As an example, we calculated the open-boundary eigenenergy of the thermal lattice when controlling t3 to 0.5 Qc and found that the eigenstate within the bandgap exhibits hierarchical edge and corner localization as shown in Fig. 3A. By numerically calculated eigenstates (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), we can clearly identify the outer corner states, inner corner states, outer edge states, and inner edge states. We then experimentally realized the tunable localizations of energy by modulating the intercell and interface coupling strengths, which confirm the feasibility of creating reconfigurable interfaces by engineering the eigenvectors (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our finding of reconfigurable high-order topological bound states in non-Hermitian thermofluid lattice reveals rich topological properties that arise from the synergy between Hermitian advection and skewed Hermitian thermal conduction. The inserts of reconfigurable domain walls promise the shifts of the complex eigenmodes, thus enabling the significant hierarchies of topological bound states at arbitrary positions within the trivial bulk states. Both the thermal coupling strengths and on-site advections are available to generate such domain walls, respectively, in the real-value and imaginary band. Our findings demonstrate that active control of thermal localization can be achieved by engineering the interface configurations, which powers up the way for controllable thermal management and mass concertation. Moreover, the dynamic reconfiguration strategy proposed in this study can be extended to other active system, such as electric circuits (36, 37), photonic chip (38), and spatiotemporal topological systems (39).

Materials and Methods

Numerical Simulations.

The finite element simulations were performed using a commercial solver package (COMSOL Multiphysics). In these simulations, we considered the flow medium to be water. The coupling strength can be effectively controlled by adjusting the width of the intracell and intercell fluid channels. Specifically, we set the medium on the diagonal direction to spin clockwise and the rest to spin counterclockwise at the same speed, consistent with the description in SI Appendix, Fig. S1.

To observe the hierarchical topological states in the thermal lattice, we manipulate the width of the intercell and the intracell fluid channels simultaneously. For the reconfigurable strategies involving the rectilinear interface and square-like interface mentioned in the main text, we conducted simulations as shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5. In these simulations, we observed the merged edge states and corner states, confirming the controllability of the bound states. Furthermore, we emphasize that the reconfigurable bound states can also be induced by the Hermitian convection. The presence of imbalanced on-site energy along the interface breaks the insulating interior, leading to the emergence of internal topological modes. From the projective real spectra of the 2D thermal lattice with interfaces, we can clearly observe the existence of nontrivial edge modes. Theoretically, we divide the entire area along the designed interfaces. The amplitude of convection remains the same in different regions, but the sign is reversed, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S6. Consequently, we can observe energy localization near the designed interfaces, while the energy in other regions dissipates rapidly. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the two methods discussed in the main text are unaffected by the material properties, as our reconstructed local states are sensitive only to the coupling ratio or the gain–loss boundaries, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7. We varied the thermal conductivity of the liquid from 1 to 15 W/(m K), and the thermal profiles remained unchanged, further confirming the system’s robustness. Furthermore, we present simulation results for the more complex reconfiguration regions in SI Appendix, Fig. S9. It can be observed that the energy is localized both on the original boundaries of the thermal lattice and on the reconfigured interfaces. This further demonstrates the robustness and effectiveness of our method.

Experiments.

In the experimental setup, we utilized 3D printing technology to fabricate samples that demonstrate the hierarchical high-order topological states and bound states in thermal HOTIs, as depicted in Fig. 2A. These samples consisted of a total of 324 sites, where every four sites formed a unit structure. Each site featured cylindrical columns with the same radius, and coupling channels were present to connect adjacent sites. The cylinders and coupling channels were constructed using photosensitive resin with a wall thickness of 1 mm.

To maintain a fluidic environment and enable custom adjustments in the coupling channels, different fluidic channels were employed to connect the fluidic units. The entire system was assembled by incorporating an electromagnetic drive module on each cylinder and embedding a coupling channel on the cylinder wall. Fluid was then introduced into each area to create a fluidic environment. The entire fluid lattice structure is fabricated using 3D printing technology with resin. The shaded region adjacent to the casing represents graphite, functioning as the negative electrode, while the central cylinder corresponds to the copper wire, serving as the positive electrode as depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S8. Each fluid site is accompanied with a magnet to generate a magnetic field for the system. The electrolyte undergoes radial movement driven by the electric field force and simultaneous tangential movement due to the magnetic field force.

For the excitation of topological states induced by thermal coupling strength, we adjusted the width of the intracell channels to achieve a suitable thermal coupling strength ratio while keeping the energy input the same. According to Newton cooling law, the total coupling energies within each channel are directly proportional to the heat exchange area. By varying the heat exchange area, we could observe distinct temperature profiles, ranging from average temperature distributions with larger heat exchange areas to local temperature distributions with smaller heat exchange areas. To maintain the same on-site energy, periodic weak electromagnetic forces were applied to drive the fluid in diagonal and antidiagonal directions with equal strength but opposite directions.

In terms of the excitation source, we applied periodically alternating cold and heat sources on the two-dimensional lattice and observed the temperature evolution process using an infrared camera. For the reconfiguration of bound states discussed in the main text, we replaced the fluid channels at the corresponding interfaces, thereby adjusting the heat transfer strength of the adjacent fluid sites. This allowed us to actively control the bound states. Additionally, for the bound states induced by convection, we divided the lattice into different regions and imposed periodic advection with varying intensities in each region.

This work focuses on the study of 2D non-Hermitian HOTI. By utilizing stacked structures, we can expect to achieve 3D topological insulators (40). It is important to note that this requires synergetic modulation of both in-plane and out-of-plane couplings. Experimentally, this corresponds to adjusting the direction of fluid rotation and the width of fluid channels along different crystal axes.
