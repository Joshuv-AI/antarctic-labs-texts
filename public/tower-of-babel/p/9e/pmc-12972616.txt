Copper phosphate nanozymes combat implant-related infections by inducing cuproptosis-like death and inhibiting the two-component system
Authors: Yaxin Liu, Yang Zheng, Dasheng Tian, Yiwei Sun, Min Wang, Sihai Li, Erxu Tao, Yujing Liao, Fei Huang, Tao Wang, Xianwen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImplant-related infections, characterized by recalcitrant biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, and immune evasion, pose a severe clinical burden in orthopedics due to the limited therapeutic efficacy of conventional approaches. Addressing this challenge requires innovative strategies capable of simultaneously disrupting biofilms and overcoming bacterial defense mechanisms. To address this, we developed flower-like copper phosphate (CuP) nanozymes with dual enzymatic activities (peroxidase- and glutathione peroxidase-like) that synergistically eradicate biofilms for implant-related infections through cuproptosis-like death and two-component system (TCS) inhibition. In vitro antimicrobial experiments demonstrated that CuP nanozymes significantly inhibited and disrupted bacterial biofilms. These CuP nanozymes generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) while depleting glutathione (GSH), disrupting bacterial membranes, and disrupting metabolic processes such as the TCA cycle and glycolysis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed suppressed expression of oxidative phosphorylation genes and quorum-sensing regulators, confirming dual targeting of bacterial viability and communication. In vivo, CuP nanozymes effectively eliminated biofilm infections on implants, reduced inflammatory responses, and demonstrated excellent biocompatibility. By integrating enzymatic ROS amplification, metabolic interference, and TCS inhibition, this strategy offers a transformative approach to combat resistant implant infections, surpassing conventional therapies in both efficacy and safety.Keywords: Nanozymes, Cuproptosis-like death, Two-component system, Bacterial biofilm, Implant-related infection

Introduction

With the continuous growth of the global population, the aging demographic, and the increasing incidence of orthopedic trauma and disease, the use of orthopedic implants has been steadily increasing [1,2]. With the widespread adoption of implants in modern orthopedic treatments, implant-related infections account for 25.6% of all healthcare-associated infections in the United States [3]. As a result, implants play a critical role in modern medicine, particularly in the field of orthopedic treatment. However, owing to the inherently high exposure risk associated with implant surgeries, implant-related infections occur frequently after orthopedic implant procedures and can lead to a range of severe complications, such as inflammation, nonunion of fractures, and osteomyelitis [4,5]. Unfortunately, most implants currently used in clinical practice lack sufficient and effective antibacterial properties. On the one hand, implant surfaces provide an ideal environment for bacterial adhesion and colonization, especially in medical devices such as orthopedic implants [6]. The inert surfaces of these devices create favorable conditions for the formation and development of biofilms. Studies have shown that once bacteria complete the processes of attachment, colonization, biofilm maturation, and dispersal on the surface of orthopedic implants, bacterial biofilms are established [6,7]. During this process, after bacteria achieve irreversible adhesion, they begin to secrete large amounts of extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs) [8]. EPSs not only encapsulate bacteria but also promote their aggregation. Subsequently, bacteria employ two-component system (TCS) and quorum sensing (QS) mechanisms to synthesize and secrete signaling molecules, enabling nearby bacterial communities to sense each other's presence, thereby facilitating colony expansion and biofilm maturation. Targeted inhibition of the TCS and QS pathways is thus a crucial antibacterial strategy against biofilm-associated infections, which directly interferes with bacterial regulatory networks and effectively suppresses biofilm formation and bacterial virulence. Biofilms act as physical barriers, isolating bacteria from the host immune system and preventing phagocytes and cytotoxic immune cells from eliminating bacteria within the biofilm. Furthermore, bacteria within biofilms exhibit significantly reduced sensitivity to antibiotics compared with their planktonic counterparts. As a result, once a biofilm forms on the surface of an implant, the bacteria become highly resilient [9]. Conventional antibiotic treatments are often only effective at killing a small number of planktonic bacteria, while biofilms on the implant surface remain largely unaffected [10,11]. In cases where implant-related infections occur postoperatively and neither the patient's immune system nor antibiotic therapies prove effective, a second surgical procedure to replace the implant is often required to prevent potentially severe consequences. However, even a second surgery does not entirely eliminate the risk of reinfection. This imposes significant physical, psychological, and financial burdens on patients, causing unnecessary stress and suffering. To address this challenging clinical issue, it is imperative to develop a nonsurgical and effective treatment for biofilm infections, particularly those that are capable of combating antibiotic-resistant bacteria [12]. In recent years, nanozymes have garnered significant attention in the field of antimicrobial therapy because of their advantages, such as simple preparation and stable performance [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]].

Nanozymes not only possess the unique properties of nanomaterials but also exhibit enzyme-like catalytic activities [[21], [22], [23]]. Among them, peroxidase-like (POD-like) nanozymes can utilize hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as a substrate to generate large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as ·OH, for antibacterial applications [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. This unique property effectively prevents the development of bacterial resistance to nanozymes. In recent years, copper-based nanozymes have been rapidly developed and widely used in antimicrobial research and have shown excellent antimicrobial efficacy [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. In the microenvironment of bacterial infections, large amounts of reduced glutathione (GSH) are produced. GSH scavenges free radicals and protects cells from oxidative damage, thus playing a protective role [33,34]. Moreover, certain bacteria can utilize GSH to promote biofilm formation. As a result, elevated GSH levels not only diminish the antibacterial effects of ROS but also facilitate biofilm development. However, copper-based materials exhibit enzyme-like activity similar to that of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), which can deplete GSH, thereby increasing their antibacterial efficacy. The peroxidase-like activity of copper-based nanozymes, coupled with their GSH-Px-like activity, further amplifies their antibacterial and antibiofilm effects. Building on these findings, researchers have conducted in-depth investigations into the antimicrobial mechanisms of copper. Studies have revealed that excessive Cu2+ can bind to lipoate-containing proteins in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, leading to abnormal oligomerization of these proteins and the loss of iron-sulfur cluster proteins, thereby disrupting the TCA cycle, a critical metabolic pathway [35]. Additionally, Cu2+ can induce bacterial toxicity by damaging the oxidative respiratory chain. This phenomenon of bacterial death caused by Cu2+ overload is termed “cuproptosis-like death [[36], [37], [38]].” Cuproptosis-like death occurs due to the accumulation of excessive Cu2+ within bacterial cells, and its bactericidal effects are not hindered by bacterial cell wall barriers or antibiotic resistance, enabling highly efficient bacterial killing [39,40]. However, despite the great potential of copper-based nanozymes in antimicrobial therapy, their practical application faces several challenges [41,42]. These challenges include the limited efficacy of single enzyme-catalyzed therapies, physical barriers posed by biofilms that impede ROS penetration and insufficient biofilm disruption by a single class of cuproptosis-like death. As a result, single-mechanism ROS-based therapies often fail to achieve the desired antibacterial and antibiofilm effects [42,43]. To overcome these limitations, including the catalytic efficiency of ROS, biofilm penetration barriers, bacterial resistance, and the constraints of single-mechanism treatments, further research and development are essential. Addressing these challenges could open new avenues and provide innovative solutions for improving the treatment of biofilm-related infections [44]. In addition, TCS and QS are key mechanisms by which bacteria regulate their physiological behavior. TCS enables bacteria to sense external environmental signals and adjust their physiological states accordingly, whereas QS plays a pivotal role in biofilm formation. Through QS, bacteria communicate by signaling molecules, sense population density, and initiate biofilm formation at appropriate times [45,46]. In complex biofilm infection environments, bacteria may simultaneously utilize TCS and QS to integrate multiple signals, modulating their physiological states and behaviors to withstand external pressures and antibiotic challenges. Recent studies have demonstrated that QS inhibitors exhibit significant antibacterial and antibiofilm effects [47,48]. In addition to these antimicrobial mechanisms, the combined inhibition of TCS and QS represents a promising strategy to suppress bacterial communication and colony aggregation, thereby effectively impeding biofilm formation and maturation and potentially boosting the efficacy and reliability of antibacterial and antibiofilm therapies. This multimechanism, synergistic approach offers a novel direction for addressing complex bacterial infections and developing innovative treatment strategies.

Copper phosphate (CuP) nanozymes with dual enzyme activities, which have significant antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy and promote bone infection repair, were prepared (Scheme 1). In addition to its antibacterial mechanism, the antibacterial mechanism of CuP nanozymes mainly involves the following aspects. First, the generation of abundant ROS damages bacterial cell walls and membranes, leading to cell lysis and protein leakage. Second, Cu2+ overload disrupts critical metabolic processes such as glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and the TCA cycle, further promoting cuproptosis-like death. Specifically, CuP nanozymes inhibits the activity of bacterial respiratory chain complexes IV and V and significantly downregulates the expression of genes related to the TCA cycle and glycolysis. Additionally, excessive Cu2+ induces lipid peroxidation, further exacerbating the copper-induced bacterial death process. Notably, CuP nanozymes significantly downregulated the expression of genes associated with the TCS and QS pathways, which is closely related to its exceptional antibacterial and antibiofilm effects. In short, this study demonstrates the significant potential of CuP nanozymes with dual enzyme-like activities to treat implant-related bacterial biofilm infections by synergistically inducing bacterial cuproptosis-like death and inhibiting TCS and QS, providing a novel and effective strategy for antibacterial and antibiofilm therapies, with remarkable potential for the treatment of implant-related bacterial biofilm infections.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of CuP nanozymes

CuP nanozymes were successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal method (Fig. 1A). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image revealed that CuP nanozymes presented a flower-like morphology (Fig. S1). It possesses a larger specific surface area (19.84 m2/g) compared with nano materials of conventional morphologies (Fig. S2). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging and elemental mapping further confirmed the uniform distribution of Cu (green), P (yellow), and O (red) across the surface of CuP nanozymes, indicating the successful construction of a three-dimensional flower-like architecture (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) images of CuP nanozymes were obtained (Fig. 1C). The particle size analysis revealed that the diameter of CuP nanozymes was approximately 2.5 μm, which aligns well with the SEM observations (Fig. S3). The stability assessment demonstrated that the particle size of CuP nanozymes showed no obvious change after incubation in a neutral environment (pH 7.4) for 24 h, verifying their excellent stability under physiological conditions (Fig. S4). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of CuP nanozymes showed characteristic diffraction peaks in the 2θ range of 5–75°, which are consistent with the standard diffraction pattern of CuP nanozymes (JCPDS card No. 22-0548) (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, the SAED image corresponded to the XRD pattern. Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the chemical states of Cu, P, and O, providing further insights into the elemental composition and bonding states (Fig. 1E). The Cu 2p spectrum displayed two main peaks at 935.57 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and 955.39 eV (Cu 2p1/2), with a spin-orbital splitting value of approximately 19.82 eV, accompanied by two prominent satellite peaks, confirming the presence of Cu2+ (Fig. 1F). The P 2p3/2 spectrum exhibited a main peak at 133.59 eV, indicating the presence of P5+ (Fig. 1G). The O 1s spectrum showed a primary peak at 531.6 eV, corresponding to the oxidized state of O2− (Fig. 1H). The zeta potential of CuP nanozymes was determined via a zeta potential meter (Fig. S5). The relatively negative surface charge correlated with the peak shown in the O 1s spectrum at 533.09 eV. These results collectively confirmed the successful synthesis of flower-like CuP nanozymes. These nanoflower structures possess a high specific surface area because of their multilayered, flower-like morphology. This characteristic provides a wealth of active surface sites.

Enzyme activities of CuP nanozymes

This study validated the activities of the two enzyme-like properties (POD-like and GSH-Px-like) of CuP nanozymes through a series of experiments. POD-like activity can use exogenous H2O2 as a catalyst, triggering a Fenton-like reaction to produce ROS in an acidic environment (Fig. 2A). Moreover, CuP nanozymes, with their GSH-Px-like activity, can catalyze the conversion of GSH into GSSG. First, the POD-like activity of CuP nanozymes in an acidic environment was assessed using TMB as a chromogenic substrate. Compared with that of TMB coincubated with CuP nanozymes alone, the absorbance of TMB cocatalyzed by CuP nanozymes with H2O2 increased significantly at 652 nm (Fig. 2B). At the same H2O2 concentration, the color of the solution gradually increased as the concentration of CuP nanozymes increased, and the absorbance at 652 nm also increased accordingly (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, when the concentration of CuP nanozymes was fixed, the absorbance peak at 652 nm was positively correlated with the H2O2 concentration (Fig. 2D). Similarly, the OPD probe experiment produced consistent results, indicating that CuP nanozymes exhibited excellent peroxidase-like catalytic activity (Fig. 2E–G). The microenvironment of bacterial biofilms contains abundant reducing substances that can effectively neutralize external oxidants. The consumption of these reducing substances may disrupt the internal balance of the bacterial environment, amplify the effects of ROS, and ultimately accelerate bacterial death. To investigate whether CuP nanozymes can deplete reducing substances, DTNB was used as a probe. After coincubation of CuP nanozymes with glutathione (GSH), the characteristic peak intensity of the complex formed by GSH and DTNB gradually weakened and eventually disappeared over time. In a reaction system with a 50 μg/mL concentration of CuP nanozymes, the characteristic peak disappeared after 3 h, demonstrating the high sensitivity of CuP nanozymes to GSH (Fig. 2H). When the material concentration reached 400 μg/mL, GSH was completely consumed within 30 min, indicating that CuP nanozymes possessed excellent GSH-Px-like activity (Fig. 2I). The time required to deplete a fixed amount of GSH was negatively correlated with the concentration of CuP nanozymes (Fig. 2J). These findings indicated that CuP nanozymes exhibited excellent POD-like activity and GSH-Px-like activity and were capable of generating large amounts of ROS and depleting GSH. To further evaluate the catalytic activity of CuP nanozymes, the steady-state catalytic kinetics were investigated at room temperature, and the steady-state kinetic parameters, including the Michaelis-Menten constant Km and the maximum initial velocity (Vmax), were calculated to assess their catalytic ability (Fig. 2K and L). The Km value of CuP nanozymes is 4.206 mM for H2O2. The Vmax value of CuP nanozymes is 1.606 μM s−1 for H2O2. These results illustrated that CuP nanozymes can effectively promote the decomposition of H2O2. The enzyme-catalyzed reaction rate of CuP nanozymes constructed in this paper outperforms that of a number of nanozymes in relation to what has now been reported in the literature (Table S1). Interestingly, CuP nanozymes were able to release Cu2+ under acidic conditions (pH 5.5 and 6.5), and the release of Cu2+ was pH dependent (Fig. 2M). This makes them promising candidates for further investigation as potential therapeutic agents for implant-related infections.

In vitro antibacterial efficiency assessment

In the previous section, the enzymatic activity of CuP nanozymes was verified. In this study, the effects of CuP nanozymes on bacteria in vitro were evaluated at 37 °C for 3 h H2O2 is a commonly used clinical disinfectant for sterilization and is often applied to wound surfaces to prevent infection. However, high concentrations of H2O2 can cause tissue damage and delay healing. In contrast, low concentrations of H2O2 can react with CuP nanozymes, which possess POD-like enzymatic activity, to catalyze the production of a large amount of hydroxyl radicals (·OH) in the acidic environment of bacterial infections, achieving superior antibacterial effects. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were selected as representative gram-positive cocci and gram-negative bacilli models, and the inhibitory effects of CuP nanozymes were first detected for these two types of bacteria in a coated plate assay at 37 °C for 3 h. The antibacterial performance of the nanozymes was evaluated by adding or omitting low concentrations of H2O2 from CuP nanozymes solutions of varying concentrations. Compared with the CuP group, the CuP + H2O2 group had significantly greater antimicrobial effects on MRSA and E. coli (Fig. 3A–G). CuP nanozymes showed significant bactericidal activity against both strains even at a very low concentration (10 μg/mL), and the antimicrobial effect increased with increasing concentration. When CuP nanozymes concentration was 25 μg/mL with the addition of H2O2, the survival rate of MRSA decreased to as low as 0.72% (Fig. 3B). Similarly, at a 50 μg/mL concentration of CuP nanozymes with H2O2, the survival rate of E. coli decreased to 0.49% (Fig. 3H). Next, MRSA and E. coli were stained with N01 and PI dyes, which fluoresce green and red, respectively. Green fluorescence indicates live bacteria, whereas red fluorescence indicates dead bacteria. The live/dead ratios of MRSA (Fig. 3C) and E. coli (Fig. 3I) were determined after incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. The results revealed that the CuP + H2O2 group presented the highest ratio of dead bacteria, followed by the CuP group, whereas the H2O2 group and the control group presented the lowest ratios. These findings are consistent with previous results, further confirming the bactericidal properties of CuP nanozymes. To assess the effectiveness of ROS generation, bacterial chemokines were evaluated via 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH-DA) staining at 37 °C for 3 h (Fig. 3D–J). Bacteria subjected to different treatments were stained with DCFH-DA to observe ROS production. The results revealed that both the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups produced green fluorescence, with the CuP + H2O2 group exhibiting relatively stronger fluorescence. This confirmed that CuP nanozymes exhibit excellent POD-like activity, efficiently generating ROS in different bacterial environments. Finally, to further observe the bactericidal effects of the material on the two bacterial strains, SEM was used to examine the morphology of bacteria treated with CuP + H2O2 at 37 °C for 3 h (Fig. 3E–K). In the control and H2O2 groups, the bacterial surfaces appeared smooth, and their morphology was intact and regular. E. coli retained its rod-like shape, whereas MRSA maintained its spherical shape. However, after treatment with CuP or CuP + H2O2, both bacterial membranes exhibited varying degrees of shrinkage and rupture, with the CuP + H2O2 group showing greater damage to MRSA and E. coli. These results suggested that CuP nanozymes significantly disrupted bacterial membranes, likely through ion release and the action of large amounts of ·OH, leading to bacterial destruction. The leakage of bacterial proteins was further evaluated via a BCA protein assay kit. Significant protein leakage was observed in both bacterial strains treated with CuP and those treated with CuP + H2O2, with the latter causing the most pronounced protein leakage (Fig. 3F–L). These findings were consistent with the SEM observations of bacterial structural integrity. To emphasize the clinical superiority of CuP nanozymes, a drug resistance evaluation was conducted with vancomycin as the positive control. MRSA was treated separately with CuP nanozymes or vancomycin; residual viable bacteria posttreatment were continuously subcultured for 14 passages, and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of each agent against the subcultured bacteria was determined to assess resistance development. The results indicated that continuous subculture of vancomycin-treated residual MRSA resulted in a dramatic increase in drug resistance, with the MIC of vancomycin against the 14th passage being 2048-fold higher than that against the first passage. In contrast, CuP nanozymes-treated MRSA showed significantly attenuated resistance development, with only an 8-fold increase in CuP nanozymes MIC after 14 passages (Fig. S6). This confirmed that CuP nanozymes effectively alleviate bacterial drug resistance, demonstrating distinct advantages over vancomycin. On the basis of the above experimental results, CuP nanozymes effectively kill bacteria by releasing ions, generating ·OH through their POD-like enzymatic activity, and depleting GSH. These findings suggest that CuP nanozymes hold great potential as an efficient therapeutic strategy for implant-related infections. These findings suggest that the use of CuP nanozymes, as an effective therapeutic strategy, can efficiently kill bacteria through ion release and POD-like enzyme activity, which generates ·OH and depletes GSH. This makes CuP nanozymes highly promising for the treatment of implant-related infections.

In vitro evaluation of antibiofilm efficiency

On the surface of infected implants, bacteria can secrete EPSs to form biofilms, which protect them from attack by the immune system and antibiotics. This is the primary reason for the recalcitrance, persistence, and antibiotic resistance associated with biofilm infections. Therefore, effectively eliminating biofilms is a critical prerequisite for treating bacterial biofilm infections on implants. MRSA and E. coli were selected as representative gram-positive cocci and gram-negative bacilli, respectively, to study the ability of CuP nanozymes to eradicate biofilm infection. The biofilms were treated with H2O2, CuP, or CuP + H2O2, while the untreated biofilms served as the control group. First, the integrity of the biofilm was assessed via crystal violet staining. The results showed that both the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups significantly inhibited and disrupted the biofilm. In the CuP group, biofilm staining noticeably decreased, whereas in the CuP + H2O2 group, biofilm integrity was severely compromised. These findings were significantly different from those observed in the control and H2O2 groups (Fig. 4A–F). Quantitative analysis via crystal violet staining confirmed that both the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups strongly inhibited the biofilm, with the CuP + H2O2 group demonstrating a more pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 4B–G). Similarly, both the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups presented significant biofilm disruption, with the CuP + H2O2 group exhibiting a stronger destructive effect (Figs. S7 and S8). Additionally, the three-dimensional structure and degree of biofilm destruction after different treatments were observed via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Fig. 4D–I). The results revealed varying degrees of biofilm disruption in the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups, with the remaining biofilms appearing thin and scattered. The CuP + H2O2 group presented a more pronounced destructive effect. In contrast, the biofilms in the control and H2O2 groups maintained intact three-dimensional structures, with high bacterial survival rates (Fig. 4C–H). Furthermore, SEM confirmed the ability of CuP + H2O2 to disrupt the biofilm structure and eliminate bacteria (Fig. 4E–J). The statistical results revealed that both bacterial biofilms were effectively removed by CuP nanozymes (Fig. S9). These results demonstrated that CuP nanozymes possessed strong biofilm-inhibiting and biofilm-destroying capabilities, which has significant implications for treating bacterial biofilm infections on implants. The mechanism of biofilm destruction may be related to the release of large amounts of ROS by CuP nanozymes and the depletion of reduced glutathione (GSH). The consumption of large amounts of GSH reduced the protective effect of the biofilm, while the ROS penetrated and attacked the biofilm, causing bacterial destruction. This synergistic effect has been proven to effectively disrupt biofilms. This finding suggested that CuP nanozymes may be able to effectively disrupt biofilms in implant-related infections.

Antibacterial mechanisms of CuP nanozymes, including dual enzyme-like activities, cuproptosis-like death and TCS inhibition

CuP nanozymes, which have POD-like activity, can generate large amounts of ROS to kill bacteria and destroy biofilms. The following experiments were conducted to elucidate the possible antibacterial mechanism of CuP nanozymes in previous work. First, the surface morphology of the MRSA/E. coli after different treatments was investigated via SEM. This difference may be due to the ability of POD-like enzymes to produce large amounts of ·OH, which leads to bacterial disruption. To verify this, bacteria subjected to different treatments were stained for ROS production with a DCFH-DA probe. The extent of protein leakage from bacteria (MRSA and E. coli) after different treatments was also examined via a BCA protein assay kit. The disruption of bacterial membranes, green fluorescence of ROS and severe bacterial protein leakage in the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups suggested that CuP nanozymes have good antibacterial potential because they produce large amounts of ROS in the presence of enzyme-like substances.

To further investigate the antibacterial mechanism of CuP nanozymes, transcriptional analysis was performed on MRSA treated with PBS or CuP + H2O2. Significant differences in the expression of protein-coding genes were observed between the two groups, with a total of 848 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) identified. A volcano plot revealed 435 upregulated DEPs and 413 downregulated DEPs (Fig. 5A). A heatmap of overall gene expression revealed marked differences between the untreated group and the CuP + H2O2-treated group (Fig. 5B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that the significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched primarily in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, and TCS/QS (Fig. 5C). Heatmaps of these regulatory pathways were analyzed (Fig. 5D). Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted to determine the importance of the DEGs in the biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories. Enrichment analysis indicated that metabolic processes, antioxidant activity, catalytic activity, translational regulatory activity, transcriptional regulatory activity, and transport activity were closely associated with the mechanism by which CuP nanozymes kill bacteria (Fig. S10). The enriched pathways and associated genes between the control group and CuP + H2O2 group were analyzed to further understand the antibacterial mechanism of CuP nanozymes. The results were as follows: First, the significant downregulation of genes related to the oxidative phosphorylation pathway suggested that bacterial ATP metabolism may be disrupted. Oxidative phosphorylation is a critical pathway for ATP synthesis and involves the sequential action of complexes I, II, III, IV, and V in the oxidative respiratory chain. However, gene enrichment analysis revealed that the expression of genes associated with complexes IV and V in the bacterial respiratory chain was significantly downregulated after CuP + H2O2 treatment. These complexes play vital roles in bacterial oxidative phosphorylation. By inhibiting the function of these complexes, bacterial ATP synthesis is suppressed, leading to an insufficient energy supply and impairing bacterial growth and reproduction. Furthermore, CuP nanozymes inhibited glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thereby disrupting bacterial energy metabolism and cell proliferation. In the glycolysis pathway, the expression of key enzymes such as phosphofructokinase (PFKA), which catalyzes the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; phosphoglycerate mutase (gpmA), which catalyzes the conversion of 3-phosphoglycerate to 2-phosphoglycerate; and pyruvate kinase (PK), which catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate and ATP, was significantly downregulated. These enzymes are critical for the glycolytic process, and their inhibition reduces bacterial glucose utilization efficiency, severely impairing glycolysis and further weakening bacterial energy acquisition. More importantly, the expression of multiple key genes in the TCA cycle, including dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLD), dihydrolipoamide succinyltransferase (DLST), pyruvate dehydrogenase (DLAT), korA, and components of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHB), was significantly downregulated. The downregulation of these genes severely disrupted the TCA cycle, affecting bacterial metabolic balance and growth and ultimately leading to bacterial death. Studies have shown that interference with mitochondrial respiration and the TCA cycle can induce copper-dependent bacterial death. Copper accumulation in bacteria or cells can directly bind to sulfur ligands in the TCA cycle, inducing the aggregation of lipoylated proteins and the loss of iron-sulfur cluster proteins, leading to protein toxicity, stress, and copper degradation. These results suggested that CuP + H2O2 treatment significantly downregulated the expression of genes related to oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle in bacteria, resembling the cuproptosis-like death mechanism observed in eukaryotic cells [35]. To verify this, the activities of respiratory chain complexes IV and V were measured in bacteria in different groups. The activities of complexes IV and V decreased in the CuP and CuP + H2O2 treatment groups, which is consistent with the enrichment analysis results and confirms that CuP nanozymes disrupts respiratory chain function (Fig. 5E and F). Additionally, qPCR was used to validate the downregulation of genes related to the TCA cycle by the nanozymes. The genes associated with DLST, gpmA, and PK were significantly downregulated in the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups, indicating that CuP nanozymes inhibited the TCA cycle and glycolysis (Fig. 5G–I). The TCS is one of the most important signaling systems in bacteria. When bacteria and biofilms are not completely eradicated, residual bacteria can use signaling systems such as TCS and QS to revive biofilms, regulate drug efflux pumps, and increase resistance. This is a major reason why biofilms are difficult to eliminate. Enrichment analysis revealed that key genes in the TCS and QS systems, such as LuxS, agrA, and agrC, were significantly downregulated in the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups. Similarly, qPCR validation of the key regulatory gene agrA in these systems confirmed its downregulation (Fig. 5J). By interfering with the quorum-sensing mechanism of MRSA, CuP nanozymes inhibited and disrupted biofilm formation, further enhancing their antibacterial and antibiofilm effects. To validate the direct effect of CuP nanozymes on QS systems, autoinducer (AHL) rescue experiments were performed (Figs. S11 and S12). Exogenous supplementation of AHL during CuP treatment significantly reversed the biofilm-disrupting efficacy of CuP nanozymes, and the CuP + AHL group exhibited markedly attenuated biofilm inhibition compared with the CuP group alone. To further exclude the interference of bacterial death and verify the direct transcriptional regulation of TCS and QS pathways by CuP nanozymes, qPCR assays were conducted in bacterial cultures treated with CuP nanozymes at subminimum inhibitory concentrations (Sub-MICs) (Fig. S13). Under this condition, bacterial viability was retained at over 80%, with no distinct bactericidal effect observed, and the transcriptional level of agrA was significantly suppressed by 38%. This result eliminated potential interference arising from bacterial death, solidly confirming that CuP nanozymes directly inhibit the TCS and QS pathways at the transcriptional level.

Overall, the results of this study indicated that the antibacterial mechanism of CuP nanozymes involved primarily a cuproptosis-like death mechanism, which disrupted the oxidative respiratory chain, blocked ATP synthesis, and interfered with the TCA cycle, thereby achieving effective antimicrobial activity. Moreover, CuP nanozymes effectively inhibited and disrupted biofilm formation by downregulating TCS and QS, thus blocking bacterial communication (Fig. 6). Compared with AgNPs, CuP nanozymes exhibit greater biofilm penetration due to their flower-like morphology [49]. Compared with photothermal agents, CuP nanozymes are less likely to generate high temperatures that damage normal tissues and are more biocompatible [50]. The synergistic effect of the cuproptosis-like death mechanism and signal transduction inhibition provides a novel perspective on the antibacterial mechanism of CuP nanozymes, highlighting their tremendous potential in treating implant-related bacterial biofilm infections.

In vivo treatment with CuP nanozymes to eliminate bacterial biofilm infections from implants

As described in the previous section, in vitro experiments confirmed that CuP nanozymes exhibited excellent antibacterial and antibiofilm properties. In subsequent experiments, an in vivo biofilm infection model was established with mouse implants to investigate the antibiofilm ability of CuP nanozymes in vivo. The subcutaneous implant infection model was adopted for this in vivo study based on two key rationales: first, the core pathological hallmarks of implant-related biofilm infections (bacterial adhesion, surface biofilm formation, and local infection status) can be faithfully mimicked by this model; second, straightforward, real-time visualization and documentation of infection progression and therapeutic outcomes via macroscopic imaging were enabled at the dorsal subcutaneous implantation site, thereby facilitating assessment of wound healing (erythema, edema and ulceration) throughout the treatment course. A schematic diagram illustrates the process of biofilm infection modeling and subsequent treatments (Fig. 7A). On day 0, PEEK implants that had been cultured with MRSA for 2 days were implanted into the backs of all the mice to form biofilms. The mice were then randomly divided into four groups based on different treatments: the control group, H2O2 group, CuP group, and CuP + H2O2 group. On days 3 and 7, PBS, H2O2, CuP, or CuP + H2O2 was injected at the infection site. During the experiment, the mice were weighed on days 0, 1, 3, 5, 10 and 15, and digital images of the implant wounds were taken on days 1, 5, 10, and 15 to macroscopically observe infection and recovery traces at the implant site throughout the treatment period (Fig. 7B). The control and H2O2 groups presented significant redness, swelling, ulcers, and implant exposure or detachment at the wound site, indicating that biofilm infections at the implant site were not effectively suppressed. Compared with the control and H2O2 groups, the CuP group presented small areas of ulceration in the skin tissue after the initial treatment, but the ulcerated areas did not expand significantly during subsequent treatments, and infection symptoms were effectively controlled. Notably, in the CuP + H2O2 group, no signs of skin ulceration were observed at the implant site on the backs of the mice from the initial treatment until the end of the treatment cycle, indicating that bacterial biofilm infections on the implants were effectively treated in this group. These results suggested that the addition of a small amount of H2O2 enhanced the bactericidal effects of CuP nanozymes, effectively eliminating bacterial biofilms. At the end of the treatment on day 15, the PEEK implants in the control and H2O2 groups were covered with large amounts of purulent necrotic tissue, whereas the PEEK implants in the CuP group contained only a small amount of residual purulent tissue. In contrast, the PEEK implants in the CuP + H2O2 group had the smoothest surfaces with no visible signs of infection (Fig. 7C). These findings were corroborated by SEM (Fig. 7D). Ultrasonic oscillation of the implants and homogenization of the surrounding subcutaneous tissue were subsequently performed for bacterial SPM analysis and statistical evaluation. Compared with the control and H2O2 groups, the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups presented significantly lower residual bacterial counts on the implant surface and in the surrounding tissue, with the CuP + H2O2 group showing the least residual bacteria. This demonstrated the strong antibacterial and biofilm-removal effects of the combination of CuP nanozymes and H2O2 (Fig. 7E–H). The mice were weighed eight times during the experiment, and the results revealed that the CuP + H2O2 group experienced the least weight loss due to infection (Fig. S14). To further validate the above results, immunological analysis was performed on the skin tissue surrounding the implant infection site (Fig. 7I). H&E staining revealed that the control and H2O2 groups presented fragmented epithelium and high levels of inflammatory cell infiltration, whereas the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups presented significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and more intact squamous epithelium, confirming that inflammation was successfully controlled. Compared with those in the other three groups, Masson staining revealed increased proliferation of subcutaneous fibroblasts, increased collagen fiber deposition, and a greater number of hair follicles in the CuP and CuP + H2O2 groups. Additional histopathological and immunological staining assays, including Giemsa staining, immunohistochemical staining of inflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α) and angiogenesis-related markers (VEGF, CD31), further verified the therapeutic efficacy of CuP nanozymes at the infection site (Fig. S15). Moreover, after 15 days of treatment, no significant bacterial infiltration was detected in the CuP + H2O2 group, which was consistent with previous results and confirmed that CuP + H2O2 significantly inhibited biofilm invasion at the infection site. In summary, CuP + H2O2 demonstrated excellent therapeutic efficacy against MRSA-induced bacterial biofilms on implants and effectively eliminated MRSA biofilm infections in vivo.

RNA sequencing analysis of the therapeutic effect of CuP nanozymes

To further investigate the mechanism of CuP + H2O2 in treating implant-related biofilm infections, six mice were randomly divided into a control group and a CuP + H2O2 group. After the implant-related infection model was successfully established in mice, the mice were treated for 15 consecutive days. The mice were then sacrificed, and the corresponding tissues were collected for transcriptome sequencing. First, a volcano plot was generated to analyze the DEGs between the two groups (Fig. 8A). A heatmap of overall gene expression revealed significant differences between the control group and the CuP + H2O2 group (Fig. 8B). KEGG analysis revealed that the significantly differentially expressed genes were enriched primarily in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, B-cell receptor signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and Staphylococcus aureus infection pathway (Fig. 8C). GO analysis indicated that the significantly differentially expressed genes were enriched mainly in pathways related to bacterial defense responses and immune responses (Fig. 8D). All of these pathways exhibited selective downregulation. Additionally, heatmaps were used to analyze the downregulated genes in the aforementioned major pathways (Fig. 8E–G). The downregulation of genes such as S100A8, S100A9, Lyn and Casp4 inhibits the inflammatory response and attenuates tissue damage. In short, the downregulation of all the above pathways suggested that CuP nanozymes were effective in the treatment of implant-related infections in vivo.

In vivo biocompatibility assessment of CuP nanozymes

Peripheral blood was collected for hemolysis and biochemical tests, aiming to further evaluate the biosafety of CuP nanozymes. The concentrations of CuP nanozymes used were 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μg/mL. The results revealed that the hemolysis rates in all the groups were less than 5%, indicating that the concentrations of CuP nanozymes used in the biological experiments were within a safe range (Fig. S16). Moreover, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cocultured with CuP nanozymes at a series of gradient concentrations in vitro, and the biocompatibility of CuP nanozymes was systematically evaluated using a CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay kit. The CCK-8 results clearly demonstrated that HUVECs retained high cell viability within a safe range upon treatment with CuP nanozymes at all tested concentrations (Fig. S17), which solidly verifies the negligible cytotoxicity of CuP nanozymes toward normal mammalian cells. H&E-stained images of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) in each treatment group revealed no pathological changes (Fig. 9A). Additionally, in the biochemical tests, the levels of ALT, AST, creatinine, and BUN remained normal after treatment in all the groups (Fig. 9B). These results demonstrated that CuP nanozymes exhibited high biocompatibility and could be considered efficient, safe, and low-toxicity materials for treating implant-related infections.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully synthesized dual-enzyme-active CuP nanozymes via a hydrothermal reaction for the treatment of implant-related bacterial biofilm infections. The flower-like CuP nanozymes, enriched with multiple active enzyme sites, exhibited excellent POD-like and GSH-Px-like activities while releasing a significant amount of antibacterial Cu2+ ions under acidic conditions. Antibacterial experiments demonstrated that CuP nanozymes possess strong in vitro antibacterial and biofilm-eliminating capabilities. Furthermore, bacterial RNA-seq analysis revealed that the combination of CuP nanozymes with H2O2 disrupted MRSA metabolic processes, including oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and TCS. In vivo animal experiments revealed that CuP nanozymes combined with low concentrations of H2O2 effectively eliminated MRSA biofilm infections. Transcriptome sequencing further demonstrated that CuP nanozymes not only downregulated genes associated with MRSA infection but also suppressed genes in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, B-cell receptor signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway. This dual action inhibited bacterial activity while preventing excessive inflammation, which could trigger autoimmune responses or tissue damage. Additionally, GO enrichment analysis confirmed that tissues treated with CuP nanozymes presented downregulation of bacterial defense and immune response pathways. In summary, the use of CuP nanozymes with dual enzyme-like activities represents a promising biomedical material strategy for treating implant-related infections. The most innovative aspect of this study is the ability of CuP nanozymes to achieve potent bacterial biofilm elimination through synergistic cuproptosis-like death and TCS. However, this study included only a single animal model, which has several limitations. In the future, we will explore this topic more deeply in terms of clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O), ethylene glycol (CH2OH)2) and disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Glutathione (GSH) and tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co. Phenylenediamine (OPD) and 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) were obtained from J&K Chemical Co. A reactive oxygen assay kit was purchased from Beyotime (China). Deionized water was prepared via a Direct-Q3 purification system from Millipore (USA).

Synthesis of flower-like CuP nanozymes

Three-dimensional flower-like CuP nanozymes were prepared via hydrothermal synthesis. A mixed solution was first prepared with 1 mM CuSO4·5H2O and 25 mL of (CH2OH)2 and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. Then, 15 mL of aqueous Na2HPO4 containing 132 mg was added, and stirring was continued for 1 h. The mixture was added to a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and heated in an oven at 120 °C for 4 h. The reactor was cooled naturally to room temperature. CuP nanozymes were collected after centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min), washed twice with deionized water and ethanol, and vacuum dried via a vacuum freeze dryer (LGJ-10C) for 10 h. The prepared CuP nanozymes were stored at low temperature (4 °C).

Live/dead bacterial staining assay

We divided the experimental groups into four groups: (1) the control group; (2) the H2O2 group (100 μM); (3) the CuP group; and (4) the CuP + H2O2 group. The groups were subjected to live/dead bacterial staining tests after coincubation with bacteria in acidic solution. The bacterial suspensions were stained with a live/dead bacterial activity kit containing N01 and PI. Then, the cells were incubated under light-free conditions for 25 min. Dead bacteria (red fluorescence) and live bacteria (green fluorescence) were observed under a laser scanning microscope (Sunny, CSIM-130, 60 × ).

ROS staining

We divided the experimental groups into four groups: (1) the control group; (2) the H2O2 group (100 μM); (3) the CuP group; and (4) the CuP + H2O2 group. After the bacteria of all the groups were coincubated in an acidic solution, the bacteria in each group were incubated with a 2′,7’,dichlorobis(hydroxyfluorescein) diacetate probe to detect the production of reactive oxygen species in each group. Staining was performed with DCFH-DA for half an hour, the samples were washed with PBS, and reactive oxygen species production was observed under a fluorescence microscope, which emitted green fluorescence (Sunny, CSIM-130).

Crystal violet staining

Biofilms cultured in 24-well plates were treated with PBS, H2O2, CuP or CuP + H2O2. The plates were rinsed three times with PBS. The biofilm remaining at the bottom of the plate was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with 10% crystal violet solution for 20 min. The staining solution was aspirated, and the plate was washed to remove the floating color. The stained biofilm was photographed and observed with a camera and then dissolved in 30% ethanol. The biomass of the biofilm was evaluated by measuring the absorbance via an enzyme marker.

3D reconstruction of biofilms via CLSM

The biofilms cultured in confocal dishes were treated with PBS, H2O2, CuP, or CuP + H2O2 to observe the three-dimensional structure of the biofilms after different treatments. After various treatments, the biofilms were stained via the Bestbio LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Activity Kit in a dark environment. 3D reconstruction was subsequently performed via CLSM (Sunny, CSIM-130) and subsequently processed via ZEN software.

Preparation of SEM for biofilm samples

The MRSA and E. coli biofilms cultured on the wafer surface were treated with PBS, H2O2, CuP, or CuP + H2O2 for 6 h. The biofilms were subsequently fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for 12 h. The biofilms were dehydrated sequentially with 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100% ethanol/water mixtures for 10 min at room temperature. After lyophilization, the samples were sprayed with gold. Finally, the biofilms were observed by scanning electron microscopy (HITACHI SU8600).

Preparation of PEEK sheets for implants

First, custom-made circular PEEK sheets with a thickness of 0.5 mm and a diameter of 8 mm were sterilized by soaking in 75% medical ethanol for 4 h, followed by thorough rinsing with sterile PBS three times to remove residual ethanol, and then placed in 24-well plates. Then, 2 mL of MRSA solution (107 CFU mL−1) was added to each well and incubated for 2 days at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the medium was changed every 24 h. Bacterial biofilms were cultured on the surface of the PEEK sheets after 2 days, and finally, the PEEK sheets were collected to establish a mouse implant model of biofilm-associated infections.

In vivo implant infection mouse model

Forty BALB/c mice were divided into four groups: the control, H2O2 (100 μM), CuP and CuP + H2O2 (100 μM) groups. A mouse biofilm infection model was used to evaluate the ability of each treatment group to eliminate implant-related bacterial biofilm infection in vivo. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital, and their backs were depilated, disinfected and incised. The prepared polyetheretherketone biofilm sheets were subsequently implanted subcutaneously, and the skin wounds were sutured immediately. Some mice died accidently during the experimental procedures due to improper anesthesia or other uncontrollable factors and were excluded from the study. The final number of mice in the smallest group was 8, and subsequent experiments were conducted with 8 mice per group. On days 3 and 7, 200 μL of CuP + H2O2 solution was injected into the surface of the implants in each treatment group. The untreated group was injected with an equal amount of PBS. Photographs of the infected site of each mouse were taken on days 1, 5, 10, and 15. The mice in each group were euthanized on day 15. PEEK sheets and peri-implant tissues were carefully removed, immersed in 1 mL of PBS, and then vortexed/ultrasonicated to collect residual bacteria. The number of residual bacteria in the PEEK and surrounding soft tissues was determined via the SPM method, with three replicates per group.

In vivo histologic analysis of an implant infection model

The peri-implant tissues of the different groups were infiltrated with formaldehyde, dehydrated with alcohol solution and sectioned via paraffin embedding. The sections were subsequently analyzed with a Leica SP1600 microcomputer (Leica, Hamburg, Germany). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Giemsa staining were used to evaluate inflammation and bacterial residues. All slides were observed under a light microscope (Olympus, IX70, Japan).

In vivo biosafety assessment

Histological observations of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys) of the rats in each group were carried out via H&E staining to evaluate the biosafety of each treatment regimen.
