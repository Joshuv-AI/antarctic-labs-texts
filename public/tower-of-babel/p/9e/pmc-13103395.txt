Impact of climate change on the suitable niches of an ornithophilous neotropical orchid (Elleanthus brasiliensis) and its pollen vectors
Authors: Marta Kolanowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is no doubt that climate change not only affects the spatial distribution of organisms but also alters ecological interactions that are crucial for the maintenance and functioning of ecosystems. Much research has been devoted to assessing the impact of global warming on animal-mediated pollination, which is vital for the reproduction of vascular plants. Most analyses have focused on insect pollen vectors, as these are the most common animals involved in pollination. The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of climate change on the distribution of suitable niches of the tropical orchid (Elleanthus brasiliensis) and four hummingbirds reported to serve as pollen vectors for this species (Thalurania glaucopis, Phaethornis pretrei, Phaethornis squalidus, Ramphodon naevius), as well as to estimate the locations of climatic refugia where both orchid and pollinator will co-occur in the future. All species studied will lose suitable niches in different Brazilian biomes as a result of global warming. In the reduced, fragmented potential range of E. brasiliensis, not all orchid populations will have a chance of cross-pollination. Of the birds included in the study, T. glaucopis will be the most important orchid pollinator, being available for basically all of the plant populations. The most endangered populations of E. brasiliensis are located in the southern part of the orchid range where no pollen vectors except of T. glaucopis are predicted to occur in the future. The partial lack of pollen vectors and the fragmentation of geographical ranges will pose serious threats to the long-term survival of the studied orchid. The areas identified in this study as suitable for the occurrence of both the orchid and its pollinator(s) should be considered as climatic refugia and receive additional attention when planning conservation activities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43348-4.

Introduction

Climate change is widely recognised as a major driver of biodiversity loss1. Rising temperatures and changes in precipitation patterns are shifting the distribution of species, leading to range expansions, contractions and local extinctions2. Although climate change is a global phenomenon, species around the world are not responding uniformly to the challenge3, and responses vary due to differences in the level of exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity of individual organisms4. However, global warming not only affects individual species, but also alters the ecological interactions that support community composition and ecosystem dynamics5,6. One of the most important ecosystem services threatened by climate change is animal-mediated pollination7 which plays an essential role in the reproduction of flowering plants8. As estimated9, more than 260,000 of angiosperms rely on animals as pollen vectors10 and about 300,000 animal species visit flowers for pollen and nectar rewards11. On a global scale, climate change is affecting the phenology, local abundance and large-scale distribution of plants and their pollinators12.

From an evolutionary perspective, animal vectors enhance pollen transfer to stigmas, but this reliance on mutualists for reproduction increases plant vulnerability to habitat disturbance13. Any change in local pollinator composition, abundance and/or foraging behaviour is predicted to affect the reproductive success and genetic diversity of plant populations14. While generalist plants, which can be pollinated by a variety of species, mostly insects, are usually considered less vulnerable to changes in insect composition, more specialised interactions are more sensitive to pollinator deficiency due to the impossibility of replacing the unique pollen vector with another species13,15. In the world of flowering plants, orchids are one of the most specialised when it comes to pollinators. The Orchidaceae family is known for its specialised pollination mechanisms16. It is one of the two largest families of flowering plants encompassing over 28,000 accepted species17,18 classified in about 600 genera19. Representatives of the family are distributed throughout all continents except Antarctica, but the Neotropical region has been identified as a hotspot of orchid diversity, with around 14,700 species16,20. Notably, each orchid species, on average, has one pollen vector21. Among Orchidaceae, insects are the most common pollinators, and only 3.5% of the orchids analysed by Ackerman et al21. were recorded as ornithophilous. Orchid-pollinating birds are exclusively nectarivorous and belong mainly to the hummingbird family (Trochilidae) in the New World22 and the sunbird family (Nectariniidae) in South Africa23.

While numerous studies have focused on the response of insect-pollinated plants to climate change24–27, little is known about the threats to ornithophilous species. Some limited analyses were conducted to estimate the impact of global warming on hummingbird-pollinated plants28 and nectarivorous birds29, but no study was devoted to evaluate the possibility of spatial mismatch between ornithophilous plants and their pollinators. This topic is important considering that bird-pollinated flowers may represent up to 22% of Angiosperms in tropical communities30. Ornithophily therefore constitutes an important component for plant reproduction in the Tropics30. Given that orchids31 are among the world’s most threatened organisms, it is crucial to assess the potential impact of global warming on their future distribution and the maintenance of one of the most vital ecosystem services: pollination.

It is estimated that almost 1,000 known species of orchid are pollinated by hummingbirds32. However, pollination events have only been well documented in a relatively small number of cases33. Although pollen transfer by Trochilidae has been reported to occur in many orchid genera, including both terrestrial and epiphytic species, these birds appear to be particularly drawn to Elleanthus C. Presl, Cochlioda Lindl. and Comparettia Poepp. & Endl32.. This study assessed the impact of climate change on the distribution of suitable niches for the ornithophilous orchid Elleanthus brasiliensis (Lindl.) Rchb. f. and its four reported pollen vectors. Based on the analyses conducted, the locations of climatic refugia where the orchid and its pollinators will co-occur in the future were estimated, ensuring the plant’s reproductive success.

Methods

Studied pollination network

Elleanthus brasiliensis was selected as the subject of the study because, unlike many other hummingbird-pollinated orchids, it is both pollinator-dependent and protandrous, preventing self-fertilisation. Therefore, the reproduction of this orchid species is entirely dependent on the presence of animal pollen vectors. Also, in contrast to numerous other Orchidaceae species, there are multiple reports on the pollination of E. brasiliensis34. Furthermore, both the orchid and its bird pollinators have a relatively broad distribution and numerous georeferenced occurrence records are available for both species. These data were crucial for conducting reliable niche modelling, which forms the basis of the analyses presented here.

The studied plant species is found mostly in the Atlantic Forest in eastern and southern Brazil, but some authors reported it also from Guiana36. E. brasiliensis occurs at elevations between 700 and 1750 m37. It has not yet been evaluated by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) for its threat status. It grows as an epiphyte or lithophyte with pendant stems bearing dark green, lanceolate leaves arranged alternately. Its inflorescence consists of up to 25 white-reddish flowers. The most striking feature of E. brasiliensis is the large amount of mucilaginous secretion that covers its globular inflorescences38.. This fluid not only helps to retain water, preventing the meristem from drying out, but also forms a physical barrier enclosing the flower buds and the outer parts of the bases of the floral tubes, preventing nectar thieves and reducing feeding by insect herbivores38,39. The flower of this orchid is protandrous and self-compatible, but pollinator-dependent35.

The pollination mechanism of E. brasiliensiswas described in detail by Nunes et al34. who recognized three representatives of Trochilinae that carry pollinia of E. brasiliensis - the violet-capped woodnymph (Thalurania glaucopis, Trochilini), the dusky-throated hermit (Phaethornis squalidus, Phaethornithinae) and the saw-billed hermit (Ramphodon naevius, Phaethornithinae). In general, hummingbird visits to E. brasiliensis were short and the birds moved repeatedly between flowers and plants. Pollinia were removed from the flower by a lever mechanism triggered by inserting a hummingbird beak into the floral tube. This movement causes the column to move towards the lower part of the beak where the pollinia are attached. The deposition of pollinia on the stigmatic surface occurs by the same mechanism34. In 2003, Singer35 reported observation of pollination by the planalto hermit (Phaethornis pretrei, Phaethornithinae). According to the IUCN, all pollinators of E. brasiliensis are considered to be of least concern (LC)40–43. They are characterised by a relatively broad distribution and share feeding behaviour. All four bird species consume the nectar of various flowering plants, as well as feeding on small arthropods44–46.

Thalurania glaucopis is commonly found in Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay at the elevation up to 900 m40,46. It occurs also in Uruguay, although it is not breeding in this country. The violet-capped woodnymph occurs in primary forest, scrublands, and suburban and urban parks46. Phaethornis pretrei is widely distributed in Brazil south of the Amazon rainforest, eastern Bolivia, northern Argentina and in Paraguay42,45. It occurs in a variety of habitats from lowland areas of about 400 m up to 2100 m of elevation42,45. Phaethornis squalidus is found in southeastern Brazil. It occurs in southern Minas Gerais and Espírito Santo south to Santa Catarina, and also in the Iguazu Falls area of western Paraná. It inhabits the understory of humid primary and secondary forests from the coastal zone up to 2300 m of elevation41,44. Ramphodon naevius occurs in a narrow stripe of southeastern Brazil from the states of Minas Gerais and Espírito Santo south to eastern Santa Catarina and northernmost Rio Grande do Sul. It is found in the understory of the humid coastal Atlantic Forest up to an elevation of 900 m43,45.

List of localities

Databases of localities of E. brasiliensis47, T. glaucopis48, P. pretrei49, P. squalidus39 and R. naevius50 were compiled from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (https://www.gbif.org/). Additionally, previously published data on the occurrence of E. brasiliensis were used51. Only records that could be georeferenced with a precision of 1 km were used in the ecological niche modelling (ENM) analyses. The duplicate presence records (records within the same grid cell) were removed using MaxEnt version 3.3.252. To further reduce sampling bias53, the localities were rarified using 5 classes of topographic heterogeneity (< 0.2, 0.2–0.4, 0.4–0.6, 0.6–0.8, 0.8–1.8) which was calculated by the elevation from each raster pixel and the eight cells neighboring the focal cell54. Species records were reduced to a minimum distance of 20 km in areas of low topographic heterogeneity and 2 km in highly heterogeneous areas. The complete list of localities used in this study is available in Table S1.

Model calibration and settings

The modelling of the current and future distributions of the studied species was performed using the maximum entropy method implemented in MaxEnt version 3.3.252 based on presence-only observations. This method was chosen because the tropical region has been relatively poorly explored in terms of species diversity and distribution55, which prevents the construction of reliable species absence datasets. Bioclimatic variables which are derived from the monthly temperature and rainfall values in 30 arc-seconds of interpolated climate surface data downloaded from WorldClim v. 2.1 were used for the analyses56. The study area was reduced to (2.16°N-36.44°S, 60.91–33.24°W) to improve the models57.

Pearsons’ correlation coefficient was computed using SDMtoolbox 2.3 for ArcGIS54(Table S2) and highly correlated (> 0.8) variables were removed from the ENM analyses to prevent problems associated with autocorrelation. In all analyses, the maximum number of iterations was set to 10,000 with the convergence threshold set to 0.00001. The neutral regularization multiplier value and auto features were used. The “random seed” option generated a random test partition and background subset for each run, with 30% of the samples were used as test points. The run was performed as a bootstrap with 100 replicates. The output was set to logistic. The “fade by clamping” function in MaxEnt was used to prevent extrapolations outside the environmental range of the training data. All analyses of GIS data were carried out using ArcGIS 10.6 (Esri, Redlands, CA, USA). The accuracy of the models was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) and True Skill Statistic (TSS)58.

Climate projections and emission scenarios

The future extent of the orchid and hummingbirds suitable niches for 2080–2100 was predicted using four projections for three Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSPs) describing alternative pathways of future socio-economic development - SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5. These storylines differ in predicted greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, expected level of radiative forcing and anticipated achievement of sustainable development goals59. The SSP1-2.6 scenario assumes CO2 emissions cut to net zero around 2075. In SSP2–4.5 with intermediate GHG emissions consider continuation of CO2 emissions at current levels until 2050. Finally, SSP5–8.5 is based on assumption of high CO2 emissions which triple by 2075. The estimated climate warming in 2080–2100 in analysed scenarios vary from 1.3 to 2.4 °C (SSP1–2.6) to 3.3–5.7 °C (SSP5–8.5)59, In this study projections developed by the Max Planck Institute Earth System Model (MPI-ESM1.2) for the High-Resolution Model Intercomparison Project (HighResMIP)60 were used, as this forecast is the most reliable within the analyzed geographical area61.

To visualize and evaluate the bioclimatic preferences of the plant and its pollinators, predicted niche occupancy (PNO) profiles for studied species were created62. To compare potential ranges of species studied which were modelled for near present climatic conditions with future simulation, all models were converted into binary rasters based on presence thresholds equalled to calculated minimum training presence (logistic threshold). Using the Goode homolosine as a projection, the maps were compared and each grid cell was assigned to one of the four categories: −1 (range expansion), 0 (no occurrence), 1 (present in both models), 2 (range contraction). The coverage of each cell class was calculated to estimate the possible habitat loss or expansion of each species using SDMtoolbox 2.3 for ArcGIS54.

Results

Model evaluation and limiting factors

The generated models received high scores for AUC (0.947–0.994) indicating good reliability of the analyses (Table 2). Values of TSS varied between 0.58 and 0.94. The minimum training presence (Table 2) was 0.515 for E. brasiliensis models and lower for hummingbirds (T. glaucopis − 0.017; P. pretrei – 0.0115; P. squalidus – 0.015, R. naevius – 0.032). The near-present models are consistent with the known distribution of the orchid and its pollinators (Fig. 1). However, some known populations of E. brasiliensis apparently occur in areas of somewhat lower bioclimatic suitability.

The generated PNO profiles showed a rather high similarity of the bioclimatic preferences of the studied orchid and hummingbirds (Fig. 3). However, some discrepancies in ecological tolerances for several variables are observed between E. brasiliensis and its pollinators. According to the plots created, the orchid prefers habitats characterised by lower isothermality index (bio3; 45–60%) than bird species (bio3 of 50–70). Furthermore, E. brasiliensis occurs in areas with lower precipitation of the wettest (bio13–100–180 mm) and higher precipitation of the driest month (bio 14–100–150 mm) than hummingbirds (bio13–180–380 mm, bio14–0–120 mm). The preferred mean temperature of the coldest quarter (bio11) is lower in E. brasiliensis (10–17 °C). The other discrepancy is observed in the precipitation seasonality (bio15) which is generally narrower in the studied orchid. The orchid has a wider tolerance in terms of the precipitation of the coldest quarter (bio19) than the hummingbirds studied.

Changes in suitable niches’ coverage.

Elleanthus brasiliensis is expected to lose 13–45% of its currently suitable niches (Fig. 4; Table 3), with the strongest contraction predicted under SSP3-7.0. Habitat loss is projected in both the northern and southern parts of its range, with the most pronounced reduction in the southernmost and westernmost areas. Small expansions are predicted in limited areas of moist broadleaf Atlantic Forest (mata atlântica) across all scenarios.

Among the hummingbirds, Thalurania glaucopis and Ramphodon naevius are projected to lose suitable niches across several biomes, including caatinga, cerrado and pantanal (Fig. 4; Table 3), with future suitability concentrated mainly in the southern Atlantic Forest and pampa regions. T. glaucopis is predicted to lose 9–58% of its suitable area. The response of R. naevius is scenario-dependent, ranging from < 6% loss in SSP2-4.5 to > 50% loss in SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 (13% loss under SSP1-2.6). Notably, R. naevius and Phaethornis squalidus are the only species predicted to expand their potential ranges in some regions.

Phaethornis pretrei is expected to experience relatively minor range contraction (0.2–8%) under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0, increasing to nearly 13% under SSP5-8.5 (Fig. 4; Table 3). Habitat loss is projected mainly near the Amazon River mouth, along the slopes of the Acarai Mountains, and within the Gran Chaco.

Phaethornis squalidus is projected to expand its suitable niches primarily in the north-western part of its current range (Fig. 4; Table 3). However, habitat gains exceed losses only under SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5. Overall changes in suitable niche coverage range from + 13.93% (SSP1-2.6) to −46.88% (SSP5-8.5).

Impact of climate change on pollinator availability

As evaluated in this study, T. glaucopis is the most important pollinator of E. brasiliensis which will remain available for the orchid within its reduced geographical range (Table 4; Fig. 5). The predicted climate change will not result in spatial mismatch between the violet-capped woodnymph and studied plant.

Ramphodon naevius is currently available for about 38% of the orchid populations. As a result of global warming the saw-billed hermit will be available for 40–66% of E. brasiliensis populations (Table 4; Fig. 5). The hummingbird will be absent only in the southern part of the orchid geographical range.

The potential range of P. squalidus currently overlaps with almost 70% of E. brasiliensis range. The expected future availability of the dusky-throated hermit as an orchid pollinator varies between almost 79% in SSP1-2.6 to 95% in SSP3-7.0 scenarios (Table 4; Fig. 5). Regions which will be suitable for E. brasiliensis occurrence but will not be inhabited by P. squalidus are located near Lagoa dos Patos (SSP3-7.0) and along slopes of highlands west of Lagoa dos Patos (SSP5-8.5). Also, southern parts of the orchid potential range will be unsuitable for P. squalidus (SSP1-2.6).

Phaethornis pretrei currently serves as pollinator for approximately 68% of E. brasiliensis populations. The overlap in the geographical ranges of the planalto hermit and the orchid studied will not change significantly (Table 4; Fig. 5). In SSP5-8.5 scenario the availability of the pollinator will be slightly lower (65%) and in SSP3-7.0 it will be slightly higher (72%) than currently observed. Spatial mismatch will mostly be observed in the southern part of E. brasiliensis geographical range.

Discussion

Effect of global warming on geographical distribution of species studied

All analysed species will lose their suitable niches across variety of Brazilian biomes as a result of global warming. The most damaging for the orchid will be the SSP3-7.0 climate change scenario which will cause loss of more than 45% of E. brasiliensis niches. The most damaging scenario in terms of habitat loss for Thalurania glaucopis will be SSP5-8.5 in which the hummingbird will face 58% reduction in the potential range. The other three analyses bird species studied will face the most significant habitat loss in the SSP3-7.0 scenario.

Not only will E. brasiliensis face a loss of suitable niches of 12–45%, but its geographical range will also be severely fragmented, placing this orchid at high risk of extinction63. Increasing geographical isolation of populations can lead to reduced gene flow, resulting in genetic drift and erosion64. Additionally, small, isolated populations may experience high levels of inbreeding65, which often leads to reduced reproduction and offspring survival65. The fragmentation of E. brasiliensis geographical range can also affect population dynamics by reducing the species’ ability to survive unpredictable events64 and by influencing demographic stochasticity66.

It is important to emphasise that the life cycle of each orchid species depends on the availability of mycorrhizal fungi, which regulate seed germination, protocorm growth and adult nutrition67. The geographical distribution of Orchidaceae representatives is therefore closely linked to the distribution of their symbionts68. To date, no mycological analyses have been carried out to identify the fungal partners of E. brasiliensis, so this factor could not be included in the ENM analyses. Future changes in the distribution of mycorrhizal fungi, which are important for the well-being of the orchid population, cannot be assessed at this time.

In the case of the hummingbirds studied, their geographical ranges will be altered but not severely fragmented, except in the northern parts where isolation will be higher. All four pollen vectors studied are considered to be sedentary with a potential for short-distance spatial migration. Two of them, T. glaucopis and R. naevius, are lowland species occurring from the littoral zone up to 500–900 m40,43. Phaethornis species have a wider elevational range, and it was found up to 2300 m of elevation41,42,69. At this stage, it is difficult to assess the actual possibility of migration, either spatially or altitudinally, to habitats that will become suitable for the birds as a result of global warming. Noteworthy, hummingbirds are territorial and tend not to disperse too much when foraging hereby their spatial relocation can be further limited70,71.

All E. brasiliensis pollinators feed on nectar from a wide variety of flowering plants69. They also feed on small arthropods collected from the vegetation69. Given the diversity of food sources, it is likely that even if the local biotic composition changes, all the hummingbirds studied will be able to find a suitable foraging niche, but with rising temperatures the species may be forced to adapt to higher altitude conditions. While Phaethornis representatives are already adapted to occur at different altitudes, two other species studied may face problems with physiological adaptations to lower oxygen availability and barometric pressure, associated with a decrease in metabolic rate and an increase in time spent in a torpid state72.

Another interesting result of the analyses carried out is that all four birds studied are characterised by very similar tolerance to 19 bioclimatic variables, although they represent two different subfamilies of Trochilidae - Thalurania glaucopis is classified within emeralds (Trochilinae) while P. pretrei, P. squalidus and R. naevius belong to hermits (Phaethornithinae). Interestingly, these sympatric hummingbirds occupy similar ecological niches, despite the potential increase in competitive interactions that this creates. This similar pattern of lack of clear niche partitioning has previously been recognised in sympatric breeding Antarctic fulmarine birds73. Further studies should be undertaken to examine the spatio-temporal habitat use of these species to assess their resource segregation (spatial, temporal or dietary)74. Understanding the factors that support the coexistence of the studied pollen vectors is crucial for a more comprehensive assessment of their chances of survival under climate change.

Availability of pollen vectors and location of climate refugia

Elleanthus brasiliensis exhibits functional protandry and it is pollinator-dependent34. This means that, unlike plants that can become autogamous in stressful environments, the availability of pollen vectors is crucial for its reproduction and long-term survival. As assessed in this study, the availability of hummingbird pollinators for E. brasiliensis will not be significantly reduced. Of the birds included in the study, T. glaucopis will be the most important pollinator of the orchid, being available for basically all plant populations. The least overlap will be observed between E. brasiliensis and R. naevius. The populations of E. brasiliensis that are most at risk are located in the southern part of their range, where no pollen vectors other than T. glaucopis are predicted to occur in future. It is noteworthy that this species of hummingbird can also be found in fragmented forests75, and it has even been reported in cultivated urban gardens76. The ability to use human-altered habitats where nectar resources are present could improve the chances of survival of the violet-capped woodnymph in the future.

As shown in this study, in the highly fragmented future geographical range of E. brasiliensis, very few areas will be suitable for the occurrence of its known pollen vectors. In general, the orchid populations in Chapada Diamantina, Serra do Mar and Serra Geral have the best chance of reproductive success and long-term survival. These regions should therefore be considered as climatic refugia for the species studied. However, due to the complex life cycle of tropical orchids, the areas relatively buffered from contemporary climate change should also support the ecological networks of E. brasiliensis over time.

This study only evaluated the availability of known pollen vectors. As previously mentioned, there have been comparatively few observations of orchid pollinia transfer by birds, despite the extraordinary diversity of Orchidaceae species. It is possible that E. brasiliensis can be pollinated by other species of hummingbird that have not previously been reported35. It is also important to note that, although no reports of a shift in the bird pollen vector of orchids have yet been published, it cannot be ruled out that orchids will be pollinated by different species of Trochilidae under new climatic conditions. It should be noted that, while most orchids have evolved specialised pollination systems and often rely on one or a few pollen vectors, some species can be pollinated by a variety of animal vectors, such as hummingbirds, bumblebees and solitary bees (e.g. Gomphichis validaRchb. f77.). Elleanthus brasiliensis seems to be adapted only to hummingbird pollen transfer, as the upward-pointing lip cannot serve as a landing platform, making it difficult for bees such as Trigona to access the flower. The shape, colour and lack of scent of the flower also suggest pollination by hummingbirds78.

Much remains unknown about which trait modifications are critical for pollinator shifts79–81. Flowers pollinated by hummingbirds often exhibit a combination of characteristics that promote visits by Trochilidae (such as copious, dilute nectar and a narrow shape), while avoiding bees (such as a lack of nectar guides, a landing platform and scent)82,83. However, some Dudleya species (Crassulaceae) with semi-tubular corollas appear to be adapted to pollination by both bees and hummingbirds84. It is noteworthy that the evolution of Trochilidae pollination has primarily been documented in one direction: from ancestral bee pollination to derived hummingbird pollination85,86. Records of hummingbird pollinator shifts are extremely rare and have so far only been found to occur as a result of extreme weather events87.

It is important to explore the impact of global warming on plant-hummingbird pollination systems, especially for the effective conservation of plants. Changes in climatic conditions will not necessarily result in the co-extinction of interacting species88, as Trochilidae species can tolerate the removal of individual nectar-producing plant species from an ecosystem because they are generally not particularly selective about their nectar sources89. In human-altered landscapes, a shift towards generalisation in hummingbird assemblages has been observed, promoting more robust communities capable of sustaining hummingbird populations90. Conversely, many plants are adapted to a particular pollen vector or have a very limited pool of pollinators, so their reproductive success will decrease dramatically in the absence of pollinators91. Therefore, while hummingbird survival is not usually related to the presence of any specific flowering plant species, many plants will become extinct in the absence of their pollen vector. The procedure for estimating changes in the geographical distribution of plants and their pollinating hummingbirds, as presented in this paper, is therefore crucial for identifying climate refugia that should be included in local protected area systems to increase the long-term effectiveness of preserving rare, pollinator-specialised plant species.

Taking into account the modelling limitations presented in this manuscript, the presence of mycorrhizal fungi is also an important factor affecting the distribution of Orchidaceae species. Data on the composition of Elleanthus fungal endophytes are scarce92 and it is currently impossible to evaluate the impact of the availability of specific mycorrhizal partners on the occurrence of E. brasiliensis, which could modify the location and extent of climate refugia25. Furthermore, since E. brasiliensis primarily grows as an epiphyte, it is advisable to evaluate its phorophyte specificity to assess potential changes in the distribution of its plant hosts in the context of global warming, as well as to evaluate its presence in the identified climatic refugia93.

Importantly, the projected climatic contraction and fragmentation of suitable niches for E. brasiliensis may be further exacerbated by ongoing habitat loss and landscape transformation in Brazil. The reduction of native vegetation due to agricultural expansion and cattle ranching, as well as urbanization and real estate speculation, continues to reduce the availability of suitable habitats and ecological connectivity, particularly in the Atlantic Forest biome94,95. Since resilience to climate change depends not only on climatic stability but also on habitat integrity, the preservation and restoration of remaining forest fragments is essential to maintain ecological stability and viable conditions for species persistence under rising temperatures96,97. This is especially relevant for biodiversity hotspots such as the Atlantic Forest and Cerrado, where high levels of endemism coincide with extreme anthropogenic97–99.

Conclusions

This is the first study to assess the impact of climate change on the spatial availability of hummingbird pollen vectors for a Neotropical orchid species. As indicated by the analyses conducted, all species studied will face habitat loss as a result of global warming, and in the reduced potential range of E. brasiliensis, not all orchid populations will have a chance for cross-pollination. The lack of a pollen vector and the fragmentation of the geographical range will constitute serious threats to the long-term survival of the orchid. The areas identified in this study as suitable for the occurrence of both the orchid and its pollinator(s) should be considered as climatic refugia and receive additional attention when planning conservation activities.

Another interesting finding of the study is that co-occurring hummingbirds share a bioclimatic niche despite representing different subfamilies. This poor niche partitioning, which affects competition, should be further investigated to improve conservation of South American Trochilidae.
