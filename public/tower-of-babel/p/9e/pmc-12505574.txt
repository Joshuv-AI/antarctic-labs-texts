Understanding challenges in healthcare access: qualitative insights from healthcare providers and people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances
Authors: Emilie Op de Beeck, Hannah Ledegen, Gytha Slechten, Josefien Van Olmen, Hans De Loof, Hilde Bastiaens, Caroline Masquillier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEquitable access to healthcare is fundamental to improving population health outcomes, yet persistent disparities leave many underserved, particularly those in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances. In Belgium, unmet healthcare needs are disproportionately concentrated among low-income groups, migrants and individuals with low educational attainment. These structural inequities are reflected in poorer health outcomes, including reduced life expectancy and limited access to essential services such as dental, mental, and specialist care. Existing research has primarily taken cross-sectional approaches, failing to capture how barriers accumulate over time. To address this gap, this study adopts a longitudinal perspective to explore the cumulative nature of healthcare access barriers among socio-economically vulnerable populations in Belgium. It also incorporates the perspectives of both patients and healthcare providers.MethodA qualitative longitudinal approach using semi-structured interviews at three time-points with 16 people living in socio-economically circumstances was conducted. In the same period, 17 semi-structured interviews with healthcare providers (HCPs), such as general practitioners (GPs), dentists, pharmacists and psychologists were carried out. The sampling strategy included a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. Interviews were audiotaped and transcribed, then reflexive thematic analysis was carried out to ensure a transparent and rigorous analysis.ResultsThe study revealed that barriers to accessing care accumulate over time, particularly in the context of a fragmented healthcare system. Individuals living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances often deprioritise their healthcare and need to repeatedly navigate the healthcare access continuum to address different health concerns, often involving multiple healthcare providers. Each iteration can introduce new provider-specific barriers, compounding the challenges and increasing the complexity of accessing healthcare. Participants with an informal social network or support from HCPs and societal organisations could temporarily overcome the barriers to access care.ConclusionThe study reveals the complex and cumulative barriers to healthcare access for socio-economically vulnerable individuals, including competing priorities, system fragmentation, and cultural and language challenges. While informal social networks provide some support, they are insufficient to address systemic issues. Integrating these networks with formal community outreach initiatives could improve coordination and access to healthcare.Keywords: Access to healthcare, Barriers, Socio-economically vulnerable people, Longitudinal qualitative research

Introduction

Access to healthcare is a cornerstone of effective healthcare systems, closely linked to improved population health outcomes [1, 2]. Achieving equitable access to healthcare is essential to ensure these benefits are distributed equally across all population groups [3]. However, disparities in healthcare access persist, leaving growing numbers of people underserved [4]. In Belgium, these disparities are closely linked to socio-economic status and are correlated by unequal distributions of social determinants of health [5, 6]. According to 2022 data, individuals from the wealthiest households did not report any unmet medical care needs due to financial reasons. In contrast, 2.6% of individuals in the poorest households reported unmet medical care needs because of financial barriers [7]. This gradient in unmet healthcare needs is growing. Evidence sources indicate that inequalities in access are further influenced by factors such as educational level, household composition, employment status, and migration background. Unmet needs are particularly pronounced among low-income households, individuals with limited education, single parents, people who are unemployed or inactive due to illness, and non-EU migrants, groups that report disproportionately high levels of unmet care, especially due to financial reasons [8].

These socio-economic inequalities are also evident in health outcomes, especially in dental care, mental healthcare, specialised care and preventive care [8, 9]. Moreover, in Belgium between 2015 and 2019, there was a correlation observed between mortality and socio-economic indicators, including education, income, housing characteristics and socio-professional status. In 2019, 87% of adults in the highest income quintile rated their health as good, compared to just 59% in the lowest quintile, a disparity larger than in most Western European countries [10]. These inequalities have substantial consequences for life expectancy: in 2020, men in the highest income quartile lived an average of 9.3 years longer than those in the lowest quartile, while for women the gap was 6.3 years [7].

They are driven by persistent structural inequities in five essential conditions identified as fundamental to health equity: income security and social protection, quality living conditions, access to health services, social and human capital, and fair employment and working conditions [11]. In Belgium, individuals from lower income quintiles are significantly more likely to live in inadequate or overcrowded housing, lack access to safe environments and green spaces, face precarious or temporary employment, and experience limited educational and civic opportunities and placing an excessive burden on vulnerable groups [8]. Importantly, many individuals do not face these vulnerabilities in isolation but live at the intersection of multiple disadvantages [12]. This intersectionality intensifies their exposure to health risks and reduces their capacity to access care or improve their living conditions. As a result, these compounded disadvantages reinforce a cycle of poor health outcomes that can persist across generations [13].

Despite often having greater health needs, vulnerable populations, including individuals in socio-economically disadvantaged circumstances and those with limited education, face significant barriers, in accessing healthcare [14]. This reality reflects the “inverse care law” [Tudor Hart, 1971), where those most in need of care often have the least access, a situation that remains strikingly relevant today [15].

A substantial body of research in different countries has further explored this principle, highlighting barriers in access to healthcare across vulnerable populations, including migrants, refugees and indigenous people [16, 17]. Reviews summarizing the findings conclude that the healthcare system fails to address the needs of these diverse groups due to cultural differences [18], financial constraints [19], linguistic barriers, poor communication, and a lack of information on navigating the healthcare system [20, 21].

Despite these valuable insights, research has largely been cross-sectional, focusing on specific populations or isolated barriers [22]. There is a notable lack of knowledge about the cumulative impact of barriers over time. Understanding how individuals living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances navigate the continuum of healthcare access while managing intersecting challenges is critical for developing effective strategies to improve healthcare accessibility and advance health equity. This study addresses this gap by exploring the interplay between access barriers and the perspectives of both people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances and healthcare providers (HCPs), offering detailed insights into the dynamic and cumulative nature of healthcare access challenges [23]. More specifically, this article aims to explore the cumulative effect of the barriers to healthcare access for people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances through a longitudinal lens and examines the perceptions of these barriers from the perspectives of both people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances and HCPs in the Belgian context. The following section outlines key features of the Belgian healthcare system.

Belgian healthcare system

Compulsory social health insurance covers 99% of Belgian residents for a large range of services. The federal government and the federates entities are responsible for health policy [24]. The federal government is responsible for regulating compulsory health insurance, health products, and the healthcare professions. The health insurance system is delivered by five non-profit national health funds, a railway workers’ fund, and a public auxiliary fund. It is governed by the National Institute for Health and Disability Insurance (INAMI-RIZIV), which sets reimbursement criteria, manages the national healthcare budget, supports healthcare professionals, ensures quality standards, and facilitates stakeholder negotiations. Every Belgian resident must be affiliated to a health insurance fund of their choice or to the public auxiliary fund [10, 25].

Healthcare provision in Belgium is guided by key principles, including independent medical practice, direct access to services without gatekeeping, free choice of both physician and healthcare facility, and a predominantly fee-for-service payment model. Healthcare providers are not directly employed by the sickness funds. Instead, they operate as independent private practitioners. Many choose to adhere to nationally negotiated agreements, becoming what are known as conventional physicians. These conventions define official tariffs and cost-sharing arrangements. Providers who do not join these agreements, referred to as non-conventional practitioners, are permitted to charge fees above the standard rates, a practice known as extra billing [24].

Patients contribute to the cost of care through out-of-pocket payments. The amount paid varies depending on the individual’s social and insurance status (with some groups eligible for preferential reimbursement), the type of service received, and previous co-payments made. These user charges apply to a broad range of services, including general practitioner consultations and home visits, specialist care, outpatient medications, physiotherapy, and daily inpatient hospital stays [26].

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by the patient-centred access to healthcare framework developed by Levesque et al. [27], which conceptualises access as a dynamic and multi-dimensional process occurring at the interface between health systems and populations, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Rather than defining access only as the physical availability or use of services, the framework views access as a continuum, reflecting patients’ abilities to engage with opportunities provided by the healthcare system. On the supply side, an accessible healthcare system is characterised by approachability, which refers to the extent to which services can be identified and understood by those in need. Acceptability involves the cultural and social compatibility between healthcare services and their users. Availability and accommodation pertain to the physical presence of services and the way they are organised to meet users’ needs. Affordability addresses the financial accessibility of care, encompassing both direct costs and indirect or opportunity costs. Finally, appropriateness captures how well the services provided align with patients’ actual health needs, including the quality and continuity of care.

Access to care continuum adapted from framework of Levesque et al. (2013)

Simultaneously, on the demand side, people need several abilities in order to access healthcare. These include the ability to perceive symptoms or risks, which refers to the capacity to recognise health needs and understand that care is available. The ability to seek involves having the autonomy, awareness, and motivation to actively look for care. The ability to reach includes the physical capacity, mobility, and access to transport necessary to get to services. The ability to pay reflects the financial means to afford care without burden. Finally, the ability to engage represents the capability and willingness to participate in care and sustain involvement throughout the treatment process.

By framing access as the interaction between these supply-side dimensions and demand-side abilities, the model captures the complexity of real-world access processes. It highlights how barriers may emerge at various stages, from the perception of need to the use and effectiveness of care and underscores the importance of both health system characteristics and population characteristics [27].

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Antwerp-North, a district in the northern part of the city of Antwerp, Belgium. Socio-economic and healthcare access inequalities are especially pronounced in this area, which is one of the most densely populated parts of the city [28], characterised by high levels of social and economic deprivation. A large proportion of children in this neighbourhood grow up in poverty, and residents experience multiple forms of disadvantage that intersect and reinforce one another [29]. The area reports some of the lowest participation rates in preventive health services, such as cancer screenings, and a high proportion of residents qualify for preferential reimbursement a marker of low income and heightened financial vulnerability [30]. In addition to socioeconomic hardship, the neighbourhood is also marked by elevated levels of public safety concerns. Recent coordinated actions by the local police reported 139 incidents over a two-week period, more than a third of which were drug-related, highlighting the scale and persistence of criminal activity in the area [31].

Research design

A descriptive, longitudinal qualitative research study about barriers in access to healthcare, using the patient-centred access to healthcare framework to define and conceptualise access described by Levesque et al. [27]. Study was conducted from April 2023 until February 2024 in Antwerp - the second largest city in Belgium [32]. A longitudinal qualitative study was carried out over the course of approximately 6 months with semi-structured interviews being conducted at three time-points with people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances. A longitudinal approach was chosen in order to better understand the evolution in barriers to healthcare access over time and to gain insights in the interplay and possible cumulative effect of these barriers. In line with methodological guidance for longitudinal qualitative research, the time frame was selected to be sufficiently long to observe changes in the phenomenon under investigation, yet short enough to maintain participant engagement and ensure data relevance [33, 34]. A cross-sectional approach was used with HCPs, including dentists, general practitioners (GPs), pharmacists, and psychologists. Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted to investigate their perspectives on access to healthcare.

Participants and sampling

Participants living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances were purposefully recruited in a district of Antwerp with the second highest percentage, 37%, of people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances [35]. Recruitment was conducted with attention to diversity to ensure a sample with broad range of characteristics. This approach acknowledges that vulnerability is shaped by intersecting factors, such as income, migration status, education level, housing conditions, and language proficiency, which interact to influence individuals’ experiences of accessing healthcare [36]. Following characteristics were considered during recruitment: educational attainment; age; gender; proficiency in the Dutch language; country of origin; duration of residence in Belgium; and household composition. Dutch proficiency was one of the factors considered in recruitment; however, to avoid excluding potential participants on linguistic grounds, individuals with limited Dutch skills were also invited. The first step was to reach social organisations who work with the study population, such as: a foodbank; non-profit organizations dedicated to supporting people living in vulnerable circumstances by providing clothes; and a community-based health promotion setting [37]. In a second step, the contact person in each organisation introduced the study to their clients and provided the names of those who were interested to the researcher executing the fieldwork (EO, GS). In a third step, the potential respondents were contacted by the researchers and provided further details about the study. To facilitate understanding, the study was explained using a visual presentation (PowerPoint) alongside a standard information sheet. Data collection

Three semi-structured interviews with people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances, were conducted over a time period of 6 months with each individual respondent, between April 2023 and February 2024. In between the three semi-structured interviews, four informal ‘check-ins’ per participant were undertaken through various means, such as phone calls, messages and regular presence at much-frequented organisations in the area, in order to further build trust and maintain engagement. Data for informal check-ins were documented through field notes. Semi-structured interview guides were used and topics of the interview guides can be found in Table 3, with open-ended questions formulated based on the access to healthcare framework described by Levesque et al. (2013). The first interview focused on exploring participants’ previous or current health challenges. A second interview, scheduled approximately three months later, aimed to capture changes in participants’ living context and health status while also gaining insight into the progression of access to healthcare issues. The third and final interview of the longitudinal study followed a similar structure of the second interview. Braun and Clarke argue the traditional concept of data saturation in line with the principles of reflexive thematic analysis, which assumes a finite point at which no new information emerges. Instead, this study drawn on the concept of information power whereby the adequacy of the sample size is determined by the richness, relevance, and quality of the data in relation to the study aim, rather than quantity alone. Malterud et al. [39], specifically note that studies involving longitudinal designs and in-depth exploration may require fewer participants due to the enhanced depth and contextual insight gained over time. Based on this principle, a sample of 16 participants was opted, which provided sufficient information power to address the research objectives. The intensity of data collection (three in-depth interviews plus ongoing informal check-ins), and the contextual depth obtained through trust-building all contributed to a high level of information power. While interviews and informal check-ins were primarily conducted in Dutch, a translation app was used when necessary to translate one or two words, because study participants had varying levels of Dutch proficiency: some had minimal understanding and relied on translation tools to translate one or two words in a question (e.g., Google Translate), others had moderate proficiency enabling basic interactions, and a subset were native speakers. To ensure comprehension, the information sheet was explained in accessible terms with visual aids (a PowerPoint). To ensure participants clearly understood the study content, informed consent forms were provided in both Dutch and the participant’s native language, using clear and accessible language.

Each interview or ‘check-in’ was held at a location and time preferred by the respondent. The semi-structured interviews lasted approximately 60 min, while the informal check-ins had an average duration of 23 min.

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted with the HCPs, lasting an average of 60 min each. These interviews were conducted in the HCPs’ practice or office at a time convenient to the HCP.

Data analysis

People living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances

The audio recordings were transcribed by the first author. NVivo (13/R1/2020) was used to efficiently store and manage the dataset. After transcription, the first author listened to the audio recordings and read through the transcripts to correct any errors and familiarise with the data. Next, reflexive thematic analysis was used to analyse the data which served as the overarching framework for identifying, analysing, and interpreting patterns across the dataset. Analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase process: familiarisation with the data, initial coding, generating preliminary themes, reviewing and developing themes, refining and naming themes, and final writing [40]. This approach recognises that themes are not passively discovered in the data but are actively constructed by the researcher through deep engagement with the material. As such, themes are understood as patterns of shared meaning, underpinned by a central organising concept, and may bring together seemingly disparate data to express latent or implicit meanings [41].

Theme development in this reflexive approach was recursive and interpretive, requiring substantial analytic judgement. Coding was carried out inductively by the first author, who coded all transcripts with a focus on barriers to healthcare access. The process was flexible and responsive, allowing codes and interpretations to evolve as familiarity with the data deepened.

Importantly, the reflexive nature of this approach acknowledges the subjectivity of the researcher. As themes do not exist independently of the researcher, they are shaped by the researcher’s theoretical stance, values, experience, and interpretive lens. To support reflexivity and ensure rigour, analytic decisions and theme development were discussed regularly with two co-authors (CM and HB), providing critical feedback and interpretive depth.

In the longitudinal component of the analysis, a diary was maintained for each participant, integrating data from all three interviews and informal check-ins. This enabled the identification of temporal patterns and evolving barriers to healthcare access. While the primary analysis was semantic and inductive, some latent elements were explored, particularly in tracing how structural and relational dynamics shaped participants’ healthcare trajectories over time [42].

Healthcare providers

The audio recordings were transcribed by the first author. After transcription, the first author listened to the audio recordings and read through the transcripts to correct any errors and familiarise with the dataset. Next, reflexive thematic analysis was used to analyse the data [42]. The transcripts were coded line-by-line systematically and thoroughly focusing on barriers in access to healthcare as perceived by the HCP.

Analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase process: familiarisation with the data, initial coding, generating preliminary themes, reviewing and developing themes, refining and naming themes, and final writing [40].

Taking two perspectives together

Following the separate analysis of the data from people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances and HCPs, a comparative thematic analysis was conducted to explore intersections, contrasts, and tensions between the two groups’ views on healthcare access. Codes and themes from the HCP interviews were systematically compared against the themes generated from participants living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances. This process allowed us to identify areas of alignment as well as divergences [27]. The themes and subthemes were then further compared with the theoretical framework of this study, the access-to-care continuum as designed by Levesque et al. [27]. All the themes were reviewed, defined and revised with two other authors (CM and HB) to accurately represent the content and meaning of the data.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness and rigor of the study, several strategies were employed throughout the research process. Credibility was strengthened by purposive sampling to ensure diversity among participants across relevant characteristics. Recruitment continued until sufficient information power was achieved [39], indicating that the sample provided adequate depth and diversity to address the study aims.

Data collection was guided by a semi-structured interview protocol that allowed flexibility to follow participants’ narratives while maintaining consistency across interviews. Interviews were conducted by trained researchers familiar with qualitative methods and the context of primary care, which enhanced the quality and reliability of the data gathered.

Dependability and confirmability were addressed through a reflexive thematic analysis approach, supported by consistent and systematic coding practices. The first author led the initial coding, and the development of themes was an iterative process involving ongoing discussion and critical reflection among three members of the research team. The reflexivity of the researcher was applied throughout all phases of the research, contributing to transparency in decision-making and interpretation. This collaborative and reflexive approach is well-recognised in qualitative research as a means to enrich the analysis by bringing multiple perspectives to bear, allowing for both complementary insights and constructive challenge.

Finally, transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, and methodological decisions, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to other settings.

Reflection as a researcher

Conducting qualitative research on the cumulative effect of barriers in access to healthcare with people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances was a deeply introspective experience. My background as a pharmacist provided a unique lens through which I engaged with participants and interpreted their experiences, while simultaneously challenging me to adopt the mindset of a researcher.

My professional experience equipped me with the skills to communicate effectively with individuals living in vulnerable circumstances. Pharmacists often encounter patients at the intersection of medical, social, and emotional challenges, which allowed me to approach participants with empathy, build trust, and create a safe space for them to share their stories. This trust was critical, as many participants faced stigmas and systemic inequities that made discussing their barriers to care a delicate process.

This research allowed me to step out of my traditional role and adopt a more exploratory, open-ended approach. While my expertise as a pharmacist was valuable, it also required careful navigation to avoid biases. I had to consciously avoid assuming solutions or leading participants towards specific responses. Instead, allowing pauses in conversation and giving participants space to express their thoughts freely and guide the dialogue.

In conclusion, this reflection strengthens the importance of integrating professional expertise with qualitative research methods. It also underscores the need for healthcare systems to leverage the perspectives of those on the frontlines, such as pharmacists, in addressing barriers to healthcare. This research not only expanded my understanding of the cumulative effects of access barriers but also deepened my commitment to fostering equity and accessibility within healthcare.

Results

This section presents the cross-sectional and longitudinal findings of a qualitative study examining barriers to accessing healthcare for people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances, complemented by HCPs’ perspectives. As illustrated in Fig. 2; Table 4, the results are organised around three interconnected themes. The results reveal that access to healthcare is shaped by a dynamic and often fragmented continuum, in which barriers accumulate across multiple stages of the healthcare-seeking journey. It was observed that navigating the continuum, and overcoming each step, is required to secure a single appointment with a healthcare provider, which is illustrated as theme 1 in Fig. 2. For each provider, and therefore for each new continuum, barriers were present not only on the patient’s side but also on the side of the HCP, as illustrated as theme 2 in Fig. 2. Furthermore, participants often needed to consult multiple providers for a single health issue. This meant that once a referral was made, individuals had to re-navigate the continuum to obtain a subsequent appointment. Each additional step in the healthcare process introduces new barriers, compounding the existing burden and further distancing individuals from timely and adequate healthcare. The third theme in Fig. 2 highlights the temporary yet crucial role of social support in overcoming access barriers. Participants with help from family, friends, or community organizations were better able to navigate the system, receiving support with interpretation, appointment scheduling, or financial tasks.

Perpetual care continuum

This study revealed that in a fragmented healthcare system, individuals often need to traverse the continuum multiple times to address a health concern. Respondents did not experience access as part of a cohesive or coordinated system, but rather as a cycle of starting over, repeatedly navigating the steps of recognition, seeking, reaching, paying for, and engaging with care. Addressing one health issue often required contact with multiple healthcare providers, each with their own access pathways. This complexity is illustrated by one participant, a first-generation migrant, who shared their experience:

“Actually, I was looking for a psychologist. They told me that I have, how to say, I must go to the general practitioner and then the general practitioner says that I can go to the psychologist. It feels a bit chaotic to first find a general practitioner and only then look for a psychologist (respondent 5, interview 1)

Secondly, Referrals to other healthcare providers did not ease access but often reactivated this care-seeking cycle. Even after recognizing the need for care, participants frequently had to restart the process at the seeking phase, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Rather than enabling a seamless pathway, referrals introduced new, provider-specific barriers that emerged both on the patient and provider sides.

For many health issues, this meant repeatedly navigating different access routes, compounding existing challenges and reinforcing the fragmentation of care. Each referral added another layer of complexity, as explored further in the cross-sectional findings below.

Provider-specific barriers in access to healthcare

Four subthemes were identified by both groups of participants regarding barriers in access to healthcare: lower priority for health due to competing priorities, overburdened healthcare services, disparities in out-of-pocket costs across healthcare services, and finally language barriers and cultural differences resulting in communication challenges. These subthemes were mapped onto the dimensions of the access-to-care continuum, as illustrated in Fig. 2, theme 2. Linking the findings to this framework provides a more comprehensive understanding of how barriers manifest across the different stages of accessing healthcare.

Lower priority for health due to competing priorities

This theme addresses the third step in the access-to-care continuum, as illustrated in Fig. 2: recognizing a health issue and initiating timely engagement with healthcare services, where two key aspects illustrate this phenomenon: the postponement of healthcare by individuals due to competing priorities, and HCPs’ perspectives on missed appointments. It is important to note that the latter is a provider interpretation rather than a direct report from patients. Together, these subthemes reveal how access to healthcare at this initial stage is shaped by the intersection of lived experience and institutional perceptions.

The postponement of healthcare by individuals due to competing priorities

The analysis revealed that access to healthcare is shaped by a complex intersection of vulnerability factors, including participants’ living environments, social networks, chronic conditions, and cultural beliefs. These factors influenced participants’ capacity to navigate the access-to-care continuum, often leading to delays or missed opportunities for healthcare.

Participants with chronic illnesses, such as cardiovascular disease and asthma, reported difficulties in managing multiple health conditions. For instance, one participant, characterised by social isolation, recent migration, and limited Dutch language proficiency, who was diagnosed with cancer during the study chose to prioritise cancer treatment, while neglecting other chronic conditions. This reflects how the intersection of individual factors can constrain patient agency and influence healthcare prioritization. As she explained,

“Fluid in legs I ignore now. Nothing to do about it. No time. Breast cancer first.” (respondent 1, interview 3)

Those with pressing housing issues and without social support or cohabitants, for example, prioritised securing stable living conditions over healthcare. One participant remarked,

“I really can’t take that on right now, to fix my eyes as well. I got a terrible roofer, so I first have to fix my house problems and then I will have my eyes checked. I can’t have everything in my head at once, step by step.” (respondent 4, interview 2)

Limited financial resources also played a central role in shaping healthcare decisions. Many participants described how limited financial resources forced them to prioritise immediate and recurring expenses related to their children, such as speech therapy or extracurricular activities. As a result, mental healthcare for themselves was often postponed or deprioritised, not because of a lack of need, but due to the difficulty of finding affordable or reimbursed psychological support. One participant, a first-generation migrant living alone and navigating the system with limited Dutch proficiency, illustrated this tension:

“Currently, I have limited income, my son is seeing a speech therapist, my daughter is in music academy, and so on. I have to pay a lot, and I don’t have much income unless I can get extra. I was looking for a reimbursed and cheaper psychologists, but I do not know where to look for one.”(respondent 13, interview 3)

Healthcare providers’ perspectives on missed appointments

Appointment-based providers, such as GPs and psychologists, reported significant difficulties in accommodating patients living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances who frequently missed or arrived late to their scheduled appointments. HCPs noted that in their view these missed appointments often resulted from patients forgetting their commitments, feeling overwhelmed by competing priorities, cultural perceptions or, in some cases, experiencing anxiety.

This issue was perceived as particularly frustrating by GPs, as it impacts their ability to efficiently allocate limited resources and meet the needs of other patients:

“We have a lot of frustration with this because they simply don’t show up for the appointments. We have to measure, it is really hard. For a while, we used a letter system where if patients arrived late, they would get a letter at home. But sometimes I run late too, and people need help, so we stopped that. Now we just talk to the patients and remind them that they need to come on time. But still, It is more than 5 times per day.“(GP3)

Psychologists also expressed frustration about missed appointments, reflecting a complex interplay between structural limitations, patient vulnerability, and the emotional labour of healthcare work. Rather than framing absences as simple non-compliance, some psychologists interpreted these disruptions as symptomatic of broader socio-economic and psychological burdens carried by their clients. They described working with a population for whom attending therapy is often challenging and complex, not only due to scheduling or financial issues, but because of internalised stigma, emotional exhaustion, or what one described as a “negative flow.” In these accounts, the absence of a patient was not only a logistical problem but also an emotionally charged moment, where therapeutic intent collided with systemic and personal constraints. :

“It is a target group that is known for not always showing up for their appointments. That’s what I think, well, that’s purely my interpretation, because we are dealing with a specific group where psychological support is even more of a taboo {…}. Clients are dealing with so many problems that they can’t make it to the appointment, even though that’s exactly the time to talk. But they’re in such a negative flow that they just can’t manage it. They sometimes don’t show up here. Sometimes it’s clear why, sometimes it’s very unclear, so yeah. That does bother me when my sessions last 1 hour and so I can’t help anyone else for 1 hour.” (psychologist 2)

Overburdened healthcare services

This theme reflects the fourth step in the access to care continuum (cfr. Figure 2, step 5): reaching, which refers to the ability of individuals to physically access and obtain services once a need has been identified. In this study, barriers were observed at this stage, predominantly on the side of HCPs. Rather than patient-related challenges, it was the limited availability of HCPs and closed registration for new patients that most often hindered access. These systemic constraints frequently obstructed participants’ efforts to move forward in the healthcare process, underscoring how reaching care is not only dependent on individual initiative but is shaped by provider capacity and structural limitations within the health system.

Results showed a recurrent pattern around the compounded barriers faced by recently arrived migrants attempting to reach primary healthcare services in Belgium. Recently arrived migrants in the study encountered a form of exclusion, shaped by their lack of an existing relationship with a GP and the structural inaccessibility of already overburdened practices. This is exemplified in a participants’ account, who is a recently arrived migrant without social support, describing their attempt to seek healthcare in a neighbourhood clinic. The pattern of being turned away, redirected, or left without guidance reinforces feelings of exclusion and highlights a critical disjuncture between formal pathways to healthcare and the lived realities of those trying to enter the system for the first time:

I had pain for two weeks and in this country you have to go to the general practitioner. I went to the clinic in the street in my neighbourhood. They are full so I could not make an appointment for the family doctor and they said they could not help me because they had too many patients. (Respondent 5, check-in moment 1)

Access to healthcare is increasingly constrained by systemic pressures, which are particularly evident in primary healthcare [43]. It became clear that these pressures were not only acknowledged by patients but also acutely felt by providers themselves. GPs described how rising patient demand and insufficient capacity have forced them to make difficult decisions about who they can accept into their practice. These decisions, while often made out of necessity, contribute to a healthcare environment in which access becomes selectively limited, especially for those without existing ties to a GP. The following reflection illustrates how provider-side limitations reinforce exclusionary dynamics within the system:

“At the moment, we have a patient intake freeze, I simply cannot take on any more patients which is really unfortunate. It was a difficult decision to make, but we realised that we could no longer manage the workload. So, now we are completely unavailable. We are only accessible for a niche group.” (GP 4)

Despite a clear desire for psychological support, many participants expressed hesitation and uncertainty around how to begin the process of accessing a psychologist. This uncertainty was compounded by perceptions of the system as overburdened, where the first steps seemed obscured by systemic complexity and perceived inaccessibility. For example, one participant, a second-generation migrant, expressed doubt about seeking help due to concerns that psychologists might not understand her cultural background. Additionally, she postponed seeking care because of the anticipated long waiting lists for specialised mental health services. This experience underscores how complex and inaccessible mental healthcare can feel for those in need:

“Yes, recently I was thinking about going to a psychologist, I didn’t know how to find the psychologist, so yeah, then I didn’t make an appointment. Not necessarily because of one thing, but because there’s so much change happening in my life, and that can have an impact. Maybe a psychologist, but I’m afraid to take that step because I think, well, all the waiting time for a dermatologist, let alone for a psychologist. I hear everywhere that it’s even worse in mental health. " (respondent 7, interview 2)

Within this theme, dental care access is as a recurring source of frustration and distress. Analysis revealed that prolonged waiting times, combined with the lasting financial strain from previous treatments, formed a persistent barrier, especially for those relying on public or low-cost services. These structural obstacles often led to delayed or forgone care and had a clear emotional and practical impact on participants’ lives:

“I have problems with the dentist, my tooth is broken. I can’t do anything. Even though I made an appointment, it’s in 4 months, that’s always the problem.” {…} “Last time, I also made an appointment for my daughter for her wisdom teeth, and I had to wait 5 months and then I have to pay so much money. I need to see a dentist, but I am unable to find one that I can afford.” (Respondent 13, Interview 3)

Both dentists and psychologists openly acknowledged the emotional and professional strain that came with their work, particularly in relation to managing new patient requests. Their reflections highlighted how structural shortages and personal burnout intersect to limit access, not due to unwillingness, but due to unsustainable working conditions. This was expressed by a dentist who shared:

“I have had a burnout before because I had too many patients and had to work too hard. I know that there is a shortage of dentists, but I almost have to hide because I’ve been overwhelmed by it before.” (Dentist 1).

Similarly, a psychologist reflected on the financial and structural barriers facing clients:

“I know, the affordable psychologists are fully booked, and the private psychologist, which is me, charges too much. Many people simply can’t afford it, so they don’t come. Yeah, it is too difficult to find an affordable psychologist” (Psychologist 2)

In contrast, pharmacists indicated that they are accessible throughout the day and do not require appointments, allowing patients to walk in at any time with their questions. The accessibility highlights the supportive role that pharmacists play in the healthcare system. As one pharmacist noted:

“The pharmacy is very approachable of course, they can walk in here all day without an appointment and then we help them where necessary.” (pharmacist 2)

Disparities in out-of-pocket costs across healthcare services

Financial barriers, representing the affordability step of the access-to-care continuum, influenced participants’ ability to access healthcare services, although the severity of these barriers varied. These financial barriers are shaped both by the high out-of-pocket costs inherent to the healthcare system and by participants’ limited resources. Some participants were entirely unable to afford any healthcare services due to a lack of financial resources, while others had to delay seeking medical care because they did not have sufficient means at the time. The financial burden was particularly evident regarding specific HCPs, including dental care, mental health services, and obtaining medicines. Although financial barriers were not present in every healthcare interaction, they became more pronounced in situations requiring out-of-pocket payments.

General practice experienced as affordable

Financial barriers were less present when accessing GPs. Participants who managed to find a GP accepting new patients generally did not report significant financial barriers, as the consultation fee was relatively low for those eligible for increased reimbursement [44]. One participant remarked:

“For people with increased reimbursement, the cost is quite low. In the past, I switched GPs because mine charged 5 euros and did not accept my increased reimbursement. Now, I only have to pay 1 euro for my general practitioner, and that works well.” (respondent 2, interview 1)

The GPs in the study worked in an underserved area where individuals often faced financial constraints. Consultations at the GP practice typically cost 1 euro for people with a low socio-economic background, which is a relatively low fee for a consultation. Participants generally did not perceive this as a significant financial barrier. Furthermore, GPs reported that they were willing to waive this fee if they observed that a patient was unable to pay this cost, demonstrating flexibility in addressing financial challenges. As one GP noted:

“Even though it’s only 1 euro here, they don’t bring it and yes then I’ll leave it like that. I’m not going to be a burden for just 1 euro.” (GP5)

Affordability challenges in dental, mental health and pharmaceutical care

Dental care in particular was perceived as inaccessible by participants due to high out-of-pocket costs. Moreover, dental care was often considered a lower priority compared to essential needs such as food, housing, and childcare. One participant, who is a first-generation migrant, and lives alone shared how their financial situation forced them to postpone treatment, highlighting the extent to which dental care was deprioritised:

“Yes, if it’s too expensive, I don’t pay. I have tooth pain, but it costs too much right now—I don’t have enough money. I should normally get dental insurance through my health fund, maybe when I start working, I might be able to go to a dentist. Not yet, not enough money.“ (respondent 10, interview 3)

Dentists reflected on the ethical tension they experienced when encountering patients with extensive dental needs but limited financial means. These accounts revealed how providers actively adapted their healthcare offerings, limiting them to reimbursed treatments, not purely based on clinical judgment, but in response to their interpretation of what was financially feasible for the patient. In this way, financial barriers were shaped both by the limitations of the healthcare system’s reimbursement policies and by how providers adapted their care based on patients’ financial situations.:

“Sometimes, I see patients with very poor dental health, and I could perform many treatments to improve it. But I know they have little money, so I only offer treatments that are reimbursed to ensure they can afford them, because the others are not reimbursed and just too expensive for my patients.” (dentist 1)

Similarly, the prohibitive cost of private mental health services created a significant barrier for participants, especially those with a first-generation migration background, seeking psychological support. Many described struggling to afford sessions that typically cost around €60, which compounded their distress and limited access to ongoing care:

“Yes, ugh, I can’t afford to pay for a psychologist because the sessions are every three months, which is a bit difficult for me to pay " (Respondent 13, interview 3)

Psychologists also acknowledged the high cost of their services and the difficulties faced by patients with limited financial resources. In Belgium, private psychologists typically charge around €60 per hour, which is often unaffordable for many patients. While there are conventional psychologists who offer services at a reduced fee of approximately €11 per hour [45], these professionals are less accessible due to an overburdened health system and long waiting lists. This highlights the broader systemic problem of insufficient affordable mental health services for patients, especially for people living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances due to the lack of resources. One psychologist commented on the issue, stating:

“Yes, we are too expensive, and I know the inexpensive psychologists don’t have enough places.”(psychologist 1)

In addition to dental and mental health services, financial challenges also extended to obtaining medication. While many chronic illness medications are reimbursed in Belgium, essential drugs such as painkillers and medications for bladder and prostate issues are often not covered. Participants frequently reported forgoing these medications due to their costs. The financial burden reflects an interplay between systemic factors, such as limited reimbursement coverage for some medicines, and patients’ limited ability to pay the costs, as illustrated by one participant who is living alone, with a first-generation migration background and language barrier in Dutch language and who expressed her struggle with affording chronic medicines and a common painkiller, stating:

“Those pills for my legs are expensive, 50 or 60 euros. Sometimes for sleep too, they’re 12 euros, I can’t buy those either. Dafalgan is also expensive, the health insurance doesn’t cover it. A small box of Dafalgan is 12 euros, and the health insurance doesn’t cover it.” (respondent 1, interview 1)

These findings underscore the effect of financial barriers on healthcare access, particularly for medications and services not covered by public health insurance.

Language barriers and cultural differences resulting in communication challenges

This theme explores how language barriers and cultural differences shape participants’ experiences at multiple points in the healthcare access continuum, both during the initial healthcare-seeking phase and after reaching healthcare providers. These challenges reflect a complex mismatch between patients’ communication needs and cultural backgrounds, and the healthcare system’s capacity to effectively respond.

From the patient perspective

Notably, respondents with a migration background articulated unfamiliarity with the gatekeeping role of GPs, reflecting distinct healthcare-seeking behaviours shaped by their cultural contexts. These participants described a healthcare landscape markedly different from their countries of origin, where hospital-based care was the norm for all health concerns. This cultural mismatch is closely linked to the healthcare-seeking step in the access-to-care continuum, which encompasses individuals’ ability to recognise health needs and navigate the appropriate entry points into the healthcare system. One participant illustrated this contrast:

" Apparently you work with a general practitioner here. I have to search for a general practitioner here and then find a pharmacist to get medication. In Palestine, we would just go to the hospital and receive medication right there.“(respondent 5, interview 1)

Additionally, some participants expressed hesitation in seeking mental health services, fearing that psychologists might not understand their cultural or religious beliefs, as one participant, who lives in a great family and is second-generation migrant stated:

“I mainly wonder if my psychologist will understand my world. I have a Moroccan background, and I don’t know, it’s something new for me. Going to a psychologist, especially knowing that the psychologist doesn’t really understand my world, that’s a different culture. I have not yet sought help.”(Respondent 7, Interview 1)

Moreover, participants expressed hesitation toward mental health services, underscoring concerns that psychologists might not appreciate or understand their cultural and religious backgrounds. One participant, who is first-generation migrant, lives alone and experience language barrier in Dutch language reflects this tension:

“I don’t understand him. He is saying something, but I do not understand it.” (respondent 1, interview 2)

Scepticism toward conventional pharmaceuticals became evident, with some participants favouring herbal remedies rooted in traditional practices. This distrust contributed to delays in seeking formal healthcare, thereby complicating health outcomes. One participant expressed this perspective: :

“We never go to a doctor because we don’t need them. We have the herbs.” (respondent 14, interview 2)

From a healthcare provider’ perspective

The analysis revealed that HCPs experienced cultural and language barriers, similar to those reported by patients in the study. These barriers were particularly evident when there was a cultural mismatch between the patient and the HCP.

Several HCPs expressed difficulty in understanding the values and norms of patients from different cultural backgrounds, which often led to challenges during consultations. For instance, psychologists mentioned how cultural differences, such as varying perceptions of eye contact, could affect the therapeutic relationship:

“The cultural difference is huge sometimes, even just how other cultures perceive it when you look into their eyes. Some cultures consider that rude, but with us it’s a sign of politeness. Yes if you don’t know that, then your sensitive conversation does become difficult.” (psychologist 3)

GPs also highlighted language barriers as a challenge in delivering effective healthcare, as the lack of a shared language hinders communication with patients. This limitation prevents them from asking necessary questions or adequately addressing patients’ concerns, ultimately obstructing the resolution of health issues. To mitigate this, GPs often request patients to involve a bilingual individual from their informal network, which notably improves communication.

“Yes, so a language barrier is indeed the most common problem during a consultation and sometimes that is indeed annoying, and you still don’t get it right or it is frustrating and then we say: yes okay, next time you should bring someone to interpret with you.” (GP 10)

However, attendance of a Dutch-speaking relative or family member might also hinder consultations as exemplified by the following statement on a psychological consultation with Afghan patients. In the end, the discussions escalated, forcing the psychologists to break off the consultation.

“She felt bad about the Taliban and wanted to talk to the psychologist about it because it affected her well-being. However, when we were discussing the Taliban, a translating family member disrupted our conversation.” (psychologist 3)

Pharmacists reported a substantial demand for antibiotics for physical health issues in their pharmacies. Respondents from Sub-Saharan African countries indicated that a prescription for antibiotics was not required in their country of origin to the pharmacists. These respondents shared that when becoming ill in Belgium, they went to the pharmacy for antibiotics without prescription, not understanding the Belgian policy on antibiotics.

“Yes, abroad you get antibiotics without a prescription. In their culture, it is normal to take antibiotics when they are a bit sick. So, there are always a lot of discussions, at least once a week. People who think they know better, for a cold, it’s amoxicillin, why not? They then really can’t understand that they can’t just get that here” (pharmacist 2)

The role of informal social networks or healthcare providers and societal organisations in overcoming barriers temporarily

The third theme highlighted the critical role of social support in mitigating access barriers, at least temporarily. Participants with strong informal healthcare networks or support from societal organizations were better equipped to navigate the healthcare system and access healthcare in a timely manner. Family members, friends, and community organizations often provided essential assistance, such as interpreting services, scheduling medical appointments, or facilitating financial transactions.

These forms of support influenced specific dimensions of the access-to-care continuum, as illustrated Fig. 2, theme 3: they enhanced the ability to seek by encouraging recognition of health needs and appropriate responses; improved the ability to reach by providing practical guidance in navigating the system and arranging appointments; and strengthened the ability to pay by helping with upfront or out-of-pocket payments. These findings underscore the importance of social networks and informal support systems in complementing formal healthcare services, particularly for individuals facing structural barriers to healthcare.

Support from formal institutions

The recently arrived migrants in the study could connect with a public centre for social services and welfare (PCSW), which facilitated their immediate referral to a GP. These social services are aware of GPs’ availabilities. Due to the extended waiting times for GPs in Antwerp, this connection with social services ensured they did not have to look for a GP themselves. Additionally, PCSW are responsible for covering the costs of healthcare, relieving migrants of the financial burden of healthcare.

“Yes, the PCSW pays for everything for the doctor and medicines.” (respondent 13, interview 3)

“The PCSW has now assigned me a general practitioner that I can go to, and that is now my general practitioner.” (respondent 14, interview 3)

Certain specialised medical services’ costs are covered by the public health insurance and often require upfront payments, presenting a challenge for individuals facing financial difficulties. To address this issue, respondents with limited resources reported seeking assistance from the hospital’s social services to arrange installment payment plans, providing a temporary solution to their financial difficulties.

“I went to the social services, and now I am allowed to pay for the ambulance monthly. That’s better; otherwise, I wouldn’t have been able to afford it.” (respondent 1, interview 3)

Financial pressures and long waiting times lay a pressure on the patients to navigate the healthcare system. In cases, proactive support from HCPs can alleviate these challenges. One participant, for example, described how their psychologist arranged an appointment with a new therapist when they relocated to another city:

“My psychologist from Antwerp, she is really nice, she called me for what is happening. And I told her the situation with psychologist here and she told me that she will fix a new psychologist here for me in Hasselt.” (respondent 5, interview 3)

In some cases, proactive support from a GP can mitigate the challenge of the repeated access-to-care navigation, as illustrated by one participant who described their GP arranging an oncologist appointment following a cancer diagnosis:

“I was lucky with my general practitioner; he made an appointment with the oncologist, and then I just went to the oncologist.” (respondent 4, interview 3)

Support from informal social network

While formal healthcare systems aim to provide equitable access, many individuals rely on informal social networks—such as family or friends—to overcome these barriers. These networks can provide critical support, such as financial assistance, transportation, or guidance in navigating complex systems. However, this reliance on informal networks is often coincidental rather than systematic, leaving individuals without such support at a distinct disadvantage.

One participant indicated having a family member in the healthcare field. When he became ill or had skin problems, he sought advice from his mother regarding the initial steps to take. Consequently, he had already navigated the first step of the access-to-care continuum, as he received guidance on the appropriate next steps.

“I always first ask my mom what I should do, because she is a nurse, so she knows what I need to do. I once had a rash, so I went to the general practitioner because my mom said I should have it checked.” (respondent 8, interview 2)

Language and digital barriers prevented two respondents from making appointments with HCPs. These respondents stated that they lacked digital access and received help from their daughters to make appointments.

“I can’t work with a mobile phone and can’t make appointments. My daughter helps me make appointments.” (respondent 1, interview 1)

Respondents mentioned the challenges in affording healthcare bills, particularly for services such as dental care. Those with an informal social network relied on financial support from family and friends, which allowed them to repay the amount in smaller instalments. Without this assistance, they noted that paying the bill themselves would have left them without sufficient money for other basic necessities.

“I asked to pay monthly, but that’s not allowed because I have too low an income. My daughter pays for it, and I pay her back monthly. My friend also helped me a bit. We always help each other very well.” (respondent 3, interview 3)

Respondents who sought a GP because they did not yet have access to a regular GP received assistance from friends and family in finding and gaining access to a GP. As one respondent stated:

“My friend here in Antwerp told me that I should go to that general practitioner.” (respondent 10, interview 2)

Discussion

This study provides a longitudinal, qualitative understanding of barriers to healthcare access among individuals living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances. By incorporating perspectives from both patients and HCPs, the study offers a comprehensive view of the challenges in access to healthcare. The use of both cross-sectional and longitudinal methods reveals how barriers accumulate over time. The findings reveal that healthcare access is not a linear process, but an ongoing process characterised by accumulating barriers at each stage and both sides of the access-to-care continuum. The continuum was observed as a process of navigating toward a concrete appointment with a HCP. Along this path, barriers frequently emerged that were specific experienced to individual HCPs. Moreover, when participants received a referral from one HCP to another, the continuum had to be re-navigated entirely, introducing a new set of provider-specific challenges. This reflects what the literature terms “high-agency” pathways, which require individuals to independently recognise health needs and navigate the healthcare system. These pathways rely heavily on cognitive, social, and material resources, that are not equally available across socio-economic groups. Consequently, individuals in vulnerable circumstances are often less able to manage this process, leading to delays or missed healthcare [46].

These provider-specific barriers were observed on both sides of the continuum On the patient side, competing priorities such as housing instability, caregiving responsibilities, and financial insecurity often deprioritised healthcare-seeking. These barriers were particularly pronounced for mental healthcare, dental services, and obtaining medication, due to both high out-of-pocket costs from the healthcare service and insufficient income of the patient. World health organisation (WHO) data reinforce this, showing that nearly 260,000 Belgian households faced catastrophic health spending, disproportionately affecting low-income groups [47].

In addition to financial barriers, participants frequently encountered limited availability of specific HCPS, such as GPs, dentists and psychologists. A constraint rooted more in the organization and capacity of the healthcare system than in individual ability. Belgium faces a shortage of GPs, with a substantial portion nearing retirement and many already unable to accept new patients [48]. This shortage is reflected in real-world experiences, doctors of the world, based in Antwerp highlights a rising number of people attending weekly walk-in sessions who because they cannot find a GP or psychologist due to saturation of primary care services [49]. Similarly, Belgium is also experiencing a shortage of dentists [50], which compounds barriers to care. Individuals eligible for increased health insurance reimbursements, an indicator of low socioeconomic status, tend to visit the dentist less frequently [51]. This aligns with the study’s findings, indicating that economically vulnerable populations not only face financial barriers to dental care but are also impacted by limited provider availability. The combination of high out-of-pocket costs [52], and reduced access to dental professionals creates an accumulation of barriers, disrupting the continuity of care and exacerbating oral health inequalities.

Cultural differences and language barriers were identified as obstacles affecting both patients and HCPs along the access-to-care continuum. Patients often feared being misunderstood due to language or cultural differences, leading to hesitation in seeking healthcare. On the provider side, language barriers and differing cultural norms around healthcare use complicated communication and understanding, often resulting in frustration and less effective care. Recent research confirms that language discordance increases medical errors, lowers treatment adherence, and heightens patient anxiety [53]. Similarly, as a 2020 study by Chauhan et al. [54] underscores that cultural barriers and communication challenges can significantly impact the quality and safety of healthcare for patients from ethnic minority backgrounds. These mismatches often contribute to feelings of exclusion and mistrust, particularly among individuals living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances, further discouraging them from seeking timely and appropriate healthcare.

These barriers intersect and amplify each other, making it even harder to navigate an already complex healthcare system. For individuals facing multiple vulnerabilities [55], competing priorities, such as securing housing, food, or caregiving responsibilities, often push healthcare down the list of immediate needs. A 2025 study, conducted in Ireland by Larkin et al. found that over one-third of people with multiple chronic conditions reported cost-related non-adherence, such as skipping appointments or medications due to financial constraints. In such cases, tighter budgets force individuals to prioritise some health concerns over others, often neglecting care perceived as less urgent [56]. This reinforces a cycle of delay and worsening health. Existing literature supports these findings, emphasizing how financial pressures and social instability contribute to delayed healthcare-seeking and poorer outcomes in socio-economically vulnerable populations [57].

The cumulative nature of these barriers mirrors the findings of a 2017 study from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, which describe health inequities as dynamic and sustained by systemic differences in healthcare opportunities [6]. Similarly, 2020 studies in Health Affairs emphasise the interconnected nature of these inequities, highlighting how systemic inefficiencies perpetuate disparities [58].

In this study, some participants relied on informal support networks, such as family or friends, for financial help, appointment scheduling, or navigating the fragmented healthcare system. This aligns with existing research highlighting the role of social networks in facilitating access to care, particularly for vulnerable populations [59]. However, this support is often incidental and unevenly distributed, leaving those without strong social network at a disadvantage. While informal networks can offer short-term relief, they are insufficient to address the broader structural barriers to healthcare access. Studies suggest that to be effective long-term, such networks must be supported and complemented by formal community outreach services [60].

Applying an intersectional lens, as outlined by MacGregor et al. [61] reveals how overlapping social identities and structural disadvantages, such as migration status, socio-economic position, and social isolation, interact to shape healthcare experiences. First-generation migrants faced compounded challenges, including difficulty finding a regular GP and navigating an unfamiliar health system. Participants without social networks reported even greater difficulty accessing care, echoing findings that migrants and socially isolated individuals face intensified language and system-navigation barriers [62]. Understanding these intersecting vulnerabilities is critical for developing targeted interventions that improve healthcare access among socio-economically disadvantaged and migrant populations.

The fragmented healthcare system, with its reliance on referrals, often introduces new barriers at each step, disrupting continuity of care—especially for those in socio-economically vulnerable situations who need care the most. These repeated disruptions make navigation harder and delay timely treatment. To address this, there is a clear need for culturally competent, community-based programs that provide direct links to services and reduce fragmentation [63]. Future research and policy should focus on developing and scaling such integrated approaches to promote equitable healthcare access and reduce persistent inequalities.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides an in-depth understanding of barriers to healthcare access by integrating the perspectives of both HCPs and individuals living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances. By including these two groups, the study bridges the gap between healthcare seekers’ lived experiences and HCPs’ perspectives, offering a more comprehensive view of access barriers.

A key strength of this study is its longitudinal approach, particularly for participants from socio-economically vulnerable backgrounds. Conducting multiple interviews over time helped build trust between researchers and respondents, leading to richer and more nuanced data on healthcare access barriers. This aligns with existing literature about collecting data with hard-to-reach groups by Chamberlain et al. [64]. By capturing changes over time, this approach minimises the risk of presenting a static, one-time perspective and instead offers a more dynamic understanding of evolving barriers.

Additionally, the study benefits from the diversity of its participants, encompassing a broad range of socio-economic vulnerabilities. This diversity provides valuable insights into how intersecting disadvantages shape individuals’ experiences with healthcare access. However, a limitation is that while the study captures a variety of perspectives, it does not include repeated accounts from individuals facing similar socio-economic circumstances. As a result, the findings reflect a wide spectrum of experiences but do not track how barriers develop for individuals in comparable situations over time. Further research should focus on a specific target population or disease to obtain more detailed insight into the longitudinal effect on barriers in access to care.

Language barriers with some participants present a notable limitation, as they may have led to missed nuances and hindered the depth of certain interviews. In a few cases, digital translation tools such as Google Translate were used to support basic understanding. While these tools facilitated communication, they are not without flaws and may have introduced interpretive inaccuracies. As such, the richness of the data and the precision of thematic interpretation, particularly regarding complex or sensitive healthcare experiences, may have been affected.

Finally, the study’s focus on a specific area of Antwerp might limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings. Comparative research in different geographical or healthcare contexts could provide further insights into how barriers manifest across various environments, thereby enhancing the broader applicability of the results.

Conclusion

This study provides an in-depth, and longitudinal understanding of the barriers to healthcare access among individuals living in socio-economically vulnerable circumstances, enriched by insights from HCPs. The findings reveal that accessing healthcare is a fragmented and dynamic process, shaped by a continuum in which barriers accumulate at every stage. Navigating toward a single appointment to a HCP requires overcoming provider-specific and patient-side challenges, each referral effectively resetting the process and introducing new obstacles. This fragmentation and repetition disproportionately burden those with the fewest resources, delaying or disrupting access to timely and appropriate care.

Barriers such as financial difficulties, competing life priorities, limited provider availability, language barriers, and cultural mismatches intersect to create a cycle of exclusion from healthcare.

While informal social networks or community organizations offer vital support, their role remains temporary and unevenly accessible.

Ultimately, these findings underscore the need for structural interventions that go beyond individual resilience. To reduce health inequities, policymakers and future research should prioritise culturally competent care, integrated community outreach, and streamlined care pathways that minimise repeated navigation and ensure continuity. Addressing these systemic shortcomings is essential for creating a more equitable and accessible healthcare system.
