Spaceborne synthetic aperture passive imaging technology for wide-area electromagnetic spectrum map generation
Authors: Shunhu Hou, Youchen Fan, Yangyang Wang, Wei Chen, Shengliang Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCurrent space-based electromagnetic spectrum sensing and imaging methods suffer from several limitations, including low efficiency in acquiring holistic situational information of radiation source clusters, inherent contradiction between field of view (FOV) and resolution, and insufficient real-time imaging capability. To address these challenges, this paper innovatively applies the synthetic aperture passive imaging method, originally used in the field of “natural thermal radiation remote sensing,” to the domain of spaceborne electromagnetic spectrum sensing, establishing a system model encompassing “ground station modeling - space propagation - spaceborne synthetic aperture passive imaging,” and analyzing the differences between the ground station signal and natural thermal radiation during model establishment and synthetic aperture imaging processes in detail. We adopt a staggered Y-shaped synthetic aperture and define its placement and imaging perspective when deployed on a satellite platform. The experiments establish a dynamic simulation scene achieving a high-precision, wide-area, real-time rapid imaging of ground station electromagnetic situation.Subject areas: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

The explosive growth of global electromagnetic devices and wireless communication equipment has led to increasingly complex electromagnetic environments.1,2 Currently, electromagnetic spectrum map generation primarily relies on ground-based platforms, which are constrained by line-of-sight limitations and cannot meet the demands for wide-swath coverage.3,4 Spaceborne sensing platforms have emerged as an ideal solution for wide-area spectrum mapping due to their wide-swath coverage, superior sensing capability, and high-altitude perspective advantage.4,5

Existing space-based sensing and mapping technologies mainly fall into three categories: optical imaging6,7,8,9; radar imaging (such as synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imaging10 that actively transmits signals and receives target echoes); and passive sensing technologies (including conventional space-based electronic sensing methods such as TDOA/FDOA,11 DOA estimation,12 and spaceborne sensor-based sensing,13 as well as passive imaging techniques such as passive detectors14,15).

In the context of large-scale complex ground station distributions observed from space platforms, these space-based sensing and imaging technologies demonstrate different applicability. For optical imaging technologies that primarily focus on detailed features of terrestrial objects with limited FOV, they typically serve as auxiliary tools for fine analysis after detecting anomalous regions in spaceborne wide-area applications. Radar imaging predominantly employs active methods, relying on transmitted signals to obtain target information, which imposes higher power requirements and risks revealing the observer. SAR, in particular, achieves high spatial resolution by synthesizing a virtual large aperture through platform motion, which, to some extent, constrains real-time capability.16 Therefore, developing passive sensing technologies with high real-time performance and a wide FOV is especially critical.

Among passive sensing technologies, traditional space-based electronic sensing methods mainly focus on the electromagnetic characteristics of individual ground stations, analyzing parameters, locations, information, and carrier attributes of electromagnetic radiation signals in a progressive manner from individual sources to clusters and then to overall electromagnetic spectrum situations. This approach shows deficiencies in acquiring and characterizing spectrum maps of radiation source clusters.17,18 Furthermore, spaceborne sensor-based sensing methods resemble traditional electromagnetic spectrum mapping approaches that require point-by-point sampling at each location and extensive time accumulation with post-processing to achieve full coverage.19 The limitation of this method lies in its slow imaging speed, as prolonged sampling and accumulation lead to sluggish response and low efficiency, making it ineffective in coping with changes in dynamic targets.

Synthetic aperture passive interferometric imaging, as a passive sensing and imaging technology, was initially applied in radio astronomy observations and has recently been extensively explored by researchers in geographical and oceanographic remote sensing.20,21,22,23,24 This method represents a forward-looking solution for wide-area electromagnetic spectrum mapping, the main advantages include: (1) high-precision imaging capability. It achieves direct imaging of a high-resolution, high-detection-rate electromagnetic spectrum situation containing richer spatial information. This technology utilizes passive detection through multiple antenna cells and achieves electromagnetic spectrum high-sensitivity imaging through interferometry, revolutionizing traditional single-point sampling and direction-finding (DF) methods. The existing DF and positioning technologies can achieve good accuracy (such as time-frequency difference localization,25 spatial spectrum estimation;26 scanning receivers;27 interferometers;28 and Doppler direction finding29), but DF technologies only provide radiation source bearing information, synthetic aperture passive imaging can construct “images” of radiation sources, directly imaging their spatial structures and distribution patterns to obtain more comprehensive spatial information than simple direction finding. In other words, DF and mapping are two separate systems. Beyond localization tasks, the electromagnetic maps generated in this study also possess multiple functions, including regional energy characterization, anomaly detection, and situation assessment; (2) wide coverage. This technology combines long and short baselines to achieve a wide FOV to some extent while realizing high resolution (short baselines capture low spatial frequencies (large-scale structures), whereas long baselines capture high spatial frequencies (fine details))30; (3) Fast imaging speed and real-time performance. Compared to current common electromagnetic spectrum mapping methods, synthetic aperture passive imaging technology offers superior real-time performance. Traditional electromagnetic spectrum maps are typically based on offline processing or periodic updates, relying on prolonged data accumulation and post-processing that cannot meet real-time electromagnetic spectrum mapping requirements with slow imaging speed. In summary, with its high-precision imaging capability, balanced high-resolution and wide-swath performance, and real-time electromagnetic spectrum mapping advantages, synthetic aperture passive interferometric imaging technology demonstrates excellent application prospects in the field of wide-area ground stations electromagnetic spectrum map generation.

To address the deficiencies of current space-based electromagnetic spectrum sensing and mapping technologies—low efficiency in acquiring holistic situational information of radiation source clusters, difficulty in balancing FOV with resolution, and insufficient real-time situation generation capability- this paper proposes a spaceborne synthetic aperture passive imaging technology for wide-area electromagnetic spectrum map generation. The main contributions are as follows.

Emphasis is placed on analyzing the ground stations modeling (in contrast to traditional extended seawater sources), the differences in path loss calculation between directional loss for point sources and area-averaging methods for extended sources during space transmission, as well as the disparities in reception mechanisms and power magnitudes between spontaneous radiation and artificial electromagnetic signals.

Since high-power ground radiation source targets differ from sea area targets, ground radiation sources lack areal characteristics and are typically treated as point sources, making them unsuitable for path loss evaluation using Equation 10. In the context of target radiation detection, this manifests as a degradation in the system’s target measurable extent with increasing detection range. Specifically for sea surface brightness temperature, since the synthetic aperture resolution cell is much smaller than the ocean scene, the brightness temperature within the resolution cell can be considered uniformly around the theoretical 300 K (equivalent to the integrated brightness temperature of the sea within the geographic area corresponding to the entire resolution cell). Therefore, after integration and averaging, the retrieved brightness temperature remains approximately 300 K. When the resolution cell contains not only 300 K sea water but also land scenes or other targets with deviating temperatures, the retrieved brightness temperature within the resolution cell will deviate from the theoretical equivalent temperature of the sea. Additionally, scenarios where only a small portion of the area corresponding to an angular resolution cell contains background radiation must be considered, comprehensively analyzing the difference between the equivalent brightness temperature of ground radiation point sources and the brightness temperature of sea areas.

In summary, within synthetic aperture radiometric systems, the approach in this paper for processing within a single resolution cell depends on the target characteristics: (1) For high-power ground station point targets, during analysis, we treat them as point sources, calculating the received signal power per unit solid angle in the actual direction after free-space attenuation. Based on the conversion relationship between brightness temperature and power (Equation 8), the final point source brightness temperature displayed in the image reflects the radiation contribution from that direction within the entire synthetic aperture field of view (FOV) corresponding to the resolution cell. (2) For sea water or terrestrial background areas: using the solid angle modeling approach of Equation 10, the brightness temperature of the sea area received is calculated based on the ratio of the actual area of the sea region to the actual geographic area corresponding to the resolution cell beam. If the sea area fully matches the resolution cell, the received brightness temperature is the actual average brightness temperature of that area.

Ground station modeling - space propagation - spaceborne synthetic aperture passive imaging

Under the high-altitude viewing conditions of spaceborne platforms, ground stations can be treated as point sources. To more realistically reproduce actual scenarios, the following modeling approach is adopted for radiation source transmitting equipment: The antenna type is assumed to be randomly selected from omnidirectional antennas, horn antennas, rectangular aperture antennas, and so forth; the main beam pointing direction is randomized with azimuth angles varying randomly between 0° and 360° and elevation angles between 0° and 90°; antenna gain values are randomly distributed within the 10 dB–50 dB range; and main beam widths are randomly selected from 10°–120°. This modeling approach accurately reflects the diverse antenna configuration characteristics that may be encountered in practical applications.

Based on the Fourier inversion imaging principle, the system performs a weighted accumulation of the complex correlation outputs from each antenna pair, collectively forming a response to a specific spatial direction. Specifically, each pair of interferometric measurements contributes to the visibility function for that direction, and the accumulation of all baseline pairs is equivalent to performing multiple samplings and weighted averaging of the same spatial frequency component. Under the requirements of spaceborne rapid response and real-time imaging, the computational speed and resource utilization of algorithms are critical. Compared to G-matrix and regularized retrieval algorithms, the fast Fourier retrieval algorithm significantly reduces computational load. This feature enables it to operate more efficiently in resource-constrained spaceborne systems, avoiding excessive system burden caused by heavy computations and high memory demands.

Configuration design and imaging perspective analysis

In space electromagnetic sensing, the selection and design of the load are of great significance. The staggered Y-shaped array is a novel array form adopted by GeoSTAR. It features an equal number of elements on each arm, with no central element at the array’s geometric center. The three arms are staggered counterclockwise, and the innermost three elements form an equilateral triangle. Compared to a sparse Y-array, this configuration slightly compromises spatial-frequency domain sampling coverage but significantly simplifies mechanical and electrical design.42

This paper presents a 36-element staggered Y-shaped synthetic aperture array layout (Figure 5A), with 12 elements per arm spaced at one-λ intervals. The corresponding UV sampling coverage is shown in Figure 5B. It exhibits hexagonal sampling, resulting in an alias-free field-of-view (FOV) resembling a hexagonal region. The antenna cells of the synthetic aperture can be selected based on frequency band requirements. For example, omnidirectional antennas can cover VHF, UHF, and L-bands; horn antennas are suitable for Ku- and Ka-bands; phased arrays may operate at L-, S-, and C-bands; parabolic reflector antennas can handle S-, C-, and X-bands. This study utilizes omnidirectional antenna elements operating in the L-band with a center frequency of 2 GHz. Detailed system parameters are listed in Table 1.

The synthetic aperture achieves a certain degree of balance between wide FOV and high resolution by combining long and short baselines. The short baseline captures low spatial frequencies (large-scale structures), the shortest baseline determines the limit of the non-aliasing FOV, while the long baseline captures high spatial frequencies (fine details), and the maximum baseline determines the achievable spatial resolution. The size of the effective FOV is limited by the main beam width of the array element antenna and the short baseline. In this paper, an omnidirectional unit is used, and the main beam width of the antenna is very wide, so the imaging FOV range formed by the hexagon is mainly affected by the short baseline. The angular resolution of the synthetic aperture is defined as the 3-dB beamwidth of its interferometric array pattern. The 3-dB beamwidth of the array is given by Han et al.43 For our designed array, the angular resolution at the sub-satellite point is approximately 1.728°.

The following analysis examines the placement and imaging perspective considerations when deploying such arrays on spaceborne platforms, based on Y-array imaging principles.

To ensure intuitive observation of radiation source distribution in the nadir region, the spatial azimuth of spectral mapping should align with actual source locations. The North-East-Down (NED) coordinate system - widely adopted for spaceborne platforms - proves particularly suitable for attitude control, sensor pointing, and local environmental awareness applications.44 This paper employs the NED system with specific orientation (see Figure 5C): One arm of the Y-array aligns with the X axis pointing to true north, the Y axis points east, and the z axis points downward (perpendicular to the XY plane). This configuration maintains constant alignment between one array arm and the local meridian direction throughout satellite operations.

Consider two incoming signals from (0°, 0°) and (15°, 15°), representing azimuth angles of 0° and 15° and elevation angles of 0° and 15°, respectively. In the Cartesian coordinate system of traditional microwave radiometer imaging, the result is shown in Figure 5D. The horizontal and vertical axes correspond to ξ = sinθr cosϕr and η = sinθr sinϕr, respectively, which represent the direction cosines in 3D space, where θᵣ is the elevation angle, and ϕᵣ is the azimuth angle. The hexagonal image contains two bright point-source regions. The full image covers an azimuth range of 0°–360°, corresponding to a 360° counterclockwise rotation starting from the positive X-axis. The distance of the bright point-source regions from the center represents the elevation angle. In the raw image formed directly by the synthetic aperture passive interferometric imaging principle, the central region corresponds to the nadir direction, with the positive X-axis pointing east and the positive Y axis pointing north. However, this is not our desired imaging perspective. Our goal is for the image in the NED coordinate system to directly reflect true geographic orientation (i.e., north in the image corresponds to true geographic north, and east in the image corresponds to true geographic east). Therefore, an imaging perspective and coordinate axis transformation is required to align the X-axis with true north (consistent with one arm of the Y-array) and the Y axis with true east. Only then can the spectral distribution in the image directly represent the true spatial distribution of radiation sources. Through analysis and derivation, the imaging perspective and coordinate axes can be transformed as follows: First, perform a vertical flip about the X-axis as the axis of symmetry; then rotate the entire image counterclockwise by 90°. In Figure 5E, the transformed image covers an azimuth range of 0°–360°, corresponding to angles measured clockwise from the positive X-axis, while the distance of bright spots from the center still represents the magnitude of the elevation angle. The X-axis points to true north, and the Y axis points to true east. The transformed image thus achieves direct correspondence with ground radiation source distribution while maintaining good consistency between the imaged data and actual spatial source locations.

Experiments and discussion

Dynamic simulation scenario construction for LEO satellite synthetic aperture passive imaging

Based on the system model and the designed array, we establish a low-orbit satellite dynamic simulation scenario for the performance verification of the “staring imaging” rapid electromagnetic spectrum situation generation.

The simulation adopts the following reasonable assumptions: (1) The propagation medium remains uniform; (2) The radiation field remains relatively stable with ergodic characteristics; (3) The polarization of radiation sources matches the antennas; (4) The influence of terrestrial/sea spontaneous radiation is negligible since the received equivalent brightness temperature is typically three orders of magnitude higher than terrestrial/sea brightness temperature due to the much stronger radiation source power; (5) The actual distance R between measurement targets and the array significantly exceeds the array size D; (6) The center frequency is much greater than the receiver bandwidth (narrowband assumption); (7) Within the receiver bandwidth, the field pattern of each antenna element remains unchanged with frequency variation; (8) For model simplification, the ground stations operate at the same frequency as the spaceborne synthetic aperture which uses omnidirectional antenna elements.

According to the established ground station model, the ground station transmits signals (AM) with a carrier voltage peak of 100 V, load resistance of 50 Ω, and transmission power of 100 W. The baseband signal has a voltage peak of 1 V and a modulation index of 0.5. The ground station is equipped with an omnidirectional antenna (Figure 2C) with 30 dB gain. The integration time is 0.1 s, carrier frequency 2 GHz, baseband signal frequency 1 MHz, receiver bandwidth 200 MHz, and sampling frequency 500 MHz.

Prior to simulating the equivalent brightness temperature map of the ground-based radiation source’s electromagnetic spectrum distribution, the spontaneous radiation brightness temperature of the terrestrial or sea surface region is first simulated. Assuming the spontaneous radiative brightness temperature of seawater or terrestrial features in the observed area is 300 K, with the incident wave direction approximately aligned with the nadir direction. Specifically, the effect of channel phase errors is the most common. Under ideal conditions (assuming errors are zero), regarding the modeling of ground stations and transmit signal parameters, these have been previously elaborated. An improved phase error correction algorithm-RCEVLA

In the previous section, we analyzed the impact of various phase errors on the imaging performance of brightness temperature distribution for wide-area radiation sources, and applied an existing algorithm for their correction and quantitative evaluation. In this section, we propose a new correction algorithm to address the practical limitations of current phase correction methods. Furthermore, by demonstrating the application of the resulting images for wide-area radiation source localization, we validate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm through the evaluation of radiation source localization accuracy.

The core principle of the external single-reference-source phase error correction method is to use a stable external reference source at a known location to measure the array response, compare it with theoretical phase values, and estimate each element’s phase deviation. However, existing studies using this method typically assume an ideal reference source for phase deviation estimation and correction, neglecting practical scenarios where the array’s reception of the reference signal may be affected by scene signals, outliers, random noise, or local interference. These factors can lead to inaccurate covariance matrix estimation and compromised correction performance. To address this, we propose a RCEVLA method to eliminate the impact of outliers on correction accuracy (The RCEVLA method principle is detailed in STAR Methods).

Later in discussion, simulations are conducted to verify the effectiveness of the proposed robust covariance estimation algorithm. Specifically, phase error correction results are obtained using the external single-reference-source method, where the reference noise heat source is set at (0°, 0°) with an equivalent brightness temperature of 1000 K, without anomalous interference. In practical space-based wide-area detection applications, the external reference source can be realized through the onboard integrated noise source module.22 This module is small in size and low in power consumption, and can be integrated into the receiver system. By switching modes at fixed intervals, it can simulate cold sources and hot sources, respectively, providing a stable reference signal for the system. Although this method can effectively support on-orbit calibration, attention still needs to be paid to the stability of the reference source itself, the impact of switch-on and switch-off on the continuity of observations, and the ability to resist abnormal interference in complex electromagnetic environments. In addition to relying on on-board hardware, errors can also be corrected by using natural celestial sources (such as the sun and the moon), known ground beacon sources, or known landmarks. To evaluate the algorithm’s robustness under practical anomalous interference signals, uniform phase errors U(0,π/2) are introduced along with varying numbers of transient anomalous signals (100-300 K random brightness temperature and random directions). Figure 12 compares phase correction errors with and without anomalous interference signals, and presents corrected errors after RCEVLA phase correction under varying numbers of outliers.

After the aforementioned wide-area electromagnetic spectrum map is generated, applications based on image information need to be carried out. This study focuses on the electromagnetic spectrum maps obtained under satellite-based imaging conditions and conducts localization analysis of key brightness temperature targets. In the brightness temperature retrieval results shown in Figure 10A, overlapping point sources are observed in certain areas. This phenomenon indicates that the actual spacing between the two incoming wave directions is beyond the system’s resolution (if their spacing were within the resolution limit, the two point sources would appear as a single source rather than exhibiting elongated features). When the two ground stations are in close proximity, due to the edge diffusion effect of pixel-level point sources during the retrieval process, the energy values in the overlapping region become significantly enhanced. If the highest peak is directly detected for localization, the energy superposition can lead to localization errors.

To address this, this paper employs a peak separation method based on centroid weighting.46 For regions with overlapping point sources, this method identifies energy peaks, divides the regions based on Euclidean distance, and calculates the centroid within each partition using the brightness temperature intensity as the weight, thereby achieving spatial separation of the overlapping point sources. This paper transfers the synthetic aperture passive imaging technology from the “natural thermal radiation remote sensing” field to the “artificial electromagnetic spectrum situation generation” field, and constructs a system model of “ground station modeling - space propagation - spaceborne synthetic aperture passive imaging” for the bottlenecks of current space-based electromagnetic spectrum mapping technologies—low efficiency in acquiring holistic situational information and insufficient real-time response capability for rapid mapping, laying a theoretical foundation for rapid imaging of space-based wide-area electromagnetic spectrum. Building on this theoretical framework, we propose a “staring imaging” rapid wide-area electromagnetic spectrum maps generation method. This method primarily includes: payload design and platform placement, coordinate system selection and imaging perspective transformation, simulation scenario construction, inversion algorithms, and performance validation. Simulation results demonstrate that real-time single-frame wide-area mapping can be achieved at the second level. In one of the mapping-based applications—target localization—an accuracy of 4.5 km@ 850 km is attained after 3-fold frequency-domain spatial interpolation, validating the feasibility and advancement of the proposed method for rapid wide-area electromagnetic spectrum maps generation. To further address practical challenges in on-orbit phase calibration for spaceborne imaging, this paper proposes an RCEVLA method, enabling high-precision phase correction under strong outlier-induced anomalous interference.

In summary, this work systematically demonstrates the feasibility of spaceborne synthetic aperture passive imaging technology for rapid wide-area electromagnetic spectrum map generation from three perspectives: theoretical framework development, methodology design and simulation validation, and engineering problem-solving. It provides methodological support for the cross-domain transfer of this technology from remote sensing to electromagnetic spectrum sensing.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. (1) Although the proposed system model incorporates key propagation factors such as FSPL, noise, and atmospheric attenuation, further investigation is needed to explore how more complex propagation effects—such as multipath interference and other channel impairments—impact imaging performance, and to develop corresponding correction methods. (2) The current study is primarily based on the simulated data, including the proposed staring imaging framework and the associated dataset. While simulation results demonstrate the potential of our approach, further validation using real spaceborne measurements is necessary to evaluate its practical performance and robustness in operational scenarios.

Experimental model and study participant details

Dataset

We provided a spaceborne wide-area electromagnetic spectrum map dataset, which comprises two components: the first part contains electromagnetic spectrum maps for ground stations with omnidirectional antennas, including both ideal error-free cases and those affected by coupling errors; the second part features maps for ground stations with randomized beam performance antennas (randomize the ground station antenna parameters: antenna type randomly selected, gains are randomly set between 10 dB and 50 dB, beam widths between 10° and 120°, azimuth angles between 0° and 360°, and elevation angles between 0° and 90°), covering both ideal error-free cases and scenarios with three types of errors. We set up 5000 ground stations randomly across the globe. Each ground station continuously transmits amplitude-modulated signals with a carrier voltage peak of 100V, a load resistance of 50 Ω, and a transmission power of 100W. The voltage peak of the baseband signal is 1V and the modulation index is 0.5. The ground stations are equipped with omnidirectional antennas or randomized beam performance antennas (the antenna type is randomly selected from parabolic antennas, horn antennas, phased arrays, etc.). The dataset contains a total of 2,578 images, with detailed information available in the supplemental information.

Data generation method

It is worth noting that the core contribution of this paper lies not in the scale of the dataset itself, but in the proposed data generation method (The data include: visibility functions obtained from the signals received by the satellite’s synthetic aperture, onboard direction-brightness temperature data, onboard image data, and brightness temperature and image data mapped to the ground). To address the current issue of low efficiency in generating wide-area electromagnetic spectrum maps, this paper proposes, through technology integration and application transfer, a synthetic aperture passive rapid mapping technology suitable for space-based wide-area electromagnetic spectrum maps. This resolves the inefficiencies and poor real-time performance of traditional “point sampling + data post-processing” spectrum mapping approaches and the conventional “single-point direction finding—accumulation—layer-by-layer deduction—cluster situation” paradigm. The proposed method compresses the update cycle of wide-area electromagnetic spectrum maps from “hours/days” down to “seconds.” The related concepts and technical path are of innovative value. Currently, we have open-sourced the code for the dataset generation method, which is available at: https://github.com/Jnhhh1/Main-Code-and-WAES-Dataset.

Method details

Calibration issue

In traditional passive remote sensing, calibration is a critical step for achieving absolute brightness temperature inversion. However, the core innovation of this paper lies in the migration of synthetic aperture passive imaging technology from the domain of “natural thermal radiation remote sensing” to that of “artificial electromagnetic spectrum situation generation.” This application shift leads to a fundamental change in calibration requirements: The objective of our framework is to rapidly generate wide-area electromagnetic spectrum maps to directly support space-based real-time warning and wide-area situational analysis. The core task is to quickly identify abnormal energy regions within complex electromagnetic environments, detect and locate potential radiation targets, and capture situational changes. Our focus is on the presence or absence of electromagnetic signals and energy, as well as variations in their direction and intensity, rather than the absolute transmission power when they are emitted from the ground. Therefore, our approach does not require precise absolute radiometric inversion and has significantly reduced dependence on traditional radiometric calibration.

Through this technological migration, we retain the inherent advantages of synthetic aperture imaging—such as high sensitivity, wide field of view, and fast imaging—while eliminating the need for the complex and frequent absolute radiometric calibration procedures required in traditional passive remote sensing. This simplification also helps reduce image generation time, i.e., lowers system response latency, which is one of the key advantages of our proposed method.

Geographic mapping details

The original sampling data acquired by the spaceborne staggered Y-shaped synthetic aperture undergo complex correlation between each pair of elements and image reconstruction, yielding brightness temperature distribution data within a hexagonal FOV. Each pixel corresponds to a complex-valued measurement representing coordinates in the direction cosine space. After 3-fold zero-padding interpolation in the spatial frequency domain, the original FOV data matrix has dimensions of 1 × 11773, corresponding to 11,773 pixels within the hexagonal FOV. To generate a continuous geographic distribution image, each FOV pixel is expanded into a square unit with a half-side length of 0.009 (corresponding to the sampling interval in the direction cosine space). For each central point, four vertex coordinates are generated, transforming the original 1 × 11773 FOV data into a 4 × 11773 vertex matrix. Subsequently, within the NED coordinate system, the direction cosine values at each vertex are converted into corresponding azimuth angles (0–360°) and elevation angles (0–90°). Applying this conversion to all 4 × 11773 vertices yields two angle matrices of identical dimensions: azimuth matrix and elevation matrix, both sized 4 × 11773.

After obtaining the incident angle at each vertex, we calculate the latitude and longitude coordinates of the ground target point corresponding to each angle based on the position of the satellite platform and the WGS-84 Earth Ellipsoid model. In this simulation scenario, the satellite platform is positioned at (63.2612° E, 41.5068° N) at an altitude of 860,774.5 m at the time of data acquisition. The calculation process first converts the satellite position into ECEF coordinates and constructs the rotation matrix from the NED coordinate system to the ECEF coordinate system. Then, pointing vectors are constructed in the NED coordinate system along the incident direction corresponding to each vertex and transformed into the ECEF coordinate system via the rotation matrix. Finally, the intersection points of these rays with the Earth’s ellipsoid surface are solved to obtain the geographic longitude and latitude of all target points.

Repeating the above calculations for all 4 × 11773 vertices yields two geographic coordinate matrices of identical dimensions: longitude matrix and latitude matrix, both sized 4 × 11773. At this stage, each original FOV pixel corresponds to a column of four vertex longitude and latitude coordinates, forming a quadrilateral unit in geographic space, with the quadrilateral’s center representing the longitude and latitude coordinate point corresponding to each incoming wave direction. The final geographic mapping image is generated as follows: for each quadrilateral unit, its four vertex longitude and latitude coordinates are used as polygon vertices, and the corresponding brightness temperature value serves as the fill color to draw a filled polygon. After rendering all units, a complete longitude-latitude brightness temperature distribution map is obtained, where the horizontal axis represents longitude, the vertical axis represents latitude, and the color indicates brightness temperature values. This method precisely maps the measurement results of the spaceborne synthetic aperture from the direction cosine space onto the WGS-84 Earth ellipsoid surface, achieving the transformation of the onboard hexagonal field-of-view image to real geographic space. For detailed implementation of the geographic mapping method, please refer to our open-source code: https://github.com/Jnhhh1/Main-Code-and-WAES-Dataset/Mapping.

Analysis of positioning accuracy and parameter quantity after different interpolation times in frequency space

In the synthetic aperture hexagonal imaging system, the positioning accuracy is closely related to the system resolution and the number of frequency space zero interpolation in the image inversion process. The higher the system resolution, the better the positioning accuracy. After the array design is completed, the angular resolution is fixed (angular resolution of 1.728°, corresponding to a ground resolution of 25.6 km at an orbital height of 850 km), so in the subsequent process, by increasing the times of frequency space zero interpolation, the positioning accuracy can be doubled without changing the system resolution. The ground station distribution scenario set in this paper is relatively complex. Some ground stations are close to each other, and there is a problem of overlapping point source pixels. Therefore, the positioning accuracy of some targets in 50 ground stations is relatively low. In the scenario without overlapping point source pixels, the positioning accuracy and image parameter quantities corresponding to different interpolation levels are as follows: without interpolation, the positioning accuracy is ≤12.8 km, and the parameter quantity is 1333; with secondary zero interpolation, the positioning accuracy is ≤6.4 km, and the parameter quantity is 5257; with third-order zero interpolation, the positioning accuracy is ≤4.5 km, and the parameter quantity is 11773; with fourth-order zero interpolation, the positioning accuracy is ≤3.2 km, and the parameter quantity is 20881; with fifth-order zero interpolation, the positioning accuracy is ≤2.6 km, and the parameter quantity is 32581. As the interpolation times increases, the positioning accuracy will further improve, and the image parameter quantity will also increase. This paper adopts a third-order frequency space zero interpolation scheme.

It is worth noting that the positioning application is just one of the multiple applications of the electromagnetic spectrum map. The rapid generation method of the wide-area electromagnetic spectrum map proposed in this paper aims directly at providing observers with intuitive and real-time electromagnetic situation information, which is precisely the core advantage of the mapping technology.
