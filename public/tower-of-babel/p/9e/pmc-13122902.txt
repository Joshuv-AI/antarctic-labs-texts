Recruiting US veterans with chronic low back pain in a multisite randomized pragmatic trial
Authors: Anna Steward, Stacie A Salsbury, Robert D Vining, Elissa Twist, Jacob McCarey, Sujatha Kedilaya, Amy L Minkalis, Rebecca Rudquist, Kena McDermott, Paul G Shekelle, Thad E Abrams, Jon D Lurie, Robert B Wallace, Anthony J Lisi, Christine M Goertz, Cynthia R Long

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on recruitment strategies for Veterans Health Administration (VA) pragmatic clinical trials (PCTs) is scarce. This study compares multiple approaches and evaluates engagement, efficiency, and representativeness in the integrated care setting of VA.MethodsWe examined recruitment and screening processes used in the Veterans Response to Dosage in Chiropractic Therapy (VERDICT) PCT. Veterans with chronic low back pain were recruited and enrolled in 4 US VA facilities from February 2021 to May 2024. We used 3 recruitment strategies: existing referrals for chiropractic services, electronic health record (EHR) prescreen, and self-referral. Pre-enrollment contacts occurred through postal mail, email, and in-person or virtual baseline visits. We used 5 sequential screening steps leading to final eligibility determination: (1) identify and notify, (2) phone screen, (3) consent, (4) eligibility questions, and (5) chiropractic consultation.ResultsWe screened 3667 veterans: 91% were identified through existing referrals, 7% through EHR prescreen, and 2% through self-referral; 62% received postal-only recruitment materials, 38% received a combination of postal + email materials, and 83% had a virtual baseline visit. Over 39 months, 766 individuals were enrolled and randomized. Due to limited VA chiropractic clinic capacity, 19% of eligible veterans were not enrolled. Across all steps of the screening process, 45% were not interested, and 35% were ineligible. Using existing referrals was the most efficient strategy, with a median time from notification to randomization of 23 days. Those emailed invitations had a median response time of 16 days, compared with 30 days for postal-only invitations. The existing referrals strategy was the most effective for recruiting racial and ethnic minorities (33%). EHR prescreen resulted in the highest proportion of female veterans (79%).ConclusionsThis study is among the few to compare multiple recruitment strategies in a VA PCT while assessing engagement, efficiency, and representativeness. VERDICT shows that PCTs can overcome recruitment challenges through adaptable, multifaceted approaches. By addressing systemic barriers and implementing email and virtual strategies, we improved recruitment engagement and diversity. These methods can inform recruitment for trials in other VA-based and real-world settings.Trial registrationClinical trial registration number: NCT04087291. Background

Recruitment and enrollment are critical to ensure clinical trials are completed on time and within budget, include a diverse and representative sample, and meet sample size requirements [1, 2]. Challenges in recruitment can lead to data collection delays and increased costs [1, 2]. Recruitment can be especially challenging and time-consuming for multisite pragmatic clinical trials (PCTs) conducted within large healthcare systems [1]. These trials often require research personnel to navigate varied regulatory requirements, accommodate site-specific organizational procedures, and tailor communication preferences to recruit participants across different locations. Investigators testing interventions delivered within the US Veterans Health Administration (VA) must balance the logistical challenges of implementing trial processes with overcoming barriers veterans face when accessing clinical care, such as transportation difficulties, long wait times, and geographic distance from VA facilities [3, 4].

A range of recruitment strategies for randomized trials has been studied, including mailing invitation letters, engaging patients in physician waiting rooms, and asking physicians to assist with recruiting participants [2]. Multifaceted approaches that use digital tools, such as identifying participants through electronic health records (EHR) and direct patient outreach via email, have become increasingly prevalent and gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic [5, 6]. While systematic reviews have summarized recruitment strategies broadly, evidence on their effectiveness in large, multisite PCTs within integrated health systems like the VA remains limited [7–9]. Establishing best practice frameworks for these settings requires standardized assessment and transparent reporting of recruitment and screening methods [10–13].

We address this gap by describing the multipronged recruitment and screening strategies used in the Veterans Response to Dosage in Chiropractic Therapy (VERDICT) trial, a parallel-group, randomized PCT conducted within VA [13]. VERDICT is 1 of 16 PCTs facilitated by the Pain Management Collaboratory to evaluate nonpharmacological approaches to pain care among US veterans and active duty military [14]. VERDICT enrolled 766 veterans to participate in a 1-year trial protocol to assess dosing of chiropractic care for managing chronic low back pain (LBP) [13]. Participants underwent 2 research phases. In the first phase, they were randomly assigned to receive either 1 to 5 chiropractic visits or 8 to 12 visits over 10 weeks. In the second phase (weeks 10 to 52), participants were randomized to either ongoing chiropractic management with prescheduled monthly visits or no scheduled visits. The primary outcome was change in LBP-related disability.

VERDICT began recruiting participants in 2021 and took place in 4 VA facilities staffed with Doctors of Chiropractic (DCs). DCs are licensed, direct-access clinicians in all 50 states who diagnose and manage non-operative musculoskeletal and neuromuscular conditions [15]. DCs are well-trained to perform an evidence-based history and examination, independently manage patient care, and refer to other medical, surgical, or associated health clinicians as indicated. Nonpharmacological therapies commonly delivered by DCs as part of chiropractic care include spinal manipulation, myofascial therapies, therapeutic exercise, patient education, and self-care recommendations.

This descriptive study examines the recruitment and screening processes of VERDICT. Our objectives were to (1) outline and evaluate 3 different recruitment strategies; (2) assess the impact of a 5-step screening process on eligibility and enrollment; and (3) compare recruitment in postal mail versus email invitations and in-person versus virtual baseline visits. We were also interested in determining how these different recruitment approaches influenced participant engagement, recruitment efficiency, and sample representativeness throughout the PCT (Table 1).

Methods

VERDICT was conducted at 4 US VA facilities: West Haven, CT; Iowa City, IA; Minneapolis, MN; and Greater Los Angeles (Greater LA), CA (Table 2). The target population included veterans aged 18 years and older who were enrolled in VA healthcare services with chronic LBP, defined as lasting 3 months or longer and pain on at least half of the days over the past 6 months, based on the National Institutes of Health Task Force recommendations [16]. VERDICT received ethics approval from the VA Central Institutional Review Board (VA CIRB 1613761) and the Yale University Institutional Review Board (Yale IRB 2000025406). Detailed trial design information, including randomization procedures, blinding, intervention measures, and primary outcomes, is reported in the published VERDICT protocol [13]. This study focuses exclusively on recruitment and screening processes used during the trial.

Recruitment procedures

Participants were recruited from February 2021 to May 2024, with recruitment starting and ending dates differing by site (Table 2). Our original protocol called for enrolling 766 veterans over 30 months (February 2021 to August 2023). Due to COVID-19 restrictions and delays, we extended enrollment by 9 months, through May 2024. Each VA site had a full-time designated onsite or virtual research study coordinator (SC) who managed participant contacts and documented recruitment activities in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database [17]. Recruitment data included the veteran’s name, address, phone number, email address, and documentation of all VERDICT-related interactions. The recruitment screening process involved 5 sequential steps: (1) identify and notify, (2) phone screen, (3) consent, (4) eligibility questions, and (5) chiropractic consultation [13].

Step 1: identify and notify

VERDICT implemented 3 recruitment strategies (Fig. 1) developed and tested in a pilot trial: existing referrals to chiropractic services, EHR prescreen, and self-referral [18]. Recruitment began in February 2021 after IRB approval in January for protocol revisions allowing virtual baseline visits. All 3 recruitment strategies (existing referrals, EHR prescreen, and self-referral) were planned during trial design and piloted in the feasibility study; however, the mode of delivery allowing for virtual baseline visits was adapted in response to COVID-19 [18].

Existing referrals: As access to chiropractic services occurs through referrals from other healthcare professionals (Table 2), potential participants were identified by monitoring chiropractic referrals (i.e., consultations) via the VA EHR. Veterans with International Classification of Disease, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes indicating LBP who had been referred to the chiropractic clinic but had not yet attended their initial visit were identified [19]. The VA CIRB required that all veterans received an invitation letter via the US Postal Service before direct contact was initiated to avoid cold-calling veterans. During the first year of recruitment, SCs waited 4 business days to allow time for postal delivery before making up to 3 calls to gauge interest and eligibility during the phone screening. After obtaining IRB approval to send emails along with postal mail in February 2022, SCs attempted phone screens 1 day after sending the email.

Step 2: phone screen

SCs provided trial details to veterans who responded to any of the recruitment strategies in step 1 and asked initial eligibility questions via phone. SCs followed a prepared script to offer key trial information, address questions and concerns, and obtain verbal consent to proceed. Additional file 2 includes the phone screen script. Eligible veterans received an email with consent materials, including a PowerPoint overview of the trial, a flowchart of activities, and an information sheet. Additional file 3 includes the consent materials.

Steps 3 (consent) and 4 (eligibility questions): baseline visit

Within 10 days before the chiropractic consultation, SCs conducted a baseline visit to review consent materials to ensure that veterans understood the materials and had enough information to make an informed decision about participating in the trial. Whether baseline visits were conducted virtually or in person was based on veteran and coordinator preference and site logistics. Veterans could complete baseline visits by phone and questionnaires on personal devices, reducing onsite resource needs for SCs. After obtaining verbal consent, veterans completed further eligibility questions. In-person baseline visits were scheduled for up to 2 h, allowing time for questionnaire completion before the visit ended. Virtual baseline screenings lasted approximately 45 min, with veterans completing questionnaires on their own electronic devices after the visit concluded. A $25 gift card was provided as compensation for the time and effort spent completing the questionnaires.

Eligibility required self-reported average pain intensity in the past week of at least 2 on an 11-point scale and a score of 4 or higher on the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire [22, 23]. Those still eligible moved on to the chiropractic consultation.

Step 5: chiropractic consultation

During the initial chiropractic visit, the VA DCs verified that veterans had an ICD-10 diagnosis of LBP and that chiropractic care was indicated based on the patient’s history and examination findings. All chiropractic consultation visits were performed in person. Veterans with conditions that suggested chiropractic care was inappropriate or who required a specialist consultation were excluded. Following the chiropractic examination, SCs addressed any concerns with eligible participants, answered their questions, and enrolled them in the study using a randomization module in REDCap, which concealed future group assignments.

Site coordinators and DCs played a central role in recruitment and consent. SCs received standardized training on eligibility screening, informed consent procedures, and data collection protocols. Training included scripted communication templates, consent checklists, and periodic quality assurance reviews to ensure consistency across sites. SCs collaborated with VA schedulers to arrange participant visits.

Reasons for exclusions were systematically documented at each recruitment step (notification, phone screen, baseline visit consent and eligibility questions, and chiropractic consultation) and categorized by “not interested” (e.g., time commitment, travel distance, lack of interest in chiropractic care) and “ineligible” (e.g., unable to schedule before DC visit, no referral, site restrictions, contraindications to chiropractic care). These data were summarized to identify barriers to participation.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for all comparisons. We also assessed differences in median days from notification to randomization using Mood’s median test and chi-square methods to compare participant demographics with those of the general VA population and the VA population with LBP [21, 24]. Because this analysis was designed to describe recruitment processes rather than test hypotheses, no additional inferential analyses were performed.

Results

Participant engagement

We identified 3756 potential participants using 3 recruitment strategies: existing referrals (3420; 91%), EHR prescreen (271; 7%), and self-referral (65; 2%). Of those identified, 89 veterans were not sent invitation letters due to their inability to complete the screening process before the chiropractic consultation or capacity issues at the clinic. This included 48 veterans from existing referrals and 41 from EHR prescreen, resulting in 3667 veterans screened (Fig. 2).

Veteran engagement at each stage of the 5-step screening process

Of the 3667 screened, 1480 (40%) responded to recruitment efforts, and 766 (21%) remained eligible during the 5-step screening process and were enrolled and randomized. Figure 2 shows the yields at each step and the reasons for exclusions resulting from either a lack of interest or ineligibility. Among veterans who were “not interested,” the most frequently cited reasons were “did not return call,” “did not want to participate,” “time commitment,” and “travel distance” (Fig. 2). At step 1 (notification), 38% of exclusions were due to disinterest. Similar patterns were observed at consent (step 3), where 15% declined for these reasons.

Veterans recruited through self-referrals had the highest eligibility rate throughout the screening process, with 54% randomized into the trial (Table 3). In comparison, 21% of veterans who engaged through existing referrals and 14% from EHR prescreen remained eligible during all 5 screening steps and were randomized.

Among the 944 veterans who completed the baseline questionnaires (step 4), 83% participated in virtual visits, while 17% completed in-person visits (Fig. 3). The majority of baseline visits were conducted virtually, reflecting participant and SC preferences. Veterans favored remote visits for convenience and avoiding early arrival before chiropractic consultations, while SCs appreciated reduced travel and reliance on VA resources.

Veteran engagement with the baseline visit activities, either in person or virtually

We sent postal-only recruitment materials to 2264 veterans through the existing referrals and EHR prescreen strategies, representing 62% of our outreach (Table 3). Additionally, we sent postal + email invitations to 1338 veterans (36%) identified solely through existing referrals (Table 3). While disinterest was similar for those contacted by postal mail-only (44%) and for those contacted by postal + email (46%), those contacted by postal + email had a higher proportion of veterans who remained eligible throughout the screening process (17% for postal-only versus 26% for postal + email).

Recruitment efficiency

Of the 3667 screened, 766 (21%) were enrolled and randomized over 39 months of recruitment. Across all steps of the screening process, 45% were not interested and 35% were ineligible (Fig. 2). Veterans recruited through self-referrals had the highest eligibility rate throughout the screening process, with 54% randomized into the trial (Table 3).

The existing referrals strategy was the most efficient recruitment method, with a median duration of 23 days from trial notification to randomization. In contrast, self-referrals and EHR prescreen had median durations of 28 and 32 days, respectively (Fig. 4). Of the 1635 veterans who shared their reasons for not being interested, the most common were time commitment (274, 17%) and travel distance (119, 7%) (Table 3).

Comparison of median days from initial contact to phone screen, baseline visit, and chiropractic consultation

Capacity limitations at VA clinics caused delays for many veterans referred to chiropractic services. Consequently, many could not schedule a consultation to VA chiropractic services during VERDICT recruitment [25]. Specifically, 713 veterans (19%) were screened out of participation in VERDICT due to these clinic capacity constraints. Median wait times from trial notification (step 1) to chiropractic consultation (step 5) varied by site: 36 days at Iowa City, 38 days at Greater LA, 15 days at West Haven, and 14 days at Minneapolis (Fig. 4). Across all sites, many veterans who scheduled prompt consultations were deemed ineligible for VERDICT participation because they could not complete the earlier steps of the screening process (steps 2–4) before the consultation visit. The Minneapolis and West Haven VA sites had a higher percentage of veterans screened out for this reason: 13% (71/552) and 12% (104/880), respectively. In comparison, Iowa City VA had 6% (84/1495), and Greater LA had 2% (19/829).

Veterans receiving both email and postal invitations were more likely to be enrolled because email responses allowed screening to be completed before chiropractic consultations were scheduled. At sites without clinic capacity constraints, appointments were often scheduled quickly, and delayed responses (e.g., from postal mail) sometimes resulted in missed enrollment opportunities. Median response time from trial notification to randomization was 16 days for participants contacted by postal + email compared to 29 to 32 days for those contacted by postal-only mail (Fig. 4). Due to clinic capacity limitations, 23% (517/2264) of potentially eligible veterans contacted by postal mail were unable to schedule a consultation at VA compared to 14% (190/1338) contacted by postal + email.

Sample representation

Most females were enrolled through EHR prescreening (79%), followed by self-referrals (57%) and existing referrals (17%). Among those randomized, existing referrals were most effective at recruiting participants who identified as racial and ethnic minorities (33%). In comparison, EHR prescreen contributed 27% and self-referrals 11%.

Per our established recruitment goals, the enrolled participants included 21% females and 30% racial and ethnic minorities, both higher than among all VA users, which consists of 11% females, 20% Black or other races, and 9% Hispanic or Latino (Fig. 5). Participants in this trial were younger, with a mean age of 51 years (range 22–87), and 49% were employed. In contrast, 48% of VA users are 65 years and older, and 37% are working. Furthermore, the percentage of rural veterans in VERDICT was 14% compared to 34% of all VA users. VERDICT had a higher percentage of females and younger veterans than the overall population seeking care for LBP in VA (Fig. 5).

Discussion

VERDICT successfully enrolled 766 veterans with chronic LBP across 4 VA facilities over 39 months. We shifted from exclusive use of postal mail and in-person baseline visits to digital recruitment and virtual visits aiming to optimize recruitment and engage participants [10]. This shift maintained quality interactions and addressed barriers related to time and travel [2, 5, 6, 18]. Invitations sent by email resulted in higher participant engagement than invitations sent only by postal mail. Veterans receiving both invitations were more likely to be eligible for the trial due to the quicker responses associated with email. This finding highlights the importance of timely communication in integrated health systems where clinic appointments may be scheduled rapidly. Faster response times enabled completion of screening steps before chiropractic consultations, reducing missed enrollment opportunities.

All 3 recruitment strategies were pre-planned during the trial design phase; however, accommodations were necessary in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and clinic capacity constraints, requiring flexible trial management [2, 18]. The strategies themselves did not change, but the mode of delivery for baseline visits was adapted to include remote baseline visits, allowing veterans to complete baseline visits via phone and questionnaires on their personal devices, improving convenience and reducing onsite resource needs. This adaptation was not simply a function of time period but a deliberate response to pandemic-related constraints and participant needs. While the timing of this adaptation contributed to the higher proportion of virtual baseline visits, both veterans and SCs preferred this option.

Engagement is crucial for building representative participant groups in trials. In VERDICT, we evaluated engagement in 2 ways: by reviewing reasons veterans showed disinterest in the trial and evaluating those interested but ineligible. The high proportion of veterans citing time commitment and travel distance as reasons for disinterest underscores the importance of offering flexible options, such as remote baseline visits, to reduce participation burden. These factors were also relevant to the chiropractic care veterans were scheduled to receive, as longer travel times and scheduling challenges could deter both trial participation and clinical care. Addressing these barriers through virtual options and streamlined scheduling may improve engagement and retention in future PCTs. Clear communication from designated SCs about the trial’s importance and the expected activities helped enhance engagement.

Although clinic capacity constraints may appear to be operational issues, they highly affected participant eligibility and, therefore, represent an important methodological consideration for PCTs. Recruitment feasibility is inherently tied to site-level resources and workflow integration. Our findings emphasized the need for trialists to understand these constraints and anticipate them in recruitment plans. Understanding and addressing these challenges is also important to optimize the VA chiropractic workforce as VA chiropractic services are projected to double between 2022 and 2027, and to plan for future VA trials [27, 28]. A key issue was inconsistent clinic capacities across sites: some clinics provided quick consultation scheduling, while others had delays. As a result, 25% of interested veterans, one-quarter of those notified about the trial, could not participate in the trial. Clinics with shorter wait times allowed veterans to move quickly from notification to consultation, while longer wait times prevented some from scheduling consultations at VA during VERDICT enrollment.

In VERDICT, recruitment strategies varied in effectiveness across subgroups: EHR prescreening was particularly effective in enrolling female veterans who had not previously sought chiropractic services, while recruitment through the existing referrals strategy was most effective for recruiting racial and ethnic minority participants. Future PCTs should consider additional targeted strategies, such as outreach to veterans who do not routinely access VA services, to improve representativeness and generalizability.

Overall, the demographic makeup of VERDICT participants was more diverse than that of general VA service users and patients seeking care for LBP within VA [21, 24, 29–31]. However, only 14% of VERDICT participants were from rural areas, consistent with the underrepresentation of rural veterans in clinical trials [32, 33]. This is in contrast to the 34% of veterans in the VA system who reside in rural communities and 19% of the US population who live in rural areas [34]. We did not analyze which recruitment strategies were most effective for rural participants, representing an important area for future research. Targeted outreach and enrollment strategies, such as recruiting in rural clinics, leveraging telehealth, and evaluating the effectiveness of mail versus EHR-based recruitment, may help improve rural representation in future PCTs [28].

Strengths and limitations

This descriptive study of VERDICT recruitment strategies had notable strengths, most importantly our robust data collection of participant status via REDCap throughout the recruitment and enrollment process. We also compared our results to the broader population of VA service users and veterans seeking care for LBP, demonstrating our sample representativeness and the potential for VA chiropractic services to reach veterans with broad demographic characteristics [21, 31].

We also had several limitations. The IRB did not permit the VERDICT trial to institute an email-only notification method, which might have saved both time and costs, allowing faster and more efficient enrollment. Additionally, we did not track the time or cost differences related to completing recruitment tasks via email and postal methods, which represented a substantial portion of the SCs’ workload and created a barrier to efficient recruitment within VA. Similar to another PCT in the Pain Management Collaboratory, we did not test recruitment strategies using randomization, which would help clarify the effectiveness of the different approaches [11].

Another limitation of our recruitment approach was that veterans needed either an initial provider referral to chiropractic care or awareness of the study to engage in the “self-referral” recruitment strategy. This requirement may have systematically excluded veterans who were less engaged with VA services or unaware of VERDICT, potentially affecting sample representativeness. To mitigate this bias, we implemented multiple outreach strategies, including mail and email invitations, EHR-based prescreening, and proactive engagement efforts such as provider education sessions about VERDICT and direct onsite interactions with veterans. These approaches aimed to increase awareness among veterans with lower VA engagement.

Although these findings are most relevant to integrated health systems such as the VA, several strategies, including EHR-assisted recruitment, combined mail/email outreach, and remote baseline visits, are broadly applicable to PCTs in other large healthcare organizations. In settings outside the USA or without veteran-specific infrastructure, these approaches may require adaptation, such as leveraging local health records or telehealth platforms.

Conclusions

This study is among the few to compare multiple recruitment strategies within a VA PCT context while simultaneously evaluating participant engagement, recruitment efficiency, and sample representativeness. VERDICT demonstrates how PCTs can overcome recruitment challenges through adaptive and personalized communication, proactive planning, and leveraging health system resources. Our recruitment outcomes were influenced by our ability to address and respond to systemic barriers and quickly implement effective and adaptable strategies, such as email invitations and virtual visits. The trial improved engagement and efficiency through multifaceted approaches, including digital strategies, leading to higher eligibility rates and greater participant diversity, predominantly female veterans. While these findings may not be universally applicable, they offer practical insights for recruitment in real-world trials and can be adapted to meet the specific needs of other researchers recruiting patients for PCTs in VA or comparable health systems.
