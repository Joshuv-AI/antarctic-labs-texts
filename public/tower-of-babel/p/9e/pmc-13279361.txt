Association of Lipid and Inflammatory Profiles With Tumor Stage in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Nanmu Yang, Ting Wang, Xiangjun Qian, Zhongyi Li, Hao Wang, Hang Xing, Yuanlin Xu, Shuaiya Ma, Bo Meng, Xianzhou Zhang, Feng Han, Yongmei Li, Dongxiao Li, Hao Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common cancers worldwide. Inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism disorders are known to influence cancer development; however, their combined effects on HCC progression remain unclear. This study examined the associations between lipid profiles and inflammatory markers with HCC stage.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, data from 90 patients with HCC and available Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage information were analyzed. Serum lipid profiles and inflammatory markers were measured, and derived indicators—including non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (non–HDL‐C), the low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C)‐to‐HDL‐C ratio, the total cholesterol (TC)‐to‐HDL‐C ratio, and the neutrophil‐to‐HDL‐C ratio (NHR)—were calculated. Correlation analysis and logistic regression were performed with adjustment for potential confounders. Interaction analysis was conducted to examine relationships among lipid–inflammatory profiles, with findings validated in an independent cohort of 200 patients with HCC.ResultsHDL‐C levels were significantly lower in patients with non‐advanced stage tumors (0.81 ± 0.27 vs. 1.15 ± 0.28, p < 0.01). HDL‐C showed a positive correlation with tumor stage (r = 0.544, p < 0.01), whereas non–HDL‐C, LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and NHR demonstrated negative correlations (r = −0.269, −0.568, −0.382, and −0.432, respectively; all p < 0.05). In univariate logistic regression analysis, higher LDL‐to‐HDL ratio (odds ratio [OR] = 0.193, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.066–0.562) and higher TC‐to‐HDL ratio (OR = 0.378, 95% CI: 0.195–0.730) were significantly associated with non‐advanced stage, and these associations persisted after adjustment for NHR.ConclusionsLipid‐inflammatory profiles were associated with HCC staging in correlation and partially adjusted regression analyses and showed apparent discriminative performance in ROC analysis. However, no independent predictors were identified in the fully adjusted multivariable model. These findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, suggesting that lipid‐inflammatory profiles may reflect staging‐related metabolic and inflammatory alterations rather than serve as independent staging markers. Further validation in large prospective studies is required.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common cancers worldwide and poses a major global health challenge, with persistently high mortality rates despite advances in treatment [1, 2]. The poor prognosis of HCC highlights the urgent need to better understand its biological behavior and identify reliable markers of disease progression. The development and progression of HCC involve complex molecular alterations, with metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory responses representing two key hallmarks [3, 4, 5, 6]. Understanding these processes and their clinical indicators is essential for improving disease characterization and management.

Cancer cells undergo significant metabolic reprogramming, including alterations in lipid metabolism [7], to support rapid growth and invasion. The liver, as a central metabolic organ, exhibits characteristic patterns of dyslipidemia during HCC development, manifested by distinct changes in circulating lipoproteins, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) [8, 9, 10]. Studies have shown that TC, TG, and LDL‐C demonstrate inverse associations with HCC in the general population [11] and in individuals with chronic liver disease. Conversely, the dose–response relationship between HDL‐C and HCC remains unclear [12]. Moreover, no single metabolic factor was associated with an increased risk of HCC; however, the presence of three or more metabolic factors was associated with a higher risk of HCC [13]. A greater number of metabolic syndrome components was also associated with a reduced risk of liver cancer [14]. Evidence for synergistic interactions among metabolic factors remains limited, warranting further investigation.

Meanwhile, the cancer‐related inflammation theory emphasizes inflammatory markers as clinical risk factors for poor prognosis and recurrence [15, 16]. Local inflammation contributes to tumorigenesis within the tumor microenvironment, whereas systemic inflammation responds to malignant neoplasms through cytokines, immune proteins, and immune cells [15, 16, 17]. Inflammatory markers such as the platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) have emerged as valuable prognostic indicators in HCC [18, 19, 20]. Furthermore, the Combined Inflammation and Pathology model, which incorporates inflammatory indicators such as PLR and NLR along with pathological features including vascular invasion and tumor stage, has demonstrated robust performance in predicting recurrence risk in patients with HCC [21].

Despite these advances in understanding the roles of lipid metabolism and inflammation in HCC [22, 23, 24], several critical aspects remain insufficiently explored. First, the specific association between lipid ratios and tumor stage has not been systematically investigated. Second, although both inflammatory markers and lipid metabolism are known to be altered in HCC, their potential interactive effects on tumor characteristics remain unclear. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that combining lipid profiles with inflammatory markers would provide novel insights into HCC characteristics. This study aims to investigate their combined associations with HCC stage and to establish a more comprehensive framework for understanding and predicting HCC progression using readily available clinical parameters.

Methods

Participants

Patient data were collected at The Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Zhengzhou University and Henan Cancer Hospital, Zhengzhou, China, from patients evaluated between January 1, 2011, and January 1, 2012. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2024‐KY‐0024‐001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. A total of 90 patients with HCC and available Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging data were included in the primary analysis. An independent cohort of 200 patients with HCC was used to validate the key findings. According to the BCLC staging system, 36 patients classified as BCLC stage 0–B were assigned to the non‐advanced group, whereas the remaining 54 patients classified as BCLC stage C–D were assigned to the advanced group. The cohort comprised 78 men and 12 women, with a mean age of 55.27 ± 10.92 years. Demographic characteristics and overall disease status were also collected. Lipid and inflammatory profiles were collected, including primary indicators such as LDL‐C, HDL‐C, TC, very‐low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL‐C), neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, red blood cell distribution width–DV, and red blood cell distribution width–SD (RDW‐SD), as well as derived indicators such as non–HDL‐C, the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, remnant cholesterol (RC; calculated as TC minus HDL‐C minus LDL‐C), and the platelet‐to‐WBC ratio (PWR). In addition, the PLR, NLR, neutrophil‐to‐HDL ratio (NHR), and lymphocyte‐to‐HDL ratio were assessed. Background information on liver status—including cirrhosis, history of fatty liver disease, hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, and comorbid hepatic encephalopathy—was obtained from ultrasound reports and medical records. Antiviral treatment history was retrieved when available. These variables were analyzed to assess their distribution across HCC stage groups and their potential associations with lipid and inflammatory profiles.

A cross‐sectional study design was used to investigate the potential associations between lipid profiles and inflammatory markers and HCC progression, as well as to explore potential interactions between these factors. An independent cohort of 200 patients diagnosed with HCC was used to validate the key findings. The inclusion criteria, data collection procedures, and laboratory measurements were consistent with those of the primary study cohort. Statistical Analysis

Double data entry was performed to ensure data accuracy. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using univariate and multivariate skewness and kurtosis coefficients. Outliers were examined using box plots and retained in the analyses unless confirmed to be data entry errors. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non‐normally distributed variables were presented as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %). A total of 90 patients with HCC were included in the primary analysis, and an independent cohort of 200 patients with HCC was used to validate the key findings. For group comparisons, one‐way analysis of variance was applied to normally distributed quantitative variables, followed by the Scheffé post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations between lipid–inflammatory profiles and tumor stage. Binary logistic regression was used to examine the associations between potential factors and tumor stage, with adjustment for potential confounders in the multivariable model. Interaction analyses were conducted to explore the combined associations among lipid–inflammatory factors. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of lipid–inflammatory markers to discriminate tumor stage in both cohorts, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify discriminative performance. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 16.0 and R version 4.4.2. All tests were two‐sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Distribution of Baseline Characteristics of the Study Sample

Tumor staging data were available for 90 patients in the training cohort. Excluding 7 patients who were lost to follow‐up, recurrence information was available for 83 cases. Recurrence occurred in 18 patients (56.25%) in the non‐advanced stage group and in 28 patients (54.90%) in the advanced stage group. The prevalence of cirrhosis did not differ significantly between the non‐advanced and advanced stage groups (71.43% vs. 74.07%, p = 0.353). Fatty liver disease was present in 8.57% and 1.89% of patients in the two groups, respectively (p = 0.141). Regarding antiviral treatment, 34.29% of patients in the non‐advanced stage group and 25.93% in the advanced stage group received antiviral therapy (p = 0.397). No significant differences were observed in these liver‐related variables between groups. The proportions of patients with a history of hepatitis were 94.29% and 96.30% in the non‐advanced and advanced stage groups, respectively. The proportions of tumor vascular invasion were 58.82% and 48.15% in the two groups, respectively. The median durations of disease‐free survival and overall survival were 10 (2.50–35.00) months and 15 (10.00–24.50) months, respectively. The median number of tumors was 1 (1–1). The mean operative durations were 202.86 ± 78.71 min in the non‐advanced stage group and 189.36 ± 77.49 min in the advanced stage group. No statistically significant differences were observed in these demographic and disease‐related variables between the two tumor stage groups, as shown in Table 1.

Comparison of Lipid–Inflammatory Profile Patterns Between Tumor Stage Groups

The comparison of lipid–inflammatory profiles between tumor stage groups showed that two variables from the original lipid profile (HDL‐C and LDL‐C), three derived lipid indicators (non–HDL‐C, the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, and the TC‐to‐HDL ratio), and one derived lipid–inflammatory indicator (the NHR) differed significantly between groups. In addition, RDW‐SD and RDW‐CV levels were significantly different between tumor stage groups.

Regarding the original indicators, HDL‐C levels were lower in patients with non‐advanced stage than in those with advanced stage (p < 0.05). RDW‐SD and RDW‐CV levels were also lower in the non‐advanced stage group (both p < 0.05). Among the derived indicators, non–HDL‐C, the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and the NHR were higher in patients with non‐advanced stage (all p < 0.05), as shown in Table 2.

Correlation Analysis of Lipid–Inflammatory Profiles and Tumor Stage

Using data from 90 patients, correlation analysis confirmed associations between lipid–inflammatory profiles and tumor stage. In the present analysis, seven lipid–inflammatory variables were significantly correlated with tumor stage, including HDL‐C, LDL‐C, non–HDL‐C, the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, the NHR, and RDW‐CV. HDL‐C was positively correlated with tumor stage (r = 0.544, p < 0.05), indicating an association with advanced stage. RDW‐CV was also positively correlated with tumor stage (r = 0.265, p < 0.05). In contrast, non–HDL‐C, the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and LDL‐C were negatively correlated with tumor stage, with r values of −0.269, −0.568, −0.382, and −0.396, respectively (all p < 0.05). The NHR was also negatively correlated with tumor stage (r = −0.432, p < 0.05), as shown in Table 3.

Regression Model Analysis of the Association Between Lipid–Inflammatory Profiles and Tumor Stage

Given that complex lipid–inflammatory patterns were correlated with tumor stage (Table 3 and Figure 2), logistic regression analysis was performed to further examine these associations in the training cohort (n = 90). Four derived lipid–inflammatory indicators (non–HDL‐C, the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and the NHR) were included as independent variables, with tumor stage as the dependent variable. In univariate logistic regression analysis, all four indicators were significantly associated with tumor stage, with odds ratios (ORs) of 0.532, 0.193, 0.378, and 0.585, respectively (all p < 0.05).

Because the relationship between lipid and inflammatory profiles remains unclear and inflammation may confound lipid metabolism, the models were further adjusted for NHR, the only significant inflammation‐related variable identified in this study. After adjustment, the association between non–HDL‐C and tumor stage was no longer statistically significant, whereas the associations of the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio and the TC‐to‐HDL ratio with tumor stage remained significant. However, when all lipid and inflammatory variables were entered simultaneously into a multivariable logistic regression model, none remained independently associated with tumor stage (all p > 0.05), as shown in Table 4.

Exploratory interaction analyses were conducted to assess potential interactions between lipid and inflammatory profiles. Given the relatively small sample size and large standard errors observed in some models, these results should be interpreted cautiously. No significant evidence of interaction was observed, and detailed results are provided in Table S1.

Additionally, ROC analysis in the training cohort demonstrated that the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and the NHR showed moderate discriminative ability for HCC stage, with AUCs of 0.667, 0.682, and 0.707, respectively (Figure S1). HDL‐C yielded an AUC of 0.217, indicating an inverse discriminative direction, which is consistent with its positive correlation with advanced stage. In the external validation cohort (n = 200), the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, the TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and NHR showed AUCs of 0.790, 0.778, and 0.863, respectively, whereas HDL‐C showed an AUC of 0.149, again reflecting its inverse discriminative direction (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this cross‐sectional study, we investigated the associations between lipid–inflammatory profiles and HCC tumor stage. HDL‐related lipid parameters and NHR were asscociated with tumor staging in correlation, univariate, and partially adjusted analyses. ROC analysis further showed apparent discrimitive performance for the LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, TC‐to‐HDL ratio, and NHR, particularly in the external validation cohort. However, none of these markers remained statistically significant in the fully adjusted multivariable model. These findings suggest that lipid‐inflammatory profiles may reflect staging‐related metabolic and inflammatory alterations, but their ROC performance should be interpreted as apparent discriminative ability rather than evidence of independent predictive value.

In the present analysis, two variables from the original lipid profile were identified: HDL‐C and four HDL‐C–related derived indicators. HDL‐C is the primary lipoprotein responsible for transporting cholesterol to the liver for elimination [25, 26]. Low HDL‐C levels have been associated with an increased risk of cancer [27, 28]. For example, higher HDL‐C levels have been associated with improved overall and disease‐free survival in patients with colorectal cancer [28]. Moreover, lower HDL‐C levels may influence systemic inflammation by reducing its anti‐inflammatory effects, thereby permitting monocyte activation and amplification of the inflammatory response [29]. In addition, one derived inflammatory indicator—the NHR—was identified as being associated with tumor stage. Correlation and partially adjusted regression analyses provided partial support for a possible association between NHR and HCC stage; however, no independent predictors were identified in the fully adjusted multivariable model. Several biological mechanisms may explain the association between elevated NHR and tumor stage in HCC. Neutrophils contribute to tumor progression by releasing cytokines and chemokines that promote tumor growth and invasion [30, 31]. Furthermore, neutrophils facilitate epithelial–mesenchymal transition, which is closely associated with advanced tumor stage and aggressive tumor behavior [32]. Because NHR integrates inflammatory (neutrophil count) and metabolic (HDL‐C) components, it may provide a more comprehensive reflection of the tumor microenvironment than traditional single‐parameter markers. Compared with conventional inflammatory markers such as the NLR, NHR uniquely combines inflammatory and lipid components and may therefore better represent systemic metabolic and inflammatory states relevant to HCC progression. The NHR has emerged as a potential biomarker reflecting the interplay between systemic inflammation and lipid metabolism and may exert age‐dependent effects through multiple molecular pathways. Previous studies have reported associations between NHR and the prognosis or treatment response of several malignancies, including lung cancer and breast cancer [33, 34, 35]. Our group's previous studies have suggested a potential influence of lipid and inflammatory profiling on various cancers, including HCC, which may provide further insight for prevention and clinical management. As a readily available biomarker, NHR may reflect the combined influence of systemic inflammation and lipid metabolism in HCC. However, because NHR did not remain significant in the fully adjusted multivariable model, our findings do not establish NHR as an independent staging marker. Its possible supplementary role should therefore be regarded as exploratory and requires confirmation in larger, prospective studies with comprehensive adjustment for established clincal confounders. Our findings are directionally align with a previous study, which indicated that a high NHR is a significant risk factor for mortality and helps predict HCC prognosis [36], but the present study was focused on tumor staging rather than survival prediction.

In the present study, strong correlations were observed between NHR and adverse lipid profiles, characterized by elevated TC and decreased HDL‐C levels during HCC progression. Given the uncertain interrelationships among lipid–inflammatory variables, particularly NHR, regression‐based adjustments were performed to evaluate its potential confounding effect on HDL‐related lipid indicators. The results suggested that NHR may partially confound associations involving HDL‐related lipid parameters. Interaction analysis was also conducted to examine potential interactions among the lipid–inflammatory indicators; however, no statistically significant interactions were identified. Previous studies have shown that obesity induces low‐grade chronic inflammation through adipose tissue remodeling, resulting in alterations in infiltrating macrophage populations [37, 38]. Moreover, HDL‐C has been reported to be negatively correlated with inflammatory markers [39]. Dysregulated neutrophil activation may alter HDL‐C composition and function and promote neutrophil production [36, 40], thereby potentially contributing to HCC progression.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the single‐center design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the cross‐sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between lipid–inflammatory profiles and tumor stage. Third, because of the retrospective design, some clinical variables had incomplete records, and potential confounding factors could not be fully controlled. To address this issue, data on cirrhosis, fatty liver, and antiviral treatment history were supplemented, and no significant differences were observed between tumor stage groups, suggesting that these factors were unlikely to substantially confound the main findings. Fourth, although significant associations were observed in univariate and partially adjusted analyses, no independent predictors were identified in the fully adjusted multivariable model, suggesting that the observed associations and ROC performance may partly reflect shared information among correlated lipid and inflammatory markers, residual confoundings, and limited statistical power. Given the relative small training cohort and the number of covariates included in the fully adjusted model, the multivariable estimates may be unstable and should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the interaction analyses were limited by the relatively small sample size and large standard errors observed in some models; therefore, these results should be considered exploratory and are insufficient to support firm conclusions regarding subgroup‐specific associations or effect modification. Future studies with more comprehensive clinical data and larger multicenter cohorts are needed to validate and extend these findings.

In conclusion, our study found that HDL‐related lipid parameters and NHR were associated with HCC staging in correlation, univariate, and partially adjusted regression analyses, and showed apparent discriminative performance in ROC analysis. However, no marker remained independently associated with staging in the fully adjuated multivariable model. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory evidence that lipid‐inflammatory profiles may reflect staging‐related metabolic and inflammatory alterations, rather than as evidence of independent predictive value or clinical applicability. Larger prospective studies are required to determine whether these markers provide incremental information beyond established clinical and staging variables.

The horizontal and vertical axes represent 1‐Specificity and Sensitivity, respectively. The AUCs for LDL‐to‐HDL ratio, TC‐to‐HDL ratio, HDL‐C, and NHR were moderate discriminative indicators for HCC stage, with AUCs of 0.667, 0.682, 0.217, and 0.707, respectively (all p < 0.01). The diagonal solid yellow line represents the reference line (AUC = 0.5), indicating no discriminative ability.
