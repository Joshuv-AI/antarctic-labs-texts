Area‐Level Income Disparities and the Timing of First Ischemic Stroke: Insights From the 2019 National Inpatient Sample
Authors: Sonia Jahangiri, Ajith Vemuri, Alireza Vafaei Sadr, Nasibeh Azadeh‐Fard, Vida Abedi, Ramin Zand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocioeconomic status plays a crucial role in determining health outcomes, particularly the age at which individuals experience their first ischemic stroke. This study investigates the relationship between ZIP‐code level median income and the age at the first ischemic stroke, considering various demographic and clinical characteristics.MethodsWe identified patients >18 years old from the 2019 National Inpatient Sample database with a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke. Multivariate linear regression adjusted for sex, race, patient location, and comorbidities was used to estimate the impact of income on the age at the first ischemic stroke.ResultsAmong 94 074 patients, representing a national estimate of 470 370, the mean age at first stroke was 70.1±13.9 years, with 49.5% women. In the lowest (<$47 999) area‐level income group, the average age of the first stroke was 4.3 years lower than the highest ($82 000+) income group (68.1±13.8 versus 72.4±13.8). After adjusting for sociodemographic factors and comorbidities, the highest‐income individuals were 2.1 years older (95% CI, 2.3–2.5) at first ischemic stroke compared with those in the lowest‐income category. Area‐level income disparities also affected stroke age across racial groups and sexes. Specifically, first stroke occurred 2.6, 0.9, and 0.9 years earlier for White, Black, and Hispanic individuals, respectively. Women experienced a 2.3‐year delay compared with a 1.9‐year delay for men.ConclusionLower income levels are associated with the first incidence of stroke at a younger age, especially among White individuals and women, highlighting socioeconomic status‐related disparities in stroke incidence. Further studies to investigate the underlying factors are warranted.Keywords: age of onset, health inequalities, income disparity, ischemic stroke, socioeconomic statusSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke, Epidemiology, Race and Ethnicity, Social Determinants of Health

Stroke continues to be a significant contributor to both mortality and disability rates worldwide. 1 , 2 Social disparities in stroke incidence remain significant today. 3 , 4 , 5 Prior research has examined the relationship between stroke incidence and factors such as race, sex, age, and socioeconomic status (SES). 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Moreover, studies have identified significant inequities in race, sex, and income among patients with stroke, affecting outcomes such as in‐hospital mortality, hospitalization costs, and disabilities. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Previous research has also highlighted patient‐level disparities in the treatment of stroke based on sex, race and ethnicity, SES, and geographic location. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

The age at which an individual experiences their first stroke significantly affects their quality of life. 20 The consequence of stroke at a younger age has unique implications, as people are often at pivotal stages in their lives, managing families, demanding careers, and social interactions that may be impaired for decades. 21 , 22 Therefore, it is crucial to understand the factors that influence the age at which the first stroke occurs. This study aims to explore the relationship between individuals’ income level and the age at which they experience their first ischemic stroke in the United States. We further investigated the relationship between income level and the age of first ischemic stroke among different races and sexes. In this study, income level refers to ZIP code‐level median income, and this area‐based measure is used consistently throughout the article. To our knowledge, no study has previously examined the association between income level and the age at the first stroke incident.

METHODS

Data Availability

The National Inpatient Sample (NIS) data set can be obtained by researchers through the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project’s Central Distributor (https://hcup‐us.ahrq.gov/tech_assist/centdist.jsp) following the completion of the required Data Use Agreement training.

Study Design and Data Set

This study used data from the NIS, a comprehensive claims‐based database representing a stratified 20% sample of all community hospital admissions in the United States. 23 The NIS, maintained by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Projectof the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, is the largest available hospital discharge sample, covering >97% of the US population. 23 Each record in the NIS is assigned a sampling weight, allowing for the creation of national estimates representative of the broader population. The database excludes inpatient rehabilitation hospitals and long‐term acute care facilities, focusing only on Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project‐participating community hospitals. The NIS is a deidentified data set that complies with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act; therefore, this retrospective cohort study did not require ethical approval or institutional review board assessment. Researchers accessed the data after signing a data user agreement, ensuring responsible use of this resource for health care use analysis. We adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines to report our findings. 24

Eligibility Criteria

Our study used the 2019 data from NIS to investigate the incidence of ischemic strokes. We extracted information from all patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes. The specific ICD‐10‐CM codes applied in this study are detailed in Table S1. The percentage of missing values for each feature was assessed. Previous studies indicate that when a sample has <5% missing data, imputation does not significantly reduce biases. 25 Because all features had <3% missing data, we opted to remove the records with missing values. A complete‐case analysis was adopted as missing data for all features was <3%. Additionally, patients <18 years of age were excluded to restrict the analysis to adult ischemic stroke admissions. Figure S1 presents the flow chart detailing the data preprocessing steps in this study.

Comorbidities were identified using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Software Refined for ICD‐10‐CM. 26 This software generates comorbidity measures that identify preexisting medical conditions not directly related to the primary reason for hospitalization but those that significantly affect outcomes such as length of stay, hospital charges, and in‐hospital mortality. 26 To specifically analyze the incidence of first‐time strokes, we excluded patients with a history of cerebrovascular disease, as it is one of the comorbidities identified.

Measures

Patient‐level data, including sex, age, race, payment method, location, baseline comorbidities (eg, alcohol use, diabetes, and hypertension), and income level, were extracted from the NIS database for analysis. Given the well‐established impact of atrial fibrillation on ischemic stroke outcomes and the fact that it is not directly captured as a comorbidity in the data set, we identified atrial fibrillation cases by reviewing all secondary diagnosis fields and incorporated it as an additional variable in the analysis. The NIS categorizes median income into quartiles: low, low to middle, middle to high, and high, based on the estimated median household income of residents in the patient’s ZIP code. The median ZIP code income is shown to correlate strongly with individual income. 13

Patient outcomes, such as length of stay, in‐hospital mortality, and disposition, were assessed using NIS database information. Additionally, a comorbidity index was developed by combining 6 different comorbidities: diabetes (both with and without chronic complications), hypertension (both complicated and uncomplicated), obesity, peripheral vascular disease, renal failure, and valvular disease. The primary outcome of interest was the age at which the first stroke incident occurred. This continuous variable represents the patient’s age during hospital admission. In this study, age and age at first stroke are equivalent, as the analysis is limited to patients with a first‐time ischemic stroke hospitalization.

In the NIS data set, race and ethnicity are reported as a single combined variable (RACE), typically derived from hospital administrative records. 23 The NIS categorizes patients as White, Black, Hispanic, Asian or Pacific Islander, Native American, and Other; however, the Other category is not specifically defined in the data. In this study, individuals were classified according to the NIS‐defined categories: White, Black, Hispanic, Asian or Pacific Islander, and Other, which includes Native American individuals and those not otherwise specified. Although these categories reflect the structure of the data set, we acknowledge the limitations of this classification in capturing the full complexity and diversity of racial and ethnic identities.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as percentages, and continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD. Pearson’s chi‐square test was used for categorical data to compare variables across income levels, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to continuous data. The relationship between the income quartile and the age at first ischemic stroke was assessed using multivariate linear regression. Model 1 included only income level as a predictor. Subsequent models incorporated additional variables alongside income level. Model 2 was adjusted for income level, sex, race, and patient location. Model 3 included adjustments for income level and comorbidities. Model 4 accounted for all variables: income level, sex, race, patient location, and comorbidities. The payment method was excluded from the regression analysis due to its high correlation with patient age and income level. Unstandardized coefficients for all models are reported with 95% CIs. Data were stratified by race and sex for a secondary analysis. All statistical analyses were calculated using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA).

RESULTS

Patient Population and Characteristics

In total, 94 074 patients experienced an incident ischemic stroke in 2019, representing a national estimate of 470 370 cases. The mean age of the study population was 70.1±13.9 years, with 49.5% being women. Most patients (69.1%) were white, and 64.5% had Medicare coverage. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (81.1%), diabetes (35.3%), and chronic pulmonary disease (14.0%). In the study population, 30.6% were classified into the low‐income quartile, 25.7% into the low‐to‐middle quartile, 24.4% into the middle‐to‐high quartile, and 19.3% into the high‐income quartile. The average ages of patients based on income quartiles were 68.1±13.8, 70.1±13.9, 70.8±13.9, and 72.4±13.8 years, respectively (P value<0.001). Diabetes, hypertension, and obesity were more prevalent among low‐income patients (P‐value<0.001).

Relationship Between Income Level and Age Across Demographic Factors

The stratified analysis by income level and the average age across various characteristics, including race, sex, payment method, and location, are presented in Table S3. The average age of first stroke incidence among women in the lowest income group was 69.9±14.4 years, whereas in the highest income group, it was 74.7±13.9 years. In the lowest income group, the average age was 70.4±13.3 years for White individuals and 64.2±13.5 years for Black individuals. Conversely, in the highest income group, the average age for White individuals was 73.7±13.3 years, whereas for Black individuals, it was 66.3±14.0 years. Interestingly, the age pattern observed among patients with stroke does not align with that of the broader population. In the highest income group, the average age of the overall population is nearly identical in both densely populated and less populated areas.

Association of Sociodemographic Factors With First Stroke Incidence

A series of multivariate linear regression models were developed to assess the extent to which age differences in ischemic stroke incidence can be attributed to income level (Table 3). Unstandardized coefficients are reported in Table 3 and were used for interpretation. For further clarity, both standardized and unstandardized coefficients are presented in Table S4 of the supplementary material. In Model 1, patients in the highest income bracket had an average age that was 4.4 years higher (95% CI, 4.2–4.5) than those in the lowest income bracket. When further adjusted with sex, race, and patient location (Model 2), the coefficient of high income compared with the lowest income decreased to 3.0 years (95% CI, 2.9–3.1). When the model was adjusted with comorbidities (Model 3), the association remained statistically significant, with the highest income level associated with an average age that was 3.4 years higher (95% CI, 3.3–3.5) than that of patients in the lowest income bracket.

In a fully adjusted model that included sex, race, patient location, atrial fibrillation, and comorbidities (Model 4), patients in the highest income category had an average age at first ischemic stroke that was 2.1 years higher (95% CI, 2.0–2.2) compared with those in the lowest income category. Male sex was statistically associated with a younger age at stroke onset compared with female sex, with men experiencing strokes an average of 2.9 years earlier (95% CI, −3.0 to −2.8). Black, Hispanic, and Asian or Pacific Islander individuals had their first stroke at average ages 5.4, 4.1, and 2.6 years younger than White patients, respectively (P value <0.001). Comorbidities such as alcohol use, depression, diabetes with chronic complications, drug use, obesity, autoimmune conditions, valvular disease, and renal failure were also statistically associated with a younger age at stroke onset (P‐value <0.001), as depicted in Table 3.

For more detailed analysis, we applied the fully adjusted regression model to 3 different data sets: 1 stratified by race, 1 by sex, and 1 by both race and sex. The regression model results, including unstandardized coefficients for various races and among men and women, are presented in Tables 4 and 5. Results with both standardized and unstandardized coefficients are provided in Tables S5 and S6. The impact of the highest income category on the age at the first stroke incident, compared with the lowest income category, is significant across all races. This effect corresponds to 2.6, 0.9, 0.9, 1.9, and 2.2 years later for White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Other races. Additionally, men have their first stroke earlier than women in each of the 5 racial groups. The age difference is 2.9 years for White, 2.7 years for Black, 2.5 years for Hispanic, 3.1 years for Asian, and 3.6 years for individuals of other races. Furthermore, alcohol use, depression, drug use, and obesity are significantly associated with a younger age of stroke incidents across all racial groups.

DISCUSSION

This is the first large study to examine the relationship between income level and the age at first ischemic stroke using national‐level data from US hospitals. We demonstrated that the average age of the first stroke was 4.3 years lower among individuals with low income compared with the individuals from the highest income category. After adjusting for the social demographic factors and comorbidities, on average, the high‐income individuals were 2.1 years older at first ischemic stroke compared with those in the lowest‐income category. Alcohol use, depression, diabetes with chronic complications, drug use, obesity, autoimmune conditions, valvular disease, and renal failure were linked to a younger age at stroke incidents, with a more pronounced effect observed in women. The data showed that men experienced their first stroke an average of 2.9 years earlier than women. The income effect was stronger in women, with those in the highest income category having strokes 2.3 years later than those in the lowest. For men, the difference was 1.9 years. Furthermore, the age differences between the lowest and highest income categories were not uniform across all racial groups. Based on the adjusted model, individuals in the highest income category experience their first stroke at older ages compared with those in the lowest income category, with increases of 2.6 years for White, 0.9 years for Black, 0.9 years for Hispanic, 1.9 years for Asian, and 2.2 years for those classified as Other races.

The findings from this study underscore the significant influence of SES, particularly income level, on the age at which individuals experience their first ischemic stroke. The observed results are consistent with prior studies highlighting the role of income in access to health care, the prevalence of risk factors, and the ability to engage in preventive health behaviors. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 For example, individuals with lower incomes may have less access to high‐quality health care, healthy foods, and opportunities for physical activity, all of which are critical for stroke prevention. The fact that income‐related disparities were most pronounced among White individuals and women may reflect differences in the social and health care environments that these groups experience. Comparatively, studies on other chronic conditions, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes, have similarly demonstrated that lower SES is linked to earlier disease onset and worse outcomes. 31

Among individuals in the lowest income group, those residing in less populated areas (micropolitan counties with populations <50 000) have a higher average age of first ischemic stroke compared with individuals living in the most densely populated areas (counties within metro areas with populations of ≥1 million). This disparity could be attributed to lower air pollution, a better quality of life in less populated regions, 32 or a lower cost of living. However, in the highest income group, the trend reverses. This difference may be due to better access to health care facilities, hospitals, and superior medical equipment in densely populated regions, 33 as high‐income individuals usually have better health care access.

The analysis revealed a notable sex disparity in the age at the first ischemic stroke. Men experienced their first stroke an average of 2.9 years earlier than female. This finding aligns with existing literature, which suggests that biological, lifestyle, and behavioral differences between men and women may contribute to this disparity. 34 , 35 For instance, men are more likely to engage in risk factors such as smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, which are known to increase the likelihood of early stroke incidents. 36 This earlier onset of stroke in men highlights the need for targeted prevention strategies and public health interventions that address these sex‐specific risk factors.

Our findings indicate that individuals with certain comorbidities experience their first ischemic stroke significantly earlier than those without these conditions. Specifically, the presence of alcohol use, depression, diabetes with chronic complications, drug use, obesity, autoimmune conditions, valvular disease, and renal failure accelerates the incidence of the first ischemic stroke by 4.7, 2.1, 0.8, 10.2, 6.8, 0.4, 0.5 and 4.0 years, respectively. These results from our fully adjusted model are consistent with existing literature, 37 , 38 reinforcing the critical role of these comorbid conditions in increasing stroke risk. It is worth noting that some of these comorbidities, including alcohol use, drug use, and renal failure, have relatively low prevalence in the data set; therefore, the corresponding results should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, our study revealed that the impact of these comorbidities on the age of first stroke is more significant among women. Women with alcohol and drug use experience their first stroke 6.3 and 11.4 years earlier, respectively, compared with 4.2 and 9.5 years earlier among men with the same conditions. This finding is also consistent with previous studies. 39

The findings of this study carry important public health and policy implications. The significant association between lower income levels and earlier onset of ischemic stroke highlights the need for targeted prevention strategies and earlier screening efforts in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Tailored community‐based interventions, improved access to primary care, and health education initiatives could play a critical role in reducing these disparities. Similar income‐related disparities in the age of first stroke have also been reported in the United Kingdom, 7 suggesting that SES remains a key determinant of stroke risk across diverse health care settings.

This study used the largest nationally representative data set to compare socioeconomic disparities in the age of stroke onset, which is a significant strength. However, our study also has limitations. First, the only socioeconomic factor available in our data set was the median income for the patient’s ZIP code. Including additional socioeconomic factors could provide a more precise analysis. Second, our data set did not include information on lifestyle factors such as body mass index, diet, and physical activity, which are known to influence stroke risk and could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the disparities observed. Third, we did not have information on laboratory and medication. Nevertheless, we included many comorbidities and investigated their effect in this study. Additionally, the data set does not capture prehospital mortality. Although the proportion of patients with ischemic stroke who die before hospital admission is relatively low, the exclusion of these cases may still introduce some bias into the analysis.

Lastly, there is a possibility of reverse causality, as younger adults may naturally have lower incomes than older adults due to income generally increasing with age. However, in our fully adjusted model, the age difference between the highest‐ and lowest‐income groups was only 2.4 years, indicating that age‐related income differences alone are unlikely to account for this association. Moreover, although it is possible that the relationship between income and stroke onset is nonlinear, our analysis was constrained by the use of ZIP code‐level median income categories rather than individual‐level continuous income data. This may have obscured more intricate patterns in the association between income and stroke risk. Future studies with access to detailed continuous income data could apply more sophisticated modeling techniques to better capture these potential nonlinear effects.

CONCLUSIONS

We found that lower income is associated with significantly earlier stroke incidents. After adjusting for demographic factors and comorbidities, individuals in the lowest income category had stroke incidents 2.1 years earlier than individuals in the highest income category. This income‐related disparity was consistent across different racial groups or between men and women, with the most significant effects observed among women and White individuals. These findings emphasize the need for targeted public health interventions to address the underlying socioeconomic inequities, particularly in vulnerable populations.
