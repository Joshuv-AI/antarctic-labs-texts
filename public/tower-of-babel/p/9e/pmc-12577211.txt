Evaluation of injury rates in soccer players before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Giulia My, Giacomo Farì, Antonella Muscella, Santo Marsigliante
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate injury rates among Serie A soccer players across three seasons, highlighting the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsFifty-four professional soccer players in the Italian Serie were assessed over the course of three seasons, including the 2019/2020 season, which was affected by the pandemic. Injuries were categorized into muscular, skeletal, and articular, and were analyzed according to player roles (defenders, midfielders, and forwards). They were also stratified by the period of the competitive season: (1) preseason, (2) first half of the season, (3) second half of the season, and (4) end of the season.ResultsThe results indicate a sharp increase in injuries during the post-COVID-19 season (2020/2021), with increases in articular injuries (65%), skeletal injuries (120%), and muscular injuries (65%), compared to the previous seasons. Among player roles, defenders were the most affected by injuries, with a notable increase in the second half of the post-COVID season (36% more injuries compared to the pre-COVID season). The injury rate among midfielders increased by 46% in the post-COVID season compared to the pre-COVID season, with the highest number of injuries occurring at the end of the season.ConclusionsThis increased injury rate in defenders and midfielders, who often position themselves in the defensive zone, could be due to muscle mass loss. Further studies are needed to explore this aspect of injury epidemiology in soccer. Ultimately, careful, and gradual training is crucial for preventing injuries and maintaining the quality of soccer players’ performances.Keywords: COVID-19, Soccer players, Italian Serie A, Muscular injuries, Skeletal injuries

Introduction

Soccer is an intermittent sport involving lower-intensity actions (i.e., running and walking), interspersed with frequent high-intensity activities (i.e., sprinting, jumping, tackling), for which the player’s muscular strength, power, and endurance are important factors for high-level performance [1–3]. Joint and bone injuries are the most common type, ranging from 9 to 34%. Soccer injuries not related to physical contact with an opponent occur during training, whereas 12-28% of all injuries are due to physical contact with another athlete, even during preparation for competitive play [4]. In previous studies, the lower extremities represent the major site of reported injuries (87%), with sprains (19%) and strains (37%) being the most common types [5]. Injury rates tend to be more frequent in the presence of high training loads, and the ratio between acute and chronic training loads has been considered a significant predictor of injuries [6–9], although this concept has been questioned and relegated to a statistical artifact [10].

Some epidemiological studies on injuries in professional soccer have also highlighted position-specific differences, although limited research has focused specifically on Serie A players. In general, injuries in soccer players tend to vary according to position on the field, with defenders often suffering more injuries, especially to the lower limbs, due to the physical demands of tackling and positioning during defensive actions [11, 12]. Midfielders, involved in both offensive and defensive actions, tend to suffer injuries related to high-intensity activities and repeated sprinting [13]. In contrast, forwards suffer fewer injuries but are at greater risk of muscle strains due to explosive movements during attacking actions [14]. Furthermore, some studies have indicated that defenders and midfielders, who spend more time in the defensive zone, may be at greater risk due to loss of muscle mass, especially in the context of periods of physical deconditioning, such as during the COVID-19 lockdown [15].

In a previous study [16], we found that serum CK and LDH values were significantly higher during the post-Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) season. Furthermore, we also found variations in the percentage of body fat and in the testosterone/cortisol ratio, concluding that the period of forced suspension due to the pandemic and the consequent rapid resumption of all activities had influenced the physical and physiological state of professional soccer players [17].

COVID-19 is a viral infection that induces severe inflammatory and oxidative stress, damaging the pulmonary alveoli [18]. Furthermore, viral particles have been isolated from several body tissues, including skeletal muscles [19], causing muscle pain, fatigue, and weakness [20]. The mechanisms underlying muscle damage caused by COVID-19 are not clear, nor have the consequences of this damage been defined; however, damage to myocytes could be the result of excessive secretion of proinflammatory cytokines associated with oxidative stress. Considering that when soccer players train or play matches without being in optimal muscular condition, the risk of injuries may increase in terms of frequency and quantity [21, 22], the COVID-19 pandemic may have led to an increase in injury rates.

In this regard, recent studies have reported an increase in injuries among English Premier League soccer players and Italian Serie A soccer players during the championship affected by COVID-19 or during the post-COVID-19 competitive season, compared to previous seasons [1, 23].

The lockdowns led to several consequences. First, there were significant reductions in the specificity, intensity, frequency, and duration of athletic training [24, 25]. Athletes at all levels, from adolescents to professionals, struggled to maintain sport-specific conditioning. Data from 12,526 athletes across six continents showed a shift towards general fitness and health, with less focus on skills, technique, flexibility, balance, muscle strength, and power [24]. Second, reopening after a disrupted season created a compressed timeframe for athletes to prepare for competitions. Both athletes and officials risked restarting with deficits in strength and flexibility [26, 27], especially for movements like sprinting or throwing. Crowded competition schedules further altered performance. For example, Polish and Croatian soccer players covered greater distances at low-intensity running and had fewer accelerations/decelerations than before COVID-19 [28, 29]. Although performance improved as the season progressed [30], the initial lower intensity increased injury risk. Tactical disorganization and reduced player interaction also affected team sports. Third, there was a rise in musculoskeletal injuries upon returning to sport, especially in the first few weeks [31, 32]. Physical deconditioning occurs rapidly with reduced training [33, 34]. Cardiovascular and muscular systems deteriorate quickly, with declines in myocardial mechanics, stroke volume, oxygen delivery, and blood volume. Skeletal muscle protein turnover, strength, and mass, as well as tendon and ligament functionality, also decrease within 1 to 2 months of detraining [27, 35]. Strength and power performance can decline by 5.1–6.6% in just two weeks [36]. Additionally, metabolic processes and hormone production slow with less training stimulus [37]. Consequently, musculoskeletal injury rates increased across all levels of competition.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the incidence and injuries types that occurred among professional soccer players in the Italian Serie A during the COVID-19 pandemic and in the year immediately before and after it. The novelty and importance of this study lie in the fact that we made comparisons over three soccer seasons, with the central one characterized by the COVID-19 pandemic (2019/2020). The injuries occurring over these three years were further stratified based on the period of the competitive season as follows: (1) pre-season, (2) first-half season, (3) second-half season, and (4) end season. Furthermore, all injuries were evaluated globally and also divided into three categories based on whether they affected the muscles, the skeleton, or the joints. Finally, injuries were correlated to three player roles: defenders, midfielders, and forwards.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The primary objective of this study was to assess injury incidence and patterns among professional soccer players across three seasons, with a specific focus on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown. Secondary objectives included analyzing changes in injury rates before and after the lockdown and exploring potential factors contributing to the observed increase in injuries, particularly among specific player roles. We carried out this retrospective study in some Italian professional soccer teams (Serie A) during the 2018–2019, 2020–2021 (COVID), and 2021–2022 seasons. Such season are, respectively, the one before, during and after the COVID-19 lockdown pandemic (Fig. 1). The 2019–2020 season suffered an interruption in performance from 9 March 2020 to 3 May 2020, then ended on 2 August 2020, while the athletic preparation period of the 2020–2021 season started only 3 weeks after the end of the COVID-19 season.

A timeline of the 2018–2019, 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 soccer seasons surrounding the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown

All players were evaluated four times during every season, which corresponds to the athletic preparation period (weeks 0–4; weeks 0–2 for 2020/2021 season), first-half season (after 12 weeks of training), second-half season (after 32 weeks of training), and end season (Fig. 1).

While training methodologies may vary slightly between clubs, all teams adhere to the same off-season and pre-season calendar, ensuring a uniform timeframe for preparation across the league.

We included players from different positions, with the clustering based on their primary roles on the field as reported by their respective teams, ensuring consistency in positional classification. All players remained with the same club and played in the same position across the three seasons, resulting in minimal differences in injury susceptibility based on their position.

All participants met the following criteria: they had played for the same team across the three seasons considered, and none of them had contracted COVID-19. The players were from various Italian Serie A teams. The soccer players were further selected in order to have 18 defenders, 18 midfielders, and 18 forwards. Exclusion criteria included players who had contracted COVID-19 during the study period, as their recovery and potential lingering effects of the virus could influence the results. Additionally, players who were unavailable due to long-term injuries or those who had changed teams during the three seasons were excluded to ensure consistency in the analysis. These criteria were applied to limit the confounding effects of COVID-19 infection and other variables, ensuring that the results more accurately reflect the impact of the pandemic on injury rates across all positions.

Anthropometric evaluation

The anthropometric assessments always were carried out in the early morning, before each workout, as previously published [16]. Specifically, height and weight were measured using calibrated scales (e.g., Tanita or InBody) and body fat percentage was assessed using Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis with standardized procedures. To ensure consistency, the same trained professionals conducted all assessments. Although equipment and methods may vary slightly between clubs, all measures were carefully standardized, allowing for reliable comparisons between players.

Training program

Since the players belonged to different teams, it should be noted that the information provided regarding the number and distribution of training sessions reflects general trends observed across the teams rather than precise schedules. However, in general, all players performed a 5–15-minute warm-up, followed by approximately 90-minute training sessions. When one match was played weekly (Sunday), training occurred Tuesday through Saturday after resting on Monday. In weeks with two matches (Wednesday and Sunday), training was held on the remaining days, excluding Saturday. During the pre-season period, which lasted four weeks each season (two weeks for the 2020/2021 season), training comprised both strength and conditioning sessions, as well as tactical and technical sessions. These sessions were held daily, except on Wednesdays and Sundays. In 2020–2021, a Saturday unloading session was added due to the lack of rest between seasons. During lockdown, home training was implemented to maintain physical performance through individualized programs from the team coaches. Coaches ensured that these programs focused on maintaining key physical attributes such as strength, flexibility, and endurance.

Injury data collection

This observational study followed the STROBE guidelines. The soccer teams’ medical staff diagnosed, classified, and recorded all injuries, in agreement with the classification system developed by the International Olympic Committee (IOC) medical committee [38]. Furthermore, in each injury tissue affected were reported. Injury diagnoses were supported by clinical methods depending on the type of injury. IOC system ranks injuries based on their affected tissue (e.g., muscular, skeletal, or articular) and severity (e.g., minor, moderate, or severe). Each diagnosis documented was supported by a combination of clinical methods, including physical examinations, medical imaging (e.g., MRI, X-rays, or ultrasound), and, when necessary, laboratory tests (e.g., blood markers for muscle damage). This standardized classification system ensured that injuries were consistently categorized across the three seasons studied, which facilitated meaningful comparisons. All investigators involved in the study were trained in the IOC injury classification system to reduce diagnostic variability. As a result, any potential differences in how injuries were assessed or categorized were minimized, increasing the reliability and comparability of the data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistic was used to summarize and describe the main features of dataset. Student’s paired t-test was used to compare the means of two related groups, specifically for anthropometric measures such as weight and BMI between seasons.

To evaluate significant differences in injury rates between competitive seasons, a Poisson mixed-effects model was employed. This model analyzed the number of injuries as a function of player roles and seasons while accounting for repeated cases, as individual players could experience multiple injuries within the same season.

The Poisson mixed-effects model approach provides a strong way to analyze longitudinal injury data, also allowing for the evaluation of the impact of role and season on injury rates and taking into consideration the dependency between injuries within the same player. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad PRISM 8 software. The results were considered significant at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.05).

Injury incidence in soccer players

Differences in injury rates were observed between the pre-COVID-19 season (2018–2019, 34 total injuries), the pandemic season (2019–2020, 33 total injuries), and the post-COVID-19 season (2020–2021, 58 total injuries, Fig. 3). Notably, there was a substantial increase in injuries during the post-COVID-19 season (2020–2021) compared to the previous two seasons. Muscle injuries had the highest incidence in all seasons, while the post-COVID-19 season also experienced a rise in joint (+ 65%) and skeletal (+ 120%) injuries (Fig. 3).

Injury incidence during the 2018–2019, 2019–2020 (COVID) and 2020–2021 seasons. Injuries are represented as the total number and as the number of muscular, articular and skeletal injuries

This increase in injury incidence, evident in the post-COVID-19 season, highlights the potential effects of disrupted training and competition programs. In particular, the significant increase in joint and skeletal injuries suggests that the detraining period may have led to a decline in physical condition, causing players to be more vulnerable to these types of injuries.

For each season, four different time points were analyzed: pre-season, first half of the season, second half of the season, and end of the season. In all three seasons, the incidence of injuries generally increased across these time points, except for the second half of the 2019/2020 season (COVID season), where the incidence decreased due to the COVID-19 lockdown that halted sports and competitive activities (Fig. 4).

Injury incidence during the 2018–2019, 2019–2020 (COVID), and 2020–2021 seasons, during four different time points: preseason (T0), first-half season (T1), second half-season (T2), and end season (T3). Total (A), muscular (B), articular (C), and skeletal (D) number of injuries

During the pre-season, only 4 and 5 players sustained injuries in the 2018/2019 and 2019/2020 (COVID-19) seasons, respectively, whereas this number rose to 10 in the 2020/2021 season. At all-time points, the incidence of injuries was higher in the 2020/2021 season compared to previous seasons (Fig. 4).

This indicates that the post-COVID-19 recovery phase was marked by a higher frequency of injuries throughout the entire competitive year, possibly due to the combined effects of rapid physical reconditioning and compressed playing schedules.

The Poisson mixed-effects model suggest a significant increase in the number of injuries in 2020–2021 (p < 0.001) compared to 2018–2019 and 2019–2020, with significant variations across the periods of the season. Injuries notably increased in the 2020 season, in the T1 (p < 0.05), T2 (p < 0.01), and especially T3 (p < 0.001) periods, compared to previous seasons. These findings highlight the importance of considering both the periods of the season and the championship years when analyzing injury data, as the disruption caused by the pandemic appears to have long-lasting effects on injury rates.

Positional differences in injury incidence in soccer players

Differences in the overall number of injuries were observed between positional roles, with defenders recording the highest rate in all seasons analyzed (Fig. 5). Thus, defenders may be more prone to injuries due to the physical demands of their position, including higher levels of contact, tackling, and defensive sprints.

For all positional categories (defender, midfielder, and forward), muscular injuries were the most common, while skeletal injuries were less frequent (Fig. 5).

Muscle injuries were more frequent throughout the 2020/2021 season and across all positional roles (Figs. 6A and 7A, and 8A). The Poisson mixed-effects model suggests that there are significant differences in the number of injuries across the seasons, with a significant increase in 2020–2021, especially for defenders and midfielders (p > 0.001, Figs. 6A and 7A, and 8A).

For defenders (Fig. 6), the mixed-effects Poisson model suggests a significant increase (p = 0.020) in the number of injuries in period T3 compared to T0 in the 2020–2021 season, while other variations were not statistically significant. This implies that, overall, the number of injuries tends to increase towards the end of the season, but the differences between seasons are not strong enough to be considered statistically significant, except for the increase observed in period T3.

Similarly, for midfielders (Fig. 7), injuries increase towards the end of the season, with a particularly significant increase observed in the 2020–2021 season compared to previous seasons (p = 0.032). Precisely, there is a significant increase in injuries in period T3 compared to T0 (p < 0.001), which is especially pronounced in the 2020–2021 season.

This increase in injury rates among defenders and midfielders towards the end of the season could be attributed to accumulated fatigue or increased intensity of competition in the closing stages, combined with the effects of post-pandemic physical deconditioning, which led to a higher incidence of injuries towards the end of the 2020–2021 competitive year.

Finally, for forwards (Fig. 8), results indicate that although there are numerical differences in the number of injuries between years and periods, these differences are not statistically significant. This may suggest that while the data still shows a trend towards higher injuries, the positional role of forwards, which generally involves less contact and more sprinting or positional play, has been less impacted by the disruptions seen in the 2020-21 season.

Discussion

Soccer has high injury rates, especially in the lower extremities, due to its physical demands. Lockdowns forced changes in training methods, and our findings show increased injury rates for elite Italian players in the 2020/2021 season, likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic disrupting their routines.

As mentioned in the summary above, soccer is associated with high injury rates, which significantly influence a player’s availability [39]. Injuries are a major concern in scientific studies, as highlighted by numerous publications [40]. Most injuries occur in the lower extremities due to the specific demands of the game [40, 41]. Recent studies have shown an increased risk of muscle injuries in soccer players who have been affected by SARS-CoV-2 infection [41–46]. Our findings show that general injury rates increased during the 2020/2021 season for players in the Italian elite compared to the two previous seasons. This increase is likely a consequence of the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted all aspects of society, including sports [47]. Lockdowns forced both amateur and professional athletes to change their training habits, particularly in terms of settings and methods [48]. It was previously demonstrated that serum CK and LDH, markers of muscle damage and inflammation, along with cortisol, a stress marker, were significantly elevated during the post-COVID-19 season. This indicates that the forced suspension period and the abrupt return to professional competition and training affected the physical and physiological condition of professional soccer players [17, 18].

The Royal Spanish Soccer Federation, upon the resumption of soccer activities after the COVID-19 pandemic in June 2020, recommended a two-phase mesocycle for the first four weeks of training [49]. This plan included a short retraining phase followed by a phase dedicated to full soccer-specific performance recovery. They emphasized the individualization of training, as each player could have experienced different levels of deconditioning, depending on previous confinements or COVID-19 infection. Another important concern was the gradual and progressive retraining for power, endurance, high-intensity intermittent exercise capacity, and speed during the pre-season phase to prevent musculoskeletal injuries [50, 51]. However, the official schedules and planning of all professional soccer leagues were extremely intense, leaving little room for a gradual resumption of competitive commitments. Consequently, the injury rates for the season following the pandemic were higher than in previous seasons, despite some preventive measures such as increasing substitutions from three to five per game and introducing refreshment pauses at the 30th and 75th minutes of matches [52].

Thus, our results align with the available scientific literature on this issue.

An interesting epidemiological study on the German Soccer League showed that the return to sport following the COVID-19 lockdown led to a statistically significant increase in injury frequency for all players, regardless of their roles [53].

In the Bundesliga, injury rates during the 2020/2021 post-COVID-19 season also increased due to detraining, rapid resumption of activities, and congested match schedules, similar to findings in other contexts [54]. However, Krutsch et al. [55] and Thron et al. [52] reported no significant changes in injury incidence during the pre- and post-COVID-19 break of the 2019/2020 season, likely due to recovery opportunities and the introduction of increased substitutions. Consistent with our findings, muscle and tendon injuries were the most common in the Bundesliga, with the thigh, knee and ankle consistently identified as the main affected areas [54].

Premier League athletes also showed an increase in injury rates during the 2020/2021 season, perhaps demonstrating a link between congestion training and athlete injuries. Again, muscle and tendon injuries were the most common, particularly affecting areas such as the thigh, knee, and ankle [23].

The global injury rate increased more than threefold following the COVID-19 lockdown, with the most reported injuries affecting the players’ muscles. There is a known correlation between indirect muscle injuries and SARS-CoV-2 infection. A retrospective observational cohort study by the Italian Sports Medicine Federation found that soccer players who had contracted SARS-CoV-2 had a 36% greater risk of indirect muscle injuries compared to those who had never been infected [43]. The authors hypothesized that the infection affects muscle function by compromising blood capillaries, thereby limiting oxidative metabolic pathways. This alteration in the aerobic metabolic system could lead to muscle fatigue, a predisposing factor for indirect muscle injury, especially in high-intensity intermittent sports like soccer [56].

Our study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines to ensure the transparent and accurate reporting of observational research. By adhering to these guidelines, methods and results are structured to promote clarity, reproducibility, and harmonized injury data collection protocols. The uniqueness of our findings is that not only muscle injuries but all musculoskeletal injuries, including joint and bone injuries, significantly increased. A review published in 2023 partially confirmed these data [23]. The authors demonstrated that more injuries occurred during the COVID-19 season (2020–2021), with the first injury occurring earlier than in the previous two seasons. This increase was seen in both muscle injuries and joint injuries, particularly ligament ruptures.

Our results can be explained by two interconnected causes. First, COVID-19 has led to prolonged physical deconditioning in soccer players who had the infection due to its negative cardiorespiratory effects. Physical exertion tolerance was reduced for many months post-infection, increasing the overall risk of injuries. This is supported by a study by Maestro et al. [57], which showed that injury frequency in professional soccer players who contracted SARS-CoV-2 was significantly higher than in the same players before the infection. Additionally, these players had a higher incidence of match-related injuries compared to those who had not contracted the virus [23]. The first cause applies to soccer teams as a whole, including players who had COVID-19 and those who did not. However, this cause does not apply to our specific case, as we selected only players who had not contracted a symptomatic infection for this study. Another cause is the challenge in gradually restoring players’ full athletic performance due to the intense and closely scheduled commitments following the 2019/2020 season. Despite recommendations for gradual retraining, athletes often faced sudden overloads, increasing the risk of general musculoskeletal injuries, both muscular and osteoarticular. This issue was highlighted in studies published before the pandemic, noting that the risk of overtraining was already present among professional soccer players, and the pandemic exacerbated this problem. Bengtsson et al. [58] provided an 11-year follow-up study of UEFA Champions League injuries, finding that competition congestion was associated with increased muscle injury [58].

The need for a longer recovery period between consecutive official matches was recognized even before the pandemic. Mohr et al. [59] stated that at least 72 h of recovery between matches is necessary to prevent injuries. This guideline should have been considered and potentially extended during the 2020/2021 season. The relationship between injuries and the time of the season shows that most injuries tend to occur towards the end of the season. This can be explained by the fact that the end of the season is typically a period of maximum overload, resulting from the fatigue accumulated throughout the entire season. The accumulation of matches, along with the tiredness and stress experienced by players, likely contributes to the increased injury rates at this time [51, 58, 60].

However, in this study, an exception occurred during the second half of the 2019/2020 season, when the injury rate dropped to 7% because of the COVID-19 lockdown and the forced stoppage of competitive activities. The increased injury rates observed towards the end of the 2020/2021 season can be attributed to a combination of these typical factors, such as the physical overload associated with the final stages of the season, and the effects of deconditioning during the lockdown period. Considering the anthropometric data we presented, which show higher body weight and BMI in the COVID-19 season (2019–2020), along with a decrease in Fat-Free Mass (FFM) and an increase in body fat percentage (BFP) in the 2020/2021 season, it is plausible that the players’ physical condition was altered post-lockdown. These changes likely impacted their recovery and ability to handle the physical demands of the post-COVID season, contributing to the increased injury rates.

Regarding player positions, our findings indicate that defenders experience the highest incidence of injuries, particularly in the latter part of the post-COVID season. However, this topic remains contentious. The existing literature lacks consensus on which position poses the greatest injury risk, with conflicting findings for defenders, midfielders, and strikers [12, 58, 61]. Goalkeepers, due to their less dynamic role, generally experience fewer injuries. Some studies suggest that player position does not significantly affect injury risk, while others present varying results [58]. Our hypothesis is that defenders are more prone to injuries because they engage in numerous high-intensity activities such as tackles and jumps to thwart opponents [62]. The period of detraining induced by lockdowns likely exacerbated this vulnerability by causing muscle mass loss (atrophy) and a reduction in fast-twitch muscle fibers among defenders. This scenario may also extend to midfielders, who frequently operate in defensive roles. However, further research is essential to delve deeper into this intriguing facet of injury epidemiology in soccer.

There are several limitations to consider in this research. First, we relied on a single database that has been previously validated and referenced for injury data collection, which may introduce biases due to potential underreporting, omissions, or misinterpretations of injury records. Second, our study focused solely on injuries occurring sustained during the sports seasons and specifically analyzing the impact of the COVID-19 lockdown, without accounting for other external factors that could influence injury rates, such as changes in individual or team-level dynamics.

While our study provides insights into the incidence and patterns of injuries among professional soccer players, it is limited by the absence of data on injury severity and burden.

Such information, including details on time lost from training or competition, as well as the medical and rehabilitation efforts required, would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of injuries on player performance and team dynamics. Future studies should aim to include these parameters to provide a more holistic picture of injury epidemiology in professional soccer. This would enable the assessment of not only the frequency and type of injuries but also their broader implications for athletes, teams, and league management. Including metrics such as the total burden of injuries and their severity could significantly enhance our understanding of how to better prevent and manage injuries in this high-performance setting. Finally, we acknowledge that team differences in training methodologies may contribute to variability in injury rates, which we did not address in this study.

Future research should aim to correlate specific training regimens with injury outcomes, providing a greater understanding of how these variations influence injury epidemiology. Additionally, longitudinal studies investigating the long-term impact of physical deconditioning after periods of enforced inactivity, such as during COVID-19 lockdowns, would help better delineate residual effects on athletes. Addressing these limitations would strengthen the interpretation of the findings and improve their applicability to injury prevention strategies.

To reduce the risk of injury during periods of increased physical demand, specific load management strategies and graded physical preparation should be employed. Individualized preseason conditioning programs, tailored to address specific player weaknesses if they result from prolonged inactivity, are essential. These programs should emphasize progressive training intensity, incorporating exercises designed to improve strength, flexibility, and endurance while carefully monitoring workload. Additionally, adjustments to game scheduling are critical to ensure sufficient recovery periods between games. Research suggests maintaining a minimum of 72 h between games to allow for adequate physical and physiological recovery [63].

Such measures not only mitigate injury risks but also optimize performance, particularly in seasons with condensed schedules, as has been observed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Incorporating these strategies into league planning and team training could significantly improve player health and availability throughout the season.

Conclusions

Since soccer is associated with high injury rates, which affect player availability, injury is a significant concern in scientific studies. During the 2020/2021 season, injuries increased due to pandemic-related disruptions that altered athletes’ training habits, physical deconditioning, and recovery time. Given these challenges, we strongly recommend that health policies for athletes—especially during pandemic periods—be updated to include more specific guidelines for injury prevention. These policies should address the residual effects of long interruptions in training and competition, with particular attention to gradual return-to-play protocols that minimize injury risk, safeguarding athletes’ health and ensuring optimal performance during high-demand periods.
