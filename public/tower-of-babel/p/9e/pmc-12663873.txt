Distinct Profiles of Choriocapillaris Involvement in Extensive Macular Atrophy With Pseudodrusen-Like Appearance and Geographic Atrophy
Authors: Alberto Quarta, Giulia Corradetti, Francesco Romano, Andrea Trinco, Alessandro Feo, Federico Corvi, Claudia Nardi, Mai Alhelaly, Rouzbeh Abbasgholizadeh, Shinichiro Chujo, Marko Popovic, Ceren Soylu, Yu-Chien Chung, Hyun Duck Kwak, Rayna Rattu, Muneeswar G. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo quantitatively compare choriocapillaris flow deficit percentages (CCFD%) between eyes with extensive macular atrophy with pseudodrusen-like appearance (EMAP) and geographic atrophy (GA) secondary to age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and to identify predictive factors for CCFD% at the atrophy margin.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 53 eyes (27 with GA and 26 with EMAP). CCFD% were computed within a 500-µm-wide marginal zone (CCFD%_M) and beyond the margin (CCFD%_BM) using compensated en face optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) images. Atrophy-to-fovea distance (AFD) was quantified by pixel-wise Euclidean analysis.ResultsEyes with EMAP demonstrated significantly higher CCFD% compared to GA both at the atrophy margin (CCFD%_M, P < 0.05) and beyond (CCFD%_BM, P < 0.01). In univariable analysis, belonging to the GA group was associated with a 5.9-unit reduction in CCFD%_M, reflecting a substantial preservation of choriocapillaris (CC) perfusion in GA relative to EMAP. AFD was greater in EMAP (2.35 ± 0.56 mm) than GA (1.74 ± 0.55 mm; P = 0.014) suggesting more eccentric positioning of the lesions in the former. A multivariable mixed-effects model confirmed disease subtype (EMAP vs. GA) as an independent determinant of CC impairment with EMAP associated with a significantly higher CCFD%_M (P < 0.01).ConclusionsCC impairment is more pronounced in EMAP than in GA, with EMAP exhibiting more extensive and diffuse CCFD, particularly at the atrophy margins. This pattern may reflect a more aggressive vascular compromise in EMAP. These findings underscore a distinct CC pathoanatomy between the two conditions, with implications for disease progression and therapeutic targeting.

The choriocapillaris (CC) supports the metabolic demands of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and outer retina, and its dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a critical driver in the pathophysiology of macular atrophy.1–3 In geographic atrophy (GA) associated with age-related macular degeneration (AMD), optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) has consistently demonstrated flow deficits in the CC surrounding atrophic lesions, implicating this region in disease expansion and photoreceptor loss.2,4,5

In 2009, Hamel et al.6 first described extensive macular atrophy with pseudodrusen-like appearance (EMAP) as a distinct clinical entity of macular atrophy. EMAP typically presents in younger patients and is characterized by bilateral, symmetric, well-circumscribed macular atrophy surrounded by diffuse reticular pseudodrusen, notably in the absence of conventional soft drusen or pigmentary abnormalities typically seen in AMD.6 Although EMAP and GA secondary to AMD share several morphologic features—most notably, outer retinal and RPE loss—the differences in the pathogenesis of these two conditions remain incompletely understood. Whether EMAP constitutes a distinct disease or represents a phenotypic variant within the broader spectrum of AMD is still a matter of ongoing investigation. Notably, histopathological correlation is currently lacking, and metabolomic analyses are still ongoing.

OCTA has provided valuable insights into CC dysfunction in both conditions.2,7 Rajabian et al.7 demonstrated widespread CC flow impairment in EMAP eyes when compared to age-matched controls, supporting the hypothesis of vascular insufficiency as a contributor to outer retinal atrophy. However, to date, no studies have directly compared CC flow characteristics between EMAP and GA, particularly in regions adjacent to the atrophic margins—where tissue remodeling may be most dynamic and clinically informative.8,9 Furthermore, potential factors that could predict the extent of CC flow deficits at the edge of atrophy have not been thoroughly investigated in either disease. The aim of this study was to quantitatively compare CC flow deficit percentage (CCFD%) within a defined marginal (or junctional) zone between eyes with EMAP and GA and to identify clinical and anatomical factors associated with CCFD% at the atrophy border.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional, retrospective study included patients diagnosed with either GA or EMAP who were imaged at Ospedale Luigi Sacco (Milan, Italy) or Doheny Eye Institute (Pasadena, CA, USA) between January 2021 and January 2025. The study was conducted in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and Italian Bioethics regulations and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because of the design of the study.

Inclusion criteria were (1) a confirmed diagnosis of EMAP or GA secondary to AMD based on clinical examination and multimodal imaging interpretation; (2) clear ocular media and absence of significant refractive error (<|6| diopters); and (3) high-quality macular swept-source OCTA (SS-OCTA) scans, defined by a signal strength greater than 7 and absence of motion artifacts.

Exclusion criteria included (1) presence of ocular or systemic conditions potentially confounding image analysis or retinal morphology (e.g., glaucoma, foveal hypoplasia, diabetic retinopathy); (2) history of intraocular surgery other than uncomplicated cataract extraction at least 6 months prior to the study visit; (3) clinical or historical evidence of macular neovascularization (MNV) or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) intravitreal injection; (4) eyes with evidence of non-exudative MNV identified on a customized en face angiographic slab spanning the RPE–Bruch's membrane (BM) complex and confirmed by the presence of a flow signal on structural OCT; and (5) coexisting vitreoretinal interface abnormalities (e.g., epiretinal membrane or vitreomacular traction) with the potential to distort retinal architecture or compromise visual acuity (VA). When available, fluorescein angiography and indocyanine green angiography were also reviewed to corroborate the absence of MNV. Both eyes were included in the analysis if they independently fulfilled eligibility criteria.

Study Population and Procedures

To be included in this analysis, all subjects were required to have undergone a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation, including detailed medical history collection, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessment, intraocular pressure measurement, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, ultra-widefield fundus photography (Optos, Dunfermline, UK), spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT), blue-light fundus autofluorescence (B-FAF; 488 nm), and 6 × 6-mm SS-OCTA scans centered on the fovea (PLEX Elite 9000; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). The PLEX Elite system employs a tunable light source laser with a central wavelength of 1050 nm and an acquisition speed of 100,000 A-scans per second.

GA was defined according to the Classification of Atrophy Meeting (CAM) criteria on SD-OCT as the presence of complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (cRORA).10 EMAP diagnosis and staging were identified based on previously published diagnostic criteria11 by two independent masked graders (FR, AQ); another grader (SS) resolved discrepancies. This included evaluation of multimodal imaging studies, exclusion of genetic phenocopies, and electrophysiology as applicable to each case.

OCT Analysis

Macular 6 × 6-mm OCT volume scans were analyzed using ZEISS OCT-A software 2.1.0.55513 by two independent, masked ophthalmologists (FR, AQ). Each scan was reviewed for proper foveal centration, integrity of automated layer segmentation, and absence of significant motion or acquisition artifacts. Layer segmentation was manually corrected if the segmentation software identified them inaccurately. Discrepancies between graders were adjudicated by a senior reviewer (SS) to reach consensus. Scans were excluded if they (1) demonstrated atrophic lesion or surrounding marginal ring that extended beyond the OCTA scan boundaries; (2) identified overlapping of marginal rings among neighboring lesions in multifocal cases that compromised spatial independence; or (3) indicated EMAP cases with non-neovascular subretinal fibrosis in the marginal zone12 (Fig. 1). Only discrete, non-overlapping multifocal atrophic regions were included in the quantitative analysis. Eligible scans were evaluated for the presence of subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs), defined as three or more discrete hyperreflective lesions located above the RPE on a single B-scan, with either dome-shaped or sharply peaked morphology.13 The presence of SDDs was recorded as a categorical variable (present/absent) confirmed on at least one additional en face imaging modality (B-FAF or infrared)14 and considered in subsequent flow deficit analyses in the marginal zone. The borders of the GA lesion were defined by recognizing the choroidal hypertransmission defect (hyperTD) on a previously described en face OCT slab.15,16 Regions of RPE loss were visualized using structural en face OCT images generated from a sub-RPE slab positioned between 64 µm and 400 µm beneath BM.17,18 Each lesion was manually segmented on the en face OCT image and cross-validated using corresponding structural B-scans.2 Segmentation boundaries for the en face slab were manually inspected and corrected if necessary.

OCTA Analysis

The en face CC slab was defined as a 16-µm layer extending from 4 µm beneath BM as per standard segmentation practices for CC visualization.20 OCTA slabs were batch processed using a previously validated custom-developed algorithms in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) for compensation analysis and ImageJ for flow deficits quantification.20–23 The en face OCTA images were compensated by pixel-wise multiplication with the inverse of the corresponding structural OCT image, extracted from identically segmented volumes. Compensated OCTA images were binarized using the Phansalkar local thresholding method with a 23.4-µm radius.20,21 CC regions directly underlying major retinal vessels were excluded from analysis to further reduce compensation inaccuracies deriving from projection artifacts.20,21 To improve measurement reliability, focal areas exhibiting signal attenuation abnormalities (e.g., hyporeflective drusen cores/calcified drusen) were manually excluded from analysis in accordance with previously described protocols.19,24,25 The hyperTDs were masked according to the methodology described in previous studies.17,19,26 In five EMAP cases, despite manual segmentation, visible large choroidal vessels were identified due to upward migration in areas of significant rarefaction of CC; the vessels were manually excluded using a customized masking protocol (Fig. 1). CCFD% were quantified as the proportion of the scan area lacking detectable flow signal, represented as dark pixels in the binary mask.2,20 No minimum size threshold was applied for FD identification, consistent with histological findings that >75% of intercapillary distances fall below 24 µm.20

Flow deficit percentages (FD%) were computed within a concentric 500-µm-wide ring circumferential to the choroidal hyperTD marginal zone (CCFD%_M), generated from the manually segmented GA boundary, and in the remaining CC slab region beyond this marginal zone (CCFD%_BM), obtained by excluding atrophic areas and marginal zone itself (Figs. 2J–L).

Atrophy-to-Fovea Distance Analysis

Given that CC flow voids are known to exhibit regional variation—even in healthy eyes—with a tendency toward increased flow deficits in the central macula compared to more peripheral regions, an additional analysis was conducted to account for and mitigate this potential confounding factor. To adjust CCFD% for the spatial relationship between each atrophic lesion and the foveal center, we performed a pixel to millimeters Euclidean distance analysis. The segmented en face structural OCTs from both groups were converted into binary masks. The masks were overlaid with a calibrated square grid, dividing the lesion area into contiguous 0.1-mm2 units. The foveal center was manually identified on structural B-scans as the point of maximum foveal depression or, in cases of foveal involvement, estimated based on preserved anatomical landmarks.10 For each grid unit that was fully encompassed within the atrophic region, a representative point was manually placed at its geometric center, effectively approximating the centroid of the unit due to the small and uniform dimensions of the squares. The Euclidean distance between each of these centroids and the foveal center was then computed to assess spatial distribution relative to the fovea.

Considering Xi, Yi as the coordinates of each grid point and X0, Y0 as the coordinates of the foveal center, the Euclidean distance was calculated as di=Xi-X02+Yi+Y02d¯=1N∑i=1Ndi

The mean of all such distances was then computed to obtain the atrophy-to-fovea distance (AFD; d). To facilitate coordinate-based spatial analysis, AFD values were decomposed into X, Y coordinates. The described method allowed for topography-independent quantification of lesion eccentricity relative to the fovea (Fig. 3).

Quantitative Measurements and Covariates

The primary outcome was to compare CCFD%_M and CCFD%_BM in EMAP and GA eyes. Secondary outcomes included patient age, diagnosis group (GA vs. EMAP), presence of SDDs in the marginal zone, hyperTD area, and AFD measured on en face OCT to assess differences in CCFD% in relation to various demographic and imaging features.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics are reported as mean% ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons between EMAP and GA eyes were performed using independent two-sample t-tests for normally distributed variables, Mann–Whitney for non-normally distributed variables, and χ2 tests or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Prior to applying parametric tests, Levene's test for equality of variances was performed for all continuous variables. In all comparisons, homoscedasticity assumptions were satisfied (P > 0.05); therefore, the standard two-sample t-test was retained. When variance heterogeneity was present, Welch's correction was applied. A Benjamini–Hochberg correction was used to adjust P values for multiple comparisons. Univariable linear mixed-effect regression identified associations between CCFD%_M and demographic, morphologic, and imaging variables, including diagnosis group, age, AFD, hyperTD area, and presence of SDDs in the marginal zone. A multivariable linear mixed-effects model evaluated the associations between CCFD%_M and clinical predictors, including age, group, SDD presence, hyperTD area, and AFD, with a random intercept for patient ID to account for bilateral eye inclusion. Model performance was evaluated using Nakagawa and Schielzeth's method for calculating marginal and conditional R2 values. The marginal R2 represents the proportion of variance explained by the fixed effects alone, and the conditional R2 reflects the variance explained by both fixed and random effects. Model diagnostics were performed to evaluate the assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs) from equivalent linear models; all VIFs were below 1.5 following orthogonalization, indicating minimal collinearity. A plot of residuals versus fitted values was used to visually assess homoscedasticity and to check for the absence of systematic trends in the residuals. Additionally, Q-Q plots of both residuals and patient-level random intercepts were used to evaluate the normality assumption for fixed and random effects, respectively. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

From an initial cohort of 89 patients screened for inclusion (47 with GA and 42 with EMAP), a total of 53 eyes from 36 patients met the eligibility and image quality criteria for analysis, including 26 eyes with EMAP (49%) and 27 eyes with GA (51%). Patients with EMAP were significantly younger (57.1 ± 2.3 years) than those with GA (79.7 ± 3.7 years; P < 0.001). VA was significantly worse in the GA group (0.42 ± 0.39 logMAR) compared to EMAP (0.16 ± 0.28 logMAR; P < 0.001). The hyperTD area was comparable between groups (3.20 ± 2.56 mm2 in EMAP vs. 2.74% ± 2.08 mm2 in GA; P = 0.587). SDDs at the lesion margin were observed in 35% of EMAP eyes and 30% of GA eyes (P = 0.925). Among EMAP eyes, 12 were in stage 1 (46%), 11 were in stage 2 (42%), and three were in stage 3 (12%). Differences in CCFD% Between EMAP and GA

CCFD%_M was significantly higher in EMAP eyes (44.41% ± 7.42%) than in GA eyes (38.52% ± 10.64%; P = 0.024). Similarly, CCFD%_BM was greater in EMAP eyes (30.33% ± 9.66%) than in GA eyes (21.10% ± 10.12%; P = 0.001) indicating more widespread CC compromise in EMAP (Fig. 4). In stage 1, CCFD%_M was 43.31% ± 9.04% and the mean CCFD%_BM was 30.13% ± 10.01%. In stage 2, these values were 44.68% ± 5.71% and 30.48% ± 8.11%, respectively. Finally, in stage 3, the mean CCFD%_M reached 50.53% ± 2.46%, and the mean CCFD%_BM increased to 37.67% ± 5.64%.

Spatial Displacement

The AFD was significantly greater in EMAP (2.35 ± 0.56 mm) versus GA (1.74 ± 0.55 mm; P = 0.014), indicating a more eccentric lesion topography in the former. In EMAP, the mean X and Y coordinates were 1.53 ± 0.43 mm and 1.87 ± 0.53 mm, respectively, whereas in GA the X and Y values were significantly lower (1.07 ± 0.43 mm and 1.30 ± 0.52 mm, respectively; P < 0.001 for both).

Univariate Analysis

Univariate mixed-effects modeling identified diagnostic group (i.e., GA vs. EMAP) as the only significant predictor of CCFD%_M, with GA associated with a 5.90% absolute reduction in CCFD%_M compared to EMAP (P = 0.027). Other clinical or anatomical parameters—including age, AFD, hyperTD area, and SDD presence—were not significantly associated with CCFD%_M (Table 3).

Multivariable Analysis

GA diagnosis remained significantly associated with reduced CCFD%_M (β = −47.18; 95% confidence interval [CI], −64.4 to −30.23; P < 0.001), independent of other covariates. Age was positively associated with CCFD%_M (β = 1.76 per year; P < 0.001), and it was the same for subretinal drusenoid deposits at the marginal zone (SDD_M) (−5.72; 95% CI, −9.32 to −2.13; P = 0.003), but larger hyperTD area and AFD at the margin were not associated with greater CCFD%_M (P > 0.05). Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we quantified CCFD% in the marginal zone and beyond in eyes with EMAP and GA using SS-OCTA and investigated their associations with relevant structural imaging biomarkers. EMAP eyes demonstrated significantly greater CC flow impairment compared to those with GA secondary to AMD, both at the peripapillary atrophic margin and beyond. Notably, this broader extent of CC dysfunction in EMAP was observed despite comparable areas of atrophy between groups, suggesting a more diffuse and potentially aggressive underlying vascular compromise. Multivariable regression analysis further confirmed that EMAP was independently associated with increased marginal CCFD%, even after adjusting for potential confounders including age, hyperTD area, and the presence of SDD. Moreover, EMAP eyes showed more eccentric lesion topography and better preserved central vision, suggesting a fundamentally different pattern of disease progression.

EMAP is a recently described phenotype of macular degeneration that may be clinically confused with GA secondary to AMD.6 However, prior studies have suggested that EMAP is characterized by more diffuse retinal and choroidal involvement, including the presence of widespread SDD and CC impairment.6,7 Although the role of the CC in GA has been extensively investigated—with evidence linking CC flow deficits to both atrophy progression and AMD severity—data on CC involvement in EMAP remain limited.2,7

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare EMAP and GA, offering novel insights. These findings support the concept that EMAP represents a distinct degenerative process with unique CC features. Additionally, the stage-stratified descriptive statistics demonstrated a numerical trend for progressive increase in CCFD% with advancing EMAP severity, supporting the hypothesis that CC dysfunction parallels morphologic progression in this disease. Patients with EMAP were significantly younger than those with GA, consistent with previous reports identifying EMAP as an early-onset phenotype.6,11 Although patients with EMAP were younger than GA patients, the hyperTD area did not differ significantly between the groups. Given the expectation that atrophic progression in GA is typically age dependent, the presence of similarly advanced atrophy in younger EMAP populations suggests a more rapid and aggressive degenerative course. These findings support the hypothesis that EMAP may represent a distinct disease process rather than a phenotypic variant of age-related GA. Although Rajabian et al.7 previously demonstrated perfusion deficits involving both the CC and deep capillary plexus (DCP), the present analysis focused on the CC for several methodological and biological reasons. First, DCP measurements are more susceptible to segmentation errors and projection artifacts in advanced atrophy, reducing reproducibility. Second, validated compensation and thresholding protocols are currently standardized only for CC analysis, enabling reliable inter-site comparison. Future studies integrating both CC and DCP analyses will be important to evaluate the relationship and sequencing of the choroidal and retinal vascular alterations. However, further validation through histopathologic correlation, as well as genome-wide and phenotypic association studies, is necessary to clarify the underlying mechanisms and disease classification.

VA was significantly better in eyes with EMAP compared to those with GA secondary to AMD, a finding that aligns with observed differences in the spatial distribution of atrophy in our cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantitatively trace atrophy distribution using pixel-wise topographic metrics. EMAP lesions exhibited significantly greater AFD and larger X and Y centroid shifts, reflecting a more eccentric pattern that tends to spare the central fovea in earlier stages. In contrast, atrophy in conventional GA typically follows a fovea-threatening or fovea-involving pattern, more directly impacting central vision at earlier stages.27,28 Despite extensive outer retinal degeneration, central VA may be relatively preserved in early-stage EMAP due to the peripheral localization of atrophy.6 These findings suggest that standard VA measures may underestimate disease severity in EMAP, as reported by Romano et al.9 in previous microperimetry studies. Therefore, spatial mapping of atrophic lesions—beyond total lesion size—may be more informative. Our results further confirm the work of Bianco et al.,29 who reported that EMAP shares morphological and growth characteristics with diffuse-trickling GA, without including a clear topographical assessment. However, our approach incorporates not just axis elongation but also centroid displacement (X and Y shifts), AFD, and lesion symmetry to enable a potential more precise spatial mapping.

CCFD% metrics revealed profound and spatially extensive microvascular compromise in eyes with EMAP compared to those with GA, both at the peripapillary atrophic margin and in extra-lesional regions. Notably, this was observed despite comparable hyperTD areas, suggesting that CC dysfunction in EMAP may be either more diffuse or potentially primary, rather than merely secondary, to RPE loss, as is sometimes postulated in AMD-associated GA.30 These findings are consistent with the work of Rajabian et al.,7 who demonstrated severe perfusion density loss in EMAP, particularly within the CC and deep capillary plexus. Their analysis revealed significantly reduced perfusion not only in atrophic and junctional zones but also in retinal areas that appeared preserved when compared to healthy controls. This pattern of diffuse vascular compromise further supports a model of primary choroidal and CC involvement in EMAP pathogenesis. In the context of GA, prior studies have emphasized the role of perilesional CC loss in disease progression. Nassisi et al.2 reported that elevated CC flow deficits in peri- and para-atrophy regions were associated with faster rates of GA enlargement. Similarly, Shi et al.31 demonstrated that the spatial distribution of CC flow impairment was predictive of subsequent GA growth. Although these studies focused on AMD-related GA, our data suggest that EMAP is associated with even more severe and extensive CC disruption both in the marginal/junctional zone and beyond, consistent with a more aggressive vascular impairment. CCFDs are known to exhibit regional variability, with a physiologic tendency toward greater flow deficits in the central macula compared to more peripheral regions, even in healthy eyes.1 In our study, we adjusted for this known spatial bias by controlling for central dependence, confirming that the extent and distribution of CC impairment in EMAP exceeds normal aging-related patterns and likely reflects vascular impairment, with CC playing a more important role in EMAP than in GA.

Regression analysis provided additional confirmation of these results. In univariable regression, diagnostic group was the only significant predictor of CCFD%_M, highlighting the robust effect of disease phenotype. Although age demonstrated a positive association with CCFD%_M in the multivariable model, this reflects within-group variation rather than the between-group effect dominated by disease type. Physiologically, modest CC rarefaction occurs with aging; however, EMAP eyes exhibited disproportionately greater flow loss even after adjusting for age, indicating that disease-driven vascular compromise is much larger magnitude compared with normal age-related decline. AFD, SDD presence, or hyperTD area alone did not predict perfusion in isolation, emphasizing the importance of multivariable modeling to disentangle potential confounding influences. Most notably, the presence of GA when compared to EMAP was associated with a substantially lower CCFD at the marginal zone confirming more severe CC perfusion in EMAP patients.

The observed effect of age further emphasizes the contribution of age-related microvascular decline in these phenotypes. This topographic variation could contribute to the unexpectedly lower total CCFD% at the marginal zone, particularly if SDDs lie outside the sampled vascular footprint. In contrast, traditional morphometric measures such as hyperTD area and AFD did not independently predict CC flow deficit. This finding suggests that quantitative atrophy metrics alone may be insufficient surrogates for vascular impairment, particularly when phenotypic group and age are accounted for. These results highlight the complexity of atrophic processes and support the integration of vascular biomarkers—such as CCFD—into multimodal classification and risk stratification frameworks. Further exploration of these patient-specific drivers or the existence of different EMAP phenotypes could enhance characterization and prognostic precision, as well as guide more tailored therapeutic approaches.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective, cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions about causality or disease progression, limiting our ability to infer temporal relationships between structural features and vascular impairment. Moreover, a GA subanalysis according to phenotypes was not possible due to the limited cohort. Additionally, a number of eyes were excluded due to the strict inclusion criteria, and further cases were excluded due to image quality or processing limitations. Although we observed a greater degree of CC compromise beyond the marginal zone, our analysis specifically focused on the marginal zone; thus, we did not quantify the RPE–BM splitting or characterize GA phenotypes. As such, the potential role of more peripherally distributed SDDs, particularly in EMAP, remains unexamined in this cohort. Finally, the relatively small sample size limited the statistical power of the study; future studies with larger, prospectively collected cohorts will be essential to validate and expand upon these findings.

In summary, this study confirmed demographic, anatomic, and choroidal vascular differences between the EMAP and GA associated with AMD. EMAP is characterized by younger onset, greater lesion eccentricity, and more extensive CC flow impairment at the marginal zone, even in the presence of preserved central vision. These findings support a model of EMAP as a diffuse, microvascular-driven atrophic phenotype and suggest that OCTA-based flow analysis may serve as a valuable tool for differentiating disease subtypes and guiding personalized management strategies. Further longitudinal studies are necessary to evaluate how baseline CC features influence progression and treatment response.
