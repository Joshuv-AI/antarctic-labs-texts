A method of estimating cervical cancers prevented by the introduction of national screening in Ireland
Authors: David Robert Grimes, Aline Brennan, Cathal Walsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer screening is a life-saving endeavour. The introduction of an organized population-based national screening programmes has potential to both reduce incidence of cervical cancer in an asymptomatic population and detect early-stage cancers for accelerated treatment. A methodology for estimating cancers prevented by such programmes has immediate utility. This work derives a model for estimating cancer prevented by screening, applied to data from Ireland’s organized national cervical screening programme since its introduction in August 2008 to August 2022. A novel Markov-chain model for human papilloma virus (HPV) induced cervical cancer was derived with realistic transition probabilities validated against literature estimates. Data from the Irish National Screening Programme (CervicalCheck) and from the National Cancer Registry of Ireland (NCRI) was applied to estimate the number of cancers prevented by screening, changes in Irish cancer detection since the implementation of screening, and treatment costs saved by screening. Since its inception in 2008, the modelling in this work suggests that CervicalCheck has prevented an estimated 5557 cancers (95% confidence interval: 5114–6000 cancers) and saved €102 million in future treatment costs (95% confidence interval: €94–110 million) not including inflation costs. Additionally, 48.8% (95% confidence interval: 41.4%–56.2%) of all cervical cancers in Ireland have been detected through screening between 2008 and 2022. National screening in Ireland has been highly effective at reducing future cervical cancers, and detecting asymptomatic cancers. The model outlined here has direct future applicability for the assessment of national and regional cervical cancer screening programmes.

Introduction

Cervical screening is a life-saving endeavour, the objective of which is to identify individuals with abnormal cells, lesions, and human papilloma virus (HPV) infections amongst the ostensibly well, in order that they may be monitored or treated before the onset of invasive disease. National and regional screening programmes follow the guidelines laid down by the World Health Organization [1], so that maximum benefit can be achieved with potential harms minimized, preventing cervical cancers.

This is crucial, as cervical cancer remains a leading cause of mortality among women, with an estimated 604 000 women diagnosed worldwide in 2020, with roughly 342 000 deaths from the disease. In 23 countries, cervical cancer is the most diagnosed cancer in women. It is also the leading cause of cancer deaths in 36 countries, the majority of which are nations in sub-Saharan Africa, Melanesia, South America, and South-Eastern Asia. In Ireland, ∼290 women are diagnosed with cervical cancer annually, with about 90 deaths each year. It is the second most common cause of cancer mortality in women aged 25–40 in the country [2].

The changes that lead to cervical cancer are usually driven by infection with HPV, a typically transient and common infection. When an oncogenic strain persists (often HPV 16, but numerous other forms have carcinogenic potential), it can lead to cervical intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN), abnormal changes of the squamous cells of the cervix with three grades of severity, ranging from the least severe grade 1 (CIN 1) to the most severe grade 3 (CIN 3). Abnormal cervical lesions of all grades can and do naturally regress, but a proportion undergo unchecked progression, resulting in invasive cervical cancer.

Aside from CIN designations, there are other grading systems employed for cervical abnormalities. Cytology results from pap smears are often classified as either low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL). Standard convention is that an LSIL finding in cytology is roughly analogous to CIN 1, while HSIL subsumes both CIN 2 and CIN 3. This is potentially confounding, as LSIL/HSIL results are typically cytology-derived, whereas CIN findings are typically confirmed by biopsy after colposcopy [3, 4], and there is significant flexibility in terminology.

When high-grade lesions are detected, they can be treated well in advance of this progression, stopping the onset of cervical cancer. The goal of screening is to prevent precisely this, reducing avoidable cancer and subsequent deaths. While cervical cancer screening has existed for decades in various forms, organized national programmes are a more recent development. The CervicalCheck programme in Ireland [3] was established in 2008, at a time of rising incidence of cervical cancer in Ireland. Since its advent, there have been significant demographic changes in Ireland, and evidence of changing sexual behaviour, all of which impacts CIN and cervical cancer rates. Specifically, Cervicalcheck is an organized screening programme, involving the creation of a register of eligible individuals, with a call-recall system and dedicated infrastructure to deliver screening, diagnosis, and treatment. Such an implementation ensures a standardized follow-up and treatment of abnormal results, as well as a full quality-assurance pathway [5].

The Irish implementation is relatively recent and an ideal experiment to gauge the impact of such an organized population-based screening programme. Data from the national screening service on the numbers of unique users, coverage, colposcopy referrals, and screening outcomes is publicly available, and it is important to ascertain an estimate of how many cancers have been prevented by its existence, and a methodology for accessing this. This is in essence a counterfactual question, but one that is amenable to mathematical modelling. In this work, we derive Markov chain models for the potential evolution of cervical abnormalities in the absence of any screening or intervention and apply these to the Irish CervicalCheck programme to estimate cancers prevented since the inception of the programme. The major advantage of this approach is that it uses measured data on prevalence of cervical abnormalities in the Irish population directly and circumvents the need to ascertain the effects of HPV vaccination and relative prevalence of various oncogenic HPV strains on transition probability to cervical abnormality. We also employ data from the National Cancer Registry of Ireland (NCRI) to gauge how cervical cancer incidence has changed in the years since the introduction of screening.

Methods

Derivation of Markov chain models

We derive Markov chain models for the potential evolution of cervical abnormalities in the absence of any screening or intervention. These models consist of six different patient states (Clear, HPV positive, CIN 1, CIN 2, CIN 3, and cancer states) with clear states and cancer states being absorbing, meaning that patients neither spontaneously regress from a cancer state nor develop lesions without HPV infection. This yields a means to model the counterfactual likely outcomes for cervical abnormalities without screening and subsequent treatment, comparing this hypothetical situation to current practice. An outline of the Markov chain model deployed is given in Fig. 1, where CIN states can progress to more advanced states or regress directly to a clear without transitioning through adjacent CIN states, representing a scenario where CIN regresses due to a cleared HPV infection, and cervical lesions directly subside as a direct consequence of no driving infection.

Markov model outline, where HPV/CIN states can persist to, progress to more advance states, or regress to clear.

We also considered two further models, one where all states can regress or progress to any adjacent state, or persist in the current state, until they enter an absorbing state and to clear CIN high-grade lesions individual transitions to lower grades of CIN before clearance. This was deemed the biologically least plausible model, as evidence to date suggests that regression is accompanied by HPV remission rather than jumps between adjacent states, although current evidence is not fine-grained enough to draw any categorical conclusion [6, 7]. As an intermediary model, we considered the situation where all CIN states regressed to a HPV infection. These models are explored in the supplementary material for completeness (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S1), and give broadly similar results to the main model considered here. It is important to note that the model outlined here (regression to clear) is the most conservative of these models, giving the low-end estimate of cancer prevented through screening.

Estimation of transition probabilities and confidence bounds

It is imperative to have reasonable estimates of the transition probabilities of various grades of cervical abnormality. These can be estimated from literature on the history of cervical cancer, HPV infection, and CIN states, with appropriate caveats. Literature on the regression, progression, and persistence of cervical lesions can be conflicting and highly heterogenous for a variety of reasons. Firstly, there is often cytological debate over the precise boundary of CIN states. Secondly, there are a range of observation times employed in different investigations, which means that clearance and regression rates can potentially be underestimated in some works. Thirdly, data on high grade CIN is ethically difficult to obtain, as persistent CIN 2 and any CIN 3 is typically immediately treated, meaning that progression rates in literature often pertain to treated CIN as opposed to direct natural history. Finally, there are high levels of heterogeneity of lesions with age and HPV subtype. Despite this, we can obtain reasonable parameter values and confidence intervals, as outlined here.

CIN 1 regression, persistence, and progression

Values for the regression, persistence, and progression of CIN 1 were taken from a 2021 meta-analysis by Loopik et al. [4] on 89 studies on the natural history of CIN states.

CIN 2 regression, persistence, and progression

CIN 2 regression and progression rates were the subject of a 2018 British Medical Journal meta-analysis [8], which looked at 36 studies involving 3160 untreated women. These constituent studies had follow-up times ranging from 3 months to 5 years, finding a high rate of CIN 2 regression (≥50%) at time-intervals over 24 months. Loopik et al. [4] reported similar rates of CIN 2 regression in their more recent work.

CIN 3 regression, persistence, and progression

CIN 3 progression rates are somewhat more difficult to estimate, largely as CIN 3 is typically treated given its potential severity. Accordingly, modern works tend to report treated case progression rates, which are artificially low, even though recurrent CIN 3 greatly increases lifetime cancer incidence [9]. Loopik et al. [4] reported a 2% progression rate to cancer, with a confidence envelope of 0%–25%. Other estimates [10] put untreated progression at between 12% [11] and 30% [12] to as high as 40% [13]. To account for this wide range, we use a wide uncertainty envelope in this parameter. CIN 3 can also regress without intervention [14], with estimates of just under 50% with similar numbers reported for young women in a recent investigation [15, 16]. All derived parameter values and 95% confidence intervals are given in the Supplementary Appendix (Supplementary Table S2), with HPV regression rates kept constant at 80% in 24 months, 10% persistent, and 10% progressive to CIN 1, consistent with the data for the most common oncogenic strains of HPV in Ireland between 2008 and 2022 (HPV16/18).

The matrices for estimation of transition probability and associated 95% confidence bounds are given in the related code and mathematical appendix (Supplementary Table S2), as well as code for implementation of the models discussed. As the sum of probabilities of states in a Markov chain model must always sum to unity [17], the 95% confidence intervals were calculated by bootstrapping methods, taking discrete steps of 0.01 along each parameters confidence interval and finding all possible combinations of parameters that summed to unity. For these, the absorbing probabilities were calculated, and a large vector of allowed values produced. Resulting vectors were approximately normally distributed with several thousand values, with mean μ and standard deviation σ of the distribution found, with 95% confidence bounds. Histograms for all models are given in the Supplementary Technical Appendix.

Screening efficacy estimation

For persistent cases, we ascertain the expected time from a given state to cancer by invoking natural history models of the evolution of cervical cancer from a given state. We also require estimates for the efficacy of treatment after screening to gauge cancers prevented. For CIN 1 and CIN 2, timely treatment makes cancer occurrence vanishingly unlikely, so we assume a 100% efficacy for CIN 1 and CIN 2. For CIN 3, McCredie et al. [18] found women given adequate treatment had a lifetime cervical cancer incidence of 0.7% (0.3%–1.9%), giving us an effective complement for CIN 3 treatment efficacy of TCIN3 = 0.993 (95% confidence interval 0.981 − 0.997).

Application to and analysis of Irish CervicalCheck and NCRI data

The Irish screening service, CervicalCheck, records all abnormal cervical outcomes, ranging from precancerous lesions to detected cancers [3, 19]. Cytology results from Pap smears are classified as either LSIL or HSIL. Standard convention is that an LSIL finding in cytology is roughly analogous to CIN 1, while HSIL subsumes both CIN 2 and CIN 3. Data from the screening service defines results as low-grade (corresponding to CIN 1) and high-grade (a juxtaposition of CIN 2 and CIN 3) as well as detected cancers. NCRI figures, also available publicly, yield total detected cancers [20] and estimated costs of treatment for cervical cancer [21]. This work considers demographic and cost projections from the NCRI on predicted cervical cancer diagnoses prior to the introduction of screening.

CervicalCheck reporting combines CIN 2 and 3 cases, a complicating factor given their markedly different invasive potential [8, 22, 23]. This is potentially confounding, as LSIL/HSIL results are typically cytology-derived, whereas CIN findings are typically confirmed by biopsy after colposcopy, with significant flexibility in terminology. To arrive at reasonable estimates for the high-grade breakdown, we employ published CervicalCheck data, containing cytology grading of HSIL grades, categorized as ASC-H (Atypical squamous cells for which a high-grade lesion cannot be excluded), moderate, and severe. On the presumption this is broadly representative of the high-grade breakdown and that moderate HSIL maps directly to CIN 2 while severe HSIL maps directly to CIN 3, we can further account for the approximate breakdown of the reported figures to estimate proportions as outlined in the mathematical appendix. We further confine analysis of colposcopy outcomes to only women newly diagnosed, and not those attending follow-up colposcopy after an initial abnormal finding, excluding those undergoing repeated follow-up colposcopy.

We furthered checked modelled predictions versus published data for validation. For CIN 1 and CIN 2, literature estimates suggests that about 1% and 5% of these lesions will progress to invasive cervical cancer [24], close to but slightly lower than the model estimates here. This is expected, however, as literature estimates include treated and screened cases in its cohort. As the model here also yields a total lifetime probability, we expect this to be higher than in real-world investigations which have finite windows and durations for data gathering.

Cancer demographics

Figure 4 depicts cervical cancer detection since 1994, and the NCRI projections for cervical cancer diagnoses from 2008 to 2022 based on demographic changes. These were generated for this work by taking average age-specific rates for 2005–07 (prior to screening) and applying these rates to annual Central Statistics Office (CSO) population estimates for years 2008–22. Immediately following the introduction of CervicalCheck in 2009, diagnosed cancers peaked at 356 cases, 27.1% greater than the NCRI projection for that year (280 cases). This is likely due to the introduction of screening detecting early-stage cancers, with detected cancers falling an average of 14.3%±4.1% below prior demographic projections by 2013, 5 years into the service.

As there is a slight offset in the reporting of CervicalCheck (September–August) and of the NCRI (Jan–Dec) there is some difficulty in precisely determining the contribution of screening, but on average between 2009 and 2016, a majority (57.1%) of diagnosed cancers were detected by the screening service. After 2016, screening detection accounted for 37.8% of all diagnosed Irish cases, depicted in Fig. 2c. In total, since the advent of CervicalCheck, 48.8%±7.4% of all cervical cancers were detected by screening. These estimates are in line with recent NCRI reports on screening detected cancers [27].

We can also make some estimation of health service savings due to screening. In 2020, the NCRI estimate was that the mean cost of treatment for cervical cancer in Ireland amounted to costs to the health service of €18 388 per case [20]. Accordingly, this would amount to €102±8 million in future treatment to the health service prevented by screening between 2008 and 2022, based on the 2016 estimate of cancer treatment costs. This estimate does not include the annual cost of inflation and is thus likely to be considerably higher when adjusted accordingly.

Discussion

Since its inception in 2008, the results of this analysis suggest that CervicalCheck has prevented a substantial number of future cancers and circumvented the human and financial costs these would otherwise incur. While cervical cancer rates were climbing in Ireland from 1994, they now appear to be falling even as the Irish population increases, with 48.8%±7.4% of all cervical cancers diagnosed being detected by screening since 2009. This suggests that cervical cancer in Ireland may have initially been underdiagnosed, but as the clinical course from the genesis of a cancer to clinical presentation can take up to a decade, it is likely that cervical screening has detected earlier cancers prior to their clinical manifestation, rendering them more amenable to treatment. Even though cancer detection is not the primary objective of screening, this is likely to be beneficial to women affected [28, 29].

The model proposed here is novel and useful primarily because by directly interrogating screening confirmed cases it allows direct estimation of the effectiveness of the screening service and quantifies the benefit to participating women. Unlike natural history models, it does not require estimates of HPV prevalence or even phenotypes in the general population, nor does it require data on screening uptake. Instead, it gives an adaptable framework for quantifying the number of cancers prevented by screening, and it is accordingly highly adaptable to other screening services with similar statistics. It also has limitations and caveats that should be elucidated. The estimate of cancers prevented assumes that cervical abnormalities and HPV have no other means of detection, evolving in the absence of corrective intervention. The comparison of the counterfactual models with current practice further presumes that high-grade cervical abnormalities are treated or followed up as appropriate after a screening result showing abnormal cells. While this is certainly true for asymptomatic lesions which are presumably most cases but does not consider alternative ways of diagnosing lesions aside from screening. However, the high rate of screening detected cancers indicates that asymptomatic cancers would be unlikely to be detected without screening, and would only become diagnosed at presentation, rendering this assumption a reasonable one based on evidence available.

This work does not directly consider the potential negative impacts of screening, such as overtreatment and false positives. False positives in screening have historically been a significant issue and has been discussed by authors of this work previously [30], but it is important to note that Ireland transitioned to a primary HPV-screening approach model in 2020 [3]. In this schema, patients are first tested for high-risk strains of HPV with a follow-up smear only indicated if the patient is HPV-positive. This has the net effect of reducing the false positive of cervical screening by approximately a factor of 10, reducing unnecessary colposcopy visits, and thus hugely decreasing potential harms of screening whilst maintaining benefits [31].

The HPV vaccine has also been available in Ireland since 2010 [30]. Following a confidence crisis stemming from antivaccine disinformation [32–34], uptake has returned to relatively high levels for girls and has since been also introduced for boys throughout the country. This is promising as modelling studies suggest HPV vaccination can virtually eradicate cervical cancers [35], and the Irish primary HPV-screening approach to screening is very robust to decreasing prevalence of HPV infection and increased vaccine uptake across all age cohorts [36]. The long-term ramifications of this on screening are not yet apparent, but early data is encouraging [37], and we expect to see a reduced number of referrals to colposcopy in future years as a direct consequence of declining rates of HPV infection and lower false positive rates of primary HPV-screening relative to liquid-based cytology approaches, even as the Irish population increases. A further advantage of the model presented here is that it does not require any estimate of transition probability from HPV infection to cervical abnormality, and will thus remain robust for future purposes even as the effects of vaccination shift the oncogenic potential of dominant strains, and future estimates of the breakdown of CIN 2 versus CIN 3 initial presentations can be estimated directly from the service data.

In addition to the previous work demonstrating the cost-effectiveness of vaccination against HPV in Ireland [38] and the clear benefits of HPV-eradication [39] more generally, the current work adds evidence that cervical screening is an effective way to reduce national cancer burden. While this work focuses only on cancers prevented by screening, it is almost certain from recent review [40] that organized screening also reduces mortality from cervical cancer substantially. Even in the era of HPV vaccination, this works provides further evidence that organized cervical screening is highly beneficial to public health, and insights from this work in an Irish context may provide a framework for quantifying direct benefits of cervical screening in other nations.
