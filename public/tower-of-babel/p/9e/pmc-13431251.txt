Utility of the Thoracic Trauma Severity Score in Predicting Outcomes in Isolated Thoracic Injuries: A 10-year Omani cohort study
Authors: Badriya AlQanubi, Darpanarayan Hazra, Aisha Breiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:The Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (TTSS) is a composite scoring system developed to assess injury severity and predict outcomes in thoracic trauma. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of TTSS in predicting clinical outcomes in patients with isolated thoracic trauma in Oman.Methods:This retrospective observational study was conducted at two tertiary academic hospitals in Muscat, Oman, between January 2014 and December 2024 and included adult patients with isolated thoracic trauma. TTSS was calculated from demographic, radiological and clinical data. Its predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic analysis for mortality, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and surgical intervention, and correlation analysis for ICU length of stay and duration of mechanical ventilation.Results:A total of 100 patients were included, of which most were male (77.0%), the mean age was 42.6 ± 17.3 years and the mean TTSS was 4.28 ± 3.35. TTSS showed significant discrimination for ICU admission (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.788, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.696–0.879; P <0.001) and was positively correlated with ICU length of stay and duration of mechanical ventilation. In contrast, TTSS showed poor discrimination for mortality (AUC = 0.643, 95% CI: 0.382–0.903; P = 0.402) and no discriminatory value for surgical intervention (AUC = 0.503, 95% CI: 0.382–0.625; P = 0.956), neither were statistically significant.Conclusion:TTSS was most useful for predicting ICU-related outcomes in isolated thoracic trauma, particularly ICU admission and respiratory support requirements. Its value for mortality and surgical intervention was limited in the current cohort, likely reflecting the very low number of deaths and the lesion-specific nature of operative decision-making.Keywords: Thoracic Injuries, Wounds and Injuries, Risk Assessment, Severity of Illness Index, Intensive Care Units, Length of Stay

1. Introduction

Thoracic trauma remains a major contributor to morbidity and mortality globally, accounting for approximately 25–35% of all trauma-related deaths.[1,2,3] Given the critical need for timely assessment and intervention, objective tools that can accurately stratify injury severity and predict patient outcomes are vital. The Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (TTSS), first introduced by Pape et al., integrates anatomical and physiological parameters including rib fractures, pulmonary contusion, age, PaO2/FiO2 (PF) ratio and pleural involvement, offering a composite score ranging from 0 to 25 to guide clinical decision-making.[4] TTSS was originally developed to assess injury severity and to predict clinically important respiratory complications, including the need for mechanical ventilation and higher-level care.

Recent studies have validated the predictive value of TTSS across various global populations. A study conducted in Egypt demonstrated that a TTSS ≥ 7 had 100% sensitivity and 97.7% specificity for predicting poor outcomes such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and mechanical ventilation need.[5] Similarly, a large-scale prospective study in India confirmed that TTSS ≥ 7.5 was significantly associated with increased morbidity and mortality among patients presenting with chest trauma.[3] More recent work has also suggested a role for TTSS in predicting intensive care unit (ICU) requirement, emphasising its potential value in trauma triage.[6] However, the reported performance of TTSS has varied across cohorts and appears to depend on the case mix, outcome selection and trauma setting.

Moreover, new technologies such as early multirow detector computed tomography (MDCT) have enhanced the accuracy of TTSS calculation, further improving its sensitivity and specificity in emergency settings. A study validating TTSS using MDCT found it to outperform both single-slice CT and chest X-ray in predicting mortality and ARDS.[6,7] However, there is some contention regarding its universal applicability. A study comparing TTSS with the Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) in patients with severe thoracic trauma concluded that TTSS was less predictive of in-hospital mortality than TRISS, highlighting the need for further prospective studies in diverse settings.[8] These differences may reflect variation in patient age, mechanisms of injury, associated extra-thoracic trauma and healthcare delivery systems across populations.

Despite its widespread use, no previous study has examined the utility of TTSS within a Middle Eastern population. Considering regional differences in trauma epidemiology, pre-hospital care and hospital infrastructure, it is essential to validate this scoring system in local contexts. In Oman and similar settings, thoracic trauma patients may be younger and more likely to present with thoracic-dominant or isolated injuries, which may influence complication rates, mortality and overall score performance compared with Western validation cohorts. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the utility and prognostic performance of TTSS in a cohort of thoracic trauma patients admitted to two tertiary academic hospitals in the Oman (Middle East), with particular attention to ICU-related outcomes, mortality and surgical intervention.

2. Methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted at two tertiary academic hospitals and trauma referral centres (Sultan Qaboos University Hospital, University Medical City, and Khoula Hospital) in Muscat, Oman. Both institutions are national centres for trauma care, serving patients from across the country. The study period spanned from January 2014 to December 2024. Patients were identified through the electronic hospital information systems using ICD-10 codes relevant to thoracic trauma. These included codes for superficial injury of the thorax, open wounds, rib and sternal fractures, thoracic spine injuries, vascular or neurological injuries, crush injuries and trauma to thoracic organs. Clinical notes were reviewed to confirm the presence of rib fractures, sternal fractures, lung contusions, pneumothorax and haemothorax. Data were collected using a structured EpiData proforma, which included demographic information, injury details, clinical and radiological findings, laboratory values, TTSS parameters as well as clinical outcomes. Outcomes included emergency department (ED) disposition, ICU admission, duration of ICU stay, mechanical ventilation, surgical interventions and in-hospital mortality. Two co-investigators independently validated the data entry to ensure accuracy and reliability.

TTSS was computed based on the following five parameters: PF ratio, number of rib fractures, pulmonary contusion extent, pleural involvement and patient age. Each parameter was assigned a score from 0 to 5, for a total score ranging from 0 to 25. Radiological parameters were extracted from standardised reports interpreted by board-certified radiologists. Based on their TTSS scores, patients were grouped into three categories: 0–5, 6–10 or 11–15.

Patients aged 18 years and older presenting to the ED with thoracic trauma were eligible for inclusion. For the purpose of this study, ‘isolated thoracic trauma’ was defined as thoracic injury being the dominant injury, no major extra-thoracic injury independently causing shock, the requirement of urgent operative intervention or determining disposition (ward/Intensive Care Unit admission) or mortality risk. Patients with severe head injury (Glasgow Coma Scale < 8), shock due to extra-thoracic solid organ injury, pre-existing chronic lung disease or discharge against medical advice were excluded. Minor associated non-thoracic injuries that did not independently affect resuscitation or disposition (ward/Intensive Care Unit admission) may have been present and are acknowledged as potential confounders.

Sample size was calculated using MedCalc software, Version 22.032 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium) with reference to the study by Kanake et al., which reported an AUC of 0.988 for TTSS in predicting complications.[3] The study aimed to include approximately 100 patients. This target was based on complication-related outcomes overall rather than mortality alone. However, only three deaths occurred in the final cohort, which is insufficient for robust assessment of mortality discrimination and represents a major limitation of the study.

The primary outcome in this study was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU admission, need for mechanical ventilation, surgical interventions and ED disposition. Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, Version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables; the Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test were used for continuous variables. Correlations between TTSS and continuous outcomes were evaluated using Spearman's rho. For receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, TTSS was treated as a continuous variable; the three TTSS categories were used only for descriptive subgroup comparisons. ROC curves were constructed to assess the predictive accuracy of TTSS for mortality, ICU admission and surgical intervention. All P values were two-sided and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 100 patients with isolated thoracic trauma were included in this study. The majority were male (77.0%) and the mean age was 42.6 ± 17.3 years. Age distribution showed that 28.0% of the patients were under 30 years old and 33.0% were in the 41–54 age group. The most common mechanism of injury was motor vehicle collision (42.0%) followed by falls (37.0%) and pedestrian injuries (8.0%). Comorbidities included diabetes (13.0%), hypertension (11.0%) and smoking (5.0%) [Table 1].

Rib fractures were present in 66.0% of patients, with the majority (56.0%) sustaining 1–3 unilateral fractures which were significantly associated with TTSS (P = 0.004). Bilateral rib fractures (n = 10, 10.0%) and flail chest (n = 4, 4.0%) were rare but clustered in higher TTSS groups. Pulmonary contusion was detected in 49 patients, with multi-lobar and bilateral involvement significantly correlating with TTSS severity (P <0.001). Pleural involvement was identified in 33.0% of patients, with pneumothorax as the most common finding (15.0%), followed by unilateral haemothorax or hemopneumothorax (13.0%); tension pneumothorax (3.0%) and bilateral haemothorax or hemopneumothorax (2.0%) were less frequent but clinically significant (P <0.001) [Table 2]. Severe variants such as bilateral haemothorax and tension pneumothorax were confined to TTSS > 10 groups. These injuries were significantly correlated with higher TTSS (P <0.001) and worse outcomes. The mean TTSS across all patients was 4.28 ± 3.35 (range: 0–15).

Based on TTSS categorisation, 75 patients (75.0%) fell in the 0–5 group, 17 (17.0%) in the 6–10 group and 8 (8.0%) in the 11–15 group. Increasing TTSS scores were significantly associated with worsened clinical outcomes, including ICU admission (P < 0.001), ED disposition (P <0.001), ventilation requirement (P = 0.01), hospital admission (P <0.001) and mortality (P = 0.04). Specifically, gas exchange impairment (measured via PF ratio) was observed in 18.0% of patients, with lowest ratios (≤150) occurring in patients requiring ICU admission. ICU need rose progressively as PF ratios declined, highlighting the utility of oxygenation status as a severity marker. Patients in the TTSS 0–5 category had a favourable course: 40.0% were discharged from the ED, ICU admission was rare (4.0%) and there were no deaths. In contrast, the TTSS 6–10 group had higher ICU admissions (17.6%), ventilation requirement (11.8%) and one mortality. Outcomes were poorest in the TTSS 11–15 group, with 62.5% requiring ICU admission, 62.5% requiring ventilation and a 25.0% mortality rate. Hospital admission rates rose significantly across groups (60.0%, 94.1% and 100%, respectively; P <0.001), while ED discharge declined (40.0%, 5.9% and 0.0%, respectively; P < 0.001). The length of ICU stay (P <0.001) and ventilation requirement (P = 0.01) also rose sharply with increasing TTSS. While surgical intervention was more frequent in higher TTSS groups, this trend did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.09) [Table 3].

ROC analysis showed that TTSS had good predictive accuracy for ICU admission, with an AUC of 0.788 (standard error [SE] = 0.046, 95% CI: 0.696–0.879; P <0.001) [Fig. 1A]. In contrast, TTSS showed poor discrimination for mortality, with an AUC of 0.643 (SE = 0.133, 95% CI: 0.382–0.903; P = 0.402), indicating no statistically significant predictive performance [Fig. 1B]. The AUC for predicting surgical intervention was 0.503 (95% CI: 0.382–0.625; P = 0.956), indicating no discriminatory value [Fig. 2A]. Patients with scores ≤4 had near-complete survival, while those with scores >14 exhibited high mortality. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a consistent decline in cumulative survival with increasing TTSS. Mean survival time was 4.383 ± 0.350 days (95% CI: 3.696–5.069) and median survival time was 4.000 ± 0.322 days (95% CI: 3.369–4.631). The curve displayed minimal censoring and a clear downward trend, although this should be interpreted cautiously given the very small number of deaths [Fig. 2B].

4. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic performance of the TTSS in a cohort of 100 patients with isolated thoracic trauma across two tertiary care centres in Oman. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study from a Middle Eastern country to investigate TTSS, adding valuable regional data to the global literature. The current study's findings suggest that TTSS was most informative for ICU-related outcomes and ventilatory support requirements in this cohort, rather than for mortality or surgical intervention.

Among the outcomes examined, ICU admission was the only outcome for which TTSS demonstrated statistically significant discriminatory performance on ROC analysis (AUC = 0.788; P <0.001), indicating its primary utility as a marker of escalation-of-care needs in this population. Similar findings were reported by Daurat et al., Martínez Casas et al. and Acharya et al., who demonstrated that TTSS scores ≥ 7–11 were predictive of ICU need, prolonged ICU stay and ventilatory support.[9,10,11] Ventilation requirement rose dramatically in patients with TTSS >10, with longer ventilation duration and ICU stay. Pulmonary contusion and impaired gas exchange (PF ratio ≤ 300) were key contributors to poor outcomes, as supported by prior work linking these factors to ARDS, intubation and prolonged ICU courses.[12,13,14,15,16] In contrast, TTSS showed poor discrimination for mortality (AUC = 0.643, 95% CI: 0.382–0.903; P = 0.402), indicating no statistically significant predictive value in this cohort. Although a trend toward higher mortality with increasing scores was observed, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Over the 10-year study period, only three deaths occurred, resulting in a very low event rate and wide confidence intervals. Accordingly, the mortality analysis was underpowered and the lack of statistical significance should be interpreted cautiously rather than as evidence of an inherent limitation of the score. This may partly explain why the current study's findings differ from some prior studies that reported stronger associations between TTSS and mortality.[17,18] In contrast, Moon et al. found no significant association between TTSS and mortality, highlighting variability across populations.[8] Several cohort-specific factors may have contributed to this weaker mortality association, including the relatively young age of the current patients, the predominance of thoracic-dominant injury patterns and differences in healthcare delivery compared with Western validation cohorts. In addition, early deaths in the ED may have had incomplete physiological or radiological documentation, further limiting accurate mortality prediction. These findings also differ from original validation studies because TTSS was primarily designed to predict respiratory complications such as ARDS and the need for mechanical ventilation, rather than mortality alone. In this context, its stronger association with ICU admission and ventilatory support in the current cohort is more consistent with its intended clinical application.

Surgical intervention was more frequent in patients with higher TTSS, however, this trend did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.09) and TTSS demonstrated no discriminatory value for predicting surgical intervention (AUC = 0.503; P = 0.956). This may reflect the multifactorial nature of surgical decisions, which depend not only on severity scoring but also on anatomic disruption, haemodynamic instability and institutional protocols. Multiple studies similarly reported that not all patients with severe thoracic trauma required operative management.[5,6,19,20] In isolated thoracic trauma, lesion-specific factors such as massive haemothorax or persistent air leak may dictate surgical intervention independent of the composite score. Another important consideration is that ICU admission, while statistically significant in this study, is partly institution-dependent and may be influenced by local practices and resource availability. Therefore, greater emphasis should also be placed on more objective end points such as duration of mechanical ventilation. Future studies should evaluate these outcomes, along with prolonged ICU stay, in a standardised manner to better assess TTSS performance.

From a clinical standpoint, TTSS remains a simple, practical and reproducible score that incorporates key physiological and radiological variables. It appears particularly useful for identifying patients likely to require ICU-level care. In the current cohort, a score of ≥11 was associated with 62.5% ICU admission and 25.0% mortality; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size (n = 8) in this group. Furthermore, the ICU admission rate of 17.6% in the TTSS 6–10 group suggests that a lower threshold may be useful as a screening tool to prompt early ICU consultation, balancing sensitivity and specificity. This observation should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires prospective validation. In resource-limited or high-volume emergency settings, this score can support rapid decision-making and help prioritise monitoring and escalation of care. However, it should not be used as a stand-alone predictor of mortality or surgical intervention in this population.

Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning have enabled the creation of complex trauma risk models that integrate large datasets.[21,22,23] These systems may offer superior predictive accuracy, particularly for mortality and long-term outcomes. However, their widespread adoption is currently constrained by high costs, infrastructure demands and a lack of prospective validation.[21,24] In contrast, TTSS offers an accessible, validated tool that can be implemented in both high- and middle-income countries. Future multicentre prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to refine TTSS thresholds, better assess mortality prediction in adequately powered cohorts, explore its integration with AI-based triage tools and evaluate its performance in polytrauma patients.

This is one of the first multi-centre evaluations of TTSS from the Middle East, enhancing the generalisability of the score to non-Western settings. Inclusion of a wide range of clinically relevant outcomes (mortality, ICU stay, ventilation, surgery) provides a comprehensive assessment of TTSS utility. Nonetheless, the retrospective design introduces possible selection and documentation bias. The very small number of deaths (n = 3) was a major limitation and substantially reduced the power to assess mortality prediction. Additionally, although the study focused on isolated thoracic trauma, minor associated non-thoracic injuries may have been present and could have acted as confounders. Exclusion of patients with multisystem injuries may also reduce external validity.

5. Conclusion

In this cohort of patients with isolated thoracic trauma, TTSS was most useful for identifying patients at risk of requiring ICU-level care and respiratory support. While higher TTSS values were associated with adverse outcomes, TTSS demonstrated poor and statistically non-significant discrimination for mortality and no discriminatory value for surgical intervention. A TTSS threshold ≥11 may be associated with higher ICU admission rates; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to the study's small sample size and should be considered hypothesis-generating. These findings should be interpreted in the context of the very low number of mortality events and cohort-specific characteristics. Accordingly, TTSS should be used as an adjunct for early triage and monitoring rather than as a stand-alone predictor of mortality or operative need. Future prospective and multicentre studies are needed to refine its thresholds, validate its integration into trauma care pathways, and assess its performance against emerging AI-based prediction models.
