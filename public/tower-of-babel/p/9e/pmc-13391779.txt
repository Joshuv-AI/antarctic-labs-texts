United States comprehensive cancer control plans: a national review
Authors: Leslie S. Given, Karin Hohman, Cynthia Vinson, Nikki Hayes, Beth Slotman, Lisa Stevens, Prajakta Adsul, Kalina Duncan, Margaret Farrell, Aubrey Villalobos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIn 2021, the United States (US) Comprehensive Cancer Control National Partnership conducted a review of US cancer control plans. Here we have summarized the methods used and the results of this review to better understand the plans’ comprehensiveness, strengths, weaknesses, and inclusion of emerging cancer prevention and control topics.MethodsFor this review, the authors adapted a tool used in a global review of national cancer control plans. The review tool was piloted, modified, and programmed into a web survey software for reviewer data entry. We obtained and reviewed plans from all 50 states, the District of Columbia, 6 US Pacific Island Jurisdictions and Puerto Rico, and 8 Tribes and Tribal organizations (N = 66).ResultsThis review highlighted many strengths (e.g., use of data) of US cancer control plans and some areas where more specific plan strategies and content are needed (e.g., resource needs). 59% of plans covered all six cancer control areas. Most addressed equitable access to services (83%), but few targeted specific groups like people with disabilities (12%). Nearly all used data for objectives (96%), and most defined implementation responsibilities (76%), though only 15% discussed financial resources or strategies.ConclusionCCC plans are data-informed documents developed with community input outlining jurisdictional priorities that help inform policies and are implemented through multisectoral coalitions. These plans offer adaptable strategies that address both cancer burden and shifting social, economic, and organizational factors. While plans are strong in areas like prevention and use of data, opportunities exist to better address financial planning, the needs of specific populations like older adults and people with disabilities, and emerging topics such as vaping. The review serves as a call to action for enhanced collaboration to address these gaps and accelerate efforts to reduce the burden of cancer.

Background

Comprehensive cancer control (CCC) is a collaborative, multi-sector approach to reduce community cancer burden. While the global concept of national cancer control programs was formalized by the World Health Organization in the early 1990s, the comprehensive cancer control (CCC) approach in the United States began as a concept in 1994 [1, 2] and has evolved into a systematic approach, supported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) National Comprehensive Cancer Control Program (NCCCP). The NCCCP funds US states, Tribes, Tribal organizations, territories, and Pacific Island Jurisdictions to convene coalitions to develop, implement, and evaluate CCC plans.

CCC plans are jurisdictional documents that typically span a period of five or more years and are updated periodically so that the most current information can be used to inform policy; they are blueprints for local action [1] that should be based on cancer data about a geographic location and include interventions known to improve cancer control. CCC plans are guided by the evidence-based priorities of the NCCCP, which include primary prevention (e.g., human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, tobacco use prevention and cessation, and sun safety), early detection and treatment (particularly for breast, cervical, colorectal, and lung cancers), cancer survivor needs (e.g., patient navigation and access to support services), and promotion of access to good health care for all. Underlying these priorities is a focus on policy, systems, and environmental change approaches and evaluation of outcomes [3].

CCC plans are developed, implemented, and monitored by coalitions, which are comprised of individuals (e.g., health care providers, cancer survivors, researchers, etc.) and organizations (e.g., health departments, universities, cancer centers, hospitals, community organizations, advocacy groups, etc.) with shared interests in cancer prevention and control. These coalitions fulfill a CDC requirement for NCCCP awardees, to leverage program resources, systems, and partnerships to achieve CCC plan objectives [4].

The US Comprehensive Cancer Control National Partnership (CCCNP), led by CDC and the National Cancer Institute (NCI), conducted a review of 66 US CCC plans from states, the District of Columbia, Tribes, Tribal organizations, and US territories and freely associated states. The purpose of the review is to provide a current understanding of the plans’ comprehensiveness, strengths and weaknesses, and inclusion of emerging cancer prevention and control topics. The last review of US plans occurred in 2011 [5]. The review results identify focal areas to inform technical assistance and training for CCC programs and coalitions and serve as a baseline for future assessments of US plans.

Methods

A group of CCC experts, including eight of the authors representing US federal health agencies and other key partners focused on domestic cancer prevention and control, adapted a validated review tool previously used in a global assessment of national cancer control plans [6–8]. The global review tool primarily focused on the mention of topics within plans. The US-adapted tool shifted that focus to capture more of the context of the review topics and if the topics were accompanied by specific strategies linked to goals and objectives. This adaptation was designed to provide a high-level national overview of plan content rather than a granular assessment of individual plan quality, aligning with the study's primary goal of identifying broad strengths and weaknesses across the country. Employing this shift away from “mentions” to “strategies”, the authors adapted the core set [7] of global review questions to better fit a US context and US cancer control terminology. Sixteen questions were added, including on new content areas reflecting the evolution of plans in the US and the impact of emerging issues such as e-cigarettes and vaping; lung cancer screening; cancer health equity concerns; aging populations; and liver cancer and hepatitis B vaccination. The small planning group piloted the adapted review tool on 6 CCC plans. As a result, all questions in the tool were edited for clarity, some questions combined, and others split into separate questions. The final US plan review tool consisted of 48 questions (Table 1). The review tool was built as a Google Form for reviewers’ ease of data entry. To provide insight into how content was coded, reviewers were instructed to look for specific strategies or activities. For example, the inclusion of “cancer clinical trials” was confirmed by statements such as, “Collaborate with other organizations to develop and distribute educational materials about the availability and importance of existing clinical research protocols and the available option to refer young adults through the age of 21 to pediatric oncology centers” (New Jersey CCC Plan 2013–2018). The availability of “survivorship follow-up care” was identified through strategies like, “Implement best practices for transition from active cancer treatment to post-treatment care and hospice services” (Iowa Cancer Plan 2018–2022). These examples illustrate the shift from merely mentioning a topic to defining a planned action, which was a focus of this review.

The authors recruited 25 reviewers (i.e., coders) from the CCCNP and the International Cancer Control Partnership (ICCP), who were knowledgeable of cancer control and CCC planning. The authors held an orientation to explain the purpose of the review and to provide instructions on use of the tool, methods for conducting a review, and coding definitions. Each plan was randomly assigned to two reviewers, who independently read the plans and answered the tool questions about plan content, including if the plan included high-level goal statements, measurable objectives, and specific strategies or activities planned to make progress toward stated plan objectives. Reviewer responses were compared to identify differences in coding. When differences were found, a third reviewer independently reviewed the plan and adjudicated responses, which was then used as the final set of data. This process was used to support consistency across reviewers and reduce the influence of individual interpretation on coded results.

We obtained plans from the CDC website or from CDC and reviewed plans from all 50 states, the District of Columbia, six US Pacific Island Jurisdictions and Puerto Rico, and eight Tribes and Tribal organizations (N = 66). We reviewed the most recent plan available if there were multiple plans for one locality. The dates covered by the plans included a range from 2007–2012 to 2021–2025 (Fig. 1). Prevention

All plans have goals in cancer prevention. Strategies to reduce obesity incidence (87%), tobacco use (86%), and to increase physical activity (88%), and HPV vaccination (86%) are seen most frequently in the plans.

Screening and early detection

Most plans (95%) include increasing the public awareness of cancer screening for asymptomatic individuals and strategies to increase screening for breast (82%), cervical (82%), and colorectal cancers (89%). Strategies for patient navigation to screening are included in 65% of plans. Fewer than half of plans address prostate cancer screening (41%) and more than half (56%) address lung cancer screening.

Survivorship

Many plans include strategies related to follow-up care for cancer survivors (85%). Plans also include strategies related to psychosocial care (65%); patient navigation for survivor services (58%); pain management (52%); and palliative care training for health care providers (64%).

Cross-cutting topics, health equity, and meeting needs of specific populations

The plan review included several questions on health equity and addressing the needs of specific populations. Plans include promoting equitable access to resources for quality and affordable cancer prevention, screening, and treatment services (83%); and addressing the needs of certain racial, ethnic, and uninsured populations (61%). Strategies for other populations such as people living with disabilities (12%) are not as prevalent among the plans reviewed (Table 2).

A limited number of plans include strategies specific to pediatric or geriatric populations. While 33% of plans include some pediatric cancer care strategies throughout, only half of those plans have a dedicated section with strategies regarding childhood and/or adolescent cancer. Of the 55 plans with strategies related to workforce development, strategies mainly focus on training for patient navigators (53%) and community health workers (38%) (detailed data are not shown in Table 2).

Support for plan implementation

The plan review also included questions to assess several elements related to plan implementation, from use of data to evaluation and monitoring, as well as other implementation factors, including governance and resources for plan implementation (Table 2).

Evaluation, use of data, and evidence

Cancer surveillance data, such as data from the CDC-funded National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR) and NCI-funded Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registries is critical for assessing cancer burden and evaluating CCC efforts. Ninety-eight percent of the plans include cancer-specific data. Specifically, 46% include cancer-specific data for all sections or goal areas, and another 52% include cancer-specific data for at least some but not all areas. Ninety-five percent of plans include other data, aside from cancer registry data and cancer risk factor data, such as data from screening programs. Plans include strategies regarding plan evaluation and monitoring (59%); some SMART (specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, and time-bound) objectives (39%); or all SMART objectives (49%). We found that 42% of plans specify an evidence base for plan strategies or interventions (26 plans) or state the evidence base for all strategies (2 plans). However, multiple reviewers indicated in an open field that while a specific evidence base is not indicated, several included strategies are evidence-based (e.g., recommended Community Guide intervention https://www.thecommunityguide.org/).

Research

Most plans include strategies related to clinical trials (70%) while fewer include strategies related to cancer research in general (44%).

Governance and resources

Plans commonly include responsibility for plan implementation (76%) and endorsement by a health department or government official (70%), but far fewer (15%) specify the financial resources needed or strategies to secure resources.

Discussion

The aim of this review is to document CCC plans’ comprehensiveness, strengths and weaknesses, and inclusion of emerging cancer prevention and control topics. The data presented indicate plans are largely comprehensive, covering the cancer continuum [1] and including goals and strategies that align with the most common and deadly cancers and with CDC’s NCCCP priorities. Comprehensiveness of cancer plan content across the cancer continuum

The cancer plans reviewed demonstrate a consistent approach to cancer prevention and control across the US states, Tribes, territories, and Pacific Island Jurisdictions funded through the CDC’s NCCCP. Nearly all plans covered the 6 areas of the continuum, from prevention to end-of-life. The content areas with most coverage—prevention, screening and early detection, and survivorship—align with the historical priorities of the NCCCP.

More than 40% of cancers can be prevented with modification to risk factors [9]. Goals focused on cancer risk reduction are critical in CCC plans and present opportunities to coordinate with other chronic disease efforts (e.g., diabetes, heart disease, stroke) that address common risks, such as tobacco, alcohol, overweight and obesity, poor nutrition, physical inactivity, and immunization [10]. Positively, all plans included strategies related to primary cancer prevention. The high number of plans that included specific strategies to increase HPV vaccination may reflect the national priority of programs over the past several years [11]. However, there are opportunities for increasing hepatitis B vaccination efforts for liver cancer prevention [12], much like the recent HPV technical assistance efforts [13]. E-cigarettes (vaping) is a newer content area for CCC plans, but we expect to see increased numbers of related strategies in updated plans as evidence continues to emerge [14]. Opportunities also exist to expand the number of plans that include prevention strategies related to sun and ultraviolet safety [15], alcohol consumption [16], and radon [17]. Cervical, breast and colorectal cancers can also be prevented by treatment of pre-cancerous lesions and polyps detected through screening of asymptomatic individuals, making these both cancer prevention and early detection and diagnosis strategies.

According to CDC’s United States Cancer Statistics, the top four cancers for incidence and mortality are lung, breast, prostate, and colorectal; there are specific screening recommendations related to each from the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) and recommendations from the Community Guide, so their inclusion as focal cancers in CCC plans is not surprising. CDC’s National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program (NBCCEDP) and Colorectal Cancer Control Program (CRCCP) funding recipients are critical partners in plan implementation, reflected in the prominence of breast, cervical, and colorectal screening strategies across plans. For prostate cancer, the USPSTF recommendation that men ages 55–69 make an individual decision with their provider may account for fewer plans including prostate cancer screening strategies [18]. Nearly half of the cancer plans reviewed include lung cancer screening objectives. Navigation, including by Community Health Workers (CHWs), increases cancer screening and diagnostic follow-up rates [19]. Accordingly, screening navigation strategies were common and are expected to increase in plans as they are updated given relatively recent Community Guide recommendations for interventions engaging CHWs for cancer screening (April 2019) and navigation to screening to improve health equity (July 2022) [20].

Patient navigation is also an important strategy for improving access to and optimizing outcomes of cancer treatment post-diagnosis [21], therefore many plans included navigation strategies for survivors as well. CCC plans focused somewhat less on cancer management (i.e., diagnostic workup, staging and multimodality treatment) compared to prevention and screening. This is likely due to the sphere of influence of the CCC coalitions that develop and implement the plans being largely situated in the public health domain versus having direct influence on cancer care delivery within health systems. However, inclusion of cancer survivors and addressing their needs remains a national priority for CDC’s NCCCP [22]. Consequently, most plans included strategies related to follow-up care for survivors, but opportunities remain to increase the number of plans with strategies supporting psychosocial care, spiritual care, palliative care and pain management, and navigation services [23].

Health equity and meeting needs of specific populations

In cancer control, health equity means that all people have “an equal opportunity to prevent cancer, find it early, and get proper treatment and follow-up after treatment is completed.” [24] Central to health equity are social drivers of health (SDOH)—the conditions in which we live, learn, work, and play. While nearly all plans included objectives to address SDOH and promote good health among racial and ethnic populations, CCC plans could also include objectives to address the factors that contribute to health disparities in rural communities and among other populations disproportionately affected by cancer. Interviews in 2021 with CCC program leaders in rural areas revealed barriers and successful strategies to engage and include rural communities in CCC plan development and implementation [25].

According to the US Census Bureau, by 2030 1 in 5 American adults is expected to be age 65 or older. Given the aging US population, and because cancer is more prevalent in older adults, there are opportunities for more CCC plans to include strategies specific to addressing the needs of geriatric patients. While cancer in children and adolescents is less prevalent, it is the leading cause of disease-related death among children from birth to age 14 in the US [26]. Only one-third of plans include strategies for pediatric cancer, so there are opportunities to increase awareness and include strategies to better serve this population [27].

Support for plan implementation

Evidence-based cancer control uses data to inform priorities and incorporate proven approaches to address priority areas. Essential to a relevant and valid CCC plan is the use of data to determine the cancer burden in a geographical region and to set targets for improvement [28]. We observed excellent inclusion of cancer-related data in the reviewed plans (98%), an improvement from a prior review published in 2011 where there was only moderate presentation of data on disease burden [5]. Because CDC-funded NPCR award recipients and NCI-funded SEER registries are key CCC partners—and because registry data are a key resource for CCC planning and evaluation—opportunities exist to increase the number of plans that include specific strategies to support registry enhancements that focus on evolving needs, such as data modernization. Another critical element of a successful CCC plan is the use of data and other information to develop SMART objectives, with baselines and targets for measuring progress. Most plans had some or all SMART objectives, but opportunities remain to convert “developmental” objectives as baseline data are established so targets can be set.

To achieve a CCC plan’s objectives and goals, it is essential to identify specific strategies with evidence of effectiveness, to indicate who is responsible for implementing them, and to determine resources required for implementation [10]. Rochester et al. [5] found in their prior review that there was limited description of these elements, and our review a decade later indicates some improvement, with further opportunities to strengthen plans. There are multiple resources available to CCC coalitions to advance the use of data and evidence in CCC plans (Table 3). In addition, while most plans included strategies related to clinical trials recruitment and access, far fewer included general cancer research strategies. This highlights a gap that could be filled through strategic partnerships (e.g., with NCI-Designated Cancer Centers, academic research institutions) and through workforce development (i.e., research literacy and skills for CCC professionals), all of which would contribute to growing the CCC evidence base. Resources for implementation of CCC plans in the US come from varied sources, including federal, state and philanthropic funding. CDC and its partners encourage NCCCP awardees and coalitions to document and report how funds are leveraged to diversify and increase community support for plan implementation [29]. The majority of CCC plans reviewed did not specify financial resource needs for plan implementation, and only 12% included strategies to secure necessary resources for plan priorities. Two plans included 1) a reference to a separate detailed resource plan or 2) specific existing funding and/or estimates for implementation of plan objectives and strategies. Moving forward, programs and coalitions could consider publishing resource plans to better reflect resource needs as well as partners’ commitment, accountability, and contributions in implementing CCC plans.

Evolving CCC programs and plans

NCCCP expectations regarding CCC plan implementation have evolved over four program cycles. For the 2022 to 2027 cycle, CDC established concrete expectations and performance measures for collaboration among CDC-funded programs; expectations about maintaining viable coalitions; and requirements related to addressing SDOH to ensure all cancer program resources can achieve common program goals [4]. CCC plans provide flexible, adaptable, and resilient approaches to reflect not only the cancer burden but also the evolving physical, economic, and organizational climate in which plans exist. For example, coalitions can adapt efforts during public health emergencies and adjust strategies to changing disparities and emerging issues indicated by surveillance. Plan revision processes include recent data and intentional engagement with diverse inputs, including policy makers, representatives of communities experiencing disparities, and others [30]. CCCNP members are committed to helping CCC coalitions demonstrate the relevance of cancer planning and the importance of strategically engaging community members in planning and implementing activities to prevent and control cancer in their communities. CDC houses current CCC plans for funded programs on their website: https://www.cdc.gov/comprehensive-cancer-control/about/programs.html

Limitations

Direct comparison of the results of this review to the 2011 assessment of cancer plans by Rochester et al. [5] was not possible due to differences in methodology. Some plans had expired and had not yet been updated at the time of the review (i.e., ended before 2021). The timing of plans may have influenced inclusion of some emerging issues, such as strategies related to vaping and lung cancer screening. Not all reviewed plans included details necessary to assess all review categories (e.g., 2 plans were state health plans, reviewed in the absence of a CCC plan). Furthermore, while our review tool captured elements related to cancer care, such as patient navigation and palliative care, it did not comprehensively assess the full spectrum of cancer management, including access to diagnostic services and treatment modalities. Although plans were independently reviewed by two reviewers and adjudicated by a third reviewer when needed, formal inter-rater reliability statistics were not calculated. Finally, it should be noted that our data collection tool was adapted to yield a high-level national overview; therefore, it may not fully capture the granular depth or overall comprehensiveness of individual state, tribal, or territorial plans. Importantly, reviewing the plans is not the same as assessing implementation of the plan; additional evaluation efforts are needed to assess a plan’s impact.

Conclusion

The CCC approach relies heavily upon the willingness and commitment of governments, organizations, and individuals to work together to reduce the burden of cancer. Overall, the review of US CCC plans revealed several foundational strengths, most notably the consistent use of data-driven goals across the cancer continuum, a strong alignment with national public health priorities, including primary prevention and improving outcomes for racial and ethnic minorities, and a focus on prevalent and deadly cancers with available screening options. Despite these notable strengths, significant opportunities remain to enhance the impact of future plans. Subsequent plan revisions should prioritize the development of concrete strategies to secure financial resources, coordinate or integrate with other chronic disease efforts, and consistently cite the evidence base for selected interventions to ensure successful implementation. Plans can also promote the expansion of strategic partnerships and workforce development to support cancer control research. Furthermore, plans must adapt to address emerging challenges, such as vaping, radon exposure, indoor tanning, excessive alcohol use, vaccine-preventable cancers (e.g., viral hepatitis and liver cancer, HPV-related cancers), lung cancer screening, and extreme weather preparedness, while also expanding survivorship components to comprehensively cover psychosocial and palliative care. Finally, to ensure that cancer control efforts benefit all populations nationwide, future plans must more deliberately address the unique needs of specific, vulnerable populations, particularly older adults, rural communities, people with disabilities, and pediatric and young adult survivors. This review points to areas for future technical assistance and training and provides a baseline for tracking progress and improvements in CCC planning in the future.
