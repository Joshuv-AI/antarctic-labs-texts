Targeting AIF to trigger RIPKs/MLKL necroptosis: a disulfiram-based strategy to reverse paclitaxel resistance in ovarian cancer
Authors: Jiaofeng Li, Wei Zhou, Hualong Tang, Wanglong Zhu, Miao He, Qing Xiong, Zhengyu Han, Xinyue Lin, Ji Chen, Xingqiang He, Mei Chen, Yuhan Yang, Xia Meng, Liping Ma, Fengtian Li, Jingyi Wang, Kun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePaclitaxel is a cornerstone first-line therapy for ovarian cancer, but its efficacy is limited by chemoresistance. Disulfiram (DSF), an FDA-approved alcohol-aversion drug, shows promising antitumor activity. However, its impact on paclitaxel sensitivity in ovarian cancer and the underlying mechanism remain unclear.MethodsThis study identified DSF as a reversal agent of paclitaxel resistance. In vitro, DSF’s chemosensitizing effect was tested on paclitaxel-resistant and parent ovarian cancer cells. Mechanistic investigations involved examining the triggering of RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL-regulated necroptosis versus caspase-dependent apoptosis, depending on cellular context. RIPK1 was inhibited with Nec-1, and the direct binding to Apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) was studied, with AIF knockdown used for validation. In vivo studies were conducted using resistant xenograft models to confirm the anticancer efficacy and observe necroptosis markers.ResultsDSF significantly improved paclitaxel chemosensitivity in vitro. Mechanistically, it selectively triggered necroptosis in paclitaxel-resistant cells with higher caspase-8 expression, while promoting apoptosis in cells with lower caspase-8 expression. RIPK1 inhibition impaired DSF-induced necroptosis and chemosensitization. Furthermore, DSF directly bound to AIF, enhancing RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL phosphorylation and necroptosis activation; this effect was suppressed by AIF knockdown. Crucially, in vivo studies confirmed that DSF boosted the anticancer efficacy of paclitaxel in resistant models, which was accompanied by upregulation of the necroptosis executor p-MLKL and no observable toxicity.ConclusionOur findings not only unveiled a novel mechanistic basis for the repurposing of DSF but also highlighted AIF-mediated necroptosis as a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome chemoresistance in ovarian cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03029-1.Keywords: Disulfiram, Necroptosis, Paclitaxel, Resistance, Ovarian cancer

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is one of the three major malignant tumors in the female reproductive system, with a high mortality rate [1]. Symptoms of ovarian cancer are insidious and often not diagnosed until advanced stages, leading to poor survival rates [2]. After surgical resection, combination chemotherapy based on paclitaxel was usually used to treat ovarian cancer [3]. Paclitaxel has been approved by United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a chemotherapy agent for ovarian cancer in 1992 [4]. However, prolonged administration of paclitaxel often leads to drug resistance, with approximately 80% of patients experiencing relapse [5]. The occurrence of chemoresistance has become the greatest challenge and urgent problem in current malignant tumor treatment [6, 7]. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of chemoresistance and screening for chemosensitizing agents are crucial for improving the prognosis of ovarian cancer patients. Previous studies have shown that there is an important relationship between the occurrence of tumor drug resistance and the escape from apoptosis [8]. Nonetheless, as research on the mechanisms of treatment resistance in malignant tumors advances, numerous studies have revealed that drug resistance of malignancies is not solely restricted to the role of anti-apoptosis, but also involves multiple factors such as inhibition of other programmed cell death, cancer stem cells, and tumor heterogeneity [9–12]. The mechanisms underlying drug resistance are highly intricate, with different types of cancer tissues/cell types evolving their own unique resistance mechanisms [13–15].

Necroptosis, a newly characterized form of programmed cell death mediated through caspase-independent pathways, manifests as lytic cell death that initiates an inflammatory cascade [16, 17]. In contrast, apoptosis achieves non-inflammatory cell death via packaging cytoplasmic contents into apoptotic vesicles. Necroptosis is induced by the ligands binding to TNF family death domain receptors [18, 19]. Under caspase-compromised conditions, phosphorylated receptor-interacting Protein 1 (RIPK1) recruits RIPK3 to catalyze its autophosphorylation and activation. Within the necrosome complex, activated RIPK3 phosphorylates the mixed-lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) pseudokinase, inducing oligomerization and subsequent plasma membrane translocation to execute lytic cell death. Increasing evidence links necroptosis suppression to chemoresistance, showing downregulation of core components (RIPKs, MLKL, and cylindromatosis CYLD) in various cancers, potentially enabling evasion of drug-induced necroptosis [20]. Malignant cells resistant to pro-apoptotic agents (e.g., inhibitors of TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) and B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2)) often exhibit heightened necroptotic vulnerability with combined apoptosis inhibition [11]. Furthermore, cisplatin-resistant malignancies display restored chemosensitivity upon necroptosis induction, whereas genetic ablation of key necroptotic regulators diminishes this efficacy [21, 22]. These findings underscore the necessity to systematically investigate necroptotic dysregulation in tumor resistance and develop targeted interventions, which may yield innovative therapeutic strategies for chemoresistant cancers.

Disulfiram (DSF), clinically designated as antabuse, received FDA approval in 1951 as an alcohol-aversion drug [23]. With over seven decades of documented clinical application, this repurposed agent demonstrates a well-established safety profile with preserved therapeutic stability and efficacy, consistently showing the absence of severe adverse effects in standard dosing regimens [24]. Recently, DSF has been proven to confer anti-cancer activity in preclinical studies [25]. DSF can target cancer stem cells to improve chemoradiation sensitivity [26]. DSF also interacts with copper to inhibit cell proliferation and metastasis in multiple tumors, including breast cancer, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, prostate cancer, and liver cancer [27]. However, the role and mechanism of DSF in reversing paclitaxel resistance in ovarian cancer remain unclear.

This study aims to evaluate the impact of DSF on chemoresistance and necroptosis in paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer and explore the underlying mechanism. Obtained data unveiled that DSF bound to AIF, thereby promoting RIPKs/MLKL necroptosis signaling and enhancing the chemosensitivity of paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer. These findings provided novel insights into enhancing chemotherapy responsiveness in ovarian cancer and support the repurposing of DSF for malignant tumor therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines, antibodies and reagents

A2780 and SKOV3 human ovarian cancer cell lines were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The paclitaxel-resistant human ovarian cancer cell line A2780/Taxol was constructed by the concentration gradient generation method. A2780/Taxol, A2780, and SKOV3 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 or M5A cell culture medium (Gibco, Invitrogen, Grand Island), which contained 10% FBS (Cytofactory Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). All cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2, and passed every 3–5 days. DSF, paclitaxel, and Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Massachusetts, USA). Antibodies against RIPK1, p-RIPK1, RIPK3, p-RIPK3, MLKL, and p-MLKL were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA). Antibody against AIF was gained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Antibodies against GAPDH and β-tubulin were purchased from HUABIO (Hangzhou, China).

CCK-8 assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to reach 80–90% confluency, followed by treatment with various concentrations of DSF or paclitaxel for 48 h, as indicated. Meanwhile, if Nec-1 was used in this experiment, cells were also pretreated with Nec-1 (100 μM) for 24 h. Finally, after incubation with CCK-8 at 37 °C for 1 h, OD values at 450 nm were detected to calculate cell viability.

Colony formation assay

In brief, cells were digested and inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of about 1 × 103 cells per well for 7 days. The cells were then incubated and treated with DSF, paclitaxel, or Nec-1. Colonies were washed twice with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) overnight, stained with 1% crystal violet (Beyotime, NanTong, China) overnight. The 6-well plates were immersed in reverse osmosis water, excess crystal violet was cleaned, and colonies were imaged and counted.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed with RIPA strong lysis solution (Beyotime, NanTong, China), subsequently scraped with a cell scraper, and centrifuged at 13000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. After obtaining supernatant, the protein concentration was quantitatively determined by the bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) protein analysis kit (Beyotime, NanTong, China). The supernatant was combined with Loading Buffer (Beyotime, NanTong, China) and subsequently denatured through boiling. The protein samples were isolated with 10% SDS-PAGE (40 µg/lane) and transferred into a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad, California, USA). The membrane was enclosed with 5% skim milk or 2% BSA solution for 60 min at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. Following this, the membrane was washed with TBST three times, incubated with the secondary antibody (ZSBG-BIO, Peking, China) at room temperature for 1 h, and then washed again with TBST three times. Protein signals were detected by an ultra-sensitive chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime, NanTong, China) and Bio-Rad Molecular Imager (Bio-Rad, USA). The relative protein expression was normalized against GAPDH using ImageJ analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell apoptosis and necrosis were detected by flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, cells were seeded in RPMI 1640 with 5% FBS in 6-well plates, and pretreated with DSF or Nec-1 for 24 h, followed by treatment with paclitaxel for an additional 48 h. According to the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection kit (KeyGen Biotech, Nanjing, China), cells were collected and resuspended in binding buffer, which contained Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI). Subsequently, the samples were incubated for 10 min at 4 °C in the dark and then analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA).

In vivo experiments

Five-week-old healthy female NOD/SCID mice (16–19 g) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Guangdong, China), and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, at a temperature of 25 °C and humidity of 55%. After a 7-day quarantine period, 200 μL of A2780/Taxol cell suspension (1 × 107 cells) was injected subcutaneously into the mice. Subsequently, the mice were then randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 6 per group), including the control, DSF, paclitaxel, and DSF combination with paclitaxel. Once the tumors became discernible, tumor size was recorded once every 2 days using calipers, with tumor volume (mm3) calculated using the formula: V = 0.52 × (length × width2). Drug treatment was initiated when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3. DSF (40 mg/kg) or paclitaxel (5 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally administered daily or every other day, respectively. The vehicle consisted of PBS, Cremophor, and DMSO, with a volume ratio of 7.5:2:0.5. Following the 22-day treatment period, all mice were euthanized for tumor excision. The final tumor volume and weight were recorded for analysis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Medical College (2023023).

Drug affinity responsive target stability

We lysed the cells in a mild lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, NanTong, China). The total protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, NanTong, China). An equal amount of protein from the lysate was aliquoted into four experiment groups, and then incubated with or without DSF at room temperature for 20 min. Thermolysin (5 mg/mL) was added to each group, followed by a 30-min incubation at room temperature. Further protein E (10 mg/mL) was added to terminate the reaction. Aliquots (20 μL) from each treated sample were loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel for separation. The gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 and photographed. Subsequently, the target protein bands were excised for mass spectrometry analysis. In brief, the excised gel bands were destained, reduced with DTT, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested overnight with trypsin at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were extracted, dried, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. Peptide separation was performed on an UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano system (ThermoFisher, USA) using a C18 column with a 30‑min gradient of 5–38% solvent B (80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid). Tandem mass spectrometry was carried out on a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher, USA) equipped with a Nano Flex ion source (ThermoFisher, USA), operating in data‑dependent acquisition mode. MS/MS data were searched against the human protein database using PEAKS Online 11 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada) with a false discovery rate of 1% at the PSM level.

Cellular thermal shift assay

Upon completion of a 48 h treatment with DSF (200 μM), A2780/Taxol cells were collected and aliquoted, followed by incubation at room temperature, 45℃, 48℃, 51℃, and 58℃ for 2 h, respectively. Cell lysates were then extracted and quantified. Briefly, cells were lysed with RIPA strong lysis solution (Beyotime, NanTong, China) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (Beyotime, NanTong, China). Subsequently, protein samples (60 μg per lane) were electrophoretically separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to 0.45 μM PVDF membranes. Briefly, the protein expression was examined by western blotting.

Hematoxylin and Eosin staining (H&E staining)

The mouse liver and kidney tissues fixed with fixative solution were dehydrated stepwise using gradient ethanol (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), cleared with xylene, and then embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were stained using a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Peking, China), and finally mounted with neutral balsam for observation under an optical microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The tumor tissue sections were immersed in sodium citrate buffer and subjected to microwave heating for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, goat serum (ZSGB-BIO, Peking, China) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to further reduce background staining. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against p-MLKL (HUABIO, Hangzhou, China). The following day, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody (ZSGB‑BIO, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 20 min. Finally, color development was performed using 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine Tetrahydrochloride (ZSGB-BIO, Peking, China), and the staining reaction was monitored under microscopic observation.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were independently repeated three times, averaged, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA). The student’s t-test (two-tailed) or the analysis of one-way ANOVA was used to check the differences. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

DSF inhibited cell proliferation and enhanced chemosensitivity of ovarian cancer to paclitaxel

The ovarian cancer cells SKOV3, A2780, and the acquired paclitaxel-resistant cells A2780/Taxol were treated with different concentrations of DSF. After that, CCK-8 assay was employed to determine cell viability. The result showed that DSF reduced survival rate of ovarian cancer cells in a concentration-dependent manner, and A2780 cells were more sensitive to DSF compared with A2780/Taxol and SKOV3cells (Fig. 1A). Moreover, colony formation assay also presented that DSF decreased the number of colonies in A2780, A2780/Taxol, and SKOV3 cells (Fig. 1B). These data indicated that DSF could inhibit ovarian cancer cell proliferation. Based on cell survival rate, 200 nM DSF was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments. In acquired paclitaxel-resistant cells, A2780/Taxol cells cotreated with DSF and paclitaxel, the paclitaxel chemoresistance was alleviated (IC50 3.38 ± 0.44 μM vs 0.10 ± 0.06 μM), compared to the cells treated with paclitaxel (Fig. 1C). Similarly, A2780 (IC50 0.41 ± 0.02 μM vs 0.01 ± 0.002 μM) and SKOV3 (IC50 3.09 ± 0.99 μM vs 1.09 ± 0.34 μM) cells treated with DSF were more sensitive to paclitaxel than the control (Fig. 1D-E). Furthermore, DSF promoted the ability of paclitaxel-inhibited colony formation in A2780/Taxol and A2780 cells (Fig. 1F-G). These data suggested that DSF was endowed with latent energy to resensitize paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer cells.

DSF heightened the activity of RIPKs/MLKL necroptosis signaling in the paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer cells

We compared necroptosis-related proteins in A2780 cells and their paclitaxel-resistant cells A2780/Taxol. It was observed that although total protein levels of RIPK1 and MLKL were upregulated in A2780/Taxol cells, the expressions of p-RIPK1, RIPK3/p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL were downregulated (Fig. 2A. These data indicated that paclitaxel resistance of ovarian cancer was involved in suppression of necroptosis activity. In addition, the protein levels of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL were not significantly changed by DSF in drug-resistant cells A2780/Taxol, while p-RIPK1, p-RIPK3, and p-MLKL were upregulated (Fig. 2B). Unexpectedly, the expression of RIPK1/p-RIPK1, p-RIPK3, and p-MLKL was suppressed by DSF in the parent cells A2780 (Fig. 2C). A similar phenomenon was observed in SKOV3 cells (Fig. 2D). Obtained results suggest that DSF-increased paclitaxel sensitivity of the resistant ovarian cells is correlated with activation of necroptosis signaling in a cell context-dependent manner.

DSF facilitated paclitaxel-induced necroptosis or apoptosis in a cell context-dependent manner

To further analyze the specific mechanism underlying the chemosensitivity of ovarian cancer to paclitaxel, we used flow cytometry to detect the effect of DSF on the death modes of ovarian cancer cells. A2780 and A2780/Taxol cells were treated with different concentrations of DSF for 48 h. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the apoptosis rate of A2780/Taxol cells was not changed significantly, while the apoptosis rate of A2780 cells was increased with the increase of DSF concentration (Fig. 3A-B). Moreover, A2780/Taxol and A2780 cells were cotreated with DSF and different concentrations of paclitaxel. Interestingly, paclitaxel-induced necrosis was increased by DSF in A2780/Taxol cells, while paclitaxel-induced apoptosis was enhanced in A2780 cells (Fig. 3C-D). These results suggested that DSF facilitated paclitaxel-induced necroptosis or apoptosis in a cell context-dependent manner.

DSF activated caspase-dependent apoptosis signaling in caspase-8-abundant ovarian cancer cells

Recently, caspase-8 has been shown to be a crucial initiator of death receptor-induced apoptosis and a suppressor of RIPKs/MLKL-dependent necroptosis [28, 29]. Here, we examined the differential expression of caspase-8 protein between A2780/Taxol and A2780 and SKOV3 cells. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, the protein expression of caspase-8 was significantly increased in A2780 and SKOV3 cells, compared to A2780/Taxol cells (Fig. 4A). Notably, DSF did not significantly impact the expression of caspase-8/3 or Cleaved-caspase-8/3 in A2780/Taxol cells with caspase-8 deficiency (Fig. 4B). However, DSF obviously downregulated caspae-8/3 and upregulated Cleaved-caspase-8/3 in A2780 and SKOV3 cells with caspase-8 overexpression (Fig. 4C-D). The above suggests that DSF could activate apoptosis signaling in caspase-8-abundant ovarian cancer cells, while triggering necroptosis in caspase-8-deficient paclitaxel-resistant cells.

RIPK1 inhibition reversed DSF-triggered necroptosis in caspase-8-deficient paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer

It is well known that Nec-1 is an inhibitor of RIPK1 activity, which can inhibit the activation of RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL signaling [7]. In order to further assess the effect of DSF on RIPK1-dependent necroptosis in drug-resistant cells, we conducted pharmacological intervention using Nec-1 in A2780/Taxol cells. Obtained data showed that although both DSF and Nec-1 did not significantly change the protein levels of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, the ability of DSF to upregulate p-RIPK1, p-RIPK3, and p-MLKL was weakened in A2780/Taxol cells treated with Nec-1 (Fig. 5A). Moreover, DSF still further enhanced paclitaxel-induced expressions of p-RIPK1, p-RIPK3, and p-MLKL. However, this tendency was observably abolished by Nec-1(Fig. 5B). Collectively, these results further verified that DSF promoted canonical RIPK1-dependent necroptosis signaling.

Necroptosis was critical for DSF-improved chemosensitivity

We next investigated the role of RIPKs/MLKL signaling in DSF-improved chemosensitivity of paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer cells. As shown in Fig. 6A, although DSF enhanced the cytotoxicity of paclitaxel in A2780/Taxol cells, this sensitization was partially abolished by the necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1. Consistent with this finding, DSF-potentiated inhibitory effect of paclitaxel on clonogenic survival was also attenuated by Nec-1 (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that Nec-1 significantly inhibited the intensive effect of DSF on paclitaxel-induced cell necrosis (Fig. 6C). These data confirmed that DSF activated RIPK1-dependent necroptosis to resensitize paclitaxel-resistant cells.

DSF potentiated antioncogenic effect of paclitaxel on chemoresistant ovarian cancer cells in vivo

To verify whether DSF could restore the sensitivity of paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer cells in vivo, we established a xenograft model in NOD/SCID mice using A2780/Taxol cells with the following groups: control, DSF, paclitaxel, and DSF plus paclitaxel. After 22 days of administration, the results showed that the growth rate of tumors was lower in the DSF plus paclitaxel group than the paclitaxel group (Fig. 7A-B). After euthanasia, the final tumor volume of the DSF plus paclitaxel group was significantly decreased, compared to the paclitaxel group. Moreover, a similar phenomenon is observed for the final tumor weight in the DSF plus paclitaxel group (Fig. 7C-E). These results indicated that DSF enhanced paclitaxel inhibition on the tumorigenicity of chemoresistant ovarian cancer cells. Furthermore, H&E staining showed no significant toxicity of DSF in the liver and kidneys, while immunohistochemical analysis showed that DSF in combination with paclitaxel increased p-MLKL expression, a critical necroptosis executor [20], in the subcutaneous xenograft tumor tissue relative to paclitaxel monotherapy (Fig. 7F-H). The above results revealed that DSF activated necroptosis and improved the efficacy of paclitaxel in the resistant ovarian cancer cells in vivo.

DSF binding to AIF intensified RIPKs/MLKL signaling and chemosensitivity

We further explored the molecular target by which DSF regulated necroptosis. The study identified multiple candidate targets of DSF through drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) experiments coupled with mass spectrometry analysis (Fig. 8A-C). Cross-referencing these candidate targets with known necroptosis regulators revealed that DSF physically interacted with AIF (Fig. 8B-C). Moreover, molecular docking analysis identified a specific binding pocket for DSF within the AIF structure (Fig. 8D). Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) further showed that DSF maintained the stability of AIF proteins under thermal stress conditions (Fig. 8E). These data validated the direct binding between DSF and AIF, suggesting that this complex formation may mechanistically underlie DSF-induced necroptosis. As expected, AIF knockdown inhibited the expression of p-RIPK1, p-RIPK3, and p-MLKL, even though it did not change the total level of these proteins (Fig. 8F-G). Furthermore, AIF knockdown reversed DSF-improved paclitaxel chemosensitivity in drug-resistant ovarian cancer cells (IC50: siAIF#1: 0.39 ± 0.07 µM, siAIF#2: 0.27 ± 0.08 µM vs siCon: 0.10 ± 0.03 µM) (Fig. 8H). The above results suggested DSF could enhance RIPKs/MLKL necroptosis signaling and chemosensitivity through binding to AIF.

Discussion

Ovarian cancer remains an intractable malignancy with high morbidity and mortality. Its poor prognosis is largely attributed to advanced-stage diagnosis, high rates of metastasis and recurrence, and resistance to systemic therapy [30]. Although the FDA approved paclitaxel as a first-line treatment for advanced ovarian cancer, its efficacy is severely compromised by chemoresistance [31]. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic agents to improve chemosensitivity is an important entry point for effectively treating ovarian cancer. Owing to the high heterogeneity of ovarian cancer, most combination regimens benefit only a small patient subset, while many such strategies are prohibitively expensive, exceeding general financial accessibility [32, 33]. So, we focus on identifying cost-effective, commonly used drugs to improve paclitaxel efficacy and prognosis for the majority.

A frequently used method for enhancing anti-cancer effect refers to drug repurposing, which represents the process of exploring existing drugs for new therapeutic indications [34]. Repurposed drugs have long been established in practice as prospective agents for tumor therapy. By unveiling novel pharmacological mechanisms, they have emerged as a focus in recent years for screening antitumor medications [34]. Undoubtedly, this approach significantly accelerates the clinical translation of drugs [35]. DSF, as an alcohol withdrawal drug, has excellent pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability. Recent studies have revealed that DSF in combination with Cu2 + exerts potent anticancer activity against various malignancies, including glioblastoma, lung, liver, breast, pancreatic, and gastric cancers [27]. Here, we found that in the absence of copper, DSF was still able to enhance paclitaxel chemosensitivity in ovarian cancer. However, the underlying mechanism needs to be clarified urgently.

Programmed cell death (PCD) pathways, such as apoptosis and regulated necrosis, are essential for normal cell turnover, organismal development, host defense against pathogens, and homeostasis [36]. Dysregulation of PCD is increasingly implicated in cancer progression [37]. Studies have revealed that apoptosis resistance is a major driver of chemotherapeutic failure. One promising strategy to overcome this obstacle is to bypass apoptosis and induce cancer cell death via alternative pathways [17]. Necroptosis represents such an alternative: a regulated form of necrotic cell death that is caspase-independent and mediated chiefly by receptor-interacting protein kinases (RIPK1/3) and mixed lineage Kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) [38]. This suggests that triggering necroptosis not only overcomes apoptosis resistance but may also enhance the activity of anticancer agents. In this study, we found paclitaxel-resistant A2780/Taxol cells exhibited impaired phosphorylation of RIPK1/3 and MLKL, indicating dysregulation of necroptotic pathway, a phenotype consistent with evasion of programmed necrosis in therapy-resistant malignancies.

Interestingly, our study verified that DSF increased the activity of RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL and paclitaxel-induced necroptosis in A2780/Taxol cells, which are deficient in caspase-8. Inversely, DSF inhibited the activity of RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL in A2780 and SKOV3 cells with caspase-8 overexpression, while promoting the cleavage of caspase-8 and caspase-3, and paclitaxel-induced apoptosis. These results revealed that the cell-context-dependent duality of DSF hinges on the differential basal expression of caspase-8. Consistent with previous studies, caspase-8 has been established as a molecular switch between apoptosis and necroptosis [39, 40]. It has been reported that abundant caspase-8 contributes to apoptosis and inhibits necroptosis by cleaving and degrading RIPK1/3, or by targeting the deubiquitinase CYLD to stabilize the polyubiquitin chain of RIPK1, thereby preventing RIPK3 activation [41, 42]. Conversely, caspase-8 deficiency renders cancer cells more susceptible to necroptosis [39].

Our further study confirmed that Nec-1, a pharmacologic antagonist of RIPK1 kinase activity [43], suppressed DSF-induced necroptosis signaling in A2780/Taxol cells, suggesting that DSF enhanced necroptosis and paclitaxel sensitivity via activation of RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL in caspase-8-deficient resistant ovarian cancer. Crucially, DSF bound to AIF, a mitochondrial protein canonically linked to caspase-independent apoptosis [44], thereby inducing RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL phosphorylation cascades, and enhancing necroptosis and chemosensitivity in vitro/vivo model. This mechanism aligns with emerging evidence that AIF functionally interfaces with RIPKs to initiate necroptosis [45]. For example, in the cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury model, RIPK3 forms a cytoplasmic complex with AIF, facilitating its nuclear translocation to trigger necroptosis [46]. Similarly, RIPK1 inhibitor (e.g., Nec-1) blocks AIF release from mitochondria to the cytosol and nuclear translocation in the necroptosis model of neuronal excitotoxicity, revealing RIPK1’s role in driving AIF-dependent death [47, 48]. In this study, Nec-1 inhibited DSF-triggered chemosensitization and necroptosis, indicating that RIPK1 kinase activity is essential for AIF-mediated necroptosis. Moreover, the translational potential of DSF is verified by its efficacy in vivo models, where DSF promoted paclitaxel-inhibited resistant xenograft growth while elevating intratumoral p-MLKL, a final executor of necroptosis [49]. Importantly, DSF’s safety profile, evidenced by the absence of liver/kidney toxicity, supported its repurposing potential, consistent with clinical studies of DSF combinations in recurrent ovarian cancer [27].

Notably, AIF knockdown inhibited RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL necroptosis signaling and DSF-mediated chemosensitization, establishing AIF as the linchpin target. In addition, our work expanded the mechanistic understanding of AIF beyond its classical apoptotic role. Although AIF is traditionally known for caspase-independent apoptosis via DNA fragmentation, its partnership with RIPK1/RIPK3 in necroptosis represents a context-dependent functional switch [50, 51]. Recent structural studies show that AIF conformational changes, regulated by post-translational modifications like PARylation, determine its pro-death vs. pro-survival functions [52]. Here, DSF binding may lock AIF in a pro-necroptotic conformation, analogous to PARP5A/RNF146 phase-separated condensates that control RIPK1 activation and necroptosis [53].

In conclusion, this study identified DSF as a potent and cost-effective agent capable of reversing paclitaxel resistance in ovarian cancer. Mechanically, DSF significantly enhanced paclitaxel efficacy by selectively activating programmed necrosis in paclitaxel-resistant cells via direct binding to AIF and leading to RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL phosphorylation cascade. Crucially, the dual death mode triggered by DSF, necroptosis in caspase-8-low resistant cells and apoptosis in caspase-8-high cells, highlighted its ability to utilize tumor heterogeneity and bypass conventional apoptosis resistance. These findings not only provided a novel mechanistic basis for the repurposing of DSF in paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer but also proposed AIF-mediated necroptosis activation as a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming chemoresistance.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, the reversal effect of DSF on drug resistance was only demonstrated in paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer; whether it applies to other resistance types (e.g., cisplatin-resistant cells) requires further investigation. Second, the absence of patient‑derived xenograft (PDX) models limits the clinical translatability of our in vivo observations. Accordingly, future PDX‑based studies are warranted to corroborate the antitumor efficacy and safety of DSF‑paclitaxel regimen in the context of resistant ovarian cancer.
