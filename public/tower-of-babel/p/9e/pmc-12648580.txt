Hepatic activating transcription factor 3 protects against systemic inflammation by attenuating lipotoxicity
Authors: Chencheng Hu, Qian Yang, Runzhi Yu, Shuwei Hu, Bing Meng, Yanyong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundActivating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) is known to play a key role in regulating lipid and lipoprotein metabolism. However, the effects of hepatic ATF3 on systemic inflammation and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsAn adeno-associated virus with hepatocyte-specific promoter was used to construct mouse models with hepatocyte-specific overexpression or knockdown of ATF3.ResultsOverexpression of human ATF3 in hepatocytes prevented high-fat (HF) diet-induced systemic inflammation in C57BL/6J mice and reversed systemic inflammation in db/db mice. Conversely, hepatocyte-specific loss of ATF3 aggravated diet-induced systemic inflammation. In co-culture studies, the anti-inflammatory effect of hepatocyte ATF3 on adipose tissue was found to be dependent on the presence of free fatty acids mixture. Mechanistically, ATF3 ameliorated HF diet-induced hepatic lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity likely mediated through AMPKα activation.ConclusionOur findings collectively indicate that hepatocyte ATF3 alleviates systemic inflammation by reducing hepatic lipotoxicity, a mechanism that may involve the activation of AMPKα. Targeting hepatic ATF3 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for systemic inflammation induced by hepatic lipotoxicity.

Introduction

Hepatic lipotoxicity-induced inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [[1], [2], [3]]. As the central hub for lipid metabolism, the liver maintains lipid homeostasis through tight regulation of de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid export [4,5]. Disruption of these processes can trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, which collectively contribute to a state of chronic low-grade inflammation within the liver [6,7]. This localized hepatic inflammation further amplifies systemic inflammation by releasing proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), establishing a vicious cycle [8,9]. Therefore, targeting hepatic lipotoxicity to reduce hepatic lipid accumulation and associated inflammatory damage, thereby synergistically modulating systemic inflammation, has become a critical focus in related research fields [10].

Activating Transcription Factor 3 (ATF3) is a member of the ATF/cAMP response element-binding transcription factor family, which binds to the consensus sequence TGACGTCA in gene promoters to regulate gene expression [11,12]. Accumulating evidence indicates that ATF3 functions as an adaptive-response gene, with its expression being induced by a variety of signals, such as genotoxic agents, endoplasmic reticulum stress, cytokines, chemokines, and lipopolysaccharide [13,14]. Through its leucine zipper domain, ATF3 can form homodimers or heterodimers with other bZIP-containing proteins, thereby acting as either a transcriptional activator or repressor [12,15,16]. In recent years, the role of ATF3 in liver regeneration, fibrosis, heart failure, and ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury has gained increasing attention. As a key early-response transcription factor, ATF3 is upregulated after partial hepatectomy (PHx) and has been demonstrated to promote liver regeneration [17,18]. In macrophages, ATF3 has been shown to inhibit carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis [19]. Moreover, cardiac fibroblast-specific ATF3 protects against heart failure, whereas mice lacking ATF3 in cardiac macrophages or myeloid cells exhibit exacerbated heart dysfunction after IR [20,21]. Collectively, these studies highlight the crucial protective role of ATF3 in various forms of tissue injury.

In line with these findings, our previous studies have shown that hepatocyte-specific ATF3 protects against atherosclerosis by regulating lipoprotein and bile acid metabolism, and prevents diet-induced metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) via interaction with hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α [22,23]. Consistent with our findings, alternate-day fasting has been shown to exacerbate atherosclerosis by suppressing hepatic ATF3 expression [24]. Moreover, macrophage-derived ATF3 inhibits gluconeogenesis and promotes fatty acid oxidation, thereby conferring protection against hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in MASH [19]. Additionally, ATF3 induced by lipopolysaccharide can suppress inflammatory gene expression by inhibiting toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling in macrophages [25]. Nevertheless, the role of hepatocyte ATF3 in systemic inflammation remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we demonstrated that hepatocyte-specific expression of human ATF3 reduced lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity in the liver and attenuated systemic inflammation. Conversely, loss of hepatocyte ATF3 produced the opposite effects. We further demonstrate that the protective role of hepatocyte ATF3 against lipotoxicity-induced systemic inflammation is likely mediated through AMPKα activation.

Material and methods

Mice and diet

C57BL/6J mice (strain #N000013), db/db mice (strain #T002407), and Atf3fl/fl mice (strain #T008416) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. AAV8-TBG -Null or AAV8-TBG-Cre (produced by Vector Biolabs) was injected into Atf3fl/fl mice to generate control floxed mice and hepatocyte-specific knockout mice (including Atf3fl/fl mice and Atf3Hep−/− mice). Hepatocyte-specific ATF3-overexpressing mice and their corresponding controls were generated by injection of C57BL/6J mice and db/db mice with AAV8-TBG-hATF3 or AAV8-TBG-Null via the tail vein. All mice were housed in a controlled environment with regulated temperature and humidity under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Unless otherwise specified, male mice were used for experiments. Mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD, Research Diet D12492, 60 % fat). Unless otherwise indicated, all mice were fasted for 5–6 h before euthanasia. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Fudan University (approved number: 20240229–195).

Plasmid and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector construction

The coding sequence of human ATF3 was cloned into an AAV vector under the control of the thyroid hormone-binding globulin (TBG) promoter to generate AAV8-TBG-hATF3. AAV8-TBG-Null (control), AAV8-TBG-Cre, and AAV8-TBG -hATF3 were produced and titrated by OBiO Technology Corp (Shanghai). Each mouse was injected via the tail vein with 3 × 1011 genomic copies of AAV viral particles, followed by high-fat diet feeding.

Chemicals

Sodium palmitate (catalog no. P9767-5G), oleic acid (catalog no. O3008-5 ML), and linoleic acid (catalog no. L9530-5 ML) were purchased from Sigma. A sodium palmitate stock solution was prepared by dissolving the powder in a preheated fatty acid-free BSA solution. The solution was then sonicated for 15 min. Following sonication, it was heated at 55 °C for 20–30 min with shaking until the sodium palmitate was completely dissolved and the solution became clear.

Adipose tissue explants experiment

The white adipose tissue was collected from mice and cut into uniform small pieces (approximately 5–10 mg each), followed by washing with PBS to remove blood vessels and connective tissue.

Cell experiments

Isolation of primary hepatocytes was performed as previously described [26]. Primary hepatocytes were isolated from mice injected with AAV8-TBG-Null or AAV8-TBG-hATF3, or Atf3Hep−/− mice and their control mice. Mouse primary hepatocytes were cultured in DMEM containing vehicle or free fatty acids (FFAs) mixture (300 μM palmitate, 300 μM oleic acid, 300 μM linoleic acid). After 24 h, we collected the media (i.e., conditioned media) and treat with freshly isolated adipose tissue explants for an additional 24 h.

Analysis of plasma proinflammatory cytokines, hepatic lipids, and reactive oxygen species levels

After 5–6 h of fasting, blood was collected from mice via retro-orbital venous plexus puncture. Fresh plasma was immediately separated by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (catalog no. 900-M54), interleukin-1β (catalog no. 900-K47), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (catalog no. 900-M126) were measured using ELISA kits from PeproTech. Approximately 100 mg of liver tissue was homogenized in methanol, and lipids were extracted in chloroform/methanol (2:1 v/v) as previously described [27]. Hepatic triglyceride and total cholesterol levels were measured using Infinity reagents from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Hepatic FFA were measured using a kit from Wako Chemicals. Hepatic reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using the OxiSelect In Vitro ROS/RNS Assay kit (catalog no. STA-347) from Cell Biolabs according to manufacturer's instructions.

mRNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and qRT-PCR was performed using PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A25778) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The reaction protocol was set as follows: 95 °C pre-denaturation for 5 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s). The mRNA expression levels of all target genes were normalized to the internal reference gene 36b4. Primers’ sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

Western blot assays were performed using total cell lysates extracts of the liver samples, as described previously [28]. Antibody against ATF3 (catalog no. ABS136180) was purchased from Absin Bioscience Inc. pACC (catalog no. 29119-1-AP) and α-tubulin (catalog no. 11224-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech Group Inc. Antibodies against ACC (catalog no. 3662S), AMPKα (catalog no. 2603), or pAMPKα (catalog no. 2535) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology.

Oil red O staining

Liver tissues were fixed in 10 % formalin and then embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT). The liver sections were stained with Oil red O. Images were acquired using an Olympus microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.2 software. All datasets were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. For data that followed a normal distribution and exhibited equal variance, an unpaired Student's t-test (with Welch's correction) was applied. If the assumptions of normality or equal variance were not met, the unpaired Mann–Whitney U test was used. All figures were generated using ImageJ or Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Data are presented as means ± SEM. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Overexpression of hepatic ATF3 attenuates high-fat diet-induced systemic inflammatory responses

Thus far, the systemic inflammatory effects mediated by hepatic ATF3 remain incompletely understood. In this study, we generated an AAV that expressed human ATF3 under the control of the thyroid hormone-binding globulin (TBG) promoter (AAV8-TBG-hATF3). When mice were fed a high-fat (HF) diet for 16 weeks, hepatocyte-specific expression of human ATF3 significantly induced hepatic ATF3 protein levels (Fig. 1A), and resulted in a 27 % reduction in plasma tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) levels (Fig. 1B) and a 35 % reduction in plasma interleukin-1β (IL-1β) (Fig. 1C), as well as a 34 % reduction in plasma monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP1) levels (Fig. 1D). Hepatic ATF3 overexpression reverses systemic inflammatory responses in db/db mice

We have shown that overexpression of hepatic ATF3 markedly reduced systemic inflammation in HF diet-fed mice (Fig. 1). To investigate whether ATF3 could also attenuates systemic inflammatory responses in db/db mice. We injected AAV8-TBG-hATF3 or AAV8-TBG-Null (control) into db/db mice by the tail vein. In our previous study, overexpression of ATF3 was shown to reduce hepatic lipid accumulation and apoptosis, suppress the expression of inflammatory and fibrotic genes in the liver, and ultimately ameliorate steatohepatitis in db/db mice [22]. Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrated that hepatocyte-specific overexpression of ATF3 increased hepatic ATF3 protein levels (Fig. 2A). This induction led to reduced plasma levels of TNFα, IL-1β, and MCP1 by 30 %, 39 %, and 35 %, respectively (Fig. 2B–D). Furthermore, hepatic overexpression of ATF3 significantly reduced the mRNA levels of Il-6, Il-1β and Mcp1 in iWAT (Fig. 2E), as well as those of Tnfα, Il-6, and F4/80 in eWAT (Fig. 2F). Ablation of hepatocyte ATF3 aggravates HF diet-induced systemic inflammatory responses

To investigate whether the loss of hepatocyte ATF3 modulates inflammatory responses, we generated hepatocyte-specific Atf3−/− (Atf3Hep−/−) mice by injecting AAV8-TBG-Cre into Atf3fl/fl mice via the tail vein. Compared with Atf3fl/fl mice, Atf3Hep−/− mice showed a reduction in hepatic ATF3 protein levels by approximately 54 % (Fig. 3A and B) after feding a HF diet for 20 weeks. Moreover, Atf3Hep−/− mice showed induced plasma levels of TNFα, IL-1β, and MCP1 by 33 %, 45 %, and 39 %, respectively (Fig. 3C–E). Consistent with those changes in circulatory system, ablation of hepatocyte ATF3 induced mRNA expression of Il-6, Mcp1 in the liver (Fig. 3F) as well as Il-1β, Mcp1, Cd68, and F4/80 mRNA in iWAT (Fig. 3G) and Il-6, Cd68 and F4/80 mRNA in eWAT (Fig. 3H). Hepatocytic ATF3 prevents HF diet-induced hepatic lipotoxicity likely mediated through AMPKα activation

Our gain- and loss-of-function studies have demonstrated that hepatocyte ATF3 protects against systemic inflammatory responses. As the central organ of lipid metabolism, the liver regulates de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid export [4,5]. Dysregulated lipid metabolism in the liver activates proinflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and NLRP3 through endoplasmic reticulum stress and oxidative stress, contributing to chronic inflammation [7,8,30]. Thus, we asked whether hepatocyte ATF3 protected against systemic inflammatory responses via reduction of hepatic lipid accumulation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. In mice overexpressing hepatocyte ATF3 significantly decreased hepatic levels of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), free fatty acid (FFA), and ROS (Fig. 4A–D), which were further confirmed by Oil red O (Fig. 4E). Since AMPKα activation exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects that may alleviate lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity [[31], [32], [33]], we further investigated its role in our experimental context. As shown in Fig. 4F, overexpression of hepatic ATF3 led to a induction in the protein levels of phosphorylated AMPKα (pAMPKα) and phosphorylated acetyl-CoA carboxylase (pACC). Consistent with this gain-of-function result, loss of hepatocyte ATF3 significantly increased hepatic levels of TG, TC, FFA, and ROS (Fig. 4G–J). Oil red O staining further revealed enhanced hepatic lipid accumulation in Atf3Hep−/− mice (Fig. 4K). Moreover, Atf3Hep−/− mice resulted in decreased hepatic protein levels of pAMPKα and pACC (Fig. 4L). Discussion

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is characterized by excessive lipid accumulation in the liver and may progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). MASLD/MASH is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events, chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus and liver-related outcomes, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. It has become a leading indication for liver transplantation [38]. A central pathological mechanism in MASH is hepatic lipotoxicity, driven by excessive intrahepatic lipid accumulation [39]. This process exacerbates liver injury and promotes disease progression by inducing oxidative stress and activating inflammatory pathways [40,41]. So far, the mechanism by which hepatic lipotoxicity contributes to systemic inflammation remains incompletely understood. In this study, we demonstrate that hepatocyte-specific expression of human ATF3 prevents systemic inflammatory responses in high-fat diet-induced C57BL/6J mice and db/db mice. In contrast, hepatocyte-specific loss of ATF3 exacerbates HF diet-induced systemic inflammatory responses. Mechanistically, hepatocyte ATF3 reduces hepatic lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity likely mediated through AMPKα activation. Targeting hepatocytic ATF3 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for systemic inflammation induced by hepatic lipotoxicity.

Hepatic lipid metabolism disorder (including imbalances in synthesis, oxidation, and efflux) plays a central role in driving intrahepatic lipid accumulation and the subsequent development of lipotoxicity [42,43]. ATF3 is well known for its anti-inflammatory properties, primarily achieved through the inhibition of NF-κB activity or TLR4 signaling in RAW 264.7 cells and primary macrophages [44,45]. In ATF3 knockout mice, the activation of mTOR/p70S6K/HIF-1α signaling exacerbated liver ischemia-reperfusion injury by upregulating proinflammatory mediators [46]. However, the role of hepatocytic ATF3 in system inflammation remains not complete understood. It has been demonstrated that hepatocytic ATF3 protects against high-fat and high-cholesterol (HFCF) diet-induced steatohepatitis by upregulating hepatic lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) [22,47]. Similarly, macrophage-specific overexpression of Atf3 alleviates Western diet-induced MASH via AMPKα-mediated FAO [19], a pathway known to suppress lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses [[31], [32], [33]]. Finally, through gain- and loss-of-function studies, we demonstrate that AMPKα activation is a likely mechanism by which ATF3 mitigates hepatic lipotoxicity.

These proinflammatory cytokines enter the bloodstream and disseminate systemically, thereby triggering or exacerbating a state of chronic low-grade inflammation [6,7]. Our previous study demonstrated that loss of adipocyte ATF3 leads to increased inflammation in both plasma and liver [48]. However, the role of hepatocytic ATF3 in the regulation of adipose tissue inflammation has not been reported before. Our in vivo data show that hepatocytic ATF3 reduces plasma levels of proinflammatory cytokines and downregulates the expression of inflammation-related genes in adipose tissue, whereas its deficiency has the opposite effects. To further validate these findings, we treated adipose tissue explants with conditioned medium derived from hepatocytes overexpressing or lacking ATF3, in the presence or absence of an FFAs mixture. Our in vitro studies indicate that hepatocyte ATF3 alleviates adipose tissue inflammation mainly by mitigating FFAs-induced lipotoxicity.

In summary, our findings show that hepatocyte ATF3 protects against systemic inflammatory responses by suppressing hepatic lipotoxicity likely mediated through AMPKα activation. These interesting findings suggest that ATF3 may be a promising target for treatment of hepatic lipotoxicity-induced various metabolic diseases.
