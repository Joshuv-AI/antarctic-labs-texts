Development and validation of the BRAFV600E prediction model from clinical and ultrasound features in papillary thyroid carcinoma: a large cohort study
Authors: Rongsen Zhang, Jia Zhong, Chengxue Jiang, Yan Tan, Xiaoye Zhang, Taiqing Zheng, Guotao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The preoperative prediction of BRAFV600E mutation could help in devising appropriate strategies for patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC). This study aimed to develop and validate a nomogram based on clinical and ultrasound features to predict BRAFV600E mutation in PTC patients.Methods:Between January 2018 and May 2023, a total of 4601 patients with PTC from one center were enrolled in this study. These patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 3220) and an internal validation set (n = 1381) at a 7:3 ratio. Patients from another center were assigned to an external validation set (n = 737). Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify independent predictors and establish the nomogram model. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate the nomogram.Results:Four variables (Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, echogenicity, shape, and echogenic foci) were selected to construct the nomogram for predicting the BRAFV600E mutation in patients with PTC. The AUCs for the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set were 0.812 (95% CI: 0.798–0.826), 0.797 (95% CI: 0.774–0.817), and 0.714 (95% CI: 0.680–0.746), respectively. Calibration curves for the nomogram showed good consistency between the predicted and actual probabilities. DCA indicated significant net benefits, supporting its clinical applicability.Conclusions:The nomogram, based on Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, echogenicity, shape, and echogenic foci, demonstrates good performance in predicting the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC. The model may serve as a useful tool for individualized risk assessment in PTC patients.

Introduction

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), which originates from thyroid follicular cells, accounts for over 80% of all thyroid cancers[1,2]. Classical PTC is typically characterized by indolent behavior, slow progression, and high cure rates[3]. However, approximately 25% of cases demonstrate more aggressive features, which are associated with shortened disease-free survival[4].

These aggressive variants may follow an adverse clinical course, manifesting as extrathyroidal extension, lymph node metastasis, distant metastases, or disease recurrence[5,6]. Accurate discrimination between aggressive variants and classical subtypes of PTC is crucial, as they require distinct management strategies. Although previous studies have attempted to use imaging features to predict aggressive PTC subtypes, the diagnostic performance remains suboptimal, with an area under the curve (AUC) of only 0.74[7]. Consequently, precise prediction of aggressive PTC variants based solely on ultrasound characteristics remains challenging.

Numerous studies have established a link between the BRAFV600E mutation and aggressive tumor behavior, as well as poor prognosis, in PTC[8–10]. As a serine/threonine kinase within the RAF gene family, BRAFV600E promotes PTC tumorigenesis by aberrantly activating the MAP kinase pathway. This mutation is significantly associated with lymph node metastasis, extrathyroidal extension, advanced disease stage, and disease recurrence in PTC[11–13]. However, genetic sequencing for its detection is invasive, costly, time-consuming, and thus not suitable for widespread use. If the BRAFV600E mutation status could be determined using straightforward clinical and ultrasound features, it would enable the non-invasive prediction of aggressive PTC behavior.

Several clinicopathological factors, including specific ultrasound features, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and lymph node metastases, have been strongly linked to the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC patients, suggesting their potential utility for preoperative prediction[14–18]. However, these studies did not incorporate several parameters together to build a practical prediction model. Xu et al[19] developed a nomogram model based solely on clinical and ultrasound features to identify the BRAFV600E mutation in 275 patients at Xianyang Central Hospital. Unfortunately, direct statistical comparison with established models was not discussed, and the diagnostic performance has not been validated. It remains a great challenge to provide a robust nomogram based on clinical and ultrasound features to predict the BRAFV600E mutation in patients with PTC.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed a cohort of 5338 PTC patients from two referral centers. Our objective was to develop and validate a prediction model based on clinical and ultrasound features for predicting the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and adhered to the STARD reporting guidelines as well as the TRIPOD items[20]. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the two participating centers (the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University; the Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital). Between January 2018 and May 2023, consecutive patients with thyroid nodules in the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University were included. The nodules were selected based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: (1) presence of the target nodule on thyroid ultrasound; (2) pathological confirmation of papillary thyroid carcinoma; (3) BRAFV600E mutation analysis. Exclusion criteria: (1) history of therapy (radiotherapy or chemotherapy); (2) missing images or poor image quality.

A flow diagram of patient selection is presented in Figure 1. The relevant demographic and clinical information for each patient was extracted from the clinical electronic record system. Finally, a total of 4601 patients were enrolled in this study. Patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 3220) and an internal validation set (n = 1381) at a 7:3 ratio. A cohort of 737 patients with PTC from the Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital between January 2019 and January 2023 was used as the external validation set. Figure 1.Flow chart of patient selection. Ultrasound examinations and imaging analysis

In our study, ultrasound examinations were performed using a Resona 7 (Mindray Medical International Ltd.) equipped with a 3–11 MHz linear transducer, an EPIQ 5 (Philips Healthcare) equipped with a 5–14 MHz linear transducer, and a LOGIQ 9 scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) that incorporated a 10–14 MHz linear transducer. Imaging parameters were adjusted by the radiologist performing the ultrasound examination and acquiring the images. During the ultrasound examination, three images of each target nodule (one largest transverse plane, one largest long-axis plane, and one Doppler ultrasound image on the largest long-axis plane) were routinely recorded. Additional images containing important features (composition, echogenicity, shape, margin, echogenic foci, etc.) were also acquired by the radiologist.

US images were independently reviewed by two radiologists (W.G.T., and Z.J., each with more than 10 years of experience in thyroid ultrasound). Neither of them had been involved in the original examinations, and both were blinded to other imaging findings and the final diagnosis. For the target nodule, each radiologist documented the ultrasound characteristics according to the 2017 American College of Radiology Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS) lexicon and User’s Guide[21,22]. The definitions of the ultrasound lexicons have been described in a previous study[23]. In case of discrepancy, a third experienced radiologist (Z.R.S., with more than 15 years of experience in thyroid ultrasound) reviewed the images to make the final decision. For patients with two or more PTCs, only the largest nodule was selected as the target for image analysis.

Data collection

The clinical data, including age, sex, nodule diameter, location, BRAFV600E mutation status, and the presence of lymph node metastasis, were collected from the patients’ electronic medical records. Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was confirmed by pathological examination. The BRAFV600E mutation analysis procedures were the same as those previously reported[16].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to screen for independent factors in the training set. The variance inflation factor was assessed to check for multicollinearity among the final predictors. Additionally, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the AUC were calculated to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the nomogram in the training set and two validation sets. Calibration plots were generated to evaluate the performance characteristics of the nomogram. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to determine the clinical utility and net benefit of the model.

The inter-operator agreement on the ultrasound features (composition, echogenicity, shape, margin, and echogenic foci) was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The ICC was calculated based on absolute agreement in a two-way random-effects model. The strength of agreement was categorized as follows: poor (ICC < 0.50), moderate (ICC between 0.50 and 0.75), good (ICC between 0.75 and 0.90), or excellent (ICC > 0.90)[24].

Data analyses were performed using MedCalc software (version 15.2.2, MedCalc Software), IBM SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., New York, USA), or R (version 4.5.0; R Development Core Team), with the “rms,” “rmda,” and “ggplot2” packages. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Inter-operator agreement

The inter-operator reliability for the five ultrasound features was excellent: composition (ICC = 0.899; 95% CI: 0.874–0.920), echogenicity (ICC = 0.951; 95% CI: 0.939–0.962), shape (ICC = 0.973; 95% CI: 0.967–0.979), margin (ICC = 0.913; 95% CI: 0.891–0.931), and echogenic foci (ICC = 0.964; 95% CI: 0.955–0.972).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a noninvasive nomogram incorporating Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, echogenicity, shape, and echogenic foci to preoperatively predict the BRAFV600E mutation in patients with PTC. The model demonstrated good discrimination, with an AUC of 0.812 (95% CI: 0.798–0.826) in the training set, 0.797 (95% CI: 0.774–0.817) in the internal validation set, and 0.714 (95% CI: 0.680–0.746) in the external validation set. Our findings suggest that this nomogram could be a valuable tool for the preoperative identification and management of PTC.

The BRAFV600E mutation rates were 71.8% (2312/3220) in the training set, 75.0% (1036/1381) in the internal validation set, and 72.7% (536/737) in the external validation set. These findings align well with the prevalence rates reported in previous literature, which range from 66.7 to 78.2%[10,15,25]. On the other hand, our reported mutation rate was notably elevated compared to the range of 25.5–48.5% found in other publications[10,26]. Geographic variation is a potential underlying cause of this heterogeneity in reported mutation rates.

Previous studies have reported conflicting findings on the associations between the BRAFV600E mutation and ultrasound or clinicopathological characteristics in PTC[17,19,27,28]. A previous systematic review demonstrated that age, sex, multifocality, lymph node metastasis, vascular invasion, extrathyroidal extension, and advanced disease stage are all significantly associated with the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC[27]. Nguyen et al also reported a significant association between the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC and several factors, including older age, larger tumor size, and a lobulated tumor margin[29]. Moreover, studies revealed that significant ultrasound characteristics, including microcalcifications, hypoechogenicity, margin, extrathyroidal invasion, and TI-RADS points, were independent risk factors for BRAFV600E mutation in patients with PTC[17–19]. However, other studies found no associations between BRAFV600E mutation and any ultrasound or clinicopathological characteristics[17,25,28,30]. In our study, lymph node metastasis and margin were not identified as effective predictors of BRAFV600E mutation in PTC. The likely reason is that our study had a relatively high proportion of microcarcinomas. In microcarcinomas, the rate of lymph node metastasis is relatively low, and malignant margin features are less pronounced.

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is a prevalent autoimmune thyroid disease characterized by lymphocytic infiltration and impaired thyroid function[31,32]. Our findings indicated that the BRAFV600E mutation was correlated with a reduced prevalence of background Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, thereby confirming the observations from earlier studies[33,34]. However, another study found that the frequency of the BRAFV600E mutation was higher in PTC with concomitant lymphocytic infiltration[35,36]. Thus, the mechanism underlying the interaction between the BRAFV600E mutation and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis in PTC remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Our DCA findings demonstrated that using this nomogram for clinical decision-making provides a net benefit for patients and outperforms the previous nomogram model established by Xu et al[19]. Our nomogram can assist clinicians in more accurately identifying PTC patients with the BRAFV600E mutation, demonstrating its potential to aid in personalized medical decision-making. However, it still requires further validation in large-scale prospective studies. Compared with the previous study, our nomogram showed lower sensitivity (68.6% vs. 81.8%) but higher specificity (86.0% vs. 66.7%), and a comparable AUC (0.812 vs. 0.806)[19]. Although Xu et al[19] developed a nomogram based on microcalcifications, hypoechogenicity, lymph node metastasis, extrathyroidal extension, and shape to predict the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC, their study was limited by a small sample size and a lack of validation.

The performance of the prediction model developed in this study declined in the external validation set (AUC: training set 0.812, internal validation set 0.797, external validation set 0.714), a pattern commonly observed in multicenter validation studies[37,38]. Possible explanations for this decline include the following. Firstly, as a major referral center in Central China, our institution tends to receive more complex cases or tertiary referrals, often presenting with pronounced malignant ultrasound features. In contrast, the external validation center, which serves a more localized, community-based population, likely includes a higher proportion of early-stage or histologically atypical nodules. Secondly, there may be differences in ultrasound equipment and standardized protocols for image acquisition across institutions.

Although our study assumes that the BRAFV600E mutation serves as a surrogate marker for aggressive PTC behavior, we acknowledge that mutation status alone cannot fully reflect the complex biological behavior of PTC. Evidence indicates that additional molecular alterations, such as TERT promoter mutations and various co-mutations, are significantly associated with more aggressive clinicopathological features, including extrathyroidal extension, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and disease recurrence[39,40]. Future prospective studies are warranted to determine whether integrating additional promoter mutations and co-mutation profiles with ultrasound features could further improve predictive accuracy and provide a more complete assessment of PTC biological behavior.

Our model demonstrates good performance using only conventional ultrasound features, although certain alternative approaches may achieve higher predictive accuracy. For instance, shear-wave elastography and contrast-enhanced ultrasound have shown potential in characterizing thyroid nodule aggressiveness[41,42]. Additionally, radiomics and deep learning models can extract high-dimensional features from ultrasound images, potentially improving the prediction of molecular mutations[43]. However, these methods often require specialized equipment and post-processing software, limiting their widespread adoption, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Our nomogram prioritizes clinical usability, accessibility, and interpretability, serving as a practical first-line screening tool for preoperatively predicting the BRAFV600E mutation.

There were limitations in our study. First, the time span of ultrasound examinations was long, and the image quality in the early stages was relatively poor. Second, only patients who underwent thyroidectomy or fine-needle aspiration were included, which may have introduced selection bias. A key limitation was the exclusion of patients on active surveillance and those with indeterminate pathological findings; consequently, the generalizability of our prediction model and findings to these low-risk, conservatively managed populations was limited, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Third, elastography and contrast-enhanced ultrasound were not included in the present study. Future studies incorporating these advanced techniques may further improve diagnostic performance and generalizability. Fourth, an additional limitation stems from the use of ultrasound scanners from different technological generations (e.g., LOGIQ 9). Although standardized acquisition protocols were followed, inherent variations in image quality and resolution among devices may have introduced variability in ultrasound features. Fifth, the model showed a decreased AUC in external validation (0.714 vs. 0.812 in the training set), indicating limited generalizability across different medical centers or ethnic groups. Future efforts should focus on multi-center harmonization, prospective external validation with larger and more diverse samples, and model recalibration to enhance clinical applicability in diverse populations. Sixth, due to the retrospective nature of this study, quantitative data on thyroid hormone levels and thyroid antibodies were not available, and the diagnosis of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis relied solely on postoperative pathological examination rather than preoperative laboratory findings. Seventh, the high prevalence of the BRAFV600E mutation in our cohort (>70%) may limit the generalizability of our findings to Western populations, where the reported mutation frequency is substantially lower. Future validation in more geographically diverse cohorts is warranted.

In summary, we developed a validated nomogram using non-invasive clinical and ultrasound features to preoperatively predict the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC. This tool shows potential for assisting clinicians in guiding individualized treatment planning.
