Aging cells, young patients: senescence in Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Isabella Y Kong, Lisa Giulino-Roth, Christian Steidl
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Agrusa et al1 examined the transcriptomic landscape of Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg (HRS) cells from 13 pediatric patients with classic Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and found enrichment of senescence-associated gene signatures and elevated expression of the antiapoptotic gene B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2).

cHL is a unique B-cell malignancy in which the malignant HRS cells constitute a minute fraction of the tumor mass, often only 1% to 2%, and are embedded within a dense and reactive inflammatory microenvironment.2 This extreme cellular rarity has historically made it difficult to isolate HRS cells and to accurately characterize their transcriptome, thereby limiting insight into the genomic and transcriptional programs that drive disease biology. Recent technical advances, including purification of HRS cells using fluorescence activated cell sorting,3 now enable more refined molecular interrogation of these elusive cells. Genomic characterization of HRS cells to date has revealed recurrent alterations that lead to dysregulation of the JAK/STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways, as well as multiple mechanisms of immune evasion. Constitutive activation of JAK/STAT signaling (driven by copy number gains in JAK2), activating mutations in STAT family members, and loss-of-function alterations in negative regulators, such as SOCS1, alter cytokine expression programs and promote the cellular fitness of HRS cells. These changes frequently co-occur with mutations in TNFAIP3 and/or NFKBIA, as well as copy number gains in components of the NF-κB pathway, which together reinforce aberrant prosurvival signaling in HRS cells.4 In parallel, frequent alterations in genes involved in immune evasion, such as arm-level gains in 9p and focal amplification of 9p24.1, drive the overexpression of programmed death-ligand 1 and 2 (PD-L1/2). HRS cells further evade immune recognition by cytotoxic CD8+ and CD4+ T cells through impaired antigen presentation, which is mediated by loss-of-function alterations in B2M that affect major histocompatibility complex class I expression, and through structural variants of the class II major histocompatibility complex transactivator (CIITA). However, genomic alterations alone do not fully capture the mechanisms that underlie immune evasion and survival in cHL. To date, transcriptomic analysis of cHL has largely focused on deconvoluting the cHL tumor microenvironment (TME) while insights into the HRS transcriptomes remain relatively sparse.

Using multiparameter flow cytometry, Agrusa et al purified HRS cells, together with immune cell populations from pediatric HL tumors, thereby enabling direct analysis of HRS cell–specific gene expression. A comparison between HRS cells and intratumoral CD30+ cells revealed upregulation of pathways associated with the cell-cycle and DNA damage checkpoint regulation. A more focused evaluation of 15 genes associated with cellular senescence revealed increased expression of antiapoptotic/senescent genes, including BCL2, MCL1, and CDKN1, suggesting that pediatric HRS cells adopt a senescence-like transcriptional state. Extending on earlier studies in which BCL2 expression was identified as a poor prognostic indicator in cHL5 and in which cHL-derived cell lines were found to be sensitive to the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax,6 the current study confirmed the sensitivity of HRS cells to venetoclax ex vivo, reinvigorating a rationale for the therapeutic targeting of antiapoptotic cellular programs. Together, these findings add texture to our understanding of HRS cell biology and suggest that cHL may be amenable to targeting with senolytic or apoptosis-directed therapeutic strategies.

Cellular senescence is characterized by stable cell-cycle arrest, resistance to apoptosis, altered metabolism, and extensive transcriptional reprogramming. A hallmark of senescent cells is the acquisition of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which consists of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, angiogenic mediators, and matrix-remodeling enzymes. Although SASP signaling can promote immune-mediated clearance of damaged cells, persistent or dysregulated SASP activity also has been shown to support tumorigenesis by reshaping the local tissue environment and promoting chronic inflammation. Senescence-associated features in HRS cells have been reported previously in HL-derived cell lines and patient samples.7,8 Agrusa et al extended these observations by demonstrating, at the transcriptomic level, that primary pediatric HRS cells exhibit a senescence-associated gene expression program. Persistent senescence signaling might enable HRS cells to tolerate oncogenic stress, immune surveillance, or therapy-induced damage while actively shaping the surrounding immune microenvironment through SASP-mediated cross talk. The existence of a coordinated senescence state in HRS cells provides an interesting conceptual framework for explaining the paradoxical coexistence of extensive immune infiltration and ineffective antitumor immunity in cHL.

An important strength of this study is the focus on pediatric cHL. Emerging evidence suggests that there are differences in cHL biology at different patient ages. For example, whole-genome sequencing of HRS cells revealed a higher mutational burden in pediatric and young adult patients than in older adults (>50 years), along with accelerated accumulation of the age-associated mutational signatures SBS1 and SBS5(4). Evaluation of the cHL TME has also identified age-related differences, including an enrichment in B-cell and mast cell expression signatures in younger patients and more pronounced macrophage- and stromal-associated signatures in older patients.9 In addition, children aged <10 years with cHL have been observed to have increased M1 macrophages and cytotoxic T cells when compared with adolescents and young adults (15-39 years) among whom M2 macrophages and LAG3+ type 1 regulatory T cells are observed at higher frequencies or older patients (>40 year) who have a predominance of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells and PD-L1+ macrophages.10 Although the study does not directly compare pediatric and adult cHL, it provides a valuable foundation for future analyses that are aimed at defining age-specific differences in HRS cell biology and TME interactions.

Moving forward, larger-scale transcriptomic and genomic analyses across diverse patient populations will be essential to define the prevalence and biologic significance of transcriptional programs, including senescence, in cHL. Comparative studies of pediatric and adult cHL tumors will be essential to define age-specific differences in HRS cell biology and tumor-immune interactions. At higher resolution, single-cell RNA sequencing and multiomic approaches will be critical for resolving the HRS cell heterogeneity and disentangling tumor-intrinsic programs from microenvironmental influences. Finally, spatial molecular imaging technologies will be indispensable for mapping HRS cells within their native tissue context and elucidating how HRS programs, including senescence-associated signaling, shape local immune architecture to potentially uncover new therapeutic vulnerabilities.
