Network attack knowledge inference with graph convolutional networks and convolutional 2D KG embeddings
Authors: Weiwu Ren, Hewen Zhang, Ying Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the challenge of analyzing large-scale penetration attacks under complex multi-relational and multi-hop paths, this paper proposes a graph convolutional neural network-based attack knowledge inference method, KGConvE, aimed at intelligent reasoning and effective association mining of implicit network attack knowledge. The core idea of this method is to obtain knowledge embeddings related to CVE, CWE, and CAPEC, which are then used to construct attack context feature data and a relation matrix. Subsequently, we employ a graph convolutional neural network model to classify the attacks, and use the KGConvE model to perform attack inference within the same attack category. Through improvements to the graph convolutional neural network model, we significantly enhance the accuracy and generalization capability of the attack classification task. Furthermore, we are the first to apply the KGConvE model to perform attack inference tasks. Experimental results show that this method can infer implicit relationships between CVE-CVE, CVE-CWE, and CVE-CAPEC, achieving a significant performance improvement in network attack knowledge inference tasks, with a mean reciprocal rank (MRR) of 0.68 and Hits@10 of 0.58, outperforming baseline methods.Keywords: Graph convolutional neural networks, Attack inference, Knowledge graph completion, Security knowledge graphSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

With the continuous development of network information technologies such as big data, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence, the landscape of network security has become increasingly complex. Network attackers leverage advanced technologies and strategies, constantly evolving and improving their attack methods, posing severe challenges to the cybersecurity of nations, enterprises, and individuals1. In particular, advanced persistent threats (APTs), such as APT282, are renowned for their complex penetration tactics. These attacks usually target specific systems and software, using a variety of attack methods to gradually infiltrate the target system to achieve their attack goals.

In response to the security threats faced in cyberspace, researchers have conducted extensive exploration, attempting to construct various models, deeply mine log information, and simulate attack scenarios to reveal the behavior patterns and intentions of attackers. For example, DeepAG3 is a framework that uses system logs to detect threats and predict APT attack paths. It employs a transformer model to detect APT attack sequences from scratch by modeling the semantic information in system logs. Hossain et al.4 were the first to reconstruct APT attacks and restore attack scenarios using traceability graphs and causal dependencies. Xiong et al.5 proposed the Conan framework, which is based on state tracking and detection. It uses finite state automata to identify the state information and semantic knowledge in audit logs, and then constructs intelligent strategies to reconstruct APT attack chains. Li et al.6 proposed a privacy-preserving attack traceability method based on graph convolutional networks (GCNs). This method introduces privacy protection mechanisms to anonymize sensitive user data, ensuring data privacy while using GCNs for attack traceability analysis. Zimba et al.7 used Bayesian networks to identify the shortest paths of APT attacks in cloud computing and simulate covert attack scenarios. Current research primarily focuses on four areas: log analysis, attack attribution, privacy protection, and attack path modeling. Researchers are leveraging technologies such as machine learning, deep learning, causal reasoning, finite state machines, and Bayesian networks to enhance APT detection and defense capabilities.

Traditional network security defenses, while effective against common threats to some extent, often struggle to cope with large-scale penetration attacks. Therefore, integrating and analyzing existing attack information, and conducting in-depth reasoning based on this information, has become a critical task. In this context, knowledge inference within knowledge graphs provides a novel approach. It utilizes attack inference and threat intelligence analysis methods to perform threat modeling and build attack prediction models. These methods help identify potential attack paths, the probability of exploitation, and the behavior patterns of attackers, enabling the prediction of possible attacks and the implementation of corresponding defense measures.

This paper aims to investigate how to achieve more effective network attack prevention through the inference of network attack knowledge. Currently, most network attacks exhibit high sustainability and stealth, making them difficult to detect and posing severe threats to network security. Therefore, in response to complex network attacks, this paper proposes a network attack knowledge inference method, KGConvE, based on graph convolutional networks. Unlike traditional inference methods, KGConvE focuses on the collaborative relationships among various attack knowledge in major open-source cybersecurity knowledge bases and threat intelligence, constructing a cybersecurity knowledge graph that associates CVE, CWE, CAPEC, and APT. At the same time, it focuses on the similar relational characteristics between attack knowledge. By aggregating the features of adjacent nodes through graph convolutional networks (GCNs), it comprehensively captures the relational information between nodes and classifies attack knowledge based on similarity. KGConvE then uses embedding representation learning to generate feature vectors for entities and relationships, and applies 2D convolutions for operations and scoring. During attack knowledge inference, KGConvE performs reasoning predictions based on the scores provided by the scoring function, ranked from highest to lowest. This enables the extraction of implicit attack knowledge from the cybersecurity knowledge graph, contributing to proactive defense and network security situational awareness in complex network environments. Related work

Knowledge inference is a key method for knowledge graph completion8,9 and has become one of the research hotspots in the field of knowledge graphs. As an important technology for advancing artificial intelligence toward higher-level cognitive intelligence, knowledge inference10,11 can be integrated with graphs to infer network attacks or network activities. Methods such as constructing attack graphs or event causal graphs with ontological structures containing information on assets, vulnerabilities, attacks, and affected hosts, combined with certain inference techniques, enable the iterative process of attacks, identification of affected assets and their scope of harm, and tracing of malicious behaviors. Current knowledge inference models can generally be classified into three categories: translation-based models, tensor decomposition-based models, and neural network-based models.

Translation-based models treat relationships as translations from head entities to tail entities in vector space. TransE12 is the most representative translation-based inference model, known for its computational efficiency and other advantages, which has led to its widespread use in various knowledge graph-based tasks. Based on this model, a series of models such as TransR13 and TransD14 have been derived.

Tensor decomposition-based models treat the (head entity, relationship, tail entity) triplet as elements in a tensor/matrix, constructing the tensor and performing representation learning through tensor decomposition methods. A typical example of this approach is RESCAL, proposed by Nickel et al.15, which performs representation learning based on third-order tensors. Later, new tensor decomposition models such as TRESCAL16 and ARE (Additive Relational Effects)17 were introduced.

Neural network-based models directly model knowledge graph triplets using neural networks to obtain vector representations of triplet elements, which are then used for further inference. The ConvE18 model was the first to apply convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to knowledge graph inference, offering significant advantages in learning and extracting semantic features of entities and relationships. Other models, such as ConvKB19 and InteractE20, are also available. A method21 for detecting APT based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), and attention mechanisms is proposed. The GKAN model proposed by Kiamari et al.22 breaks the limitations of traditional fixed convolutional structures and uses learnable spline functions to achieve flexibility in cross-layer information transmission. Ren et al.23 proposed a model that combines bidirectional dynamic graph attention with KAN network (BGCRKAN). BGCRKAN combines the feature extraction ability of graph neural network and the bidirectional dynamic graph attention mechanism to capture the multi-step, cross-node, and highly hidden attack features of APT attacks by dynamically updating the state of network nodes. Although these models have advantages in APT attack detection, they fail to take into account graph structure modeling and solve the problem of knowledge graph completion of complex graphs like KGConvE, resulting in insufficient reasoning integrity and robustness in complex APT scenarios.

GATv2, a dynamic graph attention network developed by Brody et al.24, has stronger expressive capabilities than standard GATs. The proposed MLP DNN25 model can be used to detect anomalous events in the network. CompGCN26 was proposed to co-embed nodes and relationships into a relationship graph. CompGCN leverages various entity relationship groups in knowledge graph embedding technology to collaborate and scale with the number of relationships. It is proposed that off-the-shelf encoder-decoder Transformer models can serve as scalable and general KGE27 models, achieving state-of-the-art results in KG link prediction and incomplete KG question answering. Although these models have advantages in dynamic attention, anomaly detection, relation combination, and generality, they are insufficient in reasoning the connections between potential threats and vulnerabilities, thereby identifying and understanding potential attack pathways, exploitation methods, and attack threats.

In the field of cybersecurity, knowledge inference has broad applications, including the prediction of security entities or the completion of cybersecurity knowledge graphs. Han et al.28 constructed a knowledge graph based on the MITRE CWE knowledge base and used the TransE model to perform representation learning on the structural and textual information within the knowledge graph, further enabling applications such as CWE link prediction and vulnerability exploitation prediction. Qin et al.29 constructed a vulnerability knowledge graph based on the Unified Cybersecurity Ontology (UCO) and achieved inference of hidden vulnerability relationships. To address the missing relationships between security entities in different security databases, Xiao et al.30 proposed merging heterogeneous databases into a coherent software security knowledge graph and used embedding methods to predict relationships between CVE, CWE, and CAPEC security entities. Knowledge inference based on the open-world assumption was used to uncover hidden relationships between software security entities. Wang et al.31 proposed an entity relationship prediction method based on knowledge graph embedding, using Sentence-Bert32 and Graph Attention Networks (GAT) for link prediction and classification tasks to supplement and refine software security data. Zhou et al.33 proposed a method for completing weakness information based on reverse features in security knowledge graphs, using attention mechanisms to learn domain-specific features and capture reverse features. This method addresses the problem of insufficient learning of features from different neighborhoods in existing approaches, enabling inference to complete missing vulnerability information. Li et al.34 proposed a new knowledge graph completion model called MRME-KGC, which is based on multi-view Riemannian manifold fusion to achieve this goal, reducing the noise and representation issues caused by multi-view Riemannian manifold fusion. Liang et al.35 summarized the research progress in the field of Knowledge Graph Reasoning (KGR), focusing on the evolution of methods from static models to temporal models and multimodal models, and proposed a two-layer classification method based on graph types and technical scenarios. Li et al.36 introduced a novel sparse regularization method that embeds the concept of rank-based selective sparsity into the KGC regularizer, selectively penalizing those components of the embedding vectors that exhibit significant features, thereby effectively ignoring many components that contribute little and may represent noise.

In summary, knowledge graph inference has been initially applied to cyberspace knowledge graphs, achieving significant results in inference scenarios such as hidden vulnerability relationship prediction, thus improving inference efficiency. However, these methods still face some challenges and limitations in practical applications. The handling methods for security data often fail to comprehensively capture all relevant features, leading to inaccurate model training and inference, which affects the overall system performance. Additionally, these methods often suffer from insufficient coverage of security data and lack data diversity, which can significantly impact the inference results.

It is noteworthy that the KGConvE attack knowledge inference model, proposed in this paper, has unique advantages in handling attack and vulnerability data within cyberspace knowledge graphs. This model is rare in that it combines graph convolutional networks37 to perform CVE classification and inference within the same CVE category, significantly improving the accuracy of attack inference. On the other hand, the application of the ConvE inference model to attack scenarios provides cybersecurity experts with a novel technical tool, helping to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the relationships and interactions between CVEs, thus offering strong support for attack inference analysis and cybersecurity decision-making.

Network attack knowledge inference with GCN and ConvE

Overall framework

The proposed KGConvE model is designed in three stages: knowledge graph construction, network attack knowledge classification, and attack knowledge inference, as shown in Fig. 1. The design of these three stages aims to address the challenges in APT attack inference, including multi-source data integration and knowledge graph construction, feature aggregation and classification of nodes, and deep inference for predicting complex attack relationships.

Framework of the KGConvE model.

Phase 1: network security knowledge graph construction. The primary objective of this phase is to integrate and organize various types of network security data. APT attacks involve several highly interconnected security entities, such as CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures), CWE (Common Weakness Enumeration), and CAPEC (Common Attack Pattern Enumeration and Classification). The complex relationships among these entities are often scattered across large volumes of heterogeneous data. Obtain real-time threat intelligence and attack data from cybersecurity platforms. Integrate data from different sources to form a unified cybersecurity dataset. Then, extract entities (such as CVE, CWE, CAPEC) and relationships (such as "exploits," "leads to," “associated with”) from the text data. Nodes represent entities in the cybersecurity domain, while edges represent relationships between entities, such as "CVE exploits CWE," "CVE is associated with CAPEC," "attacker uses CAPEC," etc. Convert the preprocessed data into a graph structure to form a cybersecurity knowledge graph. Each node and edge can contain attribute information, such as the severity of a vulnerability or the complexity of an attack pattern. By constructing a knowledge graph, these multi-source and heterogeneous data can be integrated into a unified structure, creating a comprehensive knowledge network that visually represents the relationships between different entities.

Phase 2: network attack knowledge classification. The goal of this phase is to utilize GCN to analyze and classify data within the knowledge graph. GCN can capture the features of each node (such as CVE) and its relationships with neighboring nodes. By aggregating the features of neighboring nodes, the model can identify similar CVEs and classify them into the same APT organization. Specifically, NLTK is used to extract the attributes of CVE, CWE, and CAPEC nodes from the knowledge graph. For CVE nodes, the corresponding node attributes are extracted and the most frequent attributes are used to determine feature words. Similarly, feature words for CWE and CAPEC nodes related to CVE are extracted by analyzing the frequency of their attributes. The extracted CWE and CAPEC feature words are integrated into the corresponding CVE, creating a feature matrix where each column represents a feature word (i.e., node attribute) and each row represents a CVE, with the last column indicating the APT organization category of each CVE. Additionally, for each APT organization’s CVE, pairwise combinations are created and duplicates are removed to form a relationship matrix, describing the associations between CVEs. Finally, the feature and relationship matrices are input into the GCN for training, validation, and testing to automatically classify CVEs within the same APT organization, such as classifying CVE-2017-11774 and CVE-2018-20250 under APT33.

Phase 3: Network Attack Knowledge Inference. Deep inference is performed using the ConvE model, with the core of this phase being the use of ConvE’s 2D convolutional embedding technique to further explore and predict potential relationships between different entities within the same APT organization. Specifically, the knowledge graph is queried to locate and export security entities within the same APT, including CVE, CWE, and CAPEC. These entities and their relationships are then converted into triplet forms, particularly where CVEs are paired with unrelated CAPECs within the same APT organization. All constructed triplets are then transformed into continuous low-dimensional vectors and input into the ConvE model. The ConvE model uses 2D convolutions to predict missing links in the knowledge graph, inferring the relationships between CVE and CAPEC, and assessing the likelihood of the relationships using a scoring function. In this way, ConvE is able to learn more about the security entities within the same APT, thereby effectively performing inference tasks between CVE and CAPEC.

Network security knowledge graph construction

In constructing the knowledge graph, the specific steps include data collection and organization, entity extraction, and relationship extraction. A knowledge graph is a structured semantic network capable of formalizing the representation of complex attack behaviors, vulnerabilities, weaknesses, attack patterns, and their interrelationships, thereby providing support for attack reasoning and threat analysis. Its construction relies on technologies such as natural language processing (NLP), graph theory, and machine learning, enabling effective analysis and reasoning in complex cyber attack scenarios. The following provides a detailed description of each step.

Data Collection and Organization. During the data collection phase, the APT reports from the threat intelligence platform are first crawled using Python’s requests or Scrapy framework, with HTML being parsed or APIs being called directly to obtain structured data. Then, based on the CVE number, the REST API of NVD is accessed to extract vulnerability descriptions, CWE associations, and CVSS scores. For the correlation data of CWE and CAPEC, hyperlinks in NVD pages are recursively crawled, or MITRE’s official database is queried directly to ensure data integrity and real-time availability. When crawling, it is necessary to set reasonable request intervals and disguise the User-Agent to avoid triggering anti-crawling mechanisms, while using BeautifulSoup or lxml to clean HTML tags, and finally store the data as structured CSV or JSON files for subsequent processing. By accessing threat intelligence platforms, such as reports and databases published by security companies like Qihoo 360, FireEye, and CrowdStrike, detailed information on mainstream APT organizations can be obtained. First, log into these platforms, search for and download publicly available reports on APT organizations, and extract the names of the APT organizations along with the CVE identifiers they use. Next, query each CVE identifier on the official CVE website38 to obtain detailed descriptions and related information. Then, by following additional CVE-related links, navigate to the NVD39 (National Vulnerability Database) page to identify associated CWEs, and click on the CWE40 to find detailed descriptions and related CAPEC41 attack patterns. After completing these steps, link each APT organization with its used CVEs, relevant CWEs, and associated CAPECs, creating a data table that includes the APT organization name, CVE identifier and description, CWE identifier and description, and CAPEC identifier and description. Finally, check and verify the accuracy and completeness of the data to ensure no important information is missing. Once this is done, export the organized data table as a CSV file.

Entity Extraction. Entity extraction is a crucial step in constructing knowledge graphs, and this process is accomplished using a multi-level reinforcement learning-based entity relationship extraction model. This model performs exceptionally well in complex APT attack scenarios and can effectively capture the hierarchical relationships among APT organizations, CVE vulnerabilities, CWE weaknesses, and CAPEC attack patterns. Developed in earlier research by our team, the model has demonstrated strong entity and relationship extraction capabilities after being validated in numerous APT attack scenarios. The specific steps include defining entity types, collecting entity data, standardizing, and word segmentation. First, low-level reinforcement learning is used to select APT organizations, CVE vulnerabilities, CWE weakness types, and CAPEC attack patterns as entities. Then, collect the relevant data for each entity from the previously organized and exported CSV file, and standardize it to unify the data format. Subsequently, data cleaning is performed to remove duplicates and irrelevant information. To better understand the entities and their roles in the attack scenarios, NLTK is used to segment the description information of the entities. The descriptions are split into individual terms, and the word after the word segmentation is used as the attribute node of the entity to enrich the attribute information of the entity. For example, the description of CVE-2017-11882, as shown in Table 1, can be segmented into terms such as “Microsoft” and "Office," which are then used as attribute nodes for CVE-2017-11882.

Relationship Extraction. Relationship extraction is performed using a multi-level reinforcement learning-based entity relationship extraction model. This model effectively extracts the relationships between entities in complex APT attack scenarios, particularly the hierarchical associations among APT organizations, CVE vulnerabilities, CWE weaknesses, and CAPEC attack patterns. In the knowledge graph, the following three primary relationships are defined: [APT, used, CVE], indicating that an APT organization uses a specific CVE vulnerability for attack; [CVE, related_to, CWE], denoting that a CVE vulnerability is related to a specific CWE weakness; and [CWE, related_to, CAPEC], meaning that a CWE weakness is related to a specific CAPEC attack pattern. Relationship extraction is a critical step in constructing the network attack knowledge graph. These relationships help uncover the associations between entities and provide the foundation for building the knowledge graph.

Based on the extracted entities and relationships, a complete knowledge graph can be constructed. First, the organized data table is exported as a CSV file for import and processing in Neo4j. Next, the CSV file is uploaded to the import directory of the Neo4j server, and Cypher queries are executed in the Neo4j browser to import the APT, CVE, CWE, and CAPEC entity nodes along with their relationship edges. Finally, the graph structure is queried to ensure that all entities and relationships are correctly connected.

Network attack knowledge classification

The knowledge graph constructed in this study is based on the network security knowledge graph of APT organizations. By identifying CVE vulnerabilities and CAPEC attack patterns associated with APTs, it enables a better understanding of the attack information of APT organizations. The primary approach is to use query statements on the constructed knowledge graph to identify security entity nodes (CVE, CWE, CAPEC) and the organized node attributes, which are then used to build a feature matrix. The sorted node attributes are obtained by word segmentation of the node description information, and then the feature words are extracted according to the node attributes and represented by 0 or 1. Next, a relationship matrix is constructed. Typically, a relationship matrix in a knowledge graph is built based on the relationships between the edges in the graph. Since CWE and CAPEC are merely used to supplement the features of CVEs, the relationship matrix is constructed solely based on the relationships among CVEs, i.e., by linking CVEs within the same APT organization pairwise. Building the knowledge graph provides a valuable foundation for security analysis, but the limitations of the data itself mean that not all CVEs associated with the same APT attack can be linked in the knowledge graph. To overcome this limitation, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) are introduced.

GCN is capable of integrating both node features and the features of neighboring nodes by iteratively aggregating information from neighboring nodes to update the node’s own representation. This allows GCN to effectively capture and utilize high-order neighborhood information within the graph structure. In the context of the knowledge graph, this means that even if certain CVE vulnerabilities do not have direct relationships, GCN can indirectly infer their relationships through their associations with CWE weaknesses or CAPEC attack patterns.

Attack knowledge inference

In the knowledge graph, GCN is used to classify CVEs into their corresponding APT organizations. Subsequently, query statements are used to find related security entities (such as CVE, CWE, CAPEC) and their relationships within similar APT organizations. These entities and relationships can be directly exported in the form of triples, typically representing the indirect relationships between CVEs, CWEs, and CAPECs. To further explore the potential relationships between entities within each APT organization, for each APT organization, every CVE is paired with a CAPEC, which has not yet established an indirect relationship within the same APT organization, to form new triples. These new triples are then learned alongside the existing explicit relationships. Using this approach, the ConvE model is capable of predicting the potential associations between CVEs and CAPECs. In the experiment, the triples are divided into a training set and a test set. The training set contains the indirect associations between CVEs and CAPECs via CWEs, while the test set consists of the newly paired triples. After training, the model performs inference on the test set and outputs predicted values, which are probabilities. Based on these probabilities, the potential relationships between CVEs and CAPECs can be identified, allowing for more accurate detection of vulnerabilities and attack patterns potentially exploited in APT attacks, thus effectively narrowing the scope of inference.

Experimental dataset

This study uses the constructed knowledge graph to query CVE, CWE, and CAPEC entities with related associations using Cypher queries. For each of these entities, we extract their node attribute words. Each node’s attribute contains multiple words, from which high-frequency words in each category of entity are selected as feature words. Specifically, the top 100 high-frequency words for CVEs, and the top 50 for CWE and CAPEC are selected. Each description of CVE, CWE, and CAPEC is then checked for the presence of these high-frequency words; a ’1’ is assigned if a word is present, and ’0’ if it is absent. This results in the formation of feature vectors for each security entity (CVE, CWE, CAPEC).

The reason for selecting the top 100 high-frequency words for CVE is that exceeding 100 dimensions would cause the matrix to become very large, increasing storage and computational complexity, as well as the costs associated with data collection and processing. This could also result in a substantial amount of redundant information, obscuring truly useful data. Conversely, fewer than 100 dimensions would lead to the loss of important information and features, which could degrade model performance. Similarly, the top 50 high-frequency words for CWE and CAPEC are selected.

Using the relationships between CVE, CWE, and CAPEC, these feature vectors are arranged in the order of CVE, CWE, and CAPEC, resulting in a total of 200 columns. The CVE ID is added in the first column of the feature vector, and the corresponding APT organization name for each CVE is added in the last column. This forms the feature matrix used in this study, as shown in Table 3. The processed dataset contains 1000 samples, each with 200 features, including 11424 triples, 1486 entities, and 421 relations. The training set consists of 500 samples, the test set consists of 300 samples, and the validation set contains 200 samples. Negative samples are generated by randomly replacing the head or tail entity in the positive triples using TransE. Our dataset is already linked to github: https://github.com/dyz1209/GA-ConvE_data. The relationship matrix is formed by pair-wise combinations of CVEs used by the same APT. Each row represents the relationship between two CVEs that share the same APT organization.

Parameter experiment

The parameter experiments in this section are divided into two categories: GCN attack knowledge classification parameter experiments and ConvE attack knowledge reasoning parameter experiments. These experiments help identify the optimal model parameters, thereby achieving the best performance in the experiments.

(1) GCN attack knowledge classification parameter experiment

This study conducted three sets of parameter experiments that affect model performance. Fig. 4 shows the model performance under three different parameter settings. As shown in Fig. 4a, the x-axis represents the number of hidden units. By fixing the other parameters, as the number of hidden units increases from 8 to 63, the model’s accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score all significantly improve. When the number of hidden units is 63, all metrics reach their maximum values: 0.995, 0.999, 0.993, and 0.996, respectively. Continue to increase hidden units, and the model performance remains stable. The selection of these specific values for hidden units aims to explore how the model’s performance changes with varying numbers of hidden units. As shown in Fig. 4b, the x-axis represents the number of epochs, which refers to the number of forward and backward passes the neural network makes over the entire training dataset. By fixing the other parameters, we adjust the epoch value to find the optimal training effect. When the number of epochs increases from 200 to 600, the overall model performance improves. With 600 epochs, the metrics reach their highest values: 0.98, 0.995, 0.924, and 0.949, indicating that the model performs best at this parameter setting. The epoch range of 200 to 600 was selected to find the optimal balance for the number of training iterations, avoiding both underfitting and overfitting. As shown in Fig. 4c, the x-axis represents dropout rate, which is a regularization parameter used to prevent the model from overfitting. By fixing the other parameters, we adjust the dropout rate to observe the effect on model performance. As the dropout rate increases from 0.1 to 0.81, the model’s accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score remain at a high level. The model performs best at a dropout rate of 0.18, with accuracy of 0.995, precision of 0.999, recall of 0.993, and an F1 score of 0.996. As the dropout rate continues to increase to 0.9, model performance declines significantly, indicating that a too-high dropout rate leads to underfitting. The range of dropout rate is chosen to find the optimal regularization strength, balancing the model’s generalization capability and training effectiveness.

Model performance of GCN under different parameters (a) model performance under different hidden units, (b) model performance under different epochs, (c) model performance under different dropout rate.

In summary, through the analysis of the GCN model’s performance under different parameter settings, the optimal parameter combination was determined: Hidden Units = 63, Epochs = 600, and Dropout Rate = 0.18. Under this configuration, the model’s accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score all reached their optimal levels, indicating that this configuration effectively utilizes the data features and enhances the model’s performance in the attack classification task. The selection of this parameter range is aimed at systematically exploring the impact of each parameter on model performance, thereby identifying the configuration combination that achieves the best classification results.

(2) ConvE attack knowledge reasoning parameter experiment

In optimizing the ConvE model parameters, the specific impact of Batches Count, Epochs, and hidden layer dimension K on the model performance metrics (MRR, Hits@1, Hits@3, Hits@10) was carefully analyzed to determine the best parameter combination. As shown in Fig. 5a, the model performance fluctuates with the increase in Batches Count. When the Batches Count is 4, MRR increases to 0.363, Hits@3 rises to 0.407, but Hits@1 drops to 0.167, and Hits@10 decreases slightly to 0.416. When the Batches Count is 5, all metrics reach their best performance, with MRR at 0.588, Hits@1 at 0.280, Hits@3 at 0.417, and Hits@10 at 0.623. After this point, although Hits@1 rises to its highest value of 0.333, the other metrics do not surpass the performance at Batches Count = 5. Therefore, based on the analysis above, Batches Count = 5 is the optimal parameter choice for the ConvE model, assuming other parameters remain constant. As shown in Fig. 5b, the change in Epochs has a significant impact on model performance. In the early stages (50 to 150 Epochs), MRR is 0.391, 0.434, and 0.429, Hits@1 rises from 0.25 to 0.33, Hits@3 remains at 0.5, and Hits@10 stays at 0.683. When Epochs is 200, all metrics drop to their lowest values, namely 0.261, 0.243, 0.333, and 0.5. Subsequently, as Epochs increase, the metrics also improve, and by the time Epochs reaches 600, all metrics reach their highest values: 0.615, 0.42, 0.583, and 0.683. Therefore, with other parameters held constant, Epochs = 600 is the optimal parameter choice for the ConvE model. The hidden layer dimension K is a key factor influencing the model’s ability to learn complex features. From the analysis in Fig. 5c, it can be concluded that as K increases, the model’s performance first rises and then decreases. When K = 200, MRR, Hits@3, and Hits@10 all reach their highest values of 0.68, 0.533, and 0.667, indicating that this dimension setting effectively captures key information in the data. Although Hits@1 decreases after K = 200, considering the performance across all metrics, K = 200 is the optimal choice. Therefore, the best parameter combination for the ConvE model is Batches Count = 5, Epochs = 600, and hidden layer dimension K = 200. This combination, based on the analysis of specific value changes, ensures that the model effectively learns data features during training while avoiding both overfitting and underfitting, thus achieving optimal performance across multiple evaluation metrics.

Comparative experiments

This section conducts two types of comparative experiments. The first experiment compared the evaluation indexes of seven classification models, namely GCN, MLP, KGCN, BGCRKAN23, GKAN22, CNN-LSTM21 and EMKNN42, aiming to validate GCN’s advantage in capturing APT attack features and their associations, as well as its higher performance in the APT attack knowledge classification task. GCN not only captures attack features and their associations, compensating for the inability of traditional methods to capture comprehensive attack features and relationships, but also validates its performance in classification tasks through experiments, further enhancing the accuracy and comprehensiveness of APT attack knowledge classification. The second experiment compares the evaluation metrics of different reasoning models, validating the effectiveness of KGConvE in solving the problem of incomplete reasoning due to the single analytical dimension in APT attacks. KGConvE not only analyzes the complex interactions between entities and relationships from multiple dimensions, thus solving the problem of incomplete reasoning caused by the single analysis dimension, but also demonstrates superior performance in reasoning tasks through experiments. This includes the comparison of KGConvE with ConvE, ComplEX, TransE, GATv224, MLP DNN25, CompGCN26, and KGT527, which further validates the inference performance of KGConvE in solving the problem of incomplete inference caused by a single dimension.

Application result analysis

To validate the effectiveness of the KGConvE attack reasoning model, the following strategy was adopted:First, GCN was used to classify CVEs into corresponding APT organizations. This was based on the premise that similar APT organizations often use similar attack methods and strategies, as well as the information in the dataset that each CVE corresponds to a specific CAPEC, providing a foundation for subsequent reasoning. Second, the ConvE model was used to learn the complex interactions between entities and relationships, reasoning about the potential implicit relationships between CVEs and other CAPECs within the same APT organization, and predicting new associations based on this. Finally, actual cases from threat intelligence were used to verify the accuracy and practicality of the reasoning results, ensuring the effectiveness and reliability of the model’s reasoning.

Using the GCN model, CVE-2021-44228 and CVE-2017-0144 were classified into the APT-C-06 organization. Based on the dataset, CVE-2021-44228 is associated with specific CWEs, which are further linked to attack patterns such as CAPEC-586, CAPEC-147, CAPEC-492, CAPEC-227, CAPEC-10, CAPEC-63, and CAPEC-81. Similarly, CVE-2017-0144 is also associated with specific CAPECs, such as CAPEC-10, CAPEC-63, and CAPEC-81. Then, the ConvE model was used to infer potential implicit relationships between CVEs and other CAPECs. Specifically, the inference was made about whether there are relationships between CVE-2017-0144 and CAPEC-586, CAPEC-147, CAPEC-492, and CAPEC-227. Finally, the predicted values for all triples containing CVEs and CAPECs were output, and the existence of relationships between CVEs and CAPECs was determined based on the probability values. According to the experimental training results, it was found that when the probability is greater than 0.8, the system can accurately predict the existence of the fact. Therefore, cases with a probability greater than 0.8 are considered to have a factual relationship, while those with a probability lower than 0.8 are considered to not have a factual relationship. Table 4 presents the calculation results for the relationships between CVE-2017-0144 and various CAPECs. According to the probability results, CVE-2017-0144 has relationships with CAPEC-586, CAPEC-147, CAPEC-492, and CAPEC-227. Figure 9 is the visual representation of this result, where the red lines represent the inferred relationships between CVE-2017-0144 and CAPEC-586, CAPEC-147, CAPEC-492, and CAPEC-227.

To validate the accuracy and practicality of the inference results, a specific threat intelligence case was selected for verification. This threat intelligence records an actual attack scenario by the APT-C-06 organization, which details how the attacker exploited the CVE-2017-0144 vulnerability and employed various attack patterns.

Specifically, the threat intelligence revealed that the APT-C-06 organization first scanned and probed the target system, discovering the CVE-2017-0144 vulnerability, which served as the entry point for the attack. CVEs, as public vulnerabilities and exposure indicators, represent security weaknesses in the system that can be exploited by attackers. The attacker then exploited the CVE vulnerability to execute malicious code, which precisely matches the description of CAPEC-586 (exploiting a software vulnerability to execute code), demonstrating how the attacker leveraged a known vulnerability to perform malicious actions. During further penetration, the attacker also employed other CAPEC attack patterns, such as CAPEC-147 (exploiting a vulnerability to bypass security measures) to circumvent the security mechanisms of the target system, and CAPEC-492 (exploiting a vulnerability to escalate privileges) to gain higher system privileges. These CAPECs represent specific attack techniques and strategies that the attacker used to progressively infiltrate the system and expand their control. Finally, the attacker used CAPEC-227 (exploiting a vulnerability for persistence attacks) to implant a persistent backdoor in the target system, enabling long-term control over the compromised system.

This attack scenario is highly consistent with the inferred relationships between CVE-2017-0144 and CAPEC-586, CAPEC-147, CAPEC-492, and CAPEC-227 using the KGConvE model. By comparing the actual attack methods in the threat intelligence with the attack patterns inferred by the model, it is clear that the model accurately captured the potential associations between the CVE-2017-0144 vulnerability and these CAPEC attack patterns. This specific threat intelligence case not only validates the accuracy of the KGConvE model’s inference results but also further demonstrates the model’s effectiveness and reliability in real-world attack scenarios. This suggests that the model can effectively leverage the knowledge graph to infer the hidden relationships between CVEs and other potentially related CAPECs, providing strong support for predicting and preventing APT organization attack behaviors.

Conclusion

This paper presents a network attack knowledge inference method based on Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN): KGConvE, which aims to aggregate features using GCN to capture relationships in APT attacks and combine 2D convolution to infer the relationships between attack knowledge from multiple dimensions. By integrating multi-source data from CVE, CWE, CAPEC, and APT organizations, a cybersecurity knowledge graph was constructed. The GCN model was used to learn complex relationships between nodes, classifying similar CVEs into the corresponding APT organizations, thereby improving classification accuracy and comprehensiveness. Next, 2D convolution was used to embed CVEs of the same type and their related CWEs and CAPECs into a low-dimensional vector space for inference, deeply exploring the potential associations between CVEs and CAPECs. Experimental results show that KGConvE achieved significant performance improvements in network attack knowledge inference tasks, with a mean reciprocal rank (MRR) of 0.68 and Hits@10 of 0.58. Compared to baseline methods, KGConvE effectively improved inference performance by comprehensively capturing attack features and their relationships through GCN. In addition, the verification through specific attack knowledge cases further demonstrates the model’s effectiveness in inferring CVE and CAPEC relationships, which significantly aids security personnel in better analyzing cybersecurity information.

However, the KGConvE method is limited to single-step inference and cannot capture global context information, which may result in limited inference outcomes or misleading information during the inference process. To address these issues, methods such as multi-step reasoning could be considered to capture more comprehensive contextual information and further enhance the model’s inference capabilities and accuracy. Such improvements would make the model more suitable for complex attack scenarios and enhance its practical application value in solving real-world security problems.
