Construction and practice of a certification system for clinical nursing preceptors: a “ license to teach” approach in China
Authors: Yinying Tang, LinyueTan, Xiuwen Chen, Huiqiong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical nursing preceptors play a vital role in bridging the gap between classroom learning and clinical practice. However, inconsistent qualification standards and a lack of systematic evaluation often lead to variability in teaching quality.ObjectiveTo describe the development and implementation of a “license to teach” certification system for lead clinical nursing preceptors and to evaluate its impact on teaching quality and faculty development.MethodsBased on the Clinical Nursing Teaching Management Standards (2021), a set of eligibility criteria was established regarding academic background, clinical experience, and professional titles. The certification process consisted of three components: (1) theoretical teaching, (2) skills demonstration, and (3) clinical teaching ward rounds. Candidates were evaluated by expert panels using standardized rubrics. A structured questionnaire was administered to collect participants' feedback on the certification system.ResultsOf 193 candidates, 152 were certified (78.6%), reporting improved teaching preparedness, theory–practice integration, and professional recognition. Questionnaire responses (n = 145) showed high satisfaction (mean > 4.7/5; Cronbach’s α = 0.936; KMO = 0.861), reflecting enhanced teaching skills and standardized practices. Nursing interns (n = 201) reported 94–96% satisfaction, and interviews with four department managers confirmed benefits for teaching standardization and professional motivation.ConclusionThe “license to teach” system represents an innovative approach to standardizing the qualification and evaluation of clinical nursing preceptors. This model enhances teaching quality and provides a framework that can be adapted in other nursing education contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08953-x.Keywords: Clinical nurse preceptor, Clinical teaching, Qualification evaluation

Introduction

Clinical nurse preceptors, who undertake clinical teaching responsibilities, are pivotal in connecting nursing interns’ theoretical knowledge with clinical practice, playing an indispensable role in cultivating competent future nurses [1]. Their role is complex and multifaceted, extending beyond knowledge transmission to encompass acting as a clinical role model, mentoring students' clinical judgment and critical thinking, providing formative feedback, facilitating professional socialization, and balancing educational duties with patient care responsibilities [2, 3]. This complexity necessitates a high level of teaching competency. However, multiple studies indicate that the role of the clinical nurse preceptor is complex, yet existing qualification certification and evaluation systems lack uniform standards, posing challenges to achieving high-quality and standardized clinical teaching [4]. Existing research often focuses on teaching quality but rarely addresses systematic qualification evaluation systems for clinical preceptors [5, 6]. For instance [7] used student experience and satisfaction to measure the effectiveness of clinical preceptorship. Abuosi et al. [8] collected data through clinical learning environment assessments and preceptor questionnaires to reflect students’ perceptions of the clinical learning environment quality. Wong and Bressington [9] used the Supervision and Nurse Teacher Evaluation Scale to understand students' views on clinical practice and to measure the quality of clinical education.Although a few previous studies have touched upon preceptors’ psychological preparation or performance indicator assessments, these have not formed standardized systems [10, 11]. Many preceptors struggle to fulfill their multifaceted roles and responsibilities effectively due to a lack of standardized education, training, and preparation, leading to inconsistent clinical teaching quality that severely impacts student internship experiences and patient safety [12, 13].

Globally, frameworks such as the National League for Nursing (NLN) Nurse Educator Competencies and World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines outline standards for nursing educators. However, these are largely designed for academic faculty or broad educational contexts, rather than for the specific training and certification of frontline clinical preceptors. In China, systematic mechanisms for assessing clinical preceptors remain underdeveloped. This gap highlights the need for a structured, practice-based certification model like the "License to Teach" system developed in this study.

This need is particularly pronounced within the specific context of China’s healthcare system and nursing education. Nursing education in China often adopts a “college-hospital joint training” model, where schools and teaching hospitals share responsibility for students’ theoretical learning and clinical practice [14, 27]. The National Health Commission (NHC) of China [15] emphasizes strengthening college-hospital collaboration, improving clinical teaching base standards, and enhancing clinical practice teaching components. Teaching hospitals undertake the clinical teaching of nursing interns and serve as crucial bases for interns to acquire practical knowledge and develop professionally [16]. Clinical nursing chief teachers play a significant role in internship teaching, undertaking teaching tasks while also receiving teaching supervision from higher education management departments. However, their selection and management have long relied heavily on informal standards, such as seniority or clinical experience, lacking a structured teaching competency assessment system. This management gap leads to inconsistencies in teaching quality and presents challenges for the clinical preceptor themselves, including role ambiguity, lack of support, and high work pressure [17, 18]. These not only impacts the well-being of preceptors but also compromises the quality of education provided to nursing students [19].

Thus, to promote the continuous, standardized development and maintenance of the clinical preceptor team, there is a pressing need to establish a standardized and feasible assessment and evaluation scheme, starting with clinical nursing chief teachers. To address these challenges, Department of Nursing Teaching and Research designed and implemented a “License to Teach” certification system for Lead Clinical Instructors, based on the hospital’s Clinical Nursing Teaching Management Standards (2021) (Appendix 1). This system was implemented as an institutional quality improvement initiative designed to standardize preceptor qualifications and enhance the overall quality of clinical teaching. It represents a structured faculty development intervention, moving beyond mere preceptor selection to actively enhance teaching competencies through an assessment-driven learning process. It aims to foster a culture of teaching excellence and provide a clear career development pathway for clinical nurses. This study aims to describe the construction and practice of this system and evaluate its implementation outcomes, with a focus on its contribution to faculty development and teaching standardization.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a prospective design to evaluate an educational intervention, the implementation of the "License to Teach" certification system for Lead Clinical Instructors. The study aimed to describe the system's construction process and assess its outcomes regarding pass rates, participant satisfaction, and perceived impact on teaching. The intervention was launched in 2021, and data for this evaluation were collected in 2025.

Construction and implementation of the certification system for clinical nursing chief teachers

Pre-examination training and standardization

The training content and assessment tools were developed by the hospital’s Nursing Teaching Committee based on national nursing education guidelines and the hospital’s Clinical Nursing Teaching Management Standards (2021) (Appendix 1).

(a) An annual core training workshop for clinical nursing preceptors was held, covering modules on teaching theory and methods, curriculum ideology, teaching ward round workshops, and experience sharing. (b) Specialized skill demonstration trainings focused on core assessment components, such as demonstration of theoretical teaching, demonstration of teaching ward rounds and demonstration of nursing skill training were conducted. (c) Regular online training was delivered through the hospital’s “Xiangya Teaching Training” APP, which provided approximately 20 h of theoretical content. This included a mandatory module (12 h) covering curriculum ideology and basic teaching skills, and elective modules (8 h) on teaching policies and management. Completion of the mandatory module was required before the certification assessment. (d) Regular clinical nursing teaching work conferences were held quarterly to provide feedback, review performance, and summarize the year’s teaching activities.. (e) Relevant assessment operation specifications were developed and published, such as the Clinical Nursing Teaching Ward Round Standards, detailing the procedures, positioning, documentation, and precautions for teaching ward rounds.

Assessment content and requirements

The certification system for Lead Clinical Instructors adopts a multi-dimensional evaluation model comprising three modules: classroom teaching, skill training, and teaching ward rounds. (a) Classroom teaching: Candidates deliver a 10-min simulated lecture on a selected specialty topic, assessed on content design, presentation skills, and use of teaching resources (Appendix 2). (b) Skill training: Candidates demonstrate one selected nursing skill through simulated teaching, evaluated on procedural standardization, teaching competence, and ability to foster clinical reasoning (Appendix 3). (c) Teaching ward rounds: Candidates conduct a ward round on a common disease within their specialty, assessed on preparation, clinical guidance, communication, and overall teaching performance (Appendix 4). Structured scoring sheets and standardized grading criteria were used, with expert panelists conducting on-site evaluations. Performance was graded as excellent, good, average, or poor. Candidates were required to achieve at least a “good” rating in all three modules to obtain certification. Those receiving “excellent” ratings across all modules were eligible to apply for specialist teaching nurse certification.

Candidate eligibility

(a) Full-time bachelor’s degree or higher; (b) Over 5 years of work experience in the specific specialty (≥ 3 years for postgraduates); (c) Intermediate professional title or higher; (d) Some experience in internship teaching. Based on these criteria, each clinical department recommends 1–2 candidates. Eligible head nurses can also apply (not counting against the departmental quota).

Assessment implementation

The hospital initiated the certification assessment for clinical nursing chief teachers in 2021. Those who passed were assigned to corresponding departments to undertake teaching responsibilities.

Evaluation of the certification system's effectiveness

The effectiveness of the certification system was evaluated from two perspectives. The assessment was conducted in 2025, following the completion of preceptor training and certification. First, general assessment information was collected, including the total number of candidates, their basic demographic characteristics, and pass rates for each assessment component. Second, assessment outcomes were evaluated using a self-developed questionnaire entitled Implementation Effect of the Certification Assessment for Clinical Nursing Chief Teachers, which was designed to capture participants’ perceptions, satisfaction levels, and suggestions regarding the certification system. The questionnaire employed a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied) and consisted of 20 closed-ended items and 4 open-ended questions. The questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability and validity, with an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.936. Exploratory factor analysis indicated good construct validity (KMO = 0.861; Bartlett's test of sphericity χ2 = 2520.209, p < 0.001). In addition, nursing interns and department managers were surveyed to assess perceived changes in departmental teaching conditions following implementation. Interns were asked to evaluate overall satisfaction with teaching quality and preceptors using a four-level scale: satisfied, relatively satisfied, basically satisfied, or dissatisfied. Department managers and preceptors were interviewed qualitatively to explore their views on the Teaching Nurse (Chief Preceptor) certification assessment and its impact on clinical nursing education.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized using Excel. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistics were expressed as counts and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables. Questionnaire reliability was tested using Cronbach’s α coefficient: α > 0.7 indicates good reliability; 0.6–0.7 indicates acceptable reliability. Structural validity was tested using Principal Component Analysis. A KMO (Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin) value > 0.8 and a significant Bartlett’s sphericity test (p < 0.05) indicated suitability for factor analysis.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya School of Nursing, Central South University (registry number E2025155). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As this study involved a survey of nursing staff, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Results

General assessment results and participant satisfaction

A total of 193 candidates participated in the certification assessment, with 152 successfully obtaining certification, resulting in an overall pass rate of 78.6%. The pass rates for individual components were: Classroom Teaching 93.3%, Skill Training 86.5%, and Teaching Ward Rounds 83.9%. A questionnaire survey was administered to those who successfully obtained certification. 148 questionnaires were returned (response rate: 97.37%), of which 145 were valid (valid response rate: 95.39%). Evaluation of the implementation effectiveness of teaching assessment certification for clinical nursing chief teachers

Participants’ evaluation of the certification system

Inductive analysis of the open-ended responses from 145 participants revealed: (a) Participants widely acknowledged that the greatest benefit of the certification system was the systematic enhancement of their personal teaching abilities and standardized teaching practices. Frequently mentioned keywords included “standardized teaching process” (49.0%), “knowledge consolidation” (46.2%), and “professional recognition” (37.2%). (b) Participants highly affirmed the design of the assessment system. The combined evaluation model of theory, skills, and teaching ward rounds was particularly praised for assessing preceptor competency while engaging students in learning. The teaching ward round assessment was mentioned 119 times, and expert commentary 73 times. (c) Constructive suggestions primarily focused on strengthening pre-assessment training and support (62.8%), including refining assessment criteria, providing teaching ward round demonstrations, and increasing assessment frequency. (d) Other supplementary suggestions included: ensuring the continuous operation of the certification system; organizing experience-sharing sessions from excellent departments and preceptors; and incorporating student satisfaction scores as an evaluation criterion. Notably, a few participants mentioned that some colleagues with associate degrees performed well in clinical teaching but were ineligible for the Lead Clinical Instructor assessment due to the degree requirement.

Departmental evaluation

Satisfaction surveys conducted among nursing interns in 2023 and 2024 collected 201 valid questionnaires. 94.5% of interns reported being relatively satisfied with the quality of teaching, and 95.5% were relatively satisfied with their preceptors. Furthermore, in interviews with 4 department managers, all expressed strong approval of the Lead Clinical Instructor certification system. Representative comments included: “The Lead Clinical Instructor assessment… standardized practices… using assessment to promote practice and teaching.” “It reduces the head nurse’s burden in teaching management… allowing more focus on nursing quality and departmental management.”“Certification fosters a sense of professional honor and intrinsic motivation among teaching nurses, significantly enhancing job satisfaction.”Suggestions for further development were also offered: “Assessment standards and methods should be regularly updated alongside advancements in clinical technology and educational theory.”

Discussion

Transition from experience-orientation to standardized certification: assessing clinical nurse preceptors

The global nursing field has long advocated for standardizing the assessment framework for clinical nurse preceptors. However, significant variations persist in clinical practice across countries and institutions. For example, the NLN Nurse Educator Competencies in the US focus on nursing faculty development within academic and educational settings [20, 21]. WHO [22] competency frameworks consider contexts of countries at different development levels, emphasizing equity and inclusivity in nursing education. Research [23] indicates high consistency between NLN and WHO nurse educator competency standards, providing a key framework for Master of Science in Nursing (MSN) programs and serving as a reference for nursing education in many countries. However, both primarily focus on institutional education or master’s level training, paying insufficient attention to the systematic training of frontline clinical preceptors. In China, the systematic construction of assessment mechanisms for preceptors remains relatively underdeveloped [24]. And some studies have attempted to build evaluation indicator systems covering comprehensive literacy, professional ability, and teaching capability, most remain at the indicator selection stage, lacking operational certification mechanisms [25, 26].

The certification system for Lead Clinical Instructors constructed in this study facilitates a shift in the selection of Clinical Nurse Preceptors from “seniority-oriented” to “competency-certified.” The certification system encompasses three core elements: theory, skills, and teaching practice, aligning closely with the evidence-based viewpoint proposed by Smith et al. [12] that “systematic selection and clear competency standards are key to selecting excellent preceptors.” By implementing standardized certification, this system effectively reduces fluctuations in teaching quality caused by individual differences among preceptors, providing nursing interns with a homogeneous, high-standard clinical learning environment. Furthermore, the structured scoring system minimizes subjective bias, ensuring the fairness and repeatability of the assessment process. Importantly, the system contributes significantly to faculty development by integrating explicit teaching standards with a formative assessment process that promotes self-directed learning, pedagogical reflection, and competency enhancement among participating preceptors. Although developed and implemented within a single tertiary hospital under a college–hospital collaborative model, the competency-based structure and standardized assessment framework may offer a practical reference for other institutions seeking to strengthen clinical nursing educator qualification mechanisms. With careful contextual adaptation and further multi-center validation, this model could potentially inform broader regional or national efforts aimed at enhancing consistency, quality assurance, and professionalization in clinical nursing education.

Emphasizing interactive teaching in clinical preceptorship practice

The results of this study show that the pass rate for the teaching ward rounds component (83.9%) was significantly lower than that for the theoretical teaching component (93.3%). Teaching ward rounds aim to assess the clinical preceptor’s ability to cultivate students comprehensive clinical competence through highly interactive and guided practice, moving beyond one-way knowledge transmission. This is a crucial indicator for evaluating clinical teaching competency. The lower pass rate directly reflects that high-level interactive teaching skills are a current weakness among clinical nurse preceptors. This finding aligns closely with subsequent questionnaire feedback: 81.5% of respondents highly affirmed the importance of the teaching ward rounds component, while 62.3% called for enhanced pre-assessment training, particularly for teaching ward rounds. Therefore, in the training and certification of clinical nursing chief teachers, strengthening specialized training in the concepts and methods of interactive teaching is key to improving the quality of teaching ward rounds and, consequently, ensuring high-quality clinical teaching. Interactive teaching is an effective method for enhancing nursing students’ qualities and can be categorized into teacher-student interaction, teaching-media interaction, and teacher-student emotional interaction [14, 27]. Interactive teaching has been widely adopted abroad, with nursing students showing higher learning interest and better feedback [28, 29]. Domestically, interactive teaching is mostly concentrated in university classroom settings, with insufficient application in frontline clinical teaching, indicating a gap in faculty development. For instance, Yu et al. [30] found that interactive e-books were more effective than printed textbooks in enhancing nursing students’ knowledge and confidence in performing nursing skills. Yao et al. [31] used clinical cases to help standardized training nursing interns master and enhance theoretical knowledge and practical skills, thereby improving their practical ability and self-directed learning.

Coexistence of achievements and challenges in the certification system

Against the backdrop of widespread staffing shortages in healthcare systems [15, 32, 33], enhancing the professional identity of Clinical Nurse Preceptors and fostering a teaching culture through institutional design offers a replicable practical model for stabilizing and developing the clinical faculty. The “License to Teach” system contributes to faculty development not only by certifying competence but also by embedding a continuous improvement mechanism. It encourages preceptors to actively engage in pedagogical training, peer learning, and reflective practice, thereby advancing both individual teaching capabilities and the collective educational capacity of the nursing unit. This study confirms that the “License to Teach” certification system for Lead Clinical Instructors has generated positive impacts at both individual and organizational levels, yet it also faces challenges during implementation that require further optimization.

The program was designed to develop a set of core teaching competencies in clinical preceptors, moving beyond interactive teaching to include: (1) effective application of theoretical knowledge in clinical instruction, (2) demonstration of standardized nursing skills with pedagogical intent, (3) facilitation of students’ clinical reasoning during patient encounters, and (4) provision of structured, formative feedback. At the individual level, the system enhances preceptors’ comprehensive competence through an “assessment-driven learning” mechanism. Participants frequently mentioned keywords like “knowledge consolidation”, “enhanced teaching ability” and “professional recognition” in feedback, reflecting high approval of the system. At the organizational level, certified preceptors become teaching role models within their departments, and standardized practical teaching elevates the overall teaching atmosphere. Despite significant effectiveness, the implementation process revealed key challenges: (a) The effectiveness of pre-assessment training needs improvement. A significant 62.3% of participants called for enhanced pre-assessment training, particularly demonstrations and interpretations for comprehensive ability assessments like teaching ward rounds. The reality is that relevant training was provided before the assessment, but the heavy workload of clinical nurses necessitates finding ways to enhance training effectiveness, which is crucial for improving faculty capability [34]. (b) Mismatch between degree requirements and actual teaching competence. Participants noted that “some colleagues with associate degrees, who are experienced and excellent clinical teachers, are excluded from certification due to degree requirements.” This suggests exploring more flexible entry mechanisms to enhance the system’s inclusivity and fairness. (c) Diversification of the assessment evaluation system. Assessment criteria need consideration of different departmental characteristics and appropriate adjustments to enhance clinical applicability.

Finally, we recognize that an institution-specific design may limit cross-institutional standardization. However, in China's current “college-hospital joint training” context, this hospital-based model provides a necessary, practical foundation. It serves as a replicable template that can inform and be integrated into future broader academic or national standardization efforts.

Looking ahead, the certification system should focus on establishing a dynamic, sustainable long-term development mechanism. This includes implementing regular qualification review and renewal systems, integrating multiple feedback channels such as student evaluations, and forming a virtuous cycle of “training –- certification –- motivation –- development” to continuously advance the cultivation of clinical nursing talent.

Limitation

This assessment scheme is based on the context of a tertiary Grade A hospital in China, and its specific standards may require adaptation for other countries, regions, or healthcare institutions at different levels. Additionally, the sample size of this study is relatively limited. It should also be noted that the evaluation of the certification system was conducted in 2025, more than two years after the initial training and certification. This time lag may have introduced potential biases, such as recall bias, acquiescence bias, or social desirability bias. Future research should expand the sample to include more hospitals at various levels and specialized departments, and consider more timely assessment intervals to further refine and validate the training and assessment system for clinical nurse preceptors.

Conclusions

The “License to Teach” system offers a standardized, innovative approach to nursing education. It ensures teaching quality, motivates instructors, and has potential for international adaptation. This study represents one of the first structured certification systems for clinical nursing chief teachers in China, providing a scalable practical framework for the selection and training of clinical nurse preceptors and offering a “Chinese solution” for constructing a replicable clinical nursing faculty certification system. It can be systematically promoted across different healthcare institutions in the future to continuously enhance the overall quality of clinical nursing education.

Relevance for clinical practice

The construction of this system standardizes the selection and certification of clinical nursing chief teachers. By ensuring the reliability of the faculty through quantified assessment, it helps improve the quality of clinical nursing teaching and provides a reference framework for the scientific management of hospital nursing education.
