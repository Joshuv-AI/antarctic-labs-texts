A bimodal dataset for diabetes research
Authors: Jiandun Li, Huiyao Zheng, Yabin Zhou, Fusong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, with the continuous booming of diabetes patients, the research on diabetes and its complications, including pathogenesis, early diagnosis and therapeutic interventions, has attracted considerable attention. However, the lack of large-scale real datasets has significantly impeded its in-depth development. To address this challenge, we hereby disclose our diabetes dataset of 5,922 examples and 190 attributes, spanning across many detailed and well-curated clinical and demographic records, e.g., BMI, lifestyle factors, family history, glycemic control, insulin-related measures, lipid and metabolic profiles, which can shed light for reliable analyses of diabetes progression, complication risks and associated physiological and metabolic factors.

Background & Summary

As a severe global crisis for public health, diabetes manifests pathological heterogeneity through distinct categories, i.e., type 1 diabetes (T1DM), type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and gestational diabetes, and systemic metabolic dysregulation could trigger end-organ complications such as retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy and cardiovascular emergency1–3. Therefore, early diagnosis of diabetes together with its complications is of great importance to manage the lifecycle of this illness. To address this issue, researchers have introduced many a machine learning model.

For instance, based on multiple health attributes, Febrian et al.4 proposed a comparative analysis via K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Naïve Bayes for diabetes prediction. Through 618 participants’ records, Fagherazzi et al.5 applied the Discriminative Dimensionality Reduction with Trees and revealed the heterogeneity of glycemic phenotypes in T1DM, and identified seven phenotypes and their related determinants. Based on a diabetic retinopathy dataset, Gadekallu et al.6 proposed a Deep Neural Network model optimized by Principal Component Analysis and Grey Wolf Optimization. With the cardiovascular complications in T2DM centered, Kee et al.7 conducted a systematic review on related machine learning models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gaussian Naïve Bayes (Gaussian NB), KNN, deep neural network, extreme gradient boosting.

To leverage the capability of machine learning, a great quantity of clinic records from real hospitals with rich attributes and annotations are necessary. Recently, Prioleau et al.8 provided a DiaTrend dataset, which consists of intensive longitudinal data from wearable medical devices, including 27,561 days of continuous glucose monitoring data and 8,220 days of insulin pump data from 54 patients with diabetes. However, the dataset does not have any attributes related to diabetes complications. Additionally, Salcedo-Tacuma et al.9 collected multiple data samples on transcriptomic features of subcutaneous and omental adipose tissues from patients with gestational diabetes and matched controls during cesarean delivery using next generation RNA sequencing. Nevertheless, it only took 5 patients into consideration, which is insufficient to generalize to real-world cases. Rodriguez-Leon et al.10 provided a dataset that encompass continuous glucose level measurements, alongside patient demographic and clinical data. These examples are sourced from 736 T1DM patients in Spain, and the time spans across 257,780 days over a four-year period. Although this dataset is multimodal, important attributes, such as diabetes history are still missing. Facing the challenge of limited 3D-spatial and quantitative information, Hahn et al.11 present a dataset consisting of optical images of full β-cell subject to diverse doses of administration. More digital features such individual islet β-cell volumes, spatial coordinates, and shape together with signal intensities for insulin are provided. Nevertheless, this dataset is comprehensive, significant attributes are missing, e.g., single-cell transcriptome and proteome data, therefore it has little impact on diabetes diagnosis and complication detection on humans. Although these datasets have successfully supervised some machine learning models for diabetes related researches, there still exist several limitations: (1) the number of patients included is limited (averaged 265); (2) the feature dimensions are also inadequate (averaged 17.5); (3) they are always monomodal-oriented, i.e., in the format of real number. As a response, this study systematically builds a bimodal (digital and text) diabetes dataset12 supported by real-world diabetes patients, including 190 attributes and 5,922 samples recorded in two months. It can facilitate related researches on diabetes pre-diagnosis, complication prediction and treatment adherence. The characteristics of this dataset is threefold: (1) it captures multi-system metabolic indicators, e.g., liver and kidney functions; (2) a wide range of patients, i.e., aging from 18 to 91 years old, are covered; (3) multiple complications, such as diabetic nephropathy and blindness are involved.

Methods

Participants

The data in this study were derived from the de-identified cross-sectional dataset of diabetes patients in the Department of Diabetes and Endocrinology at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital from February to April, 2012. The dataset12 encompasses demographic and clinical characteristics of diabetic patients, including fundamental traits and obesity indices, with a focus on their correlations and mechanistic impacts on diabetes onset. Detailed demographic information and basic physiological profiles of diabetic patients are also provided. In general, this dataset12 can enable in-depth analysis of the specific effects of glycemic status, lipid profiles, insulin levels, hepatic, renal and hematologic parameters, integrated with disease history, lifestyle factors, complication profiles and genetic predispositions, along the development and progression of diabetes mellitus.

Data cleaning and preparation

To prepare the dataset, the following steps were performed: (1) renaming variables: original variables were uniformly converted into standardized English nomenclature. (2) de-identification: all personal identifiers have been removed to secure privacy.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2019-022).

Consent for data sharing

The Committee specifically reviewed and granted a waiver of informed consent for this retrospective analysis of fully de-identified medical records. This determination is documented in the associated Chair Reviewer’s Assessment Form for Waiver of Informed Consent. Given the comprehensive de-identification of the data and the minimal risk involved, the sharing of this anonymized dataset for research purposes is within the scope of this ethical approval.

Data Records

All data in this dataset12 are stored and accessible via the Zenodo platform, comprising a single Excel file with 190 variables and 5,922 anonymized patient records. A detailed data dictionary explaining all variables is provided in the Supplementary Information. This multidimensional resource encompasses demographic characteristics, physical examinations, biochemical measurements, medical histories, lifestyle factors, and complication profiles. Collected during February to April 2012, the dataset supports epidemiological investigations and clinical analyses of diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and related comorbidities.

Technical Validation

To ensure the reliability, internal consistency, and overall quality of the dataset12, a series of validations were conducted, focusing on statistical summaries, distributional plausibility and cross-variable coherence.

Statistical and Distributional Checks. Descriptive statistics were computed for all key variables (including BMI, FPG, PG2h, lipid profiles, renal function markers, insulin measures) to screen for data entry errors and assess basic data integrity. The unimodal distributions of continuous variables and the proportions of categorical variables were reviewed for consistency with general clinical expectations.

Validation of Inter-variable Relationships and Plausibility. To verify the biological plausibility and internal consistency among core measures, we examined the relationships between anthropometric and metabolic indicators.

In Fig. 1, scatter plots between BMI and glycemic parameters were given. Linear regression analysis with 95% confidence intervals was performed to quantify their associations. The observed positive trends, along with the concentration of data points within established clinical thresholds for BMI and blood glucose, illustrated the physiological plausibility and internal coherence of these key variables.Fig. 1Scatter plots of BMI versus blood glucose measurements with linear regression. (a) Relationship between BMI and fasting plasma glucose (FPG). Solid line represents the linear fit; shaded area indicates the 95% confidence interval. (b) Relationship between BMI and 2-hour postprandial glucose (PG2h). Regression line and 95% confidence interval are shown. Clinical thresholds for BMI categories and glycemic status are indicated by vertical dashed lines and horizontal-colored bands, respectively.
