Estimation of total-tract starch digestibility using fecal starch concentration in fattening Japanese Black cattle
Authors: Masaya Matamura, Makoto Kondo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFattening Japanese Black cattle (Wagyu), renowned for producing marbled beef, derive essential energy from starch. While fecal starch concentration (FSC) can estimate total-tract starch digestibility (TTSD), equations developed for other beef production systems may not suit highly marbled beef production. This study developed a specific equation for estimating TTSD from FSC in Japanese Black cattle and compared it with data from other breed’s production systems. Experiments with 49 Japanese Black cattle across four farms yielded 116 TTSD measurements. A database from 41 published studies on other cattle breeds (190 treatment groups, 2343 animals) was also analyzed. The Japanese Black cattle-specific equation (TTSD, % = 99.7 − 0.85 × FSC, %DM; R2 = 0.89) showed a steeper slope compared to the literature-derived equation (TTSD, % = 100.1 − 0.54 × FSC, %DM; R2 = 0.79) (P = 0.06). This difference reflects characteristics of Wagyu beef production, specifically lower dietary starch concentration (32.5 vs. 50.4%DM, P < 0.05) and higher DM indigestibility (29.5 vs. 22.5%, P < 0.05). A preliminary surveillance of commercial farms revealed substantial farm-to-farm variation in starch utilization, with TTSD ranging from 70.0 to 99.4%. Introduction

Sustainable beef production requires improved on-farm efficiency and productivity, particularly in feed resource utilization1. This is especially crucial for highly marbled beef production systems using Japanese Black (Wagyu) cattle and their crossbreds, which are now raised not only in Japan but also in countries such as Australia, the United States, and European nations. While these cattle are valued for their highly marbled beef, the current production systems face challenges of high costs and low efficiency. The intensive feeding regime required for marbling development, which typically involves feeding high-concentrate diets for long periods, raises significant concerns about economic viability and feed resource sustainability1. Therefore, improving feed efficiency, especially in terms of starch utilization, is essential for both the economic and environmental sustainability of highly marbled beef production.

Starch is a valuable resource that serves as food, feed, and an energy source. In beef cattle diets, high-starch grains like corn and barley constitute 40–70% of their diet2–4. Efficient starch utilization is crucial for optimal beef production. Although starch is generally considered highly digestible5–7, incomplete starch digestion can occur8,9, resulting in productivity losses10 and increased greenhouse gas emissions during manure composting11. Such emissions contribute to environmental issues, including climate change. Therefore, understanding dietary starch digestibility is essential for achieving efficient fattening cattle production while minimizing starch loss and environmental impact.

Previous studies have established a strong negative correlation between total-tract starch digestibility (TTSD) and fecal starch concentration (FSC)12,13. This relationship has led to the development of equations to estimate TTSD from FSC in both beef and dairy cattle. These equations are particularly useful when individual feed intake and fecal output measurements are impractical, or for assessing herd digestibility on commercial farms. The general form of these equations is , where is the intercept (typically around 100), is the slope, and is the FSC (% dry matter [DM])13. According to the review by Owens et al., the slopes for fattening beef cattle and lactating dairy cows differ, with reported values of − 0.44 and − 1.07, respectively. The review also demonstrated mathematically that the slope is represented by the DM indigestibility/dietary starch concentration (DSC) ratio13. These factors are influenced by feed ingredients, grain composition ratios, and grain processing methods10,14–16. The unique characteristics of Japanese Black cattle, including their marbling-focused fattening systems, may contribute to unique starch utilization patterns. Therefore, a system-specific estimation equation is warranted to accurately evaluate TTSD in this breed, as the slope of the TTSD–FSC relationship is likely influenced by these feeding conditions (e.g., grain processing methods like steam-flaking or grinding, and the concentrate-to-roughage ratio).

This study aimed to develop a novel estimation equation for predicting TTSD from fecal starch concentration (FSC) in fattening Japanese Black cattle, which are raised under distinctly different dietary and management conditions to produce high marbling beef. This novel equation captures the steeper relationship between FSC and TTSD, which enables on-farm assessment of starch digestibility without the need for labor-intensive fecal collection or total tract digestibility trials. Specifically, we hypothesized that the slope of the FSC–TTSD relationship in fattening Japanese Black cattle would differ from values previously reported in the literature, which were based on studies of other cattle breeds, and that these differences would primarily reflect feeding system characteristics. By testing this hypothesis, we aimed to provide a tool that supports improved feed efficiency and sustainability in highly marbled beef production systems.

Material and methods

Animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines for Animal Care and Use of the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Fisheries of Japan (18Noukai #307). The experiments were conducted at 4 prefectural institutes, and all procedures were approved by the animal research assessment committee at each institute (#16-8-2018 at Mie Prefectural Livestock Research Center, #14-2-2013 at Hiroshima Prefectural Technology Research Institute, Livestock division, #1-4-2017 at Gifu Prefectural Livestock Research Center, #10-11-2017 at Shiga Prefectural Livestock Technology Promotion Center). All animal studies followed the ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals, diets and sample collection

The study encompassed four farms, utilizing 7 heifers and 42 steers, yielding a total of 116 TTSD measurements across multiple experimental periods (Table 1). The four experimental farms were selected to represent common feeding systems for Japanese Black cattle, including institutional research facilities located in major production regions. All farms followed fattening protocols in which diets were based primarily on high-concentrate rations typical of commercial Japanese Black cattle operations. Animals were weighed without fasting immediately before the period of the digestibility measurement experiment. Starch, neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and crude protein (CP) concentration ± SD in the experimental diets were 32.5 ± 6.8 (%DM), 32.6 ± 2.8 (%DM), and 13.1 ± 2.6 (%DM), respectively. Following more than a 10-day adaptation period, a consecutive 4–5-day sampling phase was implemented. Feces were collected either in their entirety daily or as partial samples twice daily (morning and afternoon). Feed samples were obtained twice during the experimental period and pooled. All refusals were collected throughout the period, pooled, and subsampled for analysis. Feces, feed, and refusal samples were stored at − 28 °C until further analysis.

Sample processing and chemical analysis

Samples (feces, feed, and refusal) were thawed and dried in a forced-air oven: feces at 60 °C for 48–72 h, and feed and refusals at 60 °C for 48 h. Dried samples were ground to pass through a 1 mm screen. Acid-insoluble ash served as an internal marker for estimating total fecal output and calculating apparent total-tract digestibility17. Although AIA is widely accepted for digestibility studies, it may be subject to variable recovery rates between feed and feces, which could introduce minor estimation errors in digestibility calculations. NDF in feed was determined using the procedure of Van Soest et al.18 with α-amylase, and CP concentration in feed was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method. A portion of each sample was then further ground using a fine grinder (Wonder Blender, Osaka Chemical) and used as samples for starch analysis19.

Literature search and data extraction

To compare our developed equation for estimating TTSD in Japanese Black cattle with equations from other beef cattle in the literature, a database of TTSD and FSC was created by conducting a comprehensive literature search on PubMed and Web of Science. The systematic search considered the following keywords: 1) beef cattle starch digestibility and 2) feedlot cattle starch digestibility. The literature search covered publications from 1993 to 2023. This approach follows the method described by Owens et al.13. Although this estimation involves certain assumptions, we minimized potential bias by using directly reported values wherever available and applying weighted regression analysis in the comparative evaluation.

A total of 67 papers were initially collected. Because this study focuses on fattening cattle under grain-based feeding systems typical of Japanese Black cattle production, exclusion criteria were applied to avoid the inclusion of non-grain feeding conditions or animals in early growth phases. Specifically, studies were excluded if the dietary concentrate proportion was less than 70% of dry matter or if the body weight of the animals was less than 200 kg. This resulted in a database of 41 studies encompassing 190 treatments and a total number of 2343 animals, as shown in Table 2. Based on these data from the literature, we developed an estimation equation relating FSC to TTSD and compared it with the equation derived from our study on Japanese Black cattle.

Preliminary surveillance at commercial farms

To investigate the variability in starch digestibility, fecal samples were collected from fattening cattle across ten commercial farms located in western parts of Japan. Ten commercial farms were selected based on their willingness to participate and their geographic and operational diversity. Farms varied in size, feed formulation, and management style to provide a preliminary but diverse overview of starch digestibility under practical conditions. A total of 182 fecal samples were randomly collected, with 5 to 81 samples obtained from each commercial farm. All fecal samples were immediately placed in sealed containers and stored at− 20 °C until further starch analysis. The DSC of each commercial farm was also measured.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.3.1) within RStudio. The regression equation was developed using the lm() function. Furthermore, to ensure comparability with the estimation equation developed from our data, weighted regression analysis was adopted for studies from the literature, with weights assigned based on the total number of animals in each treatment group. Scatter plots were generated using ggplot() from the ggplot2 package. To test the difference between slopes in our study and data from the literature, a test of the interaction term in the full regression model was conducted20. Welch Two Sample t-test was used to compare differences in DSC and dry matter indigestibility between our study and studies in the literature. The R code can be found in Supplementary Material. In statistical analyses, differences were declared significant when P < 0.05, and trends were discussed at 0.05 < P ≤ 0.10.

Results

TTSD-FSC relationships in Japanese Black cattle versus other cattle

Analysis of Japanese Black cattle data revealed a strong negative correlation between FSC and TTSD (Fig. 1, blue square). This relationship was described by the regression equation: TTSD (%) = 99.7 − 0.85 × (FSC, %DM) with a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.00% and an R2 = 0.89. The data from other cattle in the literature (Fig. 1, red circle) showed a similar negative correlation but with a notably different slope: TTSD (%) = 100.1 − 0.54 × (FSC, %DM) with an RMSE of 2.52% and an R2 = 0.79. The statistical comparison revealed a trend for a difference in slopes (P = 0.06), with Japanese Black cattle showing a more pronounced negative relationship.

The slopes of the regression equations tended to differ (P = 0.06), with 95% confidence intervals ranging from − 0.91 to − 0.80 for Japanese Black cattle and from − 0.58 to − 0.50 for other cattle from the literature. Starch digestibility estimation incorporating DSC

Analysis of TTSD using the ratio of FSC to DSC revealed improved estimation accuracy for both datasets (Fig. 2). For Japanese Black cattle, this relationship was described by the equation TTSD (%) = 100.0 − 33.5 × (FSC/DSC), with an RMSE of 0.79% and an R2 of 0.93. The 95% confidence intervals ranged from 99.8 to 100.2 for the intercept and from − 35.2 to − 31.7 for the slope. Analysis of data from the literature yielded a similar but slightly different equation: TTSD (%) = 100.4 − 28.2 × (FSC/DSC), with an RMSE of 1.23% and an R2 of 0.94. For this equation, the 95% confidence intervals were 100.1 to 100.7 for the intercept and –29.5 to − 26.9 for the slope. The difference in slopes was not statistically significant (P = 0.11).

Discussion

Our study aimed to develop specific TTSD estimation equations for Japanese Black cattle under typical fattening conditions in Japan, with the goal of improving feed resource utilization in highly marbled beef production. Regression analysis indicated that the slope of the TTSD estimation equation for Japanese Black cattle (− 0.85) tended to be steeper than that for other cattle from the literature (− 0.54), showing a trend toward statistical significance (P = 0.06). Although multiple factors could contribute to this difference, our subsequent analysis using Eq. (4) suggests that it is more likely attributable to systematic differences in feeding practices—such as higher starch intake and lower fiber digestibility.

The feeding systems designed for Japanese Black cattle are characterized by lower DSC (32.5%DM) compared to that reported in the literature (50.4%DM). This fundamental difference in dietary composition has significant implications for TTSD estimation. As demonstrated mathematically by Owens et al.13, lower DSC inherently results in a more pronounced TTSD–FSC relationship, independent of breed effects. This relationship is supported by research showing that ruminal starch digestibility averages approximately 71%21, with post-ruminal starch digestion efficiency being dependent on the quantity of starch entering the small intestine6. When large amounts of starch enter the small intestine, the capacity for enzymatic digestion can become saturated, leading to reduced digestion efficiency. This physiological limitation is particularly relevant in high-starch diets, as demonstrated by Walsh et al.22 in crossbred cattle, where increased DSC led to decreased TTSD despite consistent DMI levels. The lower DSC typically used in Japanese Black cattle production systems may therefore represent an optimization of starch utilization efficiency, as it likely maintains starch flow to the small intestine within the capacity for enzymatic digestion. This optimization could partially explain the more substantial TTSD response to FSC observed in our equation; at lower DSC, starch utilization efficiency is enhanced, leading to a more pronounced response to changes in FSC.

The higher DM indigestibility observed in Japanese Black cattle reflects a complex interaction among management practices specific to highly marbled beef production systems. A key factor contributing to this difference is the use of rice straw as the primary roughage source in the feeding system used for fattening Japanese cattle. Rice straw is characterized by a higher silica concentration (10–15%DM) and inherently lower digestibility compared to other roughage sources23–26. In studies from the literature, barley silage (12.4–21.4%DM starch27,28) or corn silage (27.7–35.9%DM starch29,30) was commonly used as a roughage source. Unlike rice straw which contributes minimal starch to the diet, these silages not only provide more digestible fibers but also contribute to overall DSC. This difference in roughage sources may affect overall diet digestibility.

As another possibility for high DM indigestibility, the feeding management of vitamin A restriction in fattening Japanese Black cattle could also be considered. Vitamin A restriction, a management practice unique to highly marbled beef production31,32, promotes marbling development through enhanced adipogenic gene expression33–35. However, this practice may indirectly affect digestive efficiency through multiple mechanisms, including compromised ruminal epithelial health. Vitamin A deficiency has been reported to cause keratinization abnormalities, such as parakeratosis, in humans36. Additionally, in juvenile cattle, vitamin A deficiency has been associated with orthokeratotic dermatopathy37. Given the critical role of vitamin A in the keratinization process, it is possible that parakeratosis also occurs in the rumen of ruminants. Cattle affected by parakeratosis experience reduced short-chain fatty acid absorption due to impaired ruminal papillae function38,39. This decline in absorption capacity may lead to altered ruminal pH, which can potentially affect fiber digestion40. Although the current study did not directly evaluate vitamin A status or ruminal epithelial structure, these findings suggest a biological hypothesis that vitamin A restriction management could be a contributing factor to the high DM indigestibility observed in Japanese Black cattle. Further research is needed to validate this hypothesis.

The incorporation of DSC into the estimation equation, by expressing fecal starch as a proportion of dietary starch, substantially improved estimation accuracy as evidenced by reduced RMSE values. This improvement suggests that accounting for DSC is crucial for accurate TTSD estimation, particularly when comparing across different feeding systems. The modification also resulted in closer alignment between the slopes of Japanese Black cattle and other cattle from the literature (–33.5 vs. –28.2), compared to the original equations (– 0.85 vs. – 0.54). These values were relatively close to the slope reported by Owens et al.13 for fattening cattle (–26.0), indicating that much of the initially observed difference in TTSD estimation can be attributed to variations in DSC. As explained by Owens et al.13, the remaining difference in TTSD estimation primarily reflects the influence of overall diet digestibility. Indeed, by incorporating DM indigestibility into our estimation equations as well as those in studies in the literature, we observed an improvement in the accuracy of the estimation equations (R2 = 0.97 and 0.96, respectively). While these findings strongly suggest that the apparent breed differences in starch utilization, as reflected in the regression equation, may be largely attributable to systematic differences in dietary management, we acknowledge that the statistical comparison alone (P = 0.11) does not provide definitive evidence. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes and more diverse dietary conditions would be valuable to confirm this conclusion.

It should be noted that the literature-derived dataset used for comparison encompasses a wide range of cattle breeds, geographical regions, feeding practices, and grain processing methods. Such heterogeneity may have contributed to variability in TTSD–FSC relationships across studies. Although weighted regression was employed to mitigate the influence of small-sample studies by improving statistical precision, this approach may also lead to disproportionate influence from large-scale studies. The diversity of production systems may still obscure breed- or system-specific patterns. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the comparative slopes, and future studies under more controlled and homogeneous conditions are necessary to confirm these findings.

Among the ten farms, approximately half showed median digestibility values of 95% or higher, indicating that many fattening cattle efficiently digested starch. Conversely, three farms had median values below 90%, suggesting that cattle on these farms lost approximately 10% of the starch consumed in their feed through fecal excretion. This investigation was conducted to preliminarily understand the actual state of starch digestibility in commercial farms, rather than in cattle raised for research purposes at research institutions. Despite the small sample size of only 10 farms, it is meaningful that large differences in starch digestibility were demonstrated among farms. However, due to the limited number of farms and incomplete feed information, in-depth statistical analyses—such as multivariate regression or group comparisons based on feeding practices—could not be conducted. The results of this preliminary survey highlight the importance of conducting research involving a larger number of farms raising Japanese Black cattle and their crossbreds to grasp the actual state of starch digestibility. While this preliminary surveillance could not identify the factors causing differences in digestibility, future research with an increased number of farms is necessary to elucidate the factors contributing to farm-to-farm variations in digestibility, focusing on aspects such as processing methods of starch-rich grains like corn, barley and rice, the amount of roughage (mainly rice straw) and so on. In addition, investigating within-farm variation and the effects of starch type and grain processing methods on digestibility may provide further insights. These efforts are expected to improve production efficiency of fattening cattle and reduce environmental impacts from starch excreted in feces.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed an estimation equation for predicting TTSD from FSC, specifically tailored to Japanese Black cattle. This equation represents a practical tool for assessing feed efficiency in highly marbled beef production systems. Compared to equations derived from other beef cattle breeds, our equation demonstrated a steeper slope, reflecting differences in feeding system characteristics. These findings suggest that fundamental principles of starch utilization can be applied across diverse production systems when dietary factors are appropriately considered. While our estimation equations provide a practical tool for estimating TTSD in highly marbled beef production, further research is needed to establish efficient monitoring protocols for commercial applications. This includes determining optimal fecal sampling strategies, particularly the minimum number of animals required to obtain representative herd-level TTSD estimates, and developing cost-effective methods for fecal starch analysis. Such methodological refinements would facilitate the practical application of these equations in commercial settings, contributing to more efficient feed utilization and potentially supporting the development of highly marbled beef production in diverse regions.
