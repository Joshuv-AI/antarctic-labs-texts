Effectiveness of a school-based health literacy intervention in improving adolescent health literacy and the intention to take health-promoting actions
Authors: Shanti Prasad Khanal, Chitra Bahadur Budhathoki, Orkan Okan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is a gap in theory-based interventions targeting adolescent health literacy. This study evaluated the impact of a school-based health literacy intervention on self-efficacy, health literacy awareness, situation, and intentions to take health-promoting actions among adolescents in community schools in Surkhet, Nepal.MethodsThis study followed a pre-test post-test quasi-experimental design. Based on identified health literacy needs, this study employed a five-step intervention mapping approach to design, implement, and evaluate health literacy interventions. A modified Delphi survey with interdisciplinary health experts was conducted to validate its main components, ensuring contextualisation. It was implemented over 13 classroom sessions in the intervention groups via the SHOWED questioning framework (What do you see here? What is happening? How does this relate to our lives? Why does the problem exist? What can we do?), led by nine interdisciplinary facilitators. Of the N = 468 participants, 144 participated in the intervention group and 324 participated in the non-intervention group. To evaluate the intervention’s effectiveness, the same self-administered questionnaires, such as HL awareness, HLS-Child-24-Nep, health-promoting actions, and self-efficacy, were used in the pre- and post-test. The intervention’s effectiveness was analysed via percentages for binary responses and paired t-tests for normally distributed responses. The generalised estimation equation model (GEE) ensured its effectiveness on the outcomes.ResultsThe intervention significantly improved HL awareness, self-efficacy, and overall health literacy, including four dimensions (access, understanding, appraising, and applying health information) across three health domains (health care, disease prevention, and health promotion). Furthermore, the intervention significantly led to the intention to take health-promoting action and its five constructs. GEE analysis confirmed the intervention’s effectiveness in terms of self-efficacy, health-promoting actions, and health literacy (p < 0.001), excluding exercise behaviour (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe study revealed that a multicomponent intervention improves adolescent health literacy and health-promoting actions. This study suggests that adolescent health literacy can be enhanced by empowering teachers, parents, and schools through multilevel interventions.

Background

Health literacy (HL) is a multifaceted concept that focuses on individuals’ ability to navigate [1, 2] and manage health information effectively to improve health and solve health problems [3, 4]. Adequate HL supports adolescents’ health-promoting behaviour, health conditions, and educational outcomes [5]. Moreover, students with low HL have been linked to lower academic accomplishments, falling behind in school, and potentially hindering their potential health [4].

Adolescence is a critical stage of development, characterised by the maturation of physical, psychological, social, economic, legal, and moral terrain [6]. In this period, they gain the capacity for self-awareness, critical thinking, autonomy, and emotional independence [7, 8]. These changes increase their susceptibility to various risks and heightened vulnerability [9]. Currently, interest in and a growing evidence base for adolescent HL (AHL) are increasing globally [3]. Therefore, adolescence has emerged as a critical demographic for HL research and interventions [10, 11].

Although its importance is highlighted, there is currently a scarcity of theory-driven HL interventions, particularly those addressing adolescents, especially in less-developed countries. For example, a recent review revealed that no intervention has explicitly aimed at improving HL [12]. Most existing school-based HL interventions are domain-specific without a holistic model [13], are frequently ignored in school curricula [14], and lack effective strategies for improving HL in schools [15]. Furthermore, evidence related to school-based intervention design, implementation, and evaluation is lacking [16]. The situation of AHL in developing countries is unclear because of the limited research conducted thus far in relatively advanced countries [17], such as Nepal. Notably, studies in developed countries have reported that adolescents’ HL situation is poor [11, 18, 19].

Adolescents constitute one-fifth of Nepal’s total population [20], and commonly experience poor health status, remaining vulnerable due to low access to healthcare facilities and services [21]. They encounter various health challenges [20], including problems related to nutrition, reproductive health [22], and mental disorders [23]. In Karnali Province, these health problems are more common and severe than they are in other provinces among adolescents, especially early marriage, teen pregnancy, limited access to family planning methods, nutritional deficiencies, and low education. The province has the second-highest total fertility rate (2.8) among the states, with a teenage birth rate of 1.8 [22] and any mental disorder [23].

In Nepal, HL represents a relatively novel concept with only limited attention, discussion, cognition, and adoption observed across the entire country and research community [24]. Additionally, HL is not explicitly targeted in school curricula and health policies, which makes supporting education and research efforts [24] difficult. Few studies have shown that school-aged adolescents seem to have low HL levels [25, 26]. In the Surkhet district, both school adolescents [24, 27] and campus girls [28, 29] face challenges in understanding HL. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of an HL intervention among school-aged adolescents in Surkhet, Nepal.

Methods

Research design

The study employed a pre-test and post-test quasi-experimental study design [30, 31]. Since randomisation was not feasible in a school-based intervention due to the school’s logistical, administrative, and policy constraints, a quasi-experimental design is deemed suitable for this study in a real-school context [32]. To minimise the selection bias, several measures were employed, including matching the multiple profiles and study outcomes of both groups at baseline to ensure comparability [33]. Similarly, this study used a generalised equation estimation (GEE) regression model to adjust covariates, ensuring internal validity [34]. The study followed a six-month HL needs analysis in interventions and comparisons supporting the control of the maturation effect [35]. The study also controlled near-place matching through sampling to minimise the interactions among the participants of both groups [36].

Study site

This study was conducted exclusively in community (government) secondary schools located in the Birendranagar municipality of the Surkhet district, Nepal. There were a total of 26 community secondary schools in the municipality, where 2,700 students were studying in this academic session [37].

Participants, sample, and recruitment

The study involved ninth-grade school adolescents as study participants. It focused on only one class because academic grade affects HL [38]. Out of the 26 community secondary schools in the municipality, four schools: Janasewa, Tripureshor, Sidda, and Nepal Rastriya Secondary Schools were selected via a three-round iterative random sampling method to match the distance between schools, minimise the risk of contamination, and consider the probability of cross-group interaction based on the schools’ locations. In the first round, four secondary schools were randomly selected; among them, Janasewa and Kopila secondary schools were relatively close. To reduce proximity, Kopila Secondary School was excluded, and the second lottery was employed. In the second round, one school was selected out of 22 (excluding the three already considered), resulting in Bhairav Secondary School. Even though Bhairav and Tripureshor schools are near each other, Bhairav School was excluded from the third round, and the final school, Siddha Secondary School, was selected by lottery from the remaining 21 schools.

From the selected schools, two schools (Janasewa Secondary School and Sidda Secondary School) were assigned to the intervention group, whereas two (Nepal Rastriya Secondary School and Tripurshor Secondary School) were assigned to the non-intervention group, randomly again through a lottery. All ninth graders were included using a census method. This process was performed to ensure the representativeness of the schools, maintain parity within the same geographical area, and ensure comparable similarity in the school administration, policy structure, and socio-economic background of the students. This process also strengthened the comparability between the groups.

Intervention development, implementation, and evaluation

The development of the intervention followed the basic steps of the intervention mapping approach, which included five steps: HL need assessment, setting goals and objectives, intervention development, implementation, and evaluation [40].

The research process began with the HL needs assessment [41] between June 4th and August 2nd, which involved concurrently collecting and analysing quantitative and qualitative data from students, HPE teachers, and school nurses. Overall, this phase evaluated and prioritised the HL needs of the students, such as low self-efficacy, low familiarity with the HL concept, poor skills in the HL dimensions and health domains, and HPAs. This phase offers key insights that inform the intervention design [41].

Based on HL needs, we conducted a two-round modified Delphi study with experts in the interdisciplinary health area to reach a consensus on specific domains of intervention to validate the intervention. The panel consisted of 17 experts in round one and 15 in round two, including five university faculty members, four school health education teachers, three school nurses, two medical doctors, and three public health officers. The Delphi survey integrates a total of 64 items based on four main themes (context/objectives, content, delivery methods, and evaluation). The first round of Delphi’s findings led to a consensus on 41 items, with 23 remaining unconfirmed. However, 11 items reached consensus in the second round, and 12 remained unconfirmed. The study excluded the non-consensus items. Based on the Delphi methods, a first draft intervention was prepared and discussed with the students, HPE teachers, and school nurses of the intervention school. Considering their feedback and concerns, the final draft of the intervention was made.

The intervention, which lasted seven weeks, consisted of 13 sessions and was implemented by nine interdisciplinary facilitators via the SHOWED questioning framework in the school’s classroom from January 16, 2023, to March 3, 2023. The nine sessions took 1.5 hour, and four sessions were held for one hour. The facilitators were university faculties, school HPE/social teachers, school nurses, public health officers, medical doctors, Ayurveda doctors, MPhil scholars, and a former drug user. To ensure effective delivery, the respective facilitator involved in orientation meetings impersonally focuses on dialogical approaches. Discussions and meetings were conducted with HPE teachers, students, and facilitators during the implementation process (Table 1).

Evaluation of the effectiveness of the intervention was a third phase. Pre-test and post-test data were collected to compare the overall effectiveness of the intervention.

Data collection tools

A standardised self-administered questionnaire was used to collect the data. The questionnaire was divided into five parts, reflected by the questionnaire subscales (1) socio-demographics, (2) HL awareness, (3) General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), (4) HLS-Child-Q24-NEP, and (5) Adolescent Health Promoting Short Form Scale (AHP-SF).

The socio-demographic profile was used to collect information about participants’ characteristics, including age, sex, caste, religion, parental education, family size, availability of TV, radio, mobile, internet, health status, and educational outcome.

Awareness about HL was assessed by employing a scale involving 25 dichotomous items that addressed HL’s meaning, advantages, consequences, and sources of health information for the target group. The study team prepared it. The participants were asked to indicate their HL awareness, with multiple responses provided (e.g., “Have you heard of HL? Yes/No”, What do you think health literacy is? (Multiple responses) (1). Obtaining health-related knowledge (yes/no), (2). Obtaining health services (yes/no), (3). The ability to obtain, understand, evaluate, and use health information. (yes/no), (4). Do not know.

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) was used to assess students’ general self-efficacy [42]. The validated scale was developed by Schwarzer & Jerusalem [42]. The scale takes 4 min to complete and has a four-point scale, such as 1 (not true at all) to 4 (Absolutely true), with an overall score of 10–40, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.76 to 0.90, with the majority in the high range [42].

To examine the HL level of school-aged adolescents, the Nepalese language version of the HLSChild-24 NEP scale was used [24]. The HLS-Child-24 NEP was adapted from the internationally established HLS-CHILD-Q15 children`s HL questionnaire [43, 44], which was originally developed and validated in German school-aged children. The HLS-Child-24 NEP assesses health literacy across four dimensions: accessing (4 items), understanding (8 items), appraising (7 items), and applying (5 items). Those are summarised under one of the following three health domains: health care (8 items), disease prevention (8 items), and health promotion (8 items). The responses were measured using a four-point scale, ranging from 1 (very difficult) to 4 (very easy). The scale was designed to address the multifaceted needs of utilising health information in modern society, combining a broad understanding of general HL [18]. The HLS-Child-Q24-NEP was pilot-tested among 55 students, and showed good reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81. In the main study with a larger sample, the tool demonstrated high reliability, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88. Additionally, strong correlations were observed between the items representing the four dimensions and the three health domains, with coefficients ranging from 0.33 to 0.59, indicating good validity.

Finally, the study utilised the AHP-SF scale to examine school-aged adolescents’ AHPs, including 21 items on nutrition (3 items), social support (4 items), health responsibility (4 items), life appreciation (4 items), exercise (4 items), and stress management (3 items). The responses were measured using a four-point scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The AHP-SF is a valid and reliable tool for evaluating adolescent health-promoting actions, with excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.90 and subscales ranging from 0.70 to 0.90 M-Y Chen, L-J Lai, H-C Chen and J Gaete [45].

The scales underwent pilot testing with 55 Nepalese students in ninth grade across four schools in three districts from April to May 2022. Cronbach’s alpha values were computed for scales demonstrating satisfactory to high internal consistency, including GSE (0.68), HLS-child-Q24-Nep (0.81), and AHP-SF (0.70).

Data collection methods

The same questionnaires were used in both phases of data collection. Baseline data were collected before implementing the intervention. Participants in the intervention group received the HL intervention, whereas the non-intervention group did not [31]. The post-test was conducted immediately after the intervention (a week) [30] from February 27 to March 3. The reason for conducting the immediate post-test was the contextual situation of the schools.

The purpose of the study was clearly explained to the participants before data collection, ensuring that there were no right or wrong answers for their confident responses. They filled out the questionnaire anonymously, similar to a paper-and-pencil test, with 20–30 students at a time. The instructions and questionnaire items were presented in Nepali. The researcher and the assistant approached participants neutrally to clarify doubts when requested. This allowed the participants to respond privately without social pressure.

Data analysis

Although the measures used in this study, such as the GSE, HL, and HPA-SF, were ordinal scales, we calculated the total scores of the items in these scales and treated them as continuous scales for analysis. This way allowed us to use parametric tests [46]. The HL awareness measure was a nominal scale.

The study used Fisher’s exact test and the Pearson Chi-square test to compare baseline profiles of both groups, to obtain an unbiased estimation of the impact of the intervention [30]. The study performed Independent t-tests to compare the differences in self-efficacy, HL, and HPAs between the intervention and non-intervention groups at the baseline. The study used frequency only to compare HL awareness among the groups at the baseline phase.

To examine the effectiveness of the intervention between the pre-test and post-test outcomes, percentage was used for nominal binary responses, and paired t-tests were used for interval variables for normally distributed data such as GSE, HL, and HPAs. The generalised estimation equation (GSE) regression model [47, 48] was used to ensure the intervention’s effect on outcomes by adjusting for covariates. The study used GEE to adjust for observed baseline differences between the intervention and nonintervention groups, controlled for confounding factors as covariates to analyse the intervention’s effect, and addressed unequal group sample sizes between the intervention and nonintervention groups.

Ethical consideration

The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (AMA, 2013). This study protocol was approved by the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) (NHRC, Ref. #2688). Before taking part in the study, parents and students provided written consent, and participation was entirely optional. Additionally, the participants ' confidentiality and the confidentiality of the information are safeguarded.

Results

Pre-test outcome measures between the two groups

The study indicated no significant differences (p < 0.05) in various sociodemographic characteristics between the intervention and the non-intervention groups, indicating overall baseline comparability. However, age, caste, living area, father’s occupation, and TV at home significantly differed (Table 2 ). Moreover, HL awareness (Table 3 ) and trust in health information sources (Table 4 ) were found to be minimal in both groups at pre-test.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate an HL intervention to improve adolescents’ HL awareness, situation, self-efficacy, and health-promoting actions. The results showed that the HL intervention positively influenced all the outcomes of the intervention groups, and there were no improvements in the non-intervention group.

HL awareness

Compared with the non-intervention groups, the intervention groups significantly increased HL awareness, including hearing, conceptualisation, benefits, and the effects of poor HL. However, there is limited evidence targeting interventions that focus on HL awareness for adolescents. HL awareness training with health professionals also improved their knowledge and ability to communicate with audiences who have low HL [49]. This finding aligns with the EU HL model [50], which suggests the need to be informed as a basic ability for critical thinking necessary for health promotion. Therefore, introducing HL as a discipline in schools could further advance this outcome.

HL situation

The study demonstrated that the intervention was significantly effective in improving the participants’ self-efficacy, HL, its four dimensions, and three health domains. However, the non-intervention group showed no improvement. Earlier studies similarly reported on adolescent total diabetes HL and self-efficacy [51], COVID-19 HL [52], and infectious disease HL [53]. Correspondingly, a problem-based learning program improved self-efficacy and HL in immigrant women in Taiwan [48]. Other studies significantly enhanced the ability of adolescents to access, understand, appraise, and apply health-related information in three health domains [54, 55]. In contrast, an educational intervention in the US did not improve AHL via the Newest Vital Sign, but there was an improvement in the e-HEALS outcome [56]. A leaflet-based health guide intervention in Brazil improved the HL scores of older people but did not significantly improve their general self-efficacy [57]. The divergent results may be due to the nature of the intervention, such as the approach, tools, setting, and contextual factors of the participants.

The intervention group’s knowledge of various health information sources increased compared with that of the non-intervention group. This finding supports the EU HL model’s access dimension, which refers to an individual’s ability to find and obtain health information [50]. Similar intervention studies also reported significantly enhanced access to health information [51, 58]. However, another study found no significant improvement, particularly among girls in Iran [59].

Consistent with our results, an educational video intervention significantly improved the participants’ ability to understand misinformation in WhatsApp messages [60]. However, the findings of some studies are different from those of our research. For instance, a problem-based learning intervention in Iran [58, 59] did not significantly improve understanding skills. The curriculum of Canada has a limited effect on students’ abilities to access, understand, communicate, and evaluate health information [61]. These divergences might arise from a lack of a theoretical framework, program follow-up, attention to empowerment, and demographic misbalance [51], as well as intervention variations such as contents, methods, materials, and contexts [62].

Similar studies have shown that educational interventions can enhance participants’ ability to appraise health information, with some studies, such as studies from Iran [51, 58], a pilot study in Germany [63], an active learning approach in the U.S [64], and a systematic review [65] showing positive short-term effects. However, few studies, such as studies of Tehran [58], Iran [59], Canada [61], and Germany [54], have shown no positive effect. Our study supports the appraisal dimension of the EU HL model, which considers interpreting, filtering, judging, and evaluating health information critically [50].

Following our findings, studies have shown that adolescents’ application of health information has significantly improved [58] in terms of oral health [66], type-1 diabetes [51], health behaviours [67], and health-related knowledge [54]. However, some studies have reported limited effects of educational intervention and school curricula on students’ health information use [59, 61]. This may be due to repetitive courses routinely delivered by teachers and passive student participation [61]. Unfriendly health materials and low health promotion links decrease engagement with health information [68]. These findings align with the EU model [50], emphasising the ability to make health decisions as a high-level HL outcome.

The intervention significantly improved HL levels in all health domains [54], including healthcare usage [69]. Adequate HL in health care leads to increased healthcare practices [70]. However, the next study partially supports low HL as a factor in increased healthcare use [71]. Intervention effects varied, including emergency care use [72] and perceived confidence in communicating with healthcare providers [73]. Similar to our findings on disease prevention, another study revealed that public health emergency courses on mental health and depression [74] and video-assisted health education on infectious diseases [53] among Chinese adolescents had a positive effect. However, an Italian study slightly improved adolescents’ knowledge of HIV [75], possibly because of a limited sample size and short duration.

We revealed limited intervention studies on health promotion domain, resulting in limited comparative data. The EU model [50] addresses health promotion concepts. Despite diverse HL models, not all address the health promotion concept [76]. From this perspective, HL is identified as a determinant of health promotion [77].

Health-promoting actions

The intervention significantly improved school adolescents’ intention to take health-promoting actions and its five constructs, including nutrition, health-responsible, life appreciation, social support, and stress management behaviour. The intervention did not impact exercise behaviour, as it did not specifically include an exercise behaviour session. Similar results were found in training interventions for high school girls in Iran [77], web-based health education in Turkey (excluding stress management) [78], structured health promotion education (excluding social support and exercise behaviours) [79], and the HL and health promotion interventions for female students (excluding stress management) [80]. HL interventions contribute to changes in healthy lifestyles [81].

Intervention studies include all aspects of HPAs [82], such as responsible behaviour, which are limited [83]. A systematic review revealed significant gains in nutritional behaviour in the post-test, but some studies reported no differences between the intervention and control groups [84]. These findings align with the EU model, highlighting HPAs as an essential outcome of HL [50].

As expected, the interactive method effectively improved HL and HPAs, indicating its importance in AHL education. The HLS-Child-24-Nep scale covers these dimensions and domains [24]. The intervention addresses skills that change HL, supporting HL as an outcome of school learning [85], health education [38]. The study reflects the urgency of integrating HL into school education, including teacher training and relevant methods for enhancing the students’ abilities to process health information. It suggests the need to prioritise HL in both educational and health policies in Nepal, including conducting a national HL survey to assess the situation across the country. Furthermore, this study can support the initiation of a health literate school program at the national, provincial, and local levels.

Limitations

In addition to its many strengths, this study has several limitations. First, quasi-experimentation without randomisation and baseline differences observed between the intervention and control groups, causing the risk of selection bias. Although we attempted to address this by adopting GEE adjustment, residual bias remains uncontrolled. Second, this study only recruited adolescent students from a single grade in community schools of a single municipality; the conclusions may have limited generalisation to other grades and contexts. Third, lack of validation of outcome measures, including HL awareness, and incomplete reporting of some psychometric properties of the HLS-Child-24-Nep, may have limited the measurements’ overall validity and reliability. Fourth, the reliance on immediate post-test data might have limited the conclusions on sustained behaviour change or long-term impact. Lastly, multiple statistical comparisons were performed without adjustment for Type I error, and the absence of sample size effect and power might have limited the practical significance of the findings.

Conclusion

This study offers evidence that adolescent students who received a contextual and theory-based HL intervention demonstrated significant improvements in HL awareness and situation, which may foster their intention for health-promoting actions. However, there is no significant improvement in exercise behaviour. The findings support the EU HL model, but further studies are needed to confirm the applicability of this model in other similar settings. The findings further suggest longitudinal studies in a large and diverse sample to explore the applicability of the intervention.
