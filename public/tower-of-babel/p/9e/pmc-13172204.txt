Comprehensive profiling of RPP40 across human cancers reveals its essential role and multidimensional clinical correlates
Authors: Yu-Nan Man, Mao-Lin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRibonuclease P/MRP subunit p40 (RPP40) is a core component of the RNase P/MRP ribonucleoprotein complex, playing a central role in RNA processing. However, its systematic characteristics, clinical significance, and functional mechanisms at the pan-cancer level remain unclear.MethodsWe systematically characterized the pan-cancer expression profile and prognostic value of RPP40 using multi-omics approaches, including pan-cancer expression profiling, CRISPR screening, single-cell RNA sequencing, immunohistochemistry, proteomics, and multi-level survival analyses. We further examined correlations between RPP40 expression and diverse clinical indicators, including tumor stage, treatment response, immune subtype, and demographic characteristics.ResultsRPP40 expression was significantly upregulated in most cancers and their subtypes, and it was identified as a broadly essential gene for cancer cell survival. High RPP40 expression was significantly associated with advanced tumor stage and poor prognosis, serving as an independent risk factor for overall survival and progression-free survival across multiple independent cohorts. Our multidimensional analytical framework further revealed significant associations between RPP40 expression and various features, including pathological stage, major tumor subtypes, and smoking history. Functionally, RPP40 likely promotes tumor proliferation by activating cell cycle pathways, and its expression displays strong cell cycle dependency.ConclusionThis study systematically elucidates the oncogenic role of RPP40 across cancers, establishing it as a novel and translationally promising pan-cancer prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target. The “multi-level clinical–molecular integrative analysis” framework developed herein provides foundational evidence and data support for subsequent precision therapeutic strategies targeting RPP40.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04919-z.

Introduction

Tumors exhibit substantial heterogeneity, with pronounced variation in molecular drivers and clinical outcomes not only across cancer types but also among subtypes within the same malignancy [1–4]. Although single-cancer studies have advanced our understanding, pan-cancer analyses remain relatively limited, hindering the identification of core mechanisms and core tissue biomarkers with broad relevance [5, 6]. Systematic comparisons across multiple malignancies are therefore crucial for uncovering universal oncogenic principles [7]. Large-scale pan-cancer analyses can reveal cross-tissue molecular patterns and their associations with patient prognosis, offering new insights for precision oncology [8, 9].

Recent research has highlighted the extensive roles of fundamental cellular processes, such as RNA processing and ribosome biogenesis, in tumorigenesis and cancer progression [10, 11]. Ribonucleoprotein complexes such as RNase P/MRP and their key protein subunits regulate the maturation of tRNA and rRNA precursors, influencing translation initiation, stress responses, and cell cycle progression, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation and survival. Aberrant expression or mutation of RNA-processing proteins has been show to alter tumor biology and shape the immune microenvironment, underscoring their importance in pan-cancer research [12, 13].

Recent advances in multi-omics and machine learning approaches have enabled systematic dissection of tumor heterogeneity and the identification of novel prognostic biomarkers across multiple cancer types [14]. For instance, integrative analyses have revealed Treg-associated programmed cell death features in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [15], deciphered genetic crosstalk between atherosclerosis and lung adenocarcinoma [16], and established prognostic models based on mitotic catastrophe heterogeneity in bladder cancer [17] and ADME gene signatures [18]. These studies exemplify the power of multi-omics frameworks in advancing precision oncology and personalized treatment strategies.

Ribonuclease P/MRP subunit p40 (RPP40), a core component of the RNase P/MRP complex, plays a crucial role in maintaining RNA processing homeostasis [19, 20]. RPP40 primarily functions as an oncogene in several solid tumors, including liver, endometrial, and breast cancers. Elevated RPP40 expression is generally associated with poor prognosis, and its tumor-promoting mechanisms involve enhancing ribosome biogenesis and modulating the tumor microenvironment [19, 21–23]. However, many major knowledge gaps persist. First, the systematic expression landscape of RPP40 across cancers, its genetic alterations, and its association with patient prognosis have not been comprehensively characterized. Second, the integration of RPP40 molecular features with clinicopathological indicators (e.g., TNM stage, histological grade, and molecular subtype), treatment response, and patient demographics remains poorly defined. These gaps hinder the evaluation of the translational potential of RPP40 as a pan-cancer prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Recent studies leveraging single-cell technologies have further highlighted the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment. In lung adenocarcinoma, integrative single-cell genomics has revealed dynamic immune evolution and identified prognostic signatures associated with disease progression [24]. Machine learning approaches have also linked EGFR mutation status to distinct immune subtypes [25], while metabolic reprogramming has been shown to influence immunotherapy efficacy through mechanisms such as SLC25A1-mediated immune resistance [26]. These findings underscore the critical role of TIME heterogeneity in cancer progression and treatment response.

To address these gaps, we employed integrated multi-cohort analyses and computational biology approaches to construct a multidimensional pan-cancer atlas of RPP40. We analyzed its expression pattern and functional pathways across diverse cancers and, more importantly, investigated its associations with multidimensional clinical indicators using robust multivariate statistical methods. Our findings provide a comprehensive evaluation of the translational potential of RPP40 as a pan-cancer biomarker and therapeutic target, and also establish a foundation for future broad-spectrum diagnostic and therapeutic strategies centered on RNA-processing mechanisms.

Methods

Data acquisition and expression profiling

Data were obtained from multiple publicly accessible cancer genomics resources, including the National Cancer Institute’s Genomic Data Commons (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas), the University of California, Santa Cruz’s XENA platform, datasets from MD Anderson Cancer Center, and the Pan-Cancer Atlas consortium [27]. Transcripts per million (TPM)-normalized gene expression data from TCGA (dataset: tcga_RSEM_gene_tpm) were retrieved from the Xena platform and processed through standardized integration, quality control, and normalization procedures [28].

Clinical and expression data were matched using unique TCGA patient identifiers. Samples lacking clinical annotations or gene expression data, as well as normal or adjacent normal tissues, were excluded. Gene symbols were standardized to the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee identifiers. To minimize cross-cancer biases, Z-scores were computed within each cancer type using the formula (x − µ) / σ, where x is the TPM value of the gene in a given sample, µ is the mean TPM value of that gene within that cancer type, and σ is the corresponding standard deviation. To reduce the influence of extreme values, samples with Z-scores < − 3 or > 3 were removed. For statistical robustness, only cancer types with at least three samples after quality control were retained for downstream analyses. Finally, all samples lacking valid RPP40 expression measurements were excluded.

Assessment of gene essentiality and single-cell expression profiling

Gene essentiality was evaluated using CRISPR–Cas9 knockout screening data from DepMap, with gene dependency quantified using Chronos scores [29]. Scores < 0 indicate that RPP40 knockout may impair cell survival, while scores < − 1 indicate that the gene is essential for maintaining tumor cell viability. To examine expression at single-cell resolution, we obtained pan-cancer single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from TISCH2 [30]. Analyses included generating cross-cancer gene expression heatmaps using the pheatmap R package, performing hierarchical clustering with Euclidean distance and Ward’s minimum variance method to identify conserved cell-type-specific expression patterns, and applying Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for dimensionality reduction to visualize high-dimensional transcriptomic data [31].

Immunohistochemical analysis of RPP40 protein expression

To characterize RPP40 expression at the protein level, we analyzed data from the Human Protein Atlas database, which provides standardized immunohistochemistry (IHC) slides and scoring across multiple tumor types (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [32]. IHC results for all available tumor types were extracted, and staining intensity distributions were quantified across four levels (negative, weak, moderate, and strong) based on the established criteria. To visually illustrate protein expression patterns, tumor types with high RPP40 expression (moderate or strong staining) were highlighted, and representative pathological slide images were downloaded. Results are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Multi-level sample grouping system

To facilitate cross-cancer and subtype-specific comparisons, we established a systematic grouping framework based on the anatomical origin of tumors. Samples were categorized into six major groups: digestive system cancers (e.g., ESCA, STAD, COAD, LIHC, and PAAD), urinary system cancers (e.g., BLCA, KIRC, KIRP, and KICH), female reproductive system cancers (e.g., BRCA, OV, UCEC, and CESC), male reproductive system cancers (PRAD and TGCT), respiratory system cancers (LUAD and LUSC), and nervous system cancers (GBM and LGG). To further delineate intra-tumor heterogeneity, detailed subtype stratification was performed for cancers with established clinicopathological subclasses. For example, breast cancer was subdivided into invasive ductal carcinoma, invasive lobular carcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancer. Colon adenocarcinoma subgroups included right-sided, left-sided, and mucinous adenocarcinoma, while stomach adenocarcinoma was stratified into diffuse-type and signet-ring cell carcinoma. Additional subtypes such as esophageal adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and seminoma were also included. Full classifications and abbreviations are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Extraction of pathological and clinical variables

Comprehensive clinical information was curated, including tumor stage, pathological invasion characteristics, molecular biomarkers, treatment history and efficacy, and patient demographic factors. Based on American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria, cases with complete T, N, and M staging were included to assess anatomical tumor burden. Indicators of invasiveness, namely lymph node invasion, lymphovascular invasion, and venous invasion, were also extracted. Treatment-related variables included new tumor events after initial treatment, history of neoadjuvant therapy, and first-line treatment responses categorized according to RECIST criteria (complete response, partial response, stable disease, or progressive disease). Key tumor biological features included post-treatment residual tumor status (R0: complete resection, R1: microscopic residual, and R2: macroscopic residual) and microsatellite instability (MSI-H, MSI-L, and MSS). The tumor microenvironment was classified using transcriptomic immunophenotyping (C1–C6 subtypes) [33]. In addition, demographic and lifestyle factors, such as sex, smoking history, alcohol use, survival status, tumor status, and contraceptive use, were collected to construct a multidimensional clinical–molecular association matrix. Coverage of variables across cancer types is summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Collation of prognostic information

To systematically evaluate the prognostic value of RPP40 across cancers, a multi-layered survival analysis strategy was implemented. Survival data from TCGA, GEO, ICGC, and ArrayExpress were integrated. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed using overall survival (OS) and clinicopathological variables to preliminarily assess prognostic associations. To strengthen generalizability, analyses were extended to multiple survival endpoints, including disease-free survival (DFS), progression-free survival (PFS), relapse-free survival (RFS), distant metastasis-free interval (DFI), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS). To quantitatively integrate effect sizes across datasets, we performed a meta-analysis using a random-effects model, which provides a conservative estimate that accounts for between-study heterogeneity. Prior to pooling, heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. Significant heterogeneity was defined as I² > 50% or Q test p-value < 0.1, in which case hazard ratios (HRs) were combined using the random-effects model. This analytic framework yields more stable cross-endpoint estimates and strengthens the evidence supporting whether RPP40 functions as a consistent prognostic factor across cancers.

Functional state correlation and proteomic analysis

Protein expression data from the Cancer Proteome Atlas (http://www.tcpaportal.org) were used to stratify into high- and low-expression groups based on median RPP40 protein levels. Differences in pathway activity were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package [34, 35]. To investigate the cell cycle-dependent expression pattern of RPP40 at the single-cell level, the GSE146773 dataset, comprising 1,152 U2OS FUCCI cells sorted by FACS and profiled using SMART-seq2, was analyzed. Cell cycle phase was determined from fluorescence intensity of canonical markers, and normalized gene expression levels were plotted along pseudotime trajectories [36]. To evaluate functional associations, the CancerSEA database was used to calculate activity scores for 14 oncogenic phenotypes via gene set variation analysis, and correlations between RPP40 expression and functional states were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis [37].

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R v4.4.1. Group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed or ordinal variables, chi-square test for categorical variables, and one-way ANOVA (or non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test) for multi-group comparisons. Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed variables, and Spearman’s rank correlation was employed for non-normal distributions. Survival analysis utilized Kaplan–Meier estimation with log-rank testing, along with univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. Meta-analysis was performed to integrate prognostic effects across datasets. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I² metric: if I² > 50% or Q test p-value < 0.05, random-effects models were applied; otherwise, fixed-effects models were used [38]. Multiple testing corrections (e.g., pan-cancer expression correlations) were implemented using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05 or false discovery rate < 0.05, as indicated in figure legends.

Results

RPP40 is a pan-cancer essential gene for cancer cell survival and specifically highly expressed in malignant cells

To systematically evaluate the essentiality of RPP40, we integrated and analyzed CRISPR–Cas9 knockout screening data from DepMap. Visualization of the Chronos dependency scores revealed values < 0 across all examined cancer types and < − 1 in most, with fibrosarcoma exhibiting the highest score and clear cell sarcoma the lowest (Fig. 1A and Online Appendix Table S4). These findings identify RPP40 as a core essential gene required for cancer cell survival across a broad spectrum of malignancies.

Immunohistochemical images demonstrating high RPP40 protein expression were obtained from Human Protein Atlas and covered eight cancer types (Fig. 1B), confirming its widespread protein-level expression in tumor tissues.

To further define its expression at single-cell resolution, we analyzed pan-cancer single-cell RNA sequencing data from the TISCH database. RPP40 expression was predominantly enriched in malignant cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Quantitative analyses demonstrated a significant positive correlation between average expression levels of RPP40 and proportion of malignant cells, and a negative correlation was observed with the proportion of CD8 + T cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B). These results suggest that within the tumor microenvironment, higher malignant cell content is associated with stronger RPP40 expression.

RPP40 expression is broadly upregulated across cancers and pathological subtypes

This study included 33 cancer types and 27 pathological subtypes from TCGA, encompassing 11,160 patients (Supplementary Table S2). Samples containing valid RPP40 expression data were retained for downstream analysis.

Systematic evaluation of RPP40 expression across anatomical tumor systems showed widespread upregulation in tumors of the female and male reproductive systems, nervous system, and respiratory system. Within the urinary and digestive systems, RPP40 expression was also elevated in most cancer types except chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (KICH) and cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) (Fig. 2A).

Meta-analysis of expression differences across cancer types revealed significantly higher RPP40 levels in tumors than in normal tissues (standardized mean difference = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.61–1.51). Due to substantial heterogeneity among studies (I² = 95.4%), a random-effects model was applied for pooling (Fig. 2B). Sensitivity analysis confirmed robust pooled results, and Egger’s test indicated no publication bias (p = 0.71) (Fig. 2C, D).

After establishing the cross-cancer upregulation trend, we examined expression across pathological subtypes. Visualization results showed that RPP40 expression was significantly upregulated in three breast cancer subtypes (BRCA-IDC, BRCA-ILC, and BRCA-TNBC), three colon adenocarcinoma subtypes (COAD-LCC, COAD-MAC, and COAD-RCC), two esophageal carcinoma subtypes (ESCA-EAC and ESCA-ESCC), two gastric adenocarcinoma subtypes (STAD_DGA and STAD_SRCC), and two endometrial carcinoma subtypes (UCEC_EEA and UCEC_SEA) (Fig. 2E).

Meta-analysis focusing on pathological subtypes also showed significantly higher RPP40 expression in tumors (standardized mean difference = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.52–1.48) (Fig. 2F). Sensitivity analysis supported the stability of the pooled results (Supplementary Fig. S1C), and Egger’s test indicated no publication bias (p = 0.27) (Fig. 2G). These findings collectively identify RPP40 as a novel and universal cancer-associated gene.

Clinical significance of high RPP40 expression: multidimensional analysis of tumor progression, treatment response, and patient characteristics

Analysis based on AJCC staging revealed that high RPP40 expression was significantly associated with advanced tumor progression. Patients with high expression showed increased frequencies of T3/T4 stage (46% vs. 28%, p < 2.2e − 16), N2/N3 stage (24% vs. 18%, p = 0.028), and M1 disease (5%, approximately 2.5 times higher than in the low-expression group, p = 0.003). The overall proportion of stage III/IV disease was also higher (57% vs. 49%, p = 5.26e − 06). These results suggest that high RPP40 expression correlates with increased risks of local invasion, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis.

Pathological grading analyses supported these trends: higher frequencies of T4 stage (14% vs. 7%) and N2/N3 stage (18% vs. 12%) were observed in the high-expression group. Further, the proportion of stage III/IV was significantly higher in the high-expression group (41%) than in the low-expression group (33%). These findings indicate that high RPP40 expression is significantly associated with increased local invasion and lymph node metastasis. However, in the large-sample analysis of M stage, no significant difference was observed in the proportion of patients with distant metastasis (M1) between the groups.

In the analyses of lymphatic invasion (n = 677), venous invasion (n = 625), and lymphovascular invasion (n = 766), the proportions of patients with invasion were comparable between the high- and low-expression groups, with chi-square p-values of 0.336, 0.773, and 0.343, respectively, none of which reached the significance threshold. These results suggest no clear association between high RPP40 expression and lymphatic or vascular invasion. Analysis of treatment-related events showed that the incidence of new tumor events was slightly higher in the high-expression group (29% vs. 25%). Among 10,301 patients, no significant association was found between RPP40 expression and receipt of neoadjuvant therapy (χ² = 0.657, p = 0.418), with both groups exhibiting low rates of neoadjuvant treatment (1% in the low-expression group vs. 2% in the high-expression group). Based on first-line treatment outcomes from 5,096 TCGA patients, the stable disease rate was significantly lower in the high-expression group (4% vs. 10%), while the proportions of progressive disease and partial response were similar between the groups.

Analysis of surgical resection outcomes indicated that the high-expression group had a higher R0 resection rate (88% vs. 83%), a lower R1 residual rate (7% vs. 15%), but a slightly higher R2 residual rate (5% vs. 2%), suggesting that high RPP40 expression is associated with a greater likelihood of complete resection. Regarding microsatellite instability, analysis of 1,825 patients revealed no significant difference between the groups (χ² = 3.623, p = 0.163), indicating no clear association between RPP40 expression and MSI status. Immunophenotyping analysis showed that the high-expression group had a higher proportion of the C2 subtype (41% vs. 16%), whereas the low-expression group had a higher proportion of the C3 subtype (45% vs. 8%), suggesting that high RPP40 expression is associated with an immune-activated tumor microenvironment (Fig. 3).

Association between RPP40 expression and variety of clinical variables

Finally, demographic analysis revealed that high RPP40 expression was significantly associated with higher all-cause mortality (34% vs. 25%, p < 2.2e − 16), higher proportion of female patients (53% vs. 49%, p = 3.80e − 05), higher rate of current smoking (28% vs. 20%, p = 5.62e − 12), and higher prevalence of detectable tumor at baseline (36% vs. 30%, p = 1.60e − 08). Within the female subgroup, high expression was negatively associated with current contraceptive use (2% vs. 7%, p = 0.038) and showed no significant association with alcohol consumption history (p = 0.716) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Prognostic value assessment and multi-endpoint validation of RPP40 in pan-cancer

To systematically evaluate the prognostic value of RPP40, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses across multiple public datasets. High RPP40 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in ACC (TCGA, multivariate HR = 2.284, 95% CI: 1.373–3.800), BRCA (TCGA, multivariate HR = 1.286, 95% CI: 1.023–1.616), MESO (TCGA, multivariate HR = 1.736, 95% CI: 1.154–2.613), OV (ICGC_OV_AU, multivariate HR = 1.333, 95% CI: 1.043–1.703), HNSC (GSE65858, multivariate HR = 1.246, 95% CI: 1.107–1.401), and LUSC (GSE29013, multivariate HR = 2.710, 95% CI: 1.348–5.447). In KIRC, this trend was confirmed in two independent cohorts: TCGA (univariate HR = 1.921, 95% CI: 1.510–2.443) and GSE167573 (univariate HR = 2.940, 95% CI: 1.177–7.340).

Notably, heterogeneity was observed in SKCM and LUAD. In SKCM, high expression was associated with increased risk in TCGA cohort (multivariate HR = 1.291, 95% CI: 1.034–1.611) but indicated a protective effect in the GSE99898 cohort (multivariate HR = 0.610, 95% CI: 0.444–0.839). Similarly, in LUAD, the GSE13213 cohort suggested a protective role (multivariate HR = 0.613, 95% CI: 0.455–0.827), whereas high expression predicted worse outcomes in the GSE29016 (multivariate HR = 1.935, 95% CI: 1.208–3.100), GSE41271 (multivariate HR = 1.303, 95% CI: 1.026–1.655), and GSE72094 (multivariate HR = 1.246, 95% CI: 1.039–1.495) cohorts (Fig. 4; Online Appendix Table S5).

To comprehensively assess the prognostic significance of RPP40, we further analyzed multiple clinical endpoints, including DFS, PFS, RFS, DFI, PFI, and DSS. Meta-analysis using a random-effects model showed that high RPP40 expression consistently conferred increased risk across various cancers, such as ACC, BRCA, SARC, LUAD, MESO, and KIRC, reinforcing its role as a broad unfavorable prognostic marker. Integrating results from univariate/multivariate Cox regression analyses and multi-endpoint meta-analysis, high RPP40 expression was generally and significantly associated with adverse outcomes (including OS, DFS, and PFS) across cancer types and independent cohorts. Although heterogeneity was observed in some SKCM and LUAD cohorts, the overall trend supports RPP40 as a candidate pan-cancer prognostic biomarker (Fig. 5).

Multi-omics evidence that RPP40 promotes tumor proliferation via cell cycle regulation

Using data from The Cancer Proteome Atlas, we analyzed pathway activities associated with differing RPP40 expression levels. In most cancer types, cell cycle pathway activity was significantly higher in the high-expression group than in the low-expression group (Fig. 6A). Gene set enrichment analysis further indicated that cell cycle-related pathways were the most significantly activated functional modules under high RPP40 expression (Fig. 6B). Analysis using the CancerSEA database showed that, with the exception of LIHC, RPP40 expression was significantly correlated with cell cycle activity score across all examined cancer types (Fig. 6C). In the GSE146773 single-cell dataset, comprising 1,152 U2OS FUCCI cells, RPP40 expression displayed dynamic fluctuations across distinct cell cycle phases (Fig. 6D). Collectively, these multi-omics findings indicate that RPP40 promotes tumor proliferation by driving cell cycle progression, representing a central mechanism underlying its oncogenic activity.

Discussion

Through systematic integration of multi-omics data and clinical information, this study constructs, for the first time, a comprehensive pan-cancer molecular atlas of RPP40, providing an in-depth evaluation of its prognostic value, clinical correlations, and functional mechanisms. While most previous pan-cancer analyses have largely focused on expression differences between tumor and normal tissues, often without addressing intra-cancer heterogeneity and its relationship with clinical subtypes [39, 40], our work establishes a multi-level clinical–molecular correlation framework that extends beyond conventional comparisons. Our findings not only confirm that RPP40 is an essential gene broadly upregulated across cancers and required for cancer cell survival but also precisely situate its aberrant expression within specific clinical contexts, including pathological subtypes, therapeutic responses, and patient characteristics. Our data offer key evidence and a clear direction for translating RPP40 from a potential biomarker into a clinically actionable tool.

We first confirmed the widespread oncogenic relevance of RPP40 at multiple levels. CRISPR–Cas9 essentiality screening data revealed that RPP40 is a pan-cancer essential gene to maintain the viability of diverse cancer cell types, consistent with the increased demand for RNA-processing complexes in rapidly proliferating cells [41]. At the expression level, both transcriptomic and proteomic analyses demonstrated significant upregulation of RPP40 expression across various cancers, including major pathological subtypes spanning the digestive, respiratory, and reproductive systems. This conserved, cross-cancer activation suggests that its tumor-promoting mechanism in highly universal and rooted in core proliferative processes. Subsequent survival analyses across multiple datasets showed that high RPP40 expression is an independent predictor of poor prognosis in cancers such as ACC, BRCA, KIRC, and MESO. Importantly, by incorporating multiple clinical endpoints, such as OS, PFS, and RFS, into a meta-analysis, we obtained robust evidence reinforcing RPP40 as a reliable risk marker. Although some degree of heterogeneity was observed across cohorts, the overall prognostic signal of RPP40 was strong and consistent.

A major advancement of this study is its expansion beyond conventional pan-cancer approaches that solely focus on differential expression or single-outcome associations. We developed an integrated analytical paradigm bridging anatomical systems, pathological subtypes, and diverse clinical phenotypes. RPP40 expression showed strong correlations with TNM progression, particularly T and N stage, as well as treatment response (e.g., stable disease rates following first-line therapy), surgical resection status, immune microenvironment subtypes, and demographic features (e.g., smoking history and sex). For example, high RPP40 expression was enriched in the C2 immune-activated tumor microenvironment subtype, indicating potential indirect effects on anti-tumor immunity and raising the possibility that RPP40 can influence or predict response to immunotherapy. Similarly, the positive correlation between RPP40 expression and smoking behavior implies that environmental exposures modulate fundamental RNA-processing genes involved in carcinogenesis. These multidimensional “molecule–clinic–population” associations situate RPP40 within a clinically meaningful context.

At the mechanistic level, convergent multi-omics evidence indicates that promotion of cell cycle progression is the central oncogenic function of RPP40. Proteomic analysis demonstrated that high RPP40 expression is strongly associated with activation of cell cycle pathways; cross-cancer correlation analyses revealed robust positive associations between RPP40 expression and cell cycle activity scores; and single-cell sequencing data revealed dynamic fluctuations in RPP40 expression across distinct cell cycle phases. Altogether, these results support a mechanistic model in which RPP40 accelerates tumor proliferation by promoting cell cycle progression. As a core component of the RNase P/MRP complex, RPP40 likely facilitates efficient processing of tRNA and rRNA precursors, sustaining high-level protein synthesis required for rapid production of key cell cycle regulators (e.g., cyclins and kinases) [42]. This mechanistic role explains why RPP40 expression is consistently upregulated across proliferative cancer subtypes [20, 43].

Furthermore, we report RPP40 as a novel, pan-cancer oncogene and prognostic biomarker. The multi-level clinical integration strategy applied here not only enhances the translational relevance of our findings but also provides a roadmap for future research. First, we believe that RPP40 can serve as a complementary molecular marker for risk stratification across multiple cancers. Second, its essentiality and strong association with cell cycle regulation highlight its potential as a broad-spectrum anti-cancer therapeutic target, specifically in refractory tumors dependent on fundamental RNA-processing pathways. Third, its association with specific immune microenvironment subtypes suggests potential utility in predicting immunotherapy response. The observed association between RPP40 and immune subtypes prompted us to explore the underlying mechanisms. Our finding that high RPP40 expression is significantly enriched in the C2 immune-activated subtype suggests RPP40 may reshape the tumor immune landscape through multiple pathways. First, RPP40 may indirectly shape immune infiltration by promoting tumor cell proliferation. As a core subunit of the RNase P/MRP complex, RPP40 drives cell cycle progression via rRNA/tRNA processing, and the resulting hyperproliferative state can release tumor antigens and DAMPs that recruit and activate antigen-presenting cells and T cells. Second, RPP40 may directly modulate tumor-immune crosstalk. Studies in endometrial carcinoma show RPP40 correlates with infiltration of CD8 + T cells, Tregs, and DCs, suggesting regulation of chemokines or adhesion molecules. Notably, the negative correlation between RPP40 and CD8 + T cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B) may reflect immune evasion within an otherwise activated microenvironment. This heterogeneous expression of RPP40 across malignant cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A), coupled with its cell cycle-dependent dynamics (Fig. 6D), may generate functionally distinct subpopulations that differentially interact with the immune microenvironment and contribute to therapy resistance. Additionally, RPP40 may influence immune-related gene expression via RNA processing. By coordinating ribosomal gene expression, RPP40 can affect translation efficiency of specific mRNAs, potentially impacting immune checkpoint molecules or antigen presentation and altering tumor susceptibility to immune attack.

Beyond its roles in cell cycle-driven proliferation and immune modulation, RPP40 may also contribute to tumor progression through metabolic reprogramming, particularly by supporting nucleotide synthesis to meet the heightened demands of accelerated RNA production. As a core component of the RNase P/MRP complex responsible for tRNA and rRNA processing, RPP40 is intrinsically linked to nucleotide utilization and synthesis. Rapidly proliferating tumor cells exhibit an increased demand for nucleotides to support sustained RNA synthesis and ribosome biogenesis [44]. The elevated expression of RPP40 may therefore not only reflect proliferative activity but also cooperate with nucleotide synthesis pathways to fuel tumor progression. This metabolic perspective provides an additional dimension to understanding RPP40’s oncogenic mechanisms and suggests that combining RPP40-targeted therapies with nucleotide metabolism inhibitors could represent a novel therapeutic strategy.

Limitations

Our study is primarily based on retrospective analyses of public databases, prospective clinical validation and experimental studies are needed to substantiate these conclusions. Future work should clarify the oncogenic mechanisms of RPP40 through in vitro and in vivo functional experiments and evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting this protein, ultimately supporting its advancement from a computationally discovered biomarker to a clinically actionable target.

Conclusion

We report that RPP40 is broadly overexpressed across cancers and essential for cancer cell survival. High RPP40 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis, tumor progression, and multiple clinical and demographic features. Mechanistically, RPP40 promotes tumor proliferation primarily by driving cell cycle progression. Through integrated multi-level clinical–molecular analyses, our results provide strong evidence and direction for the translational development of RPP40 as a pan-cancer prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target.
