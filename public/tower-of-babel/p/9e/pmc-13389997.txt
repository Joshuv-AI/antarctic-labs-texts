Intravenous vernakalant for acute termination of recent-onset atrial fibrillation in the emergency department
Authors: Karin Heller, Dana Viskin, Ophir Freund, Shir Frydman, Sharon Grinberg, Efrat Zandberg, Moaath Slieman, David Zeltser, Raphael Rosso, Sami Viskin, Ehud Chorin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly conversion of recent-onset atrial fibrillation (AF) is of clinical value. Vernakalant is an atrial-selective, rapid-acting antiarrhythmic drug. We report a large cohort of consecutive patients receiving vernakalant for the conversion of AF and review the clinical trials reporting on its safety and efficacy.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the effectiveness and safety of cardioversion of recent-onset AF with intravenous vernakalant in the emergency department.MethodsRetrospective observational study of adults presenting with recent-onset AF (≤48 hours) treated with intravenous vernakalant. Patients received either a simplified fixed-dose protocol (300 mg ± second dose of 200 mg) or a weight-adjusted protocol (3 mg/kg ± 2 mg/kg). The primary efficacy end point was conversion to sinus rhythm. Safety end points included all adverse events (AEs).ResultsAmong 328 consecutive patients treated with vernakalant (median age 61 years; 56% male), vernakalant restored sinus rhythm within minutes of administration in 268 patients (82%). First-dose conversion occurred in 68% (70% of the fixed-dose group vs 56% of the weight-adjusted-dose group; P = .015). Within a 48-hour time window, a shorter time from symptoms to treatment had a relatively low predictive ability (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.61) for predicting cardioversion. AEs occurred in 34 patients (10%) but were almost invariably mild; serious AEs occurred in only 1 patient (0.3%) but involved a fatality attributable to unrecognized severe cardiomyopathy.ConclusionIn an emergency department, intravenous vernakalant was highly effective for terminating recent-onset AF, rapidly converting 82% of AF episodes within minutes of drug administration.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Pharmacologic conversion, Arrhythmias, Vernakalant, Antiarrhythmic drugs

Introduction

Patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) carry a higher risk of cardiovascular complications during their first year of arrhythmia.1 Recent studies demonstrate that early conversion of AF followed by a sinus rhythm preservation strategy reduces cardiovascular complications.2, 3, 4 AF termination can be achieved via electrical or pharmacologic cardioversion.5 Although electrical cardioversion yields higher rates of sinus rhythm restoration, it requires sedation and exposes the patients to certain risks.6

Antiarrhythmic drugs used for the treatment of AF generally lack atrial selectivity and therefore may be associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias.7 Vernakalant is a rapid-acting, short-lasting antiarrhythmic drug that rapidly terminates AF. Its main mode of action is the blockade of the ultrarapid delayed rectifier potassium (Kv1.5) channel. Importantly, the ultrarapid delayed rectifier potassium channel is expressed primarily in the atrium, reducing concerns about ventricular arrhythmias appearing as proarrhythmic effects of the drug.8, 9, 10 Although its use is not permitted in the United States, vernakalant is approved for clinical use in Europe9 and Israel, where it is increasingly used.8 We report a large cohort of consecutive patients receiving vernakalant for the conversion of AF in an emergency department (ED), focusing on both efficacy and safety of this drug. We also review the randomized and nonrandomized clinical studies reporting on the safety and efficacy of this drug.

Methods

Vernakalant is approved for clinical use in Israel. This is a retrospective observational cohort study including all patients who presented with recent-onset AF to the ED of the Tel Aviv Medical Center between 2016 and 2024 and received intravenous vernakalant. The study was approved by the institutional review board. An informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective study design. The research reported adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki ethical guidelines.

As a routine practice in our ED, patients arriving with recent-onset AF (patients who were clinically deemed to have had AF for less than 48 hours from the onset of symptoms were included) are offered synchronized electrical cardioversion or pharmaceutical cardioversion based on the treating physician’s preference. The drug of choice in our medical facility is vernakalant. To be eligible for this treatment, patients must be hemodynamically stable (including a systolic blood pressure of ≥100 mm Hg) and have no history or clinical evidence of clinically significant heart disease, heart failure, or mitral or aortic stenosis. An on-site echocardiogram was not mandatory, but all previous echocardiographic examinations were reviewed. Contraindications to vernakalant therapy included (1) significant organic heart disease, including a left ventricular ejection fraction of <50%, or evidence of significant valvulopathy; (2) receiving oral antiarrhythmic drugs regularly or within 6 hours of presentation (not including rate-control medication) (although the formal contraindication to vernakalant involves treatment with a different intravenous antiarrhythmic drug within 6 hours) and receiving amiodarone chronically or in response to the new event of AF; and (3) a prolonged QT, defined as a corrected QT interval of >450 ms.

Between 2016 and 2022, vernakalant was given in our ED using a simplified protocol of vernakalant administration consisting of a fixed dose of 300 mg, infused intravenously over 10 minutes; after an observation period of 15 minutes, those who remained in AF received a second fixed dose of 200 mg infused over 10 minutes. In 2022, after realizing that this simplified protocol would result in doses exceeding the standard recommended dosages for patients weighing less than 100 kg, we modified the mode of administration to a standard weight-adjusted protocol. This change in practice was not driven by adverse events (AEs), given that none of the patients receiving the simplified protocol experienced any serious AEs (see below). In the weight-adjusted protocol initiated in 2022, the dose of intravenous vernakalant was 3 mg/kg (up to 300 mg) infused over 10 minutes. Patients who did not convert to sinus rhythm within 15 minutes of the first dose received a second dose of 2 mg/kg over 10 minutes. During the intravenous infusions in both protocols, noninvasive blood pressure measurement was repeated every 5 minutes. A decrease in systolic blood pressure to <100 mm Hg mandated discontinuation of the vernakalant infusion. Patients who started the infusion while having a baseline systolic blood pressure of 100–110 mm Hg received a concomitant intravenous infusion of normal saline (1 L in 1 hour) to prevent hypotension. All patients remained under observation for an additional 2-hour mandatory period of bed rest to avoid postural hypotension (a known AE of vernakalant) with continuous heart rhythm and periodic blood pressure monitoring. At this point, patients who converted to sinus rhythm were discharged home, and those who remained in AF were offered direct current (DC) cardioversion.

Data collection and outcomes

All cases treated with vernakalant were entered into a dedicated database, and additional data were collected from electronic records. We extracted baseline characteristics (demographic data, comorbidities, and medications), clinical variables (AF duration, treatment, and vital signs), and outcomes as specified below.

The primary efficacy outcome was conversion to sinus rhythm in the ED after vernakalant. Secondary outcomes were conversion to sinus rhythm after the first dose of vernakalant, conversion after the second dose, and successful DC cardioversion for those with vernakalant failure. The primary safety outcome was the occurrence of a severe AE after vernakalant treatment, defined as either significant hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 or the use of vasopressors), ventricular tachyarrhythmias, symptomatic bradycardia at <30 beats/min, sinus pause of >3 seconds, or death during the ED stay.11, 12, 13, 14 Secondary safety outcomes were any other AEs related to the treatment.

Data analysis

For analysis, our cohort was divided into 2 groups based on the rate of vernakalant infusion (simplified protocol or weight-adjusted dose). Vernakalant doses were also analyzed on a mg/kg basis. Baseline characteristics and study outcomes were compared between the 2 groups. Continuous variables were compared using t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, depending on whether the variables were normally distributed or not. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of successful cardioversion with vernakalant, including variables associated with this outcome in the univariate analysis. Analyses were performed in SPSS version 28.0, with P < .05 defined as significant.

Results

A total of 328 patients received intravenous vernakalant; all were included in our report: 214 (65%) received the simplified dose regimen, and 114 (35%) received the weight-adjusted regimen (Table 1). The overall median age was 61 years (interquartile range 48–72); 56% were males. For 126 patients, the index event was their first documented AF, whereas 202 patients (62%) reported previous events of AF. Among baseline characteristics, only a history of paroxysmal AF differed between the groups (higher in the simplified-protocol group [67% vs 51%; P < .01]). Although our protocol for vernakalant stipulated that the medication should be given to patients with symptoms lasting 48 hours or less, we identified 6 patients (1.28%) who received the medication despite reporting symptoms that lasted longer (3 with symptoms lasting 72 hours, 2 lasting 96 hours, and 1 lasting 120 hours). As expected, the total vernakalant dosage was higher in the simplified-protocol group for both the first dose (median 4.1 vs 3 mg/kg; P < .01) and the second dose (median 2.5 vs 2 mg/kg; P < .01).

Efficacy outcomes

Of the 328 patients presenting with recent-onset AF who were treated with intravenous vernakalant, 268 patients (82%) experienced conversion of AF to sinus rhythm within minutes of drug administration in the ED. The conversion rate with vernakalant was statistically similar between the groups (84% for the simplified-protocol group vs 78% for the weight-adjusted-dose group; P = .21) (Figure 1 and Table 1). AF termination during the first dose of vernakalant occurred in 213 patients (65%) and was more common with the simplified (fixed-dose) protocol (70% vs 56% for the weight-adjusted first dose; P = .015) (Figure 1). Subjects requiring a second dose had a similar rate of AF termination (48% vs 52%; P = .70) (Figure 1). Patients in the simplified-protocol group more often had a history of AF events. However, a history of AF was not associated with cardioversion by vernakalant (P = .55).

Among the 60 patients with vernakalant failure, DC cardioversion restored sinus rhythm in 50 patients (83%). The DC cardioversion rate was higher among those who received the simplified protocol (91% vs 72%; P = .046).

In univariate analysis (Table 2), a longer time from the onset of symptoms to presentation was associated with lower odds of AF termination after vernakalant (odds ratio [OR] 0.98; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.97–0.998; P = .047), whereas higher first vernakalant dose was associated with increased conversion rate (OR 1.61; 95% CI 1.11–2.35; P = .012). In multivariate analysis (Table 2, model 1), only a higher first dose of vernakalant was independently associated with successful cardioversion after vernakalant (adjusted OR 1.53; 95% CI 1.03–2.28; P < .04), meaning an additional 53% odds of conversion for every additional 1 mg/kg. A similar correlation was observed between the first dose and cardioversion after the first administration of vernakalant (Table 2). Considering the strong association between vernakalant dose and outcomes, we divided the fixed-dose group into those receiving higher or lower than 4 mg/kg of vernakalant (Table 3). Patients in the fixed-dose group who received <4 mg/kg had similar outcomes to the weight-adjusted-dose group, validating our results presented earlier. We did not find predictors for conversion to sinus after the second dose. Overall, weight (as a continuous variable) had no statistical correlation with the odds of conversion to sinus rhythm (OR 0.99; 95% CI 0.97–1.01; P = .243).

Given that the time from symptoms to treatment had a nonsignificant trend for successful cardioversion among patients treated within 48 hours of the onset of symptoms, we further assessed its predictive ability using the receiver operating characteristic curve analysis (Figure 2). Time to treatment had only a mild discriminative ability, with an area under the curve of 0.61 (95% CI 0.53–0.68). The 2 points with the highest Youden’s index (representing the optimal cutoff values for prediction) had either a low sensitivity (43%) or specificity (29%), signifying their low clinical utility to predict the cardioversion outcome (Figure 2). This demonstrates that vernakalant may be offered to patients presenting within 48 hours of the onset of symptoms, regardless of the actual duration.

Safety outcomes

34 patients (10%) experienced an AE after treatment; all but 1 of them were mild. Only 1 patient (0.3%) had a severe AE, but it was a lethal event. The fatality involved a 42-year-old female from the Philippines who presented with a 1-week history of abdominal pain and a 2-day history of palpitations. She denied previous cardiac or noncardiac illness. She presented with AF with a fast ventricular rate (reaching 206 beats/min upon presentation), but her electrocardiogram was otherwise normal and showed no evidence of pre-excitation. Her initial blood pressure was 127/97 mm Hg, and her oxygen saturation at room air was 98%. Before receiving vernakalant, she received 5 mg of intravenous verapamil to slow her ventricular rate. She received weight-adjusted vernakalant, but the infusion was discontinued after receiving half of the first dose because she complained of shortness of breath. She was transported to the computerized tomography, where pulmonary embolism was excluded. Upon returning to the ED, she developed bradycardia and hypotension (systolic blood pressure 70 mm Hg and then lower), and resuscitation efforts failed to prevent her death. Bedside echocardiography, first performed only during the resuscitation process, revealed a dilated left ventricle with severe systolic dysfunction and an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%. She was ultimately diagnosed as having severe thyrotoxic cardiomyopathy (unrecognized before her death) when blood tests drawn on presentation revealed thyroid-stimulating hormone levels of 0.008 mIU/L and total T3 levels of 354 ng/dL.

The remaining 33 AEs reported were mild, with the most common being a short-lasting metallic taste (13 patients) and mild reduction in blood pressure of no clinical significance (5 patients) (Table 4). Mild AEs occurred more frequently in the fixed-dose group (P < .01) (Table 1). Patients with AEs also received a higher first dose of vernakalant (median 4.14 [95% CI 3.7–5] vs 3.73 [95% CI 3–4.3]; P = .04).

Discussion

We report a large cohort of consecutive patients receiving vernakalant for the conversion of recent-onset AF: 328 patients received intravenous vernakalant, and 268 (82%) converted to sinus rhythm within minutes of drug infusion. Female sex and shorter symptom duration were associated with a higher conversion rate. Patients receiving the simplified fixed-dose protocol experienced a faster termination of the arrhythmia because they more often converted after the first dose. However, they experienced more frequent AEs, all of them mild.

The efficacy of vernakalant

In placebo-controlled studies of vernakalant, the conversion rate of recent-onset AF was only 1.5%–14% after the intravenous infusion of placebo, contrasting with the 37%–71% conversion rate with intravenous vernakalant in the same studies,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 clearly demonstrating the high efficacy of this drug for rapid AF termination (Table 5).11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 The conversion rate in our study was even higher, reaching 82%. The higher conversion rate observed in our study is probably related to the fact that (except for isolated exceptions) we limited the use of vernakalant to patients reporting symptoms lasting 48 hours or less, whereas previous studies included patients reporting symptoms lasting up to 72 hours.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18,20,21 These results place vernakalant among the most effective drugs for AF termination because the reported rates of conversion are 44% for intravenous amiodarone, 31%–51% for intravenous dofetilide, and 52% for intravenous flecainide.22

The safety of vernakalant

A total of 4008 patients have received vernakalant in published randomized controlled11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 (Table 5) and nonrandomized clinical trials (Supplemental Table 1).20,21,23, 24, 25 4 of these patients (0.099%) died after receiving the medication, yet not necessarily from the medication. The first case was reported in the ACT III trial and involved a patient who had severe aortic stenosis and developed hypotension and ventricular fibrillation. Because of his case, the protocol was amended to exclude patients with severe aortic stenosis.16 Another study reported a fatal exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease that occurred 24 days after the administration of vernakalant and was probably unrelated to the medication.14 The third case in which a fatality was described in the vernakalant group was not considered drug related by the investigator.18 The fourth fatality is reported here. We believe that our fatality was caused by a severe and untreated thyrotoxic cardiomyopathy that was not recognized at presentation. In this case and in the fatality with aortic stenosis, accepted contraindications to the use of vernakalant existed yet remained unrecognized before therapy was initiated. Point-of-care ultrasound is increasingly used in EDs, and it is reasonable to assume that its use would have prevented the use of vernakalant in these patients. Ideally, point-of-care ultrasound should be performed before attempting cardioversion in general and cardioversion with vernakalant in particular to detect patients with unsuspected significant heart disease. Importantly, patients presenting to an ED with recent-onset AF may have serious comorbidities. In fact, AF may be a complication of an underlying serious illness. This is demonstrated by a recent study of ≈30,000 patients presenting with recent-onset AF who underwent electric cardioversion.26 In this study, 2.8% of patients undergoing DC cardioversion for recent-onset AF died within 24 hours of their electric cardioversion.26

AF remains the most common sustained arrhythmia in adults and is associated with a higher risk of stroke, heart failure, dementia, and long-term mortality.27 Conversely, recent studies show that restoration and preservation of sinus rhythm reduce these cardiovascular complications.2, 3, 4 Our study focuses on acute cardioversion of the arrhythmia and does not address maintenance of sinus rhythm. However, evidence shows that patients who present to an ED in AF who undergo cardioversion and are discharged in sinus rhythm are more likely to be followed up by clinical electrophysiologists than those discharged in AF and rate control,28 making the option of rapid rhythm management in the ED a long-term beneficial choice.

Limitations

Our study is single center and retrospective. However, the efficacy of vernakalant has already been demonstrated in placebo-controlled trials (Table 5). Our study should be viewed as a confirmation of vernakalant’s efficacy in a large real-world cohort.

Conclusion

Our results reaffirm the use of vernakalant for cardioversion. In addition, we suggest that higher doses can be used with an added rate of success and little additional risk. Further studies are needed to determine long-term effects and pharmacodynamics for safe administration.
