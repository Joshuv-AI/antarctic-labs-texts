One transcript, two functions: the emerging roles of dual-function RNAs
Authors: Liz Maria Luke, Kai Papenfort, Cynthia Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteria use small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) and small proteins to change gene expression and modulate cellular processes in response to changing environmental conditions. In addition, several transcripts have been reported to combine base-pairing sRNA activities and coding capacity. These transcripts are known as dual-function RNAs. In some cases, the sRNA and the protein operate within the same pathway, while in other cases, they modulate separate processes in the cell. Thereby, dual-function RNAs enable bacteria to adjust their gene expression and physiology at multiple levels, which can have synergistic regulatory effects or help to synchronize the output of cellular pathways. In this review, we summarized the regulatory and physiological roles of dual-function RNAs in bacteria, including their roles in intercellular communication, virulence, stress response, and metabolism. In addition, we discuss open challenges and possible future applications for harnessing dual regulators for precise gene expression control in bacteria.Keywords: dual-function RNAs, regulatory RNAs, small proteins, bacterial gene regulation

Introduction

Small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) are the largest and most diverse group of posttranscriptional regulators in bacteria (Papenfort and Melamed 2023). The sRNAs are either transcribed as independent genes or generated through ribonucleic cleavage from the 3′ untranslated region (UTRs) of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and typically range from 50 to 300 nucleotides (nt) in length (Miyakoshi et al. 2015). Most sRNAs function by base-pairing with their target mRNAs, modulating their translation and/or stability, which is often facilitated by RNA chaperone proteins, such as Hfq or ProQ (Ng Kwan Lim et al. 2021). In addition, sRNAs can also act by protein sequestration (Wassarman 2007, Romeo and Babitzke 2018). At the physiological level, sRNAs have been documented to play crucial roles in a wide range of cellular pathways, such as stress responses (Frohlich and Gottesman 2018), intercellular communication (Papenfort and Bassler 2016), metabolic regulation (Bobrovskyy and Vanderpool 2013, Papenfort and Storz 2024), cell division (Ruhland et al. 2024), motility (Ghandour et al. 2025), iron acquisition (Salvail and Masse 2012), pathogenesis (Barquist et al. 2016), phage defense (Sprenger et al. 2024), and many more.

An average bacterial genome might encode ∼100–200 different sRNAs (Hor et al. 2020), most of which do not contain open reading frames (ORFs), and are therefore noncoding. Some sRNAs, however, harbor short ORFs that are translated into small proteins, and thus are called dual-function RNAs (Schnoor et al. 2023). In other words, dual-function RNAs are chimeric transcript exhibiting both base-pairing regulatory activities, as well as protein functions. In most cases, the regulatory activity of the sRNA was discovered first followed by the identification and characterization of the small protein. Small proteins, loosely defined as proteins <70 amino acids in size, are an underrepresented part of the cellular proteome and have frequently been missed in conventional proteomic assays due to their small size and constrains in standard genome annotations and the design of computational pipelines (Chu et al. 2015, Hemm et al. 2020, Gray et al. 2022).

This view has now been challenged by the advent of novel transcriptomic approaches (incl. ribosome profiling) and advanced proteomic and bioinformatic technologies, enabling a surge in the discoveries of microbial small proteins, as well as dual-function RNAs (Ahrens et al. 2022, Gray et al. 2022, Vazquez-Laslop et al. 2022). While many of these newly identified regulators still await functional characterization, previous work on dual-function RNAs from various bacteria has indicated their diverse cellular roles and complex regulatory mechanisms. Representative examples include Pel from Streptococcus pyogenes (Mangold et al. 2004), SR1 and SR7 from Bacillus subtilis (Gimpel and Brantl 2016, Ul Haq and Brantl 2021), Psm-mec and RNAIII from Staphylococcus aureus (Kaito et al. 2011, Bronesky et al. 2016), SgrS, AzuCR, and Spot42 from Escherichia coli (Wadler and Vanderpool 2007, Aoyama et al. 2022, Raina et al. 2022), rnTrpL from E. coli and Sinorhizobium meliloti (Melior et al. 2019, Evguenieva-Hackenberg 2022), and VcdRP from Vibrio cholerae (Venkat et al. 2021). Notably, several additional potential dual-RNAs have been reported, such as PhrS from Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Sonnleitner et al. 2011), VR-RNA from Clostridium perfringens (Shimizu et al. 2002), and Pos19 from Rhodobacter sphaeroides (Berghoff et al. 2009, Hess et al. 2014, Muller et al. 2016). However, only the translation of PhrS and Pos19 has been confirmed (Sonnleitner et al. 2008, Muller et al. 2016). In addition, dual-function RNAs have recently also been reported in archaea, demonstrating that sRNA154 from Methanosarcina mazei contains a translated small ORF (Prasse et al. 2017, Tufail et al. 2024).

By combining RNA-mediated gene expression control with protein functions, dual-function RNAs promote a tight and coordinated response to varying environmental perturbations, highlighting the emerging complexity of bacterial regulatory networks. In this review, we provide an overview of dual-function RNAs in bacteria focusing on their regulatory functions in virulence regulation, adaptation to various stress conditions, and their pivotal roles in balancing carbon and amino acid metabolism.

Dual-function RNAs acting in response to cell density and virulence

Coordinating cell density signals with virulence gene expression is essential for many bacterial pathogens to initiate infection strategies, escape immune responses, and to successfully adapt to the host environment (Papenfort and Bassler 2016). Various sRNAs have been reported to contribute to these processes (Papenfort and Vogel 2010, Gonzalez Plaza 2020), with RNAIII, the key virulence regulator from S. aureus, being the first identified dual-function RNA (Novick et al. 1993, Boisset et al. 2007) (Fig. 1). Staphylococcus aureus is an important human pathogen that produces an extensive array of virulence determinants (Touaitia et al. 2025). Virulence factor expression in S. aureus is a growth phase-dependent process: surface-associated adhesins and immune-evasion molecules are expressed at low cell densities, whereas secreted proteins are induced at high cell densities. This coordinated shift is orchestrated by the accessory gene regulator (Agr) quorum-sensing system (Vuong et al. 2000, Novick 2003). At high cell density, the accumulation of the autoinducing peptide (AIP) is detected by the membrane-bound histidine kinase AgrC, which triggers autophosphorylation of AgrC and subsequent phospho-transfer to the response regulator AgrA. Phosphorylated AgrA then activates transcription of RNAIII, the primary effector of the Agr regulon (Novick et al. 1993, Novick 2003, Bronesky et al. 2016).

RNAIII-mediated gene expression control. In S. aureus, RNAIII is the primary effector of the Agr quorum-sensing system. At high cell density, accumulation of AIPs is sensed by the AgrC histidine kinase leading to the phosphorylation of the response regulator AgrA. Phosphorylated AgrA activates the transcription of RNAIII. RNAIII contains hld gene and encodes δ-hemolysin. RNAIII also posttranscriptionally activates several target mRNAs such as hla, mgrA, esxA, and map mRNA; whereas lytM, murQ, rot, coa, spa, sbi, ltaS, sa1000, sa2093, and sa2353 are repressed. In addition, RNAIII also interacts with the RNA regulators RsaA, SprY, and SprX.

The 5′ region of RNAIII encodes δ-hemolysin (hld), a 26-amino acid (aa) cytotoxic small protein targeting host membranes causing cell lysis (Fig. 1). δ-hemolysin lyses erythrocytes, other mammalian cells, as well as subcellular structures such as membrane-bound organelles (Kreger et al. 1971). Unlike other hemolytic peptides, δ-hemolysin exhibits only limited activity against other bacteria (Verdon et al. 2009). However, δ-hemolysin has shown antimicrobial activity against E. coli and S. aureus when its aspartic acid residues are replaced by lysine. Therefore, peptide charge might be a key determinant of antimicrobial activity (Dhople and Nagaraj 2005). As pointed out above, expression of δ-hemolysin is activated by AgrA in response to AIPs, but its translation is delayed by ~1 h after RNAIII transcription possibly due to intramolecular interaction in the 3′ end of RNAIII (Balaban and Novick 1995, Benito et al. 2000), yet, the factors promoting RNAIII remodeling and thereby δ-hemolysin expression are currently unknown.

The 514 nt long RNAIII transcript harbors a complex secondary structure with 14 stem-loop (SL) motifs, designated SL1–SL14, from 5′ to 3′ (Benito et al. 2000) (Fig. 1). RNAIII SLs located in the 5′ region of the molecule typically promote target activation, whereas those in the 3′ region often mediate target repression (Bronesky et al. 2016). For example, SL2 and SL3 in the 5′ end of RNAIII facilitate the exposure of a Shine–Dalgarno sequence in the hla mRNA (encoding cytotoxic hemolysin α) and thereby facilitate its translation and a similar mechanism has been reported for activation of the map mRNA (encoding an extracellular adherence protein) by SL4 (Liu et al. 2011). An exception to RNAIII’s canonical regulatory mechanisms is stabilization of the mRNA encoding MgrA through interactions with both SL2 at the 5′, as well as SL13 at the 3′ end (Gupta et al. 2015). MgrA is a global transcriptional regulator and stabilization of the mgrA mRNA by RNAIII affects a broad range of cellular activities, including bacterial persistence (Romilly et al. 2014). Recent studies have identified SL9 of RNAIII to base-pair with and stimulate the expression of the esxA toxin mRNA (McKellar et al. 2022), which prevents S. aureus cell death and enhances its persistence in infected cells (Burts et al. 2005, Sundaramoorthy et al. 2008).

The highly conserved 3′ region of RNAIII inhibits translation of early stage infection genes by base-pairing with their ribosome-binding sites (RBS) to block translation and in some cases by facilitating ribonuclease III-mediated RNA decay (Tomasini et al. 2014). Repressed targets include staphylococcal protein A (spa) (Huntzinger et al. 2005), coagulase (coa) (Chevalier et al. 2010), cell wall recycling enzyme MurQ (murQ) (Mediati et al. 2022), SsaA-like secretory antigens (sa2353 and sa2093) (Boisset et al. 2007), fibrinogen-binding proteins (sa1000) (Boisset et al. 2007), immunoglobulin-binding protein (sbi) (Chabelskaya et al. 2014), and two targets involved in cell wall metabolism: the major cell wall autolysin (lytM) (Chunhua et al. 2012) and lipoteichoic acid synthase (ltaS) (Chunhua et al. 2012, Yan et al. 2015) (Fig. 1). Another critical target that is inhibited by RNAIII is the rot (repressor of toxins) mRNA, which encodes a virulence-associated transcriptional repressor, enabling RNAIII to exert indirect control over a wide array of secondary virulence determinants (Boisset et al. 2007). Of note, RNAIII appears to function independently of Hfq or similar helper proteins for base-pairing or for its stability (Huntzinger et al. 2005, Bohn et al. 2007, Boisset et al. 2007).

Interestingly, RNAIII has been indicated to not only base-pair with mRNAs, but also noncoding regulators, such as RsaA (McKellar et al. 2022), SprY (Le Huyen et al. 2021), and SprX (Kathirvel et al. 2016) (Fig. 1). The RsaA sRNA functions as a virulence suppressor during acute infections and inhibits the mgrA mRNA (Romilly et al. 2014, Tomasini et al. 2017) and thus might function as an RNAIII antagonist. Similarly, the prophage-encoded SprY sRNA binds to SL13 of RNAIII and prevents repression of the rot and ecb (encoding an extracellular complement binding protein) mRNAs (Le Huyen et al. 2021). Additionally, SprX sRNA binds near the 5′ region of RNAIII encoding Hld protein and has been suggested to enhance its translation (Kathirvel et al. 2016).

Another dual-function RNA identified in S. aureus is Psm-mec. Psm-mec is encoded on the “staphylococcal cassette chromosome” (SSC-mec), an accessory gene element associated with antibiotic resistance. Psm-mec, in addition to RNAIII, also participates in virulence regulation and pathogenicity (Kaito et al. 2011). Similar to RNAIII, Psm-mec encodes a 22-aa hydrophobic α-helical peptide. However, unlike other α-hemolysins, the Psm-mec peptide inhibits invasive host cell infection (Queck et al. 2009, Kaito et al. 2011). The sRNA segment of Psm-mec blocks the translation of the agrA mRNA, which leads to decreased levels of phenol soluble modulin (PSMα) and RNAIII (Kaito et al. 2011, 2013). Importantly, the acquisition of psm-mec allows methicillin-resistant S. aureus to switch between RNAIII-mediated acute infections and Psm-mec-associated chronic infections (Kaito et al. 2011, 2013). Psm-mec homologues are found in several staphylococcal species, including Staphylococcus epidermidis and Staphylococcus haemolyticus (Ikuo et al. 2014) and, analogous to RNAIII, Psm-mec functions independently of Hfq (Kaito et al. 2011).

A dual-function RNA similar to RNAIII is the Pel (pleiotropic effect locus) RNA from S. pyogenes. The 469 nt Pel transcript, encoded by the first gene of the sagABCDEFGHI operon, regulates streptolysin S processing and export in a growth phase-dependent manner (Betschel et al. 1998, Mangold et al. 2004). Pel encodes a 53-aa toxin peptide (streptolysin S), which is a key virulence factor driving the β-hemolytic activity of S. pyogenes (Nizet et al. 2000). Deletion of the pel gene increases the expression of virulence-related mRNAs, such as streptococcal inhibitor of complement (sic), NAD-glycohydrolase (nga), and M-protein (emm), and impairs the maturation of the cysteine protease SpeB (Mangold et al. 2004). However, the mechanism by which Pel exerts gene regulation over these targets is not fully understood.

Beyond the above mentioned Gram-positive pathogens, dual-function RNAs also play a crucial role in regulating virulence in Gram-negative species. For example, SgrS, the first identified dual-function RNA in Gram-negative bacteria (see below), is well known for its role in mediating glucose phosphate stress response employing the SgrT peptide and the SgrS sRNA (Wadler and Vanderpool 2007). In addition to its role in stress regulation, the SgrS homolog from Salmonella enterica base-pairs with and inhibits the sopD mRNA, encoding a pathogenicity island-derived virulence factor (Papenfort et al. 2012).

Dual-function RNAs involved in bacterial stress response

General stress response systems

Bacteria respond to various stress and starvation conditions by activating general stress response (GSR) systems, which can be triggered by one or multiple cues. Although many bacteria employ the GSR to cope with environmental changes, its regulation can vary among bacterial species (Bouillet et al. 2024). In B. subtilis, GSR activation induces the expression of ~150 SigB-dependent genes (Hecker et al. 2007, Bouillet et al. 2024). Among these, SR7 is a 185-nt long dual-function RNA induced in response to five different environmental stress conditions, i.e. exposure to ethanol, heat shock, high NaCl and manganese concentrations, and acidic pH (Mars et al. 2015, Ul Haq et al. 2021a). SR7 was initially annotated as ncr2360, among 54 newly identified sRNAs from B. subtilis (Irnov et al. 2010). The sr7 gene resides in the intergenic region between rpsD (encoding ribosomal protein S4) and tyrS (encoding tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase), overlapping the 3′ end of rpsD by 23 nt and thus likely acts as a cis-encoded antisense regulator reducing RpsD levels (Mars et al. 2015). S4 is a major RNA-binding protein of the 30S ribosomal subunit and inhibition by SR7 might result in a reduced number of active ribosomes (Schnoor et al. 2023).

SR7P, a 39-aa peptide encoded in the 5′ region of SR7, directly interacts with enolase, a glycolytic enzyme that also functions as a scaffold protein of the B. subtilis degradosome (Ul Haq et al. 2024). Interaction between SR7P and enolase facilitates the binding of RNase Y to enolase, thereby modulating RNA degradosome activity. SR7P does not appear to interact with phosphofructokinase (PfkA), another scaffolding component of the RNA degradosome (Commichau et al. 2009, Ul Haq et al. 2021b). The SR7P–enolase–RNase Y complex facilitates degradation of the rpsO and yitJ mRNAs, both of which are known substrates of RNase Y (Ul Haq et al. 2021b). SR7P homologues have been identified in various B. subtilis strains and in nine other species within the genus Bacillus, whereas no homologs were identified in other Firmicutes or elsewhere (Ul Haq et al. 2021b).

Sugar stress response

While bacteria employ the GSR to survive a broad range of environmental challenges, they also rely on stress-specific adaptation systems that are tailored to a specific stress condition (Gottesman 2019). For example, when E. coli imports and phosphorylates high concentrations of sugars using phosphoenolpyruvate–phosphotransferase systems (PTS), sugar transport and metabolism can become uncoupled, leading to the intracellular accumulation of phosphorylated sugars, resulting in sugar–phosphate stress (Vanderpool 2007, Papenfort and Vogel 2014).

At the physiological level, the glucose–phosphate stress is sensed by the transcription factor SgrR activating sgrS expression (Vanderpool and Gottesman 2007). The 227-nt SgrS transcript of E. coli is a dual-function RNA divided into three different segments: the 5′ end containing the 43-aa SgrT peptide, the central conserved region mediating base-pairing interactions with target mRNAs, and the 3′ end containing the binding site for the RNA chaperone Hfq (Bobrovskyy and Vanderpool 2014). The SgrT peptide counteracts sugar–phosphate stress by binding to PtsG glucose transporter and interferes with its activity (Fig. 2) (Wadler and Vanderpool 2007, Lloyd et al. 2017). This action of SgrT is part of a broader regulatory system mediated by the sRNA SgrS.

The role of SgrS in sugar-stress response and virulence. Accumulation of intracellular phosphosugars causes sugar–phosphate stress and activates the transcription factor SgrR, which drives the expression of the SgrST transcript, containing the SgrS sRNA and the SgrT small protein. SgrS inhibits the mRNA encoding PTS transporters (ptsG/manXYZ) and stabilizes yigL, enhancing the export of dephosphorylated sugars. SgrS also inhibits many other targets (asd, adiY, folE, purR) to promote metabolic homeostasis. The SgrT small protein blocks the activity of PtsG glucose transporter. In addition, SgrS represses the sopD mRNA, encoding virulence factor specific to Salmonella.

Here, through its base-pairing activity, SgrS mitigates sugar–phosphate stress by regulating several mRNAs. First, SgrS interacts with the RBS of the ptsG mRNA, which downregulates protein synthesis (Vanderpool and Gottesman 2004, Kawamoto et al. 2006, Maki et al. 2008). Second, SgrS also inhibits the expression of another sugar transporter encoded by the polycistronic manXYZ mRNA using multiple binding sites (Rice and Vanderpool 2011, Rice et al. 2012). Downregulation of both targets inhibits carbohydrate uptake and in both cases binding of SgrS to the target mRNA facilitates RNaseE-mediated degradation (Kimata et al. 2001, Rice and Vanderpool 2011). Third, base-pairing of SgrS with the pldB-yigL mRNA results in target mRNA stabilization and increased expression of the YigL protein (Fig. 2). YigL is a sugar phosphatase allowing dephosphorylation and export of the toxic sugars (Papenfort et al. 2013). SgrS is also known to repress a diverse set of auxiliary enzymes and transcription factors such as adiY (arginine decarboxylase gene activator), folE (GTP cyclohydrolase I), asd (aspartate semialdehyde dehydrogenase), and purR mRNA encoding a repressor of purine biosynthesis (Bobrovskyy and Vanderpool 2016). Although the precise role of regulating these additional targets has not been fully resolved, their repression likely contributes to restoring metabolic balance under sugar–phosphate stress (Raina et al. 2018). Similar to many other sRNAs in Gram-negative bacteria, SgrS depends on Hfq for stability and base-pairing with target mRNAs (Kawamoto et al. 2006, Maki et al. 2008).

Overexpression of sgrS variants encoding either the SgrS sRNA or the SgrT small protein alone were sufficient to relieve glucose–phosphate stress, implying functional redundancy between their roles (Wadler and Vanderpool 2007, Balasubramanian and Vanderpool 2013, Lloyd et al. 2017). As pointed out above, SgrS has been recruited to modulate virulence gene expression in S. enterica (Papenfort et al. 2012), suggesting a high level of functional plasticity that might indicate additional regulatory functions for SgrS in other bacteria as well. SgrS (and SgrT) homologs are conserved in a range of enterobacteria, such as Salmonella, Shigella, Klebsiella, and Erwinia, suggesting an evolutionarily conserved role in stress regulation (Horler and Vanderpool 2009).

Dual-function RNAs regulating utilization

Carbon metabolism

In environments with diverse carbon sources, bacteria implement a hierarchical metabolic strategy prioritizing the utilization of the most energetically favorable carbon source. In Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, carbon catabolite repression (CCR) ensures the preferred utilization of glucose by repressing the genes involved in the use of alternative carbon sources when glucose is present (Gorke and Stulke 2008, Durica-Mitic et al. 2018). This selective metabolism of specific carbon sources is driven by complex, multilayered systems, including transcription factors, RNA-binding proteins, sRNAs, and small proteins, working together to control the expression of transporters and enzymes involved in carbohydrate utilization. The cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) receptor protein (CRP) is a key regulator of CCR in E. coli and related species, controlling numerous genes involved in the utilization of alternative carbon sources (Youn and Carranza 2023). CRP is typically active when bound to cAMP, which is produced by the CyaA enzyme in response to glucose limitation. Among the many targets of CRP is the Spot 42 (spf) a highly abundant sRNA, which is repressed by CRP-cAMP and thus selectively expressed in the presence of glucose (Polayes et al. 1988).

Years after its initial characterization (Ikemura and Dahlberg 1973), Spot 42 was shown to associate with Hfq and base-pair with multiple mRNA targets (Moller et al. 2002a). Overexpression of Spot 42 negatively affects growth on several nonpreferred carbon sources (Beisel and Storz 2011) by repressing several mRNAs involved in the uptake and catabolism of secondary carbon sources (Fig. 3A). These targets can be broadly categorized into five main groups: (i) central metabolism, e.g. citrate synthase (gltA) (Beisel and Storz 2011) and 4-aminobutyrate aminotransferase (puuE) (Beisel et al. 2012), (ii) sugar catabolism, such as l-fucose isomerase (fucl), l-fuculose kinase (fucK) (Beisel and Storz 2011), and galactokinase (galK) (Moller et al. 2002a, b), (iii) sugar transporters, for example, lactate transporter (lldP) (Beisel and Storz 2011), sialic acid MFS transporter (nanT) (Beisel and Storz 2011, Beisel et al. 2012, Baekkedal and Haugen 2015), (iv) redox homeostasis, like pyridine nucleotide transhydrogenase (sthA) (Beisel and Storz 2011), and (v) antioxidant biosynthesis, e.g. glutathionylspermidine amidase and synthetase (gsp) (Beisel and Storz 2011). Posttranscriptional gene regulation by Spot 42 occurs through three independent single-stranded regions that mediate base-pairing with the respective target mRNAs (Beisel and Storz 2011). Beyond its role as a base-pairing sRNA, Spot 42 from E. coli also encodes a 15-aa small protein, called SpfP (Fig. 3A). Analogous to the sRNA function of Spot 42, overexpression of SpfP causes a growth defect when cells are cultivated with nonpreferred carbon sources, implying functional redundancy among the sRNA and the small protein. SpfP binds to the transcription factor CRP, and at high levels SpfP blocks CRP-dependent activation at several CRP-dependent genes such as galM, galK (in galactose medium), and malE, malK (in maltose medium) (Aoyama et al. 2022).

Regulatory activities of Spot 42 and VcdRP during carbon metabolism. (A) In presence of glucose, cAMP levels are reduced and cAMP–CRP-mediated repression of the spf gene is relieved, leading to Spot 42 expression. The Spot 42 sRNA acts as a base-pairing regulator and also encodes the small protein, SpfP. Spot 42 inhibits various mRNA targets involved in the transport and metabolism of secondary carbon sources (gltA, puuE, fucI, fucK, sthA, gsp, and so on). In addition, SpfP binds to and inhibits CRP activity. (B) When glucose is available, cAMP–CRP-mediated repression of vcdRP is relieved resulting in expression of the VcdRP transcript. VcdRP encodes the VcdR sRNA and the VcdP small protein. VcdR represses specific components PTS transporters (ptsG, nagE, treB, and ptsHI), resulting in increased cAMP–CRP levels in the cell, which ultimately reduces CTX levels. The VcdP small protein binds to and increases the activity of GltA, which affects the flux of the TCA cycle.

In addition to their functional redundancy, the SpfP coding sequence overlaps with the base-pairing region of Spot 42, suggesting that the two regulatory functions must be coordinated. Indeed, while Spot 42 mediated base-pairing is more effective at lower temperatures (30°C), SpfP production increases at higher temperatures (42°C or 45°C). As a result, at higher temperatures, translation of spfP might interfere with Spot 42 base-pairing, or SpfP activity might dominate at high temperatures when base-pairing by Spot 42 is less efficient (Aoyama et al. 2022). Notably, in addition to Hfq’s role in mediating Spot 42 stability and base-pairing function, the carbon storage regulator protein, CsrA, also binds to a single GGA site within the SpfP coding sequence. CsrA binding stabilizes Spot 42 against RNase E-mediated cleavage (Lai et al. 2022). The combined effect of Hfq, CsrA, and translating ribosomes on Spot 42 and SpfP expression and function, their relative dominance under different conditions, and the influence on each other to mediate protein-coding and/or base-pairing interactions remain an interesting question for future research (Aoyama et al. 2022).

AzuCR represents another example of dual-function RNA that modulates carbon metabolism in E. coli. Here, AzuC is the small protein, while AzuR is the base-pairing sRNA. The 164 nt AzuCR transcript, originally annotated as IS092 or IsrB, was first identified in a bioinformatic search for sRNA genes in E. coli (Chen et al. 2002). This RNA was later found to encode a 28-aa sORF (Hemm et al. 2008, Weaver et al. 2019), which is conserved in several enteric related bacteria (Raina et al. 2022). Transcription of azuCR is repressed by CRP in the absence of glucose and induced at low pH, high temperatures, and oxidative stress (Hemm et al. 2010). In addition, under anaerobic conditions, the Hfq-dependent sRNA FnrS base-pairs near the azuC RBS and inhibits its translation (Raina et al. 2022).

AzuC is an amphipathic small protein, which interacts with aerobic glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GlpD) at the inner cell membrane and enhances its activity (Raina et al. 2022). GlpD is present as both soluble and membrane-bound forms, while full activity requires association with the inner membrane through lipid–enzyme interactions or reconstitution with phospholipids in vitro (Schryvers et al. 1978, Robinson and Weiner 1980, Yeh et al. 2008). Thus, AzuC enhances dehydrogenase activity possibly by promoting GlpD association with the inner membrane (Raina et al. 2022).

As a base-pairing sRNA, AzuR binds near the RBS of cadA mRNA and blocks its translation (Raina et al. 2022). The cadA gene encodes for lysine decarboxylase and is induced under acidic growth conditions. It protects the cell against weak organic acids generated during carbohydrate fermentation under anaerobic and phosphate-starved conditions (Kanjee and Houry 2013). In several bacteria, including E. coli, V. cholerae, and S. typhimurium, the cadB (encoding lysine/cadaverine antiporter) and cadA genes are cotranscribed as part of one operon. The cadBA genes were shown to negatively regulate biofilm formation, mainly under acid stress conditions (Du et al. 2021). Another base-pairing target of AzuR is galE, the first gene of the galETKM galactose operon. AzuR binds to the coding sequence of galE and affects stability and/or Rho-dependent transcription termination of the mRNA (Raina et al. 2022). Although AzuR associates with both RNA chaperones Hfq and ProQ, only ProQ is required for its base-pairing interactions. Notably, AzuR’s base-pairing region overlaps with the azuC coding sequence, leading to possible functional interference. This implies that the function of this dual-function RNA may depend on the order in which ribosomes, Hfq, or ProQ bind to the AzuCR transcript. It is also possible that one function dominates during the initial growth phase, followed by the other (Raina et al. 2022).

The majority of bacterial sRNAs and dual-function RNAs have been discovered in E. coli, however, these regulators have also been identified in other Gram-negative bacteria, including the major human pathogen V. cholerae (Ghandour and Papenfort 2023). For example, VcdRP is a dual-function RNA that plays a critical role in synchronizing V. cholerae’s carbon metabolism (Fig. 3B). Analogous to spot42 (spf) and azuCR, vcdRP transcription is repressed by the global transcription factor CRP and this repression is relieved upon glucose availability.

Interestingly, overexpression of VcdRP downregulates production of the cholera toxin (CTX), a key virulence factor of V. cholerae (Venkat et al. 2021). The 306-nt long VcdRP transcript associates with Hfq (Huber et al. 2020) and inhibits the synthesis of several mRNAs encoding specific components of PTS transporters, including ptsG (glucose), nagE (N-acetylglucosamine), treB (trehalose), and ptsHI mRNA (encoding two nonspecific phosphocarrier proteins transferring phosphate to the PTS transporters) (Fig. 3B). A stretch of four conserved cytosines at the 3′ end of VcdR is required for base-pairing with these target mRNAs, resulting in translation inhibition and mRNA decay. These findings suggested that CTX repression by VcdRP might be a consequence of sRNA-mediated repression of the sugar transport proteins as inhibition of ptsH or ptsI have been reported to increase intracellular cAMP levels, leading to CRP activation (Wang et al. 2015). The cAMP–CRP complex subsequently represses the tcpH operon (Zahid et al. 2015), ultimately resulting in reduced CTX production (Venkat et al. 2021).

VcdRP is processed by RNase E into shorter isoforms, which contain the relevant base-pairing sequence and are sufficient for target regulation. It is thus not clear if full-length VcdRP also participates in target mRNA regulation or if processing is required for Hfq binding and/or target recognition. Previous work on the ArcZ sRNA from E. coli and Salmonella has indicated that processing of the sRNA indeed facilitates target regulation (Chao et al. 2017), however, no similar findings have been reported for dual-function RNAs.

The protein component of VcdRP is the 29-aa VcdP small protein, which interacts with citrate synthase (GltA) and increases its activity (Fig. 3B). GltA is the first enzyme of the citric acid cycle and catalyses the condensation of oxaloacetate and acetyl-CoA, producing citrate and coenzyme A (Nguyen et al. 2001). In Gram-negative bacteria, GltA forms a hexameric structure and is subject to allosteric inhibition by NADH (Weitzman and Jones 1968, Nguyen et al. 2001) and VcdP-mediated activation of GltA activity might relieve this inhibitory effect (Venkat et al. 2021). It has been speculated that when cells use glucose as the main carbon source, VcdP-mediated citrate synthase activation modulates the flux of the TCA cycle, which is balanced by the VcdR-mediated inhibition of PTS transporters. Thus, VcdR and VcdP likely function to coordinate and optimize carbon uptake and utilization in V. cholerae (Venkat et al. 2021).

The regulatory function of VcdRP shares several features with the previously described SgrST and AzuCR systems. Whereas both VcdR and SgrS inhibit the expression of PTS-encoding mRNAs, transcription of sgrST is activated by the sugar–phosphate stress response regulator SgrR (Vanderpool and Gottesman 2007), while vcdRP expression is repressed by the global regulator CRP (Venkat et al. 2021). This later feature is shared between VcdRP and AzuCR (and Spot 42) and both VcdP and AzuC function to increase the activity of a metabolic enzyme. Together, these parallels indicate a potentially conserved strategy of dual-function RNAs to coordinate sugar uptake with the metabolic status of the cell through molecular distinct, yet functionally similar, regulatory mechanisms.

Amino acid metabolism

Beyond carbon metabolism, dual-function RNAs also play important roles in amino acid metabolism. For example, in B. subtilis, the highly conserved dual-function RNA SR1 acts as a metabolic sensor that connects several metabolic pathways (Gimpel et al. 2012) (Fig. 4). SR1 is induced under gluconeogenic conditions and repressed under glycolytic growth by the catabolite repressor protein CcpN and, to a minor extent, by CcpA (Licht et al. 2005, 2008, Licht and Brantl 2006, 2009). The SR1 encoded small protein, called SR1P (39 aa) interacts with GapA (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase A), which stabilizes the gapA mRNA by an yet unknown mechanism (Gimpel et al. 2010). The glycolytic enzyme GapA also affects RNA degradation by recruiting RNases (RNase J1 and Y). Notably, SR1P binding to GapA promotes GapA-RNase J1 interaction and enhances its activity (Gimpel and Brantl 2016).

Regulatory activities of SR1 in B. subtilis. SR1 transcription is repressed under glycolytic conditions by CcpA and CcpN. Under gluconeogenic conditions, SR1 is expressed and represses translation of the ahrC mRNA, encoding a transcriptional activator of the arginine catabolic operons rocABC and rocDEF. In addition, SR1 base-pairs with kinA mRNA, inhibiting its translation and thereby affecting sporulation. SR1-encoded small protein SR1P binds the glycolytic enzyme GapA and stabilizes gapA mRNA through an unknown mechanism. SR1P also bind to components of the degradosome (e.g. RNases J1 and Y), which affects RNA turnover.

Under nutrient-depleted conditions, B. subtilis shifts to gluconeogenesis and utilizes various amino acids and nucleotides as carbon and nitrogen sources (Fujita 2009). The 205-nt SR1 sRNA is involved in arginine catabolism by base-pairing with the ahrC mRNA, encoding the transcriptional activator of the arginine catabolic operons rocABC and rocDEF (Heidrich et al. 2006). SR1 base-pairs via seven complementary regions with ahrC and inhibits its translation (Heidrich et al. 2007), reducing the expression of rocABC and rocDEF. The RNA chaperone Hfq binds both SR1 and ahrC mRNA but only facilitates ahrC translation (Heidrich et al. 2007); instead, CsrA binding promotes SR1 base-pairing with ahrC by remodeling the ahrC mRNA (Muller et al. 2019).

In addition to its role in metabolism, SR1 also regulates sporulation (Ul Haq et al. 2021a). Bacillus subtilis forms endospores as a survival strategy in response to nutrient starvation. Among the five histidine kinases that perceive and transmit environmental signals to the sporulation genes, KinA is the major histidine kinase involved in the phospho-relay (Perego et al. 1989). SR1 base-pairs with the kinA mRNA upstream of the RBS and inhibits its translation without affecting mRNA stability (Fig. 4). Repression of kinA by SR1 reduces sporulation and modulates spore physiology by increasing spore hydrophobicity and resistance to heat, ethanol, and UV radiation (Ul Haq et al. 2021a). Thus, under nutrient starvation conditions, SR1 modulates spore formation and quality. In contrast to the SR1-ahrC system, neither Hfq nor CsrA affect sporulation regulation by SR1.

Another well-known example of how bacteria coordinate amino acid metabolism in response to internal and external cues is the regulation of l-tryptophan (Trp) biosynthesis operon in E. coli (Gammaproteobacteria) and S. meliloti (Alphaproteobacteria). In E. coli, the trpEDCBA operon encodes the enzymes necessary for Trp biosynthesis (Yanofsky 1971). Transcription of the trp operon is regulated at two levels: (i) Repression by Trp repressor protein (TrpR) (Cohen and Jacob 1959) (Yanofsky and Ito 1966) and (ii) leader peptide-mediated transcriptional attenuation. After transcription attenuation, TrpL is released as a functional sRNA (rnTrpL) and also contains a small upstream ORF (uORF) encoding the 14-aa peTrpL peptide (Yanofsky 1981). Formation of rnTrpL occurs either by tryptophan-dependent leader peptide synthesis or tryptophan-independent general translation inhibition (Durfee et al. 2008). The 140-nt rnTrpL transcript base-pairs with and destabilizes the dnaA mRNA, encoding the master regulator of chromosomal replication initiation. Thus, rnTrpL might link DNA replication with nutrient availability.

Like many other E. coli sRNAs, rnTrpL relies on Hfq to facilitate base-pairing interactions (Iosub et al. 2020). Furthermore, rnTrpL negatively regulates sanA (Li et al. 2021), involved in RpoS-dependent SDS resistance under stationary phase conditions (Mitchell et al. 2017), and positively regulates mhpC, encoding an aromatic hydrolase facilitating the degradation of aromatic compounds (Manso et al. 2009, Li et al. 2021). Additionally, rnTrpL represses translation of the rsuA (16S rRNA pseudouridine synthase) and bcr (multidrug efflux pump) mRNAs in an Hfq and tetracycline-dependent manner (Li 2020).

In contrast to E. coli, the trp genes of S. meliloti are distributed across three operons: trpE(G), trpDC, and trpFBA (Merino et al. 2008). Of these, only the trpE(G) operon is transcriptionally attenuated by trpL (Bae and Crawford 1990). Additionally, S. meliloti lacks the TrpR repressor protein present in E. coli and, as a result, transcription of trpE(G) is constitutive and regulated by TrpL transcriptional attenuation in response to Trp availability. The 110-nt rnTrpL sRNA from S. meliloti base-pairs with the trpD region of the trpDC mRNA and destabilizes it, providing tryptophan-dependent repression of the trpDC operon (Melior et al. 2019). Thus, a single genetic locus in S. meliloti gives rise to two riboregulators: a cis-acting attenuator and a trans-acting sRNA to coordinate the expression of the trpE(G) and trpDC operons according to the Trp availability. A similar mechanism of coordinated expression of trpE(G) and trpDC operons is conserved in related Alphaproteobacteria, such as Agrobacterium tumefaciens (a plant pathogen) and Bradyrhizobium japonicum (a soybean symbiont) (Evguenieva-Hackenberg 2022). In these species, trpDC genes are a part of the polycistronic ppiD–trpDC–moaC–moeA operon. This operon contains ppiD, encoding a peptidyl–prolyl isomerase involved in outer membrane protein folding and translocation (Dartigalongue and Raina 1998, Furst et al. 2018), as well as moaC and moeA, encoding enzymes required for molybdenum cofactor (MoCo) biosynthesis (Iobbi-Nivol and Leimkühler 2013, Melior et al. 2019). Furthermore, rnTrpL binds to and destabilizes the sinI mRNA (Baumgardt et al. 2016, Melior et al. 2019), encoding an autoinducer synthase, potentially linking quorum sensing with Trp availability (Evguenieva-Hackenberg 2022)

In addition to the rnTrpL sRNA, in S. meliloti, the trp attenuator also encodes a 14-aa peTrpL peptide. Both rnTrpL and peTrpL can jointly or independently form an antibiotic-dependent ribonucleoprotein complexes (ARNPs) to regulate other targets (Schnoor et al. 2023). For example, under nutrient-rich conditions and in the presence of antimicrobial compounds, such as tetracycline, peTrpL accumulates and binds to an antibiotic-induced antisense RNA (asRNA), resulting in destabilization of the smeR mRNA (transcription repressor of smeABR multidrug efflux pump), while the smeAB part is stabilized (Melior et al. 2020, 2021). This posttranscriptional regulation relies on the assembly of ARNPs consisting of peTrpL, asRNA, smeR mRNA, and tetracycline. This smeR-ARNPs can also be formed in the presence of various other antimicrobial compounds that are exported by SmeAB (Melior et al. 2020, 2021). Moreover, in the presence of tetracycline, the rplUrpmA transcript, encoding ribosomal proteins L21 and L27, is destabilized by the ARNP formed by peTrpL, rnTrpL, and rplUrpmA mRNA (Melior et al. 2021).

Potential other dual-function RNAs

Dual-function RNAs might be more prevalent than previously appreciated (Aoyama and Storz 2023). Indeed, various other sRNAs with base-pairing functions have been reported to also encode putative small proteins, suggesting that the catalog of dual-function RNAs will further expand in the next few years. For example, several dual-function RNA candidates are involved in virulence gene expression and stress response: (i) the VR-RNA is positively regulated by the two-component VirR/VirS system in the anaerobic pathogen C. perfringens. VR-RNA contains a 72-aa ORF, named hyp7 (hypothetical 7 kDa protein) (Shimizu et al. 2002). The 386-nt VR-RNA sRNA regulates multiple genes, including toxin genes like collagenase (colA, κ-toxin) and phospholipase C (plc, α-toxin) (Shimizu et al. 2002). VR-RNA base pairs with 5′ UTR of colA mRNA, inducing its cleavage, which results in enhanced stability of colA mRNA and increased protein levels (Obana et al. 2010, 2013). (ii) PhrS from P. aeruginosa encodes a conserved 37 aa ORF (Sonnleitner et al. 2008), however, its function has not been characterized (Sonnleitner et al. 2011). The 212-nt long PhrS sRNA enhances the translation of the pqsR transcript, encoding a key transcriptional regulator of quorum sensing and virulence factor production (Sonnleitner et al. 2008). The oxygen-responsive regulator ANR is essential for phrS expression, linking oxygen availability and quorum sensing in P. aeruginosa biofilms (Sonnleitner et al. 2011). (iii) Another example is RSs0019 (a.k.a. Pos19; photooxidative stress induced sRNA) from R. sphaeroides, which is expressed in response to photooxidative stress (Berghoff et al. 2009). Pos19 encodes a 50-aa small protein and its translation has been confirmed in vivo (Muller et al. 2016). The 219-nt Pos19 sRNA is regulated by the alternative sigma factor σE (Berghoff et al. 2009) and regulates multiple genes involved in sulfur metabolism (Hess et al. 2014, Muller et al. 2016).

Growing evidence from ribosome profiling and peptidomics suggest that dual-function RNAs also exist in archaea. Specifically, sRNA154 from M. mazei is a recently identified dual-function RNA involved in nitrogen metabolism, which encodes a highly conserved leaderless ORF (20 aa) (Tufail et al. 2024). sRNA154 is induced by nitrogen (N) limitation and stabilizes nifH (α-subunit of nitrogenase), glnA1 (glutamine synthetase), and the mRNA encoding the nif-specific transcriptional activator NprA. In addition, the sRNA inhibits translation of glnA2 (glutamine synthetase) by masking its RBS, resulting in reduced expression (Prasse et al. 2017).

So far, only a few systematic searches for dual RNA regulators in bacteria and archaea have been performed (Friedman et al. 2017). However, this situation might well change in the next few years given the surge of available transcriptomic and proteomic datasets. Specifically, ribosome profiling experiments and proteomics can expose small ORFs in known sRNA regulators (Vazquez-Laslop et al. 2022, Aoyama and Storz 2023). Vice versa, global RNA interactome studies, e.g. by RIL-seq (RNA interaction by ligation and sequencing) (Melamed et al. 2016), might help to link base-pairing functions to previously annotated small ORFs and intergenic regions (Adams et al. 2021, Siemers et al. 2024). In addition, advanced bioinformatics tools integrating amino-acid and structure conservation of putative small proteins could further support the systematic discovery and characterization of dual-function RNAs. Interestingly, these approaches could also enable the discovery of additional small ORFs in other types of regulatory RNAs, such as sRNAs modulating protein activity, e.g. 6S RNA and CsrA binding sRNAs (Romeo and Babitzke 2018, Wassarman 2018), as well as the abundant class of cis-encoded RNAs (Georg and Hess 2018).

Of note, the base-pairing sequence and the small ORF of a dual-function RNA are not necessarily equally conserved. For example, not all SgrS homologues carry the sgrT ORF (Horler and Vanderpool 2009), while for SR1 of B. subtilis, the sequence of SR1P displays stronger conservation than the SR1 base-pairing region (Gimpel et al. 2012). These findings could provide relevant information about the evolution of these regulators, however, more comprehensive approaches involving sequence information from additional dual RNAs would be required to determine which of the two functions evolved first, or if one function has been lost in certain organisms. In the latter case, it would be conceivable that dual-function RNAs evolved from larger mRNAs carrying a regulatory RNA in their 5′ or 3′UTRs. One such example might be VcdRP, which carries the base-pairing sequence at the 3′ end of the transcript (Venkat et al. 2021) (Fig. 3B).

Summary and perspective

Over the past two decades, research into the discovery and characterization of novel bacterial sRNAs has revolutionized our understanding of bacterial gene regulation (Gottesman and Storz 2011, Wagner and Romby 2015, Papenfort and Melamed 2023). Among these, dual-function RNAs, combining base-pairing regulatory activity with small protein functions, challenge the traditional classification of coding and noncoding transcripts, prompting a reevaluation of their regulatory potential. As discussed in this review, dual-function RNAs contributing to a wide array of cellular functions have been identified and characterized in Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as in Archaea. Studies conducted in cyanobacteria also suggest potential dual-function RNAs, indicating that more such regulatory elements could be identified in future (Kopf et al. 2014, Kopf and Hess 2015, Krauspe 2022). Additionally, dual-function RNAs with both protein coding and noncoding functions play crucial roles in plants and animals (Kumari and Sampath 2015).

Beyond dual-function RNAs, providing base-pairing functions and encoding small proteins, other sRNAs have been reported to exhibit multiple regulatory activities as well. One example is the multicellular adhesive sRNA in E. coli, which exerts its regulatory function by employing both canonical-base pairing interactions (Jorgensen et al. 2012, Thomason et al. 2012), as well as a protein-titration mechanism (Jorgensen et al. 2013). Another example is the island-encoded sRNA IsrK from Gifsy-1 prophage of Salmonella, which exists in two isoforms: a full-length, translationally inactive mRNA, and a shorter IsrK sRNA that binds to the inactive transcript, activating its translation (Hershko-Shalev et al. 2016). The functional diversity of RNA molecules capable of integrating multiple roles has also been reported for riboswitches, i.e. regulatory elements in UTRs of mRNAs that undergo structural changes upon binding to specific metabolites (Kavita and Breaker 2023). Traditionally, riboswitches act in cis, influencing transcription termination and/or translation initiation of their downstream genes. However, in Listeria monocytogenes, certain S-adenosylmethionine riboswitches, such as SreA and SreB, can also function in trans by acting as noncoding RNAs. SreA and SreB bind to the 5′ UTR of prfA mRNA, the master regulator of virulence, and repress its expression (Loh et al. 2009). Consequently, SreA and SreB provide an additional layer of posttranscriptional regulation by linking metabolite sensing to virulence gene expression.

Moving forward, recent initiatives, such as global microbial smORFs catalog, which compiles over 965 million nonredundant smORFs, have shed light on the diversity, distribution, and potential functions of small proteins in microorganisms (Duan et al. 2024). Additionally, computational tools such as Alphafold (Jumper et al. 2021) and FragFold now enable systematic modeling of protein structures and high-throughput discovery of inhibitory protein fragments (Savinov et al. 2025). As dual-function RNAs emerge as a novel class of regulatory molecules, researchers have also started to explore their potential in synthetic biology. An interesting example is the synthetic dual-function RNA MgtSR or MgtRS, comprised of MgrR sRNA and MgtS small protein from E. coli (Aoyama et al. 2021). These chimeric RNAs exemplify how dual-function RNAs can be strategically engineered to modulate biological pathways, both at the sRNA level mediated base-pairing interaction and through small protein-mediated mechanisms.
