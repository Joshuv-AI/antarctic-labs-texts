The effect of group clinical supervision on the burnout levels among midwives: a cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Christine Catling, Habtamu Kasaye, Chris Rossiter, Simeon Dale, Hala Phipps, Kathryn Farrell, Helen Donovan, Jane Maguire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBurnout in midwifery is widespread globally, influencing workforce attrition and undermining maternity service provision. Large-scale systematic and structural reforms are necessary but time-consuming. Meanwhile, midwives need immediate support for wellbeing to enable them to make sense of their workplace, workload and their practice. This research aimed to examine the impact of Group Clinical Supervision (GCS) on burnout among midwives.MethodsThis superiority cluster randomised controlled trial studied registered midwives working at public maternity services in metropolitan New South Wales, Australia. Managers, agency staff and midwifery students were excluded. Sites were randomised electronically; midwives at intervention sites received monthly, hour-long, face-to-face GCS sessions. All participating midwives were surveyed six-monthly between June 2022 and November 2024. The primary outcome was burnout, measured with the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, including sub-dimensions – personal, work-related and client-related burnout – and analysed using a mixed-effects linear regression model adjusted for covariates. Secondary outcomes were perceptions of workplace culture and the GCS sessions. Only the statistician analysing data was blinded to group allocation.ResultsTwelve maternity services were enrolled, with 980 intervention participants and 1009 controls. Burnout rates were similar for both arms, but following adjustment for covariates, the intervention was associated with a small but statistically significant reduction in overall burnout scores: mean 47.3 for midwives at intervention sites (n = 882) and 50.0 for controls (n = 913), β=-2.7, 95% CI: -4.7 to -0.6] (p = 0.013). Specifically, work-related burnout was 3.0% points lower in the intervention group (p = 0.022), although reductions in client-related and personal burnout were not statistically significant. Most participants perceived their workplace culture positively, although the control group rated their overall workplace culture significantly more highly (p < 0.001). No unintended or harmful events occurred.ConclusionThis study showed that regular GCS can lower overall and work-related burnout in midwives. The overall rate of 51.5% was similar to or lower than other studies, with similarly lower rates among older midwives. There is an urgent need for systemic changes in maternity services, encompassing institutional support strategies to mitigate burnout so that staff can better manage their professional demands.Trial registration10/05/2021 with Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry - ACTRN12621000545864p.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08866-z.

Background

Burnout among midwives is a pervasive issue globally [1–5], driven by high workload and perceived lack of recognition. Burnout is characterised by energy depletion, mental distance from one’s job and reduced professional efficacy, and has been recognised as a common result of chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed [6]. In Australia, burnout is widespread among midwives, exacerbated by a workforce described as being in a state of crisis [7]. The reasons for this are multifactorial; workforce shortages [8], poor skill mix, inadequate practice environments [9] and inflexible work options and models are all significant contributing factors [3, 7]. These issues culminate in suboptimal working conditions [9] where midwives often miss breaks during shifts and are compelled to work overtime [10, 11], leading to high levels of moral distress and compassion fatigue [12] whereby quality care for women and childbearing people is not achievable due to systemic problems [13]. Additionally, workplace stress contributes to poor peer relationships [14] and is intensified by medical dominance over midwifery care in institutions [15]. These factors have a negative impact on care quality [16] and a detrimental impact on the overall culture of the workplace [14, 17–19], which likely further contributes to the widespread burnout experienced by midwives [2, 17, 18].

Given these challenges, it is understandable that there is a high attrition rate within the midwifery workforce [7, 14, 20], with early career midwives the most common subgroup to leave [1, 14, 21]. These early career midwives have been shown to suffer more work-related mental health issues than senior staff [22]. Additionally, many older experienced midwives have expedited their retirement plans, particularly in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic [23]. This has led to unbalanced skill mixes within wards and units, making adequate staffing one of the most pressing daily issues for managers [24–26]. When rosters are not adequately filled, the resultant workload for remaining team members can amplify workplace stress and burnout [13, 26]. Consequently, these issues are linked to reduced quality of care to maternity service users and families [13, 22].

To address these issues effectively, it is imperative to adopt a comprehensive, systemic approach. Suggestions have encompassed innovative models of care that promote flexible, sustainable staffing, such as well-supported midwifery continuity of care models [22]. Support from managers is essential [27]. Most pressingly, robust mechanisms to mitigate burnout and enhance the wellbeing of midwives need to be implemented immediately [7, 22, 28]. This is essential to create a resilient and thriving midwifery workforce capable of delivering high-quality care to childbearing people and their families. One recognised and recommended support strategy is Clinical Supervision for practising midwives [7, 28].

Literature regarding midwives’ wellbeing indicates that Clinical Supervision does have positive results. Recently, Archibald et al. [29] conducted a study of 47 selected midwives using clinical psychologists to deliver Clinical Supervision over 12 months with additional debriefs. Significant improvements were found in participants’ emotional wellbeing, particularly for those who attended multiple sessions. There was high satisfaction with the service, reduced burnout and lower traumatic stress symptoms, and participants felt more fulfilled and positive about their roles. However, it was unclear what model of Clinical Supervision was used, and given that psychologists have specific skillsets in counselling, this study may have been more related to therapeutic counselling than Clinical Supervision itself. Another study of 118 midwives in Northern NSW, Australia used mixed methods to provide an overview of the impact of Clinical Supervision and the experiences of the participants [30, 31]. Again, participants found that reflection in a safe space instigated motivation, re-invigorated their work, and improved communication and teamwork. This study did have two participants who were also Clinical Supervision facilitators within the cohort, who would likely be positive about Clinical Supervision, and seven of the 12 interviewees had received Clinical Supervision from the study’s first author; hence an element of bias may have contributed to the results.

Only two randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have been identified with Clinical Supervision as the intervention. One focused on subspeciality mental health nurses, and there were differences in how supervision was delivered across sites that may have affected consistency and comparability [31], while an older pilot RCT targeted midwives and obstetricians [32]. The latter study of 30 clinicians caring for challenging families facing fetal loss found significant improvements in burnout rates following six sessions of Clinical Supervision. Limitations of this study include a small sample size with limited statistical power, no follow-up, and sessions were led by psychologists, which can be costly and logistically challenging, especially in stretched healthcare systems. Therefore, given the limited evidence on the benefits of clinical supervision for midwives, we aimed to examine the impact of regular Group Clinical Supervision on burnout with a multicentre parallel randomised controlled trial (RCT). Our hypothesis was that midwives in the sites where GCS was provided would report lower burnout.

Methods

Trial design

A multicentre parallel cluster RCT comprising 12 maternity sites (clusters) was undertaken. A cluster RCT design was used as it was most suitable for evaluating clinical supervision across multiple sites to reduce contamination between participants, reflect real-world team dynamics, and allow entire health facilities to adopt the intervention consistently. Following cluster randomisation on a 1:1 basis, midwives at six intervention sites were provided with monthly face-to-face GCS from June 2022 to November 2024. Throughout the intervention period, midwives at all sites completed surveys every six months comprising three validated tools: the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) [33], the Australian Midwifery Workplace Culture (AMWoC) tool [34] and the Clinical Supervision Evaluation Questionnaire (CSEQ) [35] (the latter for intervention sites), described more fully below.

The cluster RCT was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, registration number ACTRN12621000545864p, on 10/05/2021. The methods described below conform to CONSORT [36] and TIDieR [37] reporting guidelines (Supplementary Material). Further information on the methodology and design of the study can be found in the study protocol [blinded for review] [18].

Participants and settings

This cluster RCT employed a two-tiered recruitment strategy. At the first level, maternity units, serving as the clusters, were recruited as study sites. Sites were chosen to participate in this study if they were public maternity services and predominantly based in Sydney, New South Wales (NSW), Australia. This was for logistical reasons, as the planned GCS sessions were face-to-face. Public maternity services were chosen because they operated with similar guidelines, protocols and governance structures. We approached 13 maternity sites to participate in the study; one declined. For context, the NSW state government designates public hospitals and other healthcare services to defined geographical areas through 15 Local Health Districts (LHDs) across the state, of which six cover the wider Sydney metropolitan area.

Following engagement with the Directors of Nursing and Midwifery and the Midwifery Unit Managers of antenatal, postnatal and intrapartum areas of the chosen sites, participating clusters comprised 12 maternity sites. The sites varied in their levels of clinical maternity services from Level 3 to Level 6 (Table 1). These levels delineate the clinical services within a hospital and provide a uniform language across NSW for describing clinical services, which is important for service planning and development [38].

Individual midwives within each participating site were then recruited to participate in the intervention and data collection phases. Midwifery managers, agency staff and midwifery students were excluded from participating in the GCS sessions because managers and students often had access to alternative supportive strategies in the workplace/university, and agency staff were unlikely to be able to attend regularly. We also excluded management to ensure ward/unit staff felt safe sharing their workplace issues during GCS sessions without senior staff present.

Sample size

Originally, the sample size was based on an assumption of a binary outcome measure: prevalence of moderate or high burnout among midwives, assumed at 67% in the control group, as presented in the study protocol [blinded for review] [18]. With 12 maternity sites (six per arm) and an average cluster size of 62 (midwives per site), a total of 744 respondents (373 per arm) was estimated to achieve 80% power to detect a 15% absolute reduction in moderate burnout prevalence. A 0.02 intra-cluster correlation coefficient and a two-sided significance level of 0.05 were assumed. Allowing for 20% attrition, the target sample size was 894 participants (447 per arm), corresponding to an average of 75 midwives per site.

At the study’s completion, 1989 participants were enrolled across twelve clusters, with a mean of 165 participants per site. Burnout levels were measured as a continuous variable per the validated scale [33]. Therefore, we re-evaluated the adequacy of the sample aligned to the study aim and the actual analysis method used to detect the effect of GCS on burnout levels. Additionally, continuous outcomes generally provide better statistical efficiency than dichotomised measures, allowing the detection of smaller differences [39]. Accordingly, with 12 clusters—assuming equal cluster number and size, an inter-class coefficient (ICC) of 0.02, a design effect of 4.28, and α = 0.05 - a sample size of 232 per arm provided 99% power to detect a 15-percentage point reduction in burnout levels.

Intervention

The intervention for this study was the provision of monthly GCS by two trained Clinical Supervision facilitators for two and a half years (June 2022 – November 2024). It was clear that no sessions would consist of the same content, but for consistency of the intervention, the GCS facilitation was standardised through delivery by only two facilitators. These were senior academic/clinical midwives who had undertaken 12 days’ training in the Role Development model, prior to the study intervention period [40].

Endorsed by the Australian College of Midwives, the Australian College of Nursing and the Australian College of Mental Health Nursing, the Role Development model draws on principles from Psychodrama and Sociometry to guide clinical supervision for health professionals. The model was developed in the mid-1980s by Michael Consedine in New Zealand, and strongly focuses on a reflective, supportive, and empowering approach, focusing on the exploration of professional roles rather than problem-solving. By understanding that solutions emerge only when supervisees are ready, the supervisor’s role facilitates inquiry rather than provides answers. Through various techniques, the supervisor helps the supervisee reframe situations, gain new perspectives, and move forward constructively. The approach is deeply person-centred, aiming to strengthen the supervisee’s professional identity and capacity [40]. The structure of the sessions was appropriate to midwifery, and there were no modifications made to the GCS model throughout the study period.

The intervention was delivered at cluster level. Participants at intervention sites were strongly encouraged to attend to reflect on their practice in a safe, confidential and supportive environment. GCS sessions ran for an hour and typically included 4–6 participants in each session. Sessions were offered at a time agreed with managers to ensure the highest possibility for participants to attend, which was often at 2.30 pm during the crossover of early and late shifts. Access for midwives to attend the GCS was supported by their managers, who released staff to attend depending on the day’s acuity and staffing levels. Sessions were conducted in a suitable private room, located within five minutes’ walk from the midwives’ workplace.

Participants at the control sites received no study-related GCS and no other Clinical Supervision was offered monthly by these sites. It is possible that some midwives within these sites accessed personal supervision or counselling on an individual basis. They had access to staff well-being measures as provided by the LHD.

Randomisation

Recruitment began in 2022, when 12 maternity sites (out of 13 approached), serving as clusters, were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either intervention (n = 6 clusters) or control group (n = 6 clusters). It was not feasible to randomise individual midwives to the intervention as it would be impossible for the same groups of midwives to attend Clinical Supervision sessions every month, given tightly managed rosters, and differing weekly schedules and days off. Randomisation was concurrent with the site liaison to ensure potential participants were aware of the study and their role in the trial. Randomisation of sites to either the intervention or control arm was undertaken using the RAND function in MS Excel by the lead author. Blinding to the intervention was not possible for participants and managers who were aware of whether or not they were receiving GCS. Of the research team, only the statistician analysing data was blinded to group allocation.

Study procedures

Each site assigned a Principal Investigator (PI) who was a senior midwife, typically a Midwifery Educator or Clinical Midwifery Consultant. The PIs were responsible for informing midwives about the study and engaging them to attend the GCS sessions each month. They also booked appropriate venues and assisted with data collection.

In addition to PI input, the authors held multiple sessions at the maternity sites to discuss the study with midwifery staff prior to data collection. These sessions contained information about GCS and its potential benefits as well as the requirement for completing surveys every six months. Flyers about the study were placed in the high-traffic maternity site areas, and the dates and times of the GCS sessions were co-created with the PIs, placed on the MS Teams® calendar and the study’s research webpage [Blinded for review] [18].

Upon commencement of the intervention period, PIs at the intervention sites alerted all eligible participants (midwives) and encouraged attendance at the GCS sessions on-site every month.

Data collection and analysis

Participants in both arms completed surveys and had the option of completing online or paper-based questionnaires, both of which were distributed by the on-site PI. The surveys collected demographic information, midwifery qualifications, area of primary work, years of service, midwives’ intention to leave their position or profession, and the number of GCS or other support sessions attended within the six-month period (Table 2). If participants stated their intention to leave their position or profession, additional questions on the frequency of their thoughts and reasons were asked. Workforce data on midwifery staff turnover and sick leave were also collected from relevant LHD workforce departments to correspond with each six-month period. Participants completed the surveys on six occasions throughout the study period, starting a month before the intervention (May 2022 – baseline data) and finishing at the intended end of the intervention in November 2024.

Data were collected and stored with Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a cloud-based platform for managing online surveys in research settings [41]. Any data from hardcopy versions of the online survey, and consent forms, were added onto this platform by a research assistant.

Measurements

Outcomes

The primary outcome comprised burnout levels in midwives, and secondary outcomes were the perceived quality of the GCS, perceptions of workplace culture and midwives’ intention to stay in their role/profession; all were measured at the individual level. We also collected sick leave rates and the number of midwives who have left the organisations (not reported here).

Three reliable and validated tools were used in the surveys: the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) [33], the Australian Midwifery Workplace Culture (AMWoC) instrument [34] and the Clinical Supervision Evaluation Questionnaire (CSEQ) [35] (intervention sites only). The CBI was used to measure the midwives’ burnout levels, with a 19-item, 5-point Likert scale that measures personal (6 items), work-related (7 items) and client-related levels of burnout (6 items) [33]. The overall internal consistency of the scale was excellent (Cronbach’s Alpha/α = 0.938), with domain-specific reliability coefficients ranging from 0.869 to 0.89, indicating good internal consistency across subscales. Each item was converted into a percentage score ranging from 0% (no burnout) to 100% (maximum burnout), applicable at both the item and domain/total scale levels. Overall burnout and domain-specific mean scores were calculated by averaging the scores of all items in total and the respective items within each subscale (personal, work-related, and client-related burnout).

The AMWoC is a psychometrically tested 22-item, 6-point Likert scale questionnaire that measures midwives’ perceptions of their workplace culture. It aims to understand the dimensions of a midwifery work environment and has three domains: Relationship with manager, Collegiality, and Empowerment [34]. Total scores possible range from 22 (very poor workplace culture) to 132 (excellent workplace culture). The internal consistency of the scale was very high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92), indicating strong reliability of measuring individual perceptions of workplace culture (Table 4). Lastly, the CSEQ measured the purpose, process and impact of the GCS sessions comprising 14 items on a 5-point Likert scale (results not reported in this paper).

Covariates

Participants’ demographic characteristics were collected (Table 2). In the model that evaluated the effect of intervention, both the effect of these demographic characteristics and the confounding effect of the participants’ perception of workplace culture were controlled.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the characteristics of study participants. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between the control and intervention groups were conducted using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, at both baseline and subsequent surveys. Overall, six surveys were conducted, including the baseline and five subsequent surveys, in which respondents were prospectively included in both the intervention and control groups. Each participant may have completed multiple surveys, and the overall analysis was based on all responses combined. Hence, the analysis reflects the responses provided across surveys and the N does not necessarily correspond to the number of midwives at the study sites.

Missing values in outcome variables (15%) were addressed by conducting multiple imputation by chained equations, generating 20 imputed datasets after the missingness mechanism was assessed. To evaluate the adjusted effect of GCS on burnout levels, mixed-effects linear regression models were conducted, adjusting for relevant covariates and clustering at the facility level (n = 12 clusters). Variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate analyses, as well as those supported by prior literature, were considered for inclusion to ensure theoretically relevant factors retained and residual confounding were minimised in the multivariable model. Random intercept models were used to account for facility-level clustering when estimating effects on overall burnout and the three burnout sub-domain scores.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18. Hypotheses were tested at a significance level of 0.05 with two-sided alternatives. Point estimates of coefficients were reported along with their 95% confidence intervals. Model fit was assessed using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Multicollinearity and other model assumptions were also evaluated.

Ethical processes

Ethical approval for all levels of consent was obtained from the Lead Research Ethics and Governance Office at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, Sydney (X21-0230 & 2021/ETH00414), as well as from the Human Research Ethics Committees (HRECs) of each participating site. Final ratification was provided by the HREC of the affiliated tertiary institution (ETH21-6800). Informed consent was gained from all maternity sites and participants.

Consent

Potential participants read the Participant Information Statement (PIS). Midwives completing the hardcopy versions provided written consent and those completing an online survey clicked ‘I consent to participate’ in the online PIS which opened the survey. For participants attending the GCS intervention, consent was implied by attendance. This was deemed adequate as attendance was similar to other educational or support sessions within participants’ maternity unit. A ‘Cluster Guardianship’ agreement was established within the participating LHDs which outlined that written consent was only necessary for data collection via the surveys, and not the intervention itself. Finally, due to the confidential nature of GCS, no data were collected from the sessions themselves, although the number of attended sessions were recorded by participants within the six-monthly surveys.

Given that GCS consists of reflection of workplace issues, they can sometimes cause emotional distress; hence protocols were developed for facilitators to use if any participant became distressed. The protocol listed the Employee Assistance Program telephone numbers (for free counselling), as well as other supports, and this information was also embedded into the online and hardcopy PIS and surveys. Any distress or difficulties the GCS facilitators experienced was mitigated through monthly sessions with their own external Clinical Supervisor.

Results

Description of participants by intervention group

Between June 2022 and November 2024, a total of 2012 participants were prospectively enrolled across all clusters through six consecutive surveys conducted at six-month intervals, as proposed in the RCT protocol [Blinded for review] [18]. After excluding 23 participants due to incomplete information in the first survey and also during consecutive surveys, 1989 participants were retained for analysis. While socio-demographic data were largely complete, only 1710 participants had complete outcome data (burnout items) at all surveys. The number of participants per cluster ranged from 75 to 244 (median = 164 participants). Approximately one-third of participants in both intervention (335/978, [34.3%]) and the control (339/1008, [33.6%]) groups were aged 21–30 years, and nearly all participants identified as female in both intervention (961/970, [99.1%]) and control groups (968/994, [97.4%)]. A slightly higher proportion of control group participants reported having dependants at home (543/983, [55.2%]) compared to the intervention group (481/951, [50.6%], p = 0.040), while a slightly higher number of midwives in the intervention group held a bachelor’s degree (352/977, [36.0%]) compared to the control group (322/1005, [32.0%], p = 0.045). Participants’ perceptions of workplace culture

Participants’ perceptions of their workplace culture were assessed using 22 items adapted from the AMWoC instrument. Perceptions were classified into three domains: Relationship with Manager (seven items), Empowerment (11 items) and Collegiality (four items). Item-level ratings are presented in the Supplementary File. All three domains, and most of their sub-items, showed left-skewed distribution, suggesting that most participants perceived their workplace culture positively. There were no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups in the Relationship with manager and Empowerment domains. However, significant differences were observed between the two groups in the overall workplace culture and Collegiality domain scores. The control group showed relatively higher positive perception of their workplace culture overall, with a mean sum score of 80.3 vs. 74.0 in the intervention group (p < 0.001). The Collegiality domain score was significantly higher in the intervention group (M = 18.7(2.8)) compared to the control group (M = 17.9(3.3), p < 0.001). This domain encompassed items related to mutual respect among colleagues, trust and reliance on co-workers, team friendliness, and alignment in the philosophy of care (Table 4).

Burnout

Burnout levels were assessed across three domains in the initial (baseline) survey prior to the intervention: personal, work-related and client-related burnout. No statistically significant differences were observed at baseline between the intervention and control groups in the overall burnout score (p = 0.511) or in any of the three domains (Table 5). Mean overall burnout score for all participants was 51.5% (SD = 18.4, 95% CI: 50.6 to 52.3).

The personal and work-related burnout scores were higher than the total and client-related scores in both groups, with mean scores of approximately 56% among all responses. Client-related burnout scores were lower in both the control group (M = 39.3, SD = 30.7) and the intervention group (M = 38.6, SD = 31.6) among responses to all six surveys.

Item-level burnout scores are presented in the Supplementary File. The highest mean score was observed for the work-related item assessing emotional exhaustion (“Is your work emotionally exhausting?”), with a mean of 66.4% (SD = 22.6). A large proportion of participants in both groups reported a high or very high degree of emotional exhaustion. Similarly, high mean scores were observed for personal burnout items related to fatigue, including “How often do you feel tired?” (M = 65.4%) and “How often are you physically exhausted?” (M = 65.9%). In contrast, the lowest mean scores were found in the client-related domain. For example, the item “Do you find it hard to work with clients?” had a mean score of 28.5% (SD = 24.2), and “Are you tired of working with clients?” had a mean score of 28.9% (SD = 26.5).

Effect of intervention on burnout levels

In the bivariate analysis, participant age, qualifications, work area, presence of dependants living in the household, marital status, perceptions of workplace culture, intention to leave the current position, and consideration of leaving the midwifery profession were analysed for association with burnout levels, using a significance threshold of p < 0.20 to maximise the number of covariates included in the multivariable analysis and thereby minimise residual confounding. In the unadjusted analysis, there was no statistically significant difference in burnout scores across all surveys between intervention and control participants (p = 0.592 for overall burnout). However, the mixed-effects linear regression indicates significant changes in burnout levels when the effects of confounding and other covariates were kept constant. Table 6 presents the results of the mixed-effects linear regression models conducted to determine the adjusted effects of GCS and other covariates’ association with burnout scores throughout the intervention period. Across all models, the ICCs for burnout scores were low (ICC = 0.003 for overall burnout and 0.006 for work-related burnout), indicating minimal between-facility variation in burnout levels and substantial individual-level variability.

Overall, based on the mixed-effects linear regression models, GCS resulted in a small but statistically significant reduction in overall burnout and work-related burnout. The mean overall burnout score in the intervention group was 2.6% points lower than in the control group (β=-2.6, 95% CI: -4.7% to -0.5%, p = 0.013). Similarly, mean work-related burnout was lower by 3.0% points (β=-3.0,95% CI: -5.5 to -0.4, p = 0.022) in the intervention group compared to controls. Although the point estimates were lower in the intervention group, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in personal or client-related burnout domains. Although all domains of burnout, as well as overall burnout, generally decreased over time in parallel with the provision of GCS, the data did not show statistically significant changes over time (Supplementary Material). The observed changes in burnout level were small, remaining below 1% point across the six-monthly measurement points over the study period. For example, work-related burnout was reduced by 0.37% points in average as time moves by six months forward (β = −0.37, 95% CI: −1.1 to 0.4, p = 0.343) (Table 6).

Several covariates were also statistically significantly associated with burnout scores. Age, educational qualification, work area, and intention to leave current position and considering leaving the profession were all linked to differences in burnout levels. Older midwives, particularly those aged over 60, reported significantly lower overall burnout scores compared to those aged 21–30 (β = − 7.9, 95% CI: − 12.6 to − 3.4, p < 0.001). This pattern held across all three burnout domains, suggesting a lower likelihood of developing burnout among older midwives compared to those in their twenties.

Regarding qualifications, there was no significant difference in burnout scores between those with a Bachelor of Midwifery and those with other credentials. However, midwives with a hospital-based certificate had burnout scores that were between 7.0 and 9.0% points lower than those with a bachelor’s degree, depending on the domain. The work setting also played a role – midwives working in community settings had significantly lower overall burnout compared to those working in antenatal care clinics (β = -8.4, 95% CI: -12.8 to -4.1, p < 0.001). Participants’ positive perception of workplace culture was also statistically significantly associated with lower burnout score across all domains (Table 6).

Discussion

This innovative study addressed the significant issue of burnout among a group of Australian midwives, which has substantial consequences for the wellbeing and retention of the midwifery workforce, and the quality of maternity services provided to childbearing people. It is, to our knowledge, the largest controlled trial to explore the impact of Group Clinical Supervision (GCS) on midwives’ burnout levels globally.

Our results indicate that having access to or attending GCS sessions is associated with significantly lower levels of overall burnout, largely related to lower work-related burnout scores. Provision of GCS for midwives showed a small but significant effect on work-related burnout (-3.0; 95% CI: -5.5 to -0.4, p = 0.022), highlighting a unique finding. This finding builds on previous observational studies consistently reporting the benefits of Clinical Supervision, particularly for its ability to improve staff retention, job satisfaction, and lower stress and burnout among midwives [30, 32] and other healthcare professionals [7–12]. Several mechanisms may have contributed to the positive effect of GCS on burnout levels. These include the emotional support from peers during GCS, and the provision of a safe space for midwives to express their frustration, anxiety, and self-doubt related to their professional role [43]. Despite its demonstrated benefits, the frequency and duration of Clinical Supervision, as well as supervisor competency were identified as critical factors for its effectiveness [44, 45].

The lower burnout level for the intervention group was not related solely to the participants who attended the GCS sessions, so this finding may also be related to the knowledge that a known supportive strategy was available to midwives, even if they did not take up the opportunity. This view is supported by the finding that more positive perceptions of workplace culture were significantly associated with lower burnout rates (especially personal burnout). The significant changes in work-related burnout, but not personal or client-related burnout, observed in our study may suggest the beneficial impact of wider factors in the work environment at the intervention sites. These factors, together with direct peer and professional support and skill improvement through the GCS, may be responsible for the improvement in work-related burnout, rather than addressing personal and client-related factors [45]. Although GCS may influence participants by modelling and encouraging a healthy balance between work and personal life, its focus is often on professional challenges and workplace issues. It may be insufficient to mitigate deep-rooted personal burnout arising from non-work-related stressors, such as poor work-life balance, financial or family issues, which are often too sensitive to disclose in group settings and may instead require individual one-to-one supervision [46]. Likewise, client-related burnout may not be effectively reduced due to chronic exposure to trauma and feelings of moral distress [22]. Nevertheless, the observed reduction in overall burnout in addition to the work-related burnout shows the reassuring positive effect of group clinical supervision among midwives.

Given the links between work-related burnout results and institutional factors such as work pressures and organisational support [47, 48], it is clearly important to maximise this relationship in the context of Australian midwifery services. Our study focused on GCS as one critical component of institutional support – albeit at a low intensity of one hour of supervision per month. Further research could explore the impact of different doses of Clinical Supervision relative to other workplace innovations and supports for midwives to mitigate work-related burnout and further the effects of GCS to enhance personal and client related burnout as well.

This study found an overall burnout level of 51.5% which is consistent with others [26, 49] and reflects findings from the 2005 Psychosocial Work Environment and Burnout (PUMA) study by Borritz et al. [50]. PUMA was a longitudinal investigation into how workplace factors contribute to burnout among health service professionals, including midwives, finding that the latter had the highest levels of burnout overall, and that they had been suffering for many years in this respect. More recently others have reported burnout rates in midwives, such as the EXPert study [3]. This study used the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, finding the level of work-related burnout among midwives working at a tertiary maternity service in Melbourne, Australia was alarmingly high at 63%. Again, similar to our findings, burnout was particularly prevalent among younger and early-career midwives [3]. Additionally, many midwives indicated increased alcohol consumption due to stress and a desire to leave the profession, similar to findings from the UK [51]. The authors expanded upon their work a few years later (2022) finding higher rates of burnout (72% personal and 67% work-related burnout) in the Victorian midwifery workforce [52]. Five years earlier, another large Australian study of over 1000 midwives by the Work, Health and Emotional Lives of Midwives (WHELM) consortium found 64.9% reported moderate to high personal burnout and 43.8% had moderate to high work-related burnout [1]. Following the expansion of this survey to the UK, 80% of midwives reported personal burnout, two-thirds experienced work-related burnout, and overall burnout was more common in midwives under 40, those with disabilities, or those working in hospital rotation roles [21]. Many other authors globally have found similarly high rates of burnout within the midwifery profession [1, 3, 5, 49, 53–55]. Given these findings, and the relationship between burnout and mental health, the wellbeing of midwives is of growing concern demanding intervention.

Other studies using the CBI have found that organisational interventions can reduce staff exhaustion, the core component of burnout. For example, a 2023 meta-analysis by Bes et al. [56] showed promise that organisational changes, especially those that empower staff and address workload, can help reduce burnout. Similarly, a systematic review of organisational interventions to increase healthcare staff wellbeing, found that 29 out of 33 included studies were successful in doing so [57]. Another study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic showed how higher perceived organisational support was significantly associated with a lower risk for burnout (-0.23, 95% CI: -0.26 to -0.21, p < 0.001) and lower anxiety (-0.07, 95% CI: -0.09 to -0.06, p = 0.010) [47]. The authors concluded that strengthening the connection between healthcare organisations and their staff may help alleviate psychological distress among health professionals, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes through addressing organisational factors.

Organisational factors clearly impact midwives’ rates of work-related burnout and other issues that determine their work experiences and capacity to provide high quality care, as well as the effectiveness of interventions such as GCS. These comprise issues such as staff shortages, acuity, and poor workplace cultures which have been well documented [3, 47, 49, 58, 59]. Similarly, the current study may have also shown that organisational support perceived by midwives in the intervention sites resulted in positive findings in reducing work-related burnout in addition to the intervention effect; the high rating of a negative work culture perception amongst the intervention group at baseline also justifies the small intervention effect. It is acknowledged that GCS alone may not be effective in larger scale burnout reduction in midwives unless these organisational factors are addressed.

Participants’ individual circumstances also influence their experience of burnout. Across both arms, older midwives, those working in the community, qualified with a hospital-based certificate, and midwives who had a positive perception of workplace culture tended to report lower burnout, whilst considering leaving the profession and their current position was associated with incrementally higher burnout levels. Lower rates of burnout among midwives over 60 years of age was identified in this study, which aligns in part with a US study [60] of how nurses’ age and burnout levels interacted in predicting physical and psychological work ability. It showed that physical work ability tended to decline with age, and this decline was exacerbated by higher levels of burnout. When burnout was severe, older workers also showed reduced psychological work capacity, suggesting that they may struggle to mobilise the necessary physical and mental resources to meet job demands. However, when burnout levels were low, older workers demonstrated stronger psychological work ability, implying that under favourable conditions, they may be better able to draw on cognitive, emotional, and professional strengths that develop with age. However, others have also found lower levels of burnout in younger midwives [61, 62]. Other reasons for older midwives having fewer burnout symptoms may include a greater likelihood of part-time work. Although not assessed here, part-time employment has been found to increase job satisfaction and reduce burnout among midwives working in an Australian caseload midwifery group practice, while maintaining high quality of care for service users [63]. Older midwives may be more responsive than younger cohorts to interventions such as Clinical Supervision. This may be related to their relative seniority to younger midwives and the possibility of more autonomous roles which makes it easier to attend workplace support sessions. However, this contradicts some studies that state younger healthcare staff access support at a higher rate than older staff, in particular, mental health services in the US [64].These and other findings [65, 66] underscore the importance of targeted mental health support and resilience-building strategies tailored to different generational needs.

Other demographic factors associated with lower rates of burnout were midwives having a hospital-based certificate as their highest qualification, very likely correlated with older age, and employment in community settings or, to a lesser extent, working in at least two areas. This suggests that burnout is less common in areas of practice where midwives have greater autonomy, flexibility and variety, consistent with findings of a recent systematic review [53]. Our results further highlight the need for systemic changes in midwifery services, including but not limited to Clinical Supervision, to improve midwives’ autonomy and the practice environment.

It is deeply troubling that midwives, who are profoundly committed to their profession [67], are experiencing burnout, not from a lack of passion for midwifery, but by the very systems and demands of the hospital environments in which they work - systems that often conflict with the core values and philosophy of midwifery [7, 14, 68, 69]. However, resignations may be mitigated through certain models of care whereby midwives can work to their full scope of practice and maintain relational continuity with service users. Our study showed that those working in a continuity of care model had similar levels of both burnout and thoughts of leaving their professions to those working in standard care models. Despite this, others have found that working in continuity models, such as Caseload and Midwifery Group Practice (MGP) is protective for burnout [10, 49, 70–73]. Our findings may reflect that Caseload and MGP models were not specifically categorized within the survey, and the option ‘continuity of care model’ may have been interpreted differently by some participants.

Strengths and limitations

This paper reports a large-scale cluster RCT, with nearly 2000 midwife participants at diverse sites, to examine the impact of GCS as a workplace-based initiative. Other strengths of the study include that both GCS facilitators were trained in the same Role Development Model to aid consistency of practice, most public hospital maternity units in Sydney were included, and the intervention was sustained face-to-face over 2.5 years.

Our study has several limitations, including issues related to the cluster design, missing outcome data, selection bias and a limited number of clusters. As with any cluster RCT, there is a possibility that residual confounders could influence the effect sizes and individuals’ burnout levels at the cluster level (maternity site), despite randomisation and statistical adjustment during analysis. For instance, participants in the intervention group rated workplace culture lower than those in the control group, which may suggest that the introduction of the GCS inadvertently amplified pre-existing workplace dysfunction at the intervention sites, or alternatively, that the absence of GCS at the control sites played a role. However, the intervention group had already rated their workplace culture lower than the control group at baseline, prior to the initiation of GCS sessions. This indicates little evidence of change attributable to the introduction of GCS, suggesting that the observed difference is more likely due to unmeasured institutional or structural factors, or simply to chance.

Another limitation is related to the measurement tool used. We measured burnout using the CBI, which has been criticised for issues related to validity, measurement error and responsiveness [74]. However, it has also been praised for its comprehensiveness compared with other instruments, such as the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory [75] and the Maslach Burnout Inventory [74, 76]. The CBI addresses limitations observed in other instruments by distinguishing between work-related, personal, and client-related burnout, dimensions that can interact and contribute to one another [33]. Moreover, it is widely used in studies of the psychological effects of midwifery work in Australia [22] and it allowed us to assess both overall (composite) burnout and its specific domains [33].

Our study attempted to schedule sessions to maximise attendance, with times/days negotiated with onsite Principal Investigators. However, staff shortages and high acuity on the scheduled days negatively affected participation, leading to limited time series data. These factors were beyond our control, as was the ability for participants to attend regularly, which has been shown to provide the most benefits, and these may have contributed to the small effect sizes observed related to the GCS. Additionally given that different midwives completed the surveys at each timepoint, they were not strictly comparable—which may also have limited the significant decline of burnout over time. Future research could focus on the dose effect of GCS in relation to participant burnout rates.

Although multiple imputation was used, missing data may still have introduced some imprecision. Another limitation is the relatively small number of clusters, six per intervention group, which may have constrained our ability to fully adjust for cluster-level variability. However, low ICCs for burnout scores indicate that most of the variability in our study was at the individual level, suggesting that the observed effectiveness of the GCS may be generalisable to other maternity sites.

Conclusion

Our study has shown that there is an urgent need for systemic changes in high-stress professions like midwifery to encompass institutional support strategies that enable staff to cope with their professional demands. We found a significant relationship between GCS and lower burnout rates for midwives in the intervention sites. Given the intervention was monthly, GCS may have the ability to further lower burnout if offered more frequently or scaled to accommodate all areas of maternity services and more tailored to the needs of midwives, including offering ‘closed’ groups where the same staff attend each session. This potential should be explored in further RCTs. Scaling up the provision of GCS could have profound effects on the present attrition of skilled midwives currently occurring in health services, nationally and internationally, and would ultimately positively affect the care of women, childbearing people and babies.
