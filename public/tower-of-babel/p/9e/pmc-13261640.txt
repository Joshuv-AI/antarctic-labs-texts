Opinion statement regarding the use of post-operative radiotherapy after surgery for pathological fractures of the long bones: An answer to the points raised regarding methodology and analysis
Authors: Jessica Ehne, Panagiotis Tsagkozis
Published: 2026

To the Editor, Journal of Bone Oncology,

We read with keen interest the letter by Shing Fung Lee et al. commenting on the findings of our retrospective cohort study recently published in the Journal of Bone Oncology and compliment the authors for the thorough appraisal of our paper.

We are content that this topic has attracted attention, since there is very little evidence for giving post-operative radiotherapy for pathological fractures of the long bones. It is noteworthy that there is such strong support for its use by the authors, when to our knowledge the only study directly comparing patients that had post-operative radiotherapy and the ones who did not was the landmark study published by Townsend et al, including 60 patients, and where the number of events (secondary surgery) in the whole cohort were only 5 [1], [2]. It should be noted that we did not have function or pain as our primary outcome as in the Townsend study, since implant failure is a more solid event widely used in relevant orthopaedic research, that can be objectively measured even retrospectively in reliable databases. The use of a cox regression analysis based on only 5 events per variable in the Townsend cohort is uncertain [3], [4], [5]. Another shortcoming of that study is that it included only 25% prosthetic reconstructions, which is very commonly used today (for example in most patients in our cohort). Moreover, the prosthetic implants included (Austin-Moore, Harris-Gallante etc.) are no longer used today, and were often used uncemented (not mentioned in detail in the manuscript). Furthermore, there was an imbalance in the compared groups, where 49% of patients had impending fractures in the group receiving radiotherapy, which are less prone to failure [6], as compared to 28% in the ones with surgery only. Lastly, the methodology did not account for death as a competing risk event, neither was the reason to secondary surgery evaluated.

Current guidelines and recommendations are thus primarily based on the aforementioned landmark retrospective study, with legacy data (patients treated between 1979–1992), limited representability regarding treatment methods, and inadequacy regarding the study of the outcome of secondary surgery due to tumour progression, as mentioned above. The knowledge gap was obvious, and thus our effort to provide more evidence. We are well aware of the limitations of our study, given its retrospective nature, and we have explicitly recognized them in the manuscript. Yet, since in our opinion there is an absence of higher quality evidence (prospective randomized studies pending), we believe our data provide a basis for informed decision making with patients. We agree that a good retrospective study providing an indication for the value of postoperative radiotherapy is the one of Rosen et al [7], with a more adequate cohort size and methodology, and with oncological data that more closely correspond to current treatments. Yet, in this study there was no comparison with patients not receiving radiotherapy. Moreover, there were no prostheses included. Notably, in this study the number of patients that had secondary surgery due to tumour progression was 11 out of 138 (8%), marginally higher than the proportion of patients in the osteosynthesis group who did not receive radiotherapy in our study.

In their letter, the authors raise some points for discussion. First, they object to the inclusion of patients who had preoperative radiotherapy. We chose to do this since it is closer to clinical praxis, where quite a lot of patients have received prior radiotherapy. In the clinical setting, it is difficult to set a cut-off for excluding patients with preoperative radiotherapy (time-points and doses). Importantly, when these patients were excluded from the analysis, the result did not change: post-operative radiotherapy was associated with higher tumour-related failure rate in prostheses (p = 0.009) but not osteosythesis (p = 0.257). When the analysis was limited to patients that had not received pre-operative radiotherapy and survived more than 3 months, there was no significant association (p = 0.063 for prosthesis and p = 0.499 for osteosynthesis). This would not alter our interpretation of the data.

The authors further claim that in pre-irradiated patients the radiotherapy cannot be seen as neoadjuvant. We agree, radiotherapy for a pathological fracture is rarely given in a neoadjuvant setting. They also comment on the low number of events, which is true, but then again multiple times higher than in the Townsend study. The low number of events actually stresses another important finding: the event of failure, especially among prostheses, is very low. Given a low baseline risk of the studied population, the indication of post-operative radiotherapy is per definition weak, since the number needed to treat to prevent a failure would be high [8]. They also object to the visual presentation of the findings, which is in our opinion a matter of secondary importance. The same applies for data presented as data not shown, since due to the limitations in the size of the text we were obliged to prioritize among the information to be presented in detail.

The other important point raised is the time-frame of given radiotherapy. We have done analyses at 6-week, 3-month and 6-month post-surgery time-points, and we did not find any evidence of a positive association between post-operative radiotherapy and lower implant failure rate.

Moreover, they make a point for confounding by indication and selection biases. We agree, this is the major source of uncertainty in our study. However, such confounding and selection bias is an issue with all retrospective studies. It can be mitigated by multivariable analyses, propensity score matching etc. but never completely neutralized, and only prospective randomized studies (such as the PORTRAIT) can provide data of higher quality. We have very clearly recognized limitations regarding selection bias and the retrospective nature which is prone to confounding by indication, and do not claim causal interference. We are rather reporting, discussing, and concluding on the associations we found. At the same time, to our knowledge, there is no published study with more solid data to answer our primary question, and in the absence of better evidence we consider our results as useful for clinicians.

The authors of the letter further comment on our hypothesis regarding the use of radiotherapy in arthroplasty with use of bone cement, and claim a general misconception of the effect of radiotherapy. We regret that they have misunderstood this point. We do not argue that radiotherapy is inefficient in killing tumour cells in general, nor did not examine if patients had subclinical tumour growth. Our suggestion is that the combined effect of optimal debulking of the tumour, which is easier during arthroplasty than when using an intramedullary nail, the thermal effect of cement (known to have adjuvant properties in primary bone neoplasms [9], and the use of an implant that is more tolerant to mechanical failure probably renders post-operative radiotherapy redundant. This simply means that in the clinical setting studied in this cohort, adequate surgery can render radiotherapy superfluous regarding the examined primary endpoint of implant-related failure. It should also be emphasized that patients in our cohort were as a general rule operated by experienced tumour orthopaedic surgeons and the outcome of the intervention may differ in other situations.

We recognize that retrospective cohort studies cannot provide robust evidence, acknowledge that our data is contradictory to current guidelines and look forward to the results from the upcoming multicenter prospective trial. Until then, regarding the risk of a patient having to undergo repeated surgery, we propose that radiotherapy should be individualized and discussed between the oncologist, orthopaedic surgeon and patient, rather than be given on a routine basis to all. Patients are generally fragile and have short expected survival, and when surgical treatment is optimal, they may be spared the time to attend radiotherapy clinics, especially protocols involving treatment over many weeks. This can also free radiotherapy resources in burdened healthcare systems. Whether short-course radiotherapy regimens improve functional outcomes such as pain and range of movement, as well as the overall health economic aspects of this treatment remain to be shown. Yet, under all circumstances, the relatively low risk of implant failure due to tumour growth and the absence of any association between post-operative radiotherapy and lower failure rate (especially when cemented prostheses were used) are findings that should be taken into account in clinical praxis and shared decision-making.
