SynBio-DSP: an integrated synthetic biology platform with fed-batch fermentation and continuous downstream processing for sustainable production of thermostable cellulases
Authors: Shah Faisal Mohammad, Fawad Ali, Mamirkulova Shynara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe economic viability of enzymatic lignocellulosic biomass conversion is hindered by high production costs and energy-intensive recovery of thermostable enzymes. Traditional optimization approaches treat strain engineering, fermentation, and downstream processing as separate domains, leading to suboptimal system performance. This study aimed to develop an integrated platform that synergistically combines synthetic biology, machine learning-guided bioprocess optimization, and continuous downstream processing for sustainable cellulase production.ResultsWe engineered a Bacillus subtilis chassis with a Quorum-Lysis-Modulation (QLM) system for auto-induction of hyperthermostable cellulase CtCel9A and CRISPRi-mediated cell wall weakening. The QLM strain achieved a CtCel9A titer of 9.32 ± 0.29 IU/mL, a 3.6-fold increase over conventional methods. Fermentation was optimized using Gaussian Process Regression in high-throughput microbioreactors, yielding excellent predictive performance (R2 = 0.94). A novel continuous Pulsed Electric Field-Assisted Synergistic Lysis (PEF-ASL) system was developed and optimized via Response Surface Methodology (Box-Behnken Design), achieving 96.8% enzyme recovery with specific energy consumption of 0.11 kWh/g, a 68% reduction compared to bead milling. The PEF system was operated at 100 Hz pulse frequency with 30 ± 2 µs pulse width using monopolar exponential decay waveform, and the specific energy input was calculated as 0.098 kWh/g, consistent with the measured value. Multi-objective optimization using NSGA-III balanced yield, energy consumption, water intensity, and production cost. After re-optimization with a full cost function including waste treatment, quality control, contingency, and facility overhead, Pareto front analysis identified a knee-point solution representing the optimal compromise among competing objectives. System-wide optimization reduced production costs by 52–58% and carbon footprint by 57% compared to conventional enzyme production, with techno-economic analysis for a 500 kg/year facility indicating a manufacturing cost of $132/kg, net present value of $4.7 million, and payback period of 3.8 years. The sfGFP fusion at the C-terminus reduced specific activity by 8.3% compared to unfused enzyme but enabled real-time fluorescence monitoring critical for auto-induction timing.ConclusionsThe SynBio-DSP platform establishes a new paradigm for sustainable biocatalyst manufacturing by co-optimizing cellular design and process configuration. The integrated approach demonstrates that simultaneous improvements in productivity, cost, and environmental impact are achievable for scalable, economically viable biofuel enzyme production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-026-02783-1.

Introduction

The transition toward a sustainable bio-economy requires efficient, low-cost production of robust biocatalysts for lignocellulosic biomass deconstruction [1]. Thermostable cellulases are particularly valuable due to their enhanced stability under industrial processing conditions, but their commercial production faces persistent challenges, including low expression yields and energy-intensive recovery processes [2, 3].

Conventional approaches to enzyme production optimization typically address strain engineering, fermentation, and downstream processing as separate domains [4]. While significant advances have been made in synthetic biology for microbial engineering [5, 6] and in process intensification for unit operation optimization [7], these advances often remain siloed. This fragmented approach yields suboptimal system performance, as improvements in one domain may inadvertently compromise another [8, 9]. For instance, genetic modifications that boost enzyme yield may alter cellular properties in ways that complicate downstream processing.

Continuous downstream processing offers transformative opportunities for efficiency gains, particularly in reducing energy consumption and improving product recovery [10, 11]. Concurrently, machine learning approaches enable more efficient exploration of complex bioprocess parameter spaces than traditional design-of-experiments methods [12, 13]. However, these technologies have not been systematically integrated with synthetic biology to create a cohesive platform where cellular design and process configuration are co-optimized from inception.

This study introduces SynBio-DSP, an integrated platform that synergistically combines synthetic biology, machine learning-guided dynamic bioprocess control, and continuous downstream processing for sustainable enzyme production. We demonstrate this approach using a model hyperthermostable cellulase (CtCel9A) expressed in an engineered Bacillus subtilis host. Our platform features three innovations: (1) A Quorum-Lysis-Modulation (QLM) genetic system enabling coordinated production and programmed cellular fragility [14, 15]; (2) A machine learning framework using Gaussian Process Regression for dynamic fermentation optimization beyond traditional response surface methodology [16]; (3) A novel continuous Pulsed Electric Field-Assisted Synergistic Lysis (PEF-ASL) system for low-energy, high-efficiency product recovery [17].

Through multi-objective optimization incorporating techno-economic and environmental metrics, we establish a paradigm for sustainable biocatalyst manufacturing that simultaneously maximizes yield while minimizing resource consumption and environmental impact [18].

Results

Engineered QLM strain characterization and performance

We engineered Bacillus subtilis 168 with a comprehensive Quorum-Lysis-Modulation (QLM) system to coordinate enzyme production with enhanced cell lysis susceptibility [19]. The system comprises: (i) ctcel9A under ComQXPA quorum-sensing control with a C-terminal 6xHis-tag and sfGFP reporter [20]; (ii) CRISPRi targeting pbpA, dacA, and mreB under an IPTG-inducible Pspank promoter to weaken the cell wall [15]; (iii) a PBSX lysis cassette under a temperature-sensitive cI857-pR promoter; and (iv) chaperone operons (groES-groEL, dnaK-dnaJ-grpE, tig) under a strong synthetic promoter P104 to enhance soluble expression [21]. The hierarchical regulatory design follows a temporal coordination strategy: quorum sensing serves as the primary metabolic sensor ensuring production only after sufficient biomass accumulation, IPTG-inducible CRISPRi provides independent control over cell wall weakening timing, the temperature-sensitive cI857-pR promoter enables clean external lysis triggering without chemical inducers, and constitutive chaperone expression ensures continuous folding support throughout the production phase. A schematic of this regulatory hierarchy is provided in Supplementary Figure S4.

The QLM strain initiated CtCel9A expression at OD600 = 18.3 ± 1.2, coinciding with ComX pheromone accumulation to 62.5 ± 4.8 nM. CRISPRi induction reduced target gene expression (pbpA: 72 ± 3%, dacA: 68 ± 4%, mreB: 55 ± 5%; p < 0.001) and decreased cell wall thickness by 41% (28.5 ± 1.8 nm vs. 48.3 ± 2.1 nm in the wild type; p < 0.001). Chaperone co-expression increased soluble CtCel9A yield by 58 ± 6% and specific activity by 22 ± 3%. Temperature-induced lysis at 42 °C achieved 92 ± 2% cell disruption efficiency with < 5% enzyme activity loss, validating the programmed cellular fragility concept. However, complete thermal lysis resulted in net functional enzyme recovery of only 78.4 ± 3.1% after accounting for thermal denaturation during the extended 45–60-min treatment at 42 °C. The sfGFP fusion at the C-terminus, separated by a flexible (GGGGS)₃ linker, reduced specific activity by 8.3% (4750 ± 110 IU/mg for fusion versus 5180 ± 140 IU/mg for unfused enzyme; p = 0.023) but enabled real-time fluorescence monitoring essential for precise auto-induction timing in the QLM system [22].

Metabolic burden analysis revealed progressive growth rate reduction across strain variants: wild-type B. subtilis 168 (μmax = 0.72 ± 0.03 h⁻1), QLM strain without induction (μmax = 0.68 ± 0.03 h⁻1, 8% reduction from constitutive chaperone expression), QLM with CRISPRi induction only (μmax = 0.58 ± 0.04 h⁻1, additional 15% reduction from dCas9 and guide RNA synthesis), and fully induced QLM with enzyme production (μmax = 0.45 ± 0.04 h⁻1, 37% total reduction). Complete fermentation profiles including growth curves, CtCel9A activity, sfGFP fluorescence, glucose consumption, and dissolved oxygen for all four variants are shown in Supplementary Figure S10. Despite this growth penalty, volumetric productivity increased 3.6-fold as specific enzyme production rate (qp) increased from 0.12 to 0.52 IU/mg DCW/h [23, 24]. Genetic stability assessment over 60 generations demonstrated plasmid retention of 98.2 ± 0.8% at 20 generations, 96.1 ± 1.2% at 40 generations, and 93.4 ± 1.8% at 60 generations, with CtCel9A titer decreasing by 4.2% and 8.7% at 40 and 60 generations, respectively. Sequencing confirmed structural stability of CRISPRi guide RNAs and the ComQXPA module throughout the testing period [19].

Machine learning-guided fermentation optimization

We employed a sequential experimental strategy combining Latin Hypercube Sampling (24 initial runs) with Bayesian optimization over five iterations in BioLector XT microbioreactors [25]. A Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model with a Matern 5/2 kernel was implemented using GPyTorch and trained with enzyme titer as the primary response [16]. The model explored a multidimensional parameter space including: glucose (5–30 g/L), temperature (28–42 °C), pH (6.0–8.0), agitation (800–1200 rpm), induction OD600 (10–30), and IPTG concentration (0–0.8 mM). Each optimization parameter was specifically selected based on its known interaction with QLM system components: glucose affects ComX pheromone accumulation kinetics, temperature modulates the quorum-sensing circuit, cI857 repressor, and protein folding simultaneously, pH influences ComX peptide stability, and agitation affects pheromone accumulation through mixing-dependent cell density sensing [20]. The induction OD600 range (10–30) was expanded beyond the experimentally determined auto-induction threshold (18.3 ± 1.2) to capture environmentally induced shifts in quorum-sensing sensitivity. The temperature range (28–42 °C) spans the full functional spectrum of the cI857 repressor (complete repression at 28 °C, progressive denaturation beginning at 32–35 °C, > 95% inactivation at 42 °C) to explore potential benefits of partial leaky lysis gene expression [14].

The GPR model identified an optimal dynamic fermentation protocol consisting of three phases: Phase I (growth at 37 °C, pH 7.2 until OD600 = 12); Phase II (temperature shift to 32 °C, pH adjustment to 6.8); and Phase III (induction with 0.15 mM IPTG at OD600 = 15) with exponential glucose feeding (μ = 0.15 h⁻1). This optimized protocol yielded a CtCel9A titer of 9.32 ± 0.29 IU/mL, representing a 3.6-fold improvement over the unoptimized control (2.58 ± 0.14 IU/mL; p < 0.001). The GPR model converged on an induction OD600 of 15, lower than the experimentally observed quorum-sensing threshold of 18.3, because the temperature shift to 32 °C at OD600 = 12 slowed growth rates sufficiently that CRISPRi-mediated cell wall weakening was established before ComX-driven enzyme production initiated at approximately OD600 20–22 under these conditions. This temporal staggering proved critical for maximizing yield. The specific activity of purified CtCel9A (4750 IU/mg) refers to IMAC-purified enzyme quantified by Bradford assay; crude broth specific activity was 1,370 IU/mg total extracellular protein, with CtCel9A representing 28.7% of total extracellular protein. The GPR model demonstrated excellent predictive performance (R2 = 0.94, Q2 = 0.89), confirming its effectiveness for dynamic bioprocess optimization [12]. Response surface methodology optimization of continuous PEF-ASL system

We developed a novel continuous Pulsed Electric Field-Assisted Synergistic Lysis (PEF-ASL) system for efficient enzyme recovery and optimized it using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with a Box-Behnken Design (BBD) [26, 27]. Four key independent variables were investigated: PEF field strength (X₁: 2–6 kV/cm), enzyme residence time (X₂: 2–10 min), thermolysis temperature (X₃: 60–75 °C), and stabilizer concentration (X₄: 2–12%). A total of 46 experimental runs, including 5 center points for error estimation, were conducted. The PEF system (HVP-830, Suematsu Electronics, Japan) was operated with monopolar exponential decay pulses (0.5 µF capacitance), pulse width of 30 ± 2 µs, pulse frequency of 100 Hz, and coaxial electrode geometry (stainless steel 316L, inner electrode 6 mm, outer electrode 10 mm, gap 2 mm, treatment volume 2.8 mL). The 100 Hz frequency provided sufficient inter-pulse interval (10 ms) for thermal dissipation, maintaining temperature increase below 3 °C during treatment. Specific energy input was calculated as W = (0.5 × C × V2 × f × t) / (V_chamber × flow rate), yielding 0.098 kWh/g, consistent with the measured value of 0.11 ± 0.02 kWh/g [17, 28].

ANOVA results confirmed the statistical significance of all models (p < 0.0001) with high R2 values (> 0.89) and non-significant lack of fit, indicating excellent model adequacy and predictive capability [29]. The complete ANOVA results and model validation statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Canonical analysis of response surfaces identified optimal operating conditions at: X₁ = 4.2 kV/cm, X₂ = 6.5 min, X₃ = 68 °C, X₄ = 8.5%. At these conditions, the PEF-ASL system achieved 96.8 ± 1.1% enzyme recovery with a specific energy consumption of 0.11 ± 0.02 kWh/g, representing a 68% reduction compared to conventional bead milling (0.34 ± 0.04 kWh/g; p < 0.001) [8, 17]. The incremental improvement of PEF-ASL over thermal lysis alone (92 ± 2% disruption but only 78.4% net functional recovery) stems from two synergistic mechanisms: PEF-induced electroporation at ambient temperature enables initial enzyme release without thermal stress, while brief 68 °C thermolysis exploits CtCel9A thermostability and simultaneously denatures mesophilic host proteins, providing partial purification. The PBSX lysis cassette serves a preparatory function, a brief 2-min thermal pulse at 42 °C following fermentation partially activates cell wall hydrolases (N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase), weakening the peptidoglycan and reducing the PEF field strength required for complete permeabilization from an estimated 6–8 kV/cm to 4.2 kV/cm, directly contributing to the 68% energy reduction [28]. A process flow diagram showing the sequential lysis cascade is provided in Supplementary Figure S5. The integrated system demonstrated synergistic effects between PEF-induced membrane permeabilization and mild thermolysis, enabling high-efficiency enzyme release while leveraging the intrinsic thermostability of CtCel9A [17]. The continuous operation stability of the PEF-ASL system over an 8-h run is demonstrated in Supplementary Figure S3. System-wide integration and sustainability assessment

We integrated the optimized fermentation (GPR) and downstream processing (RSM) models in SuperPro Designer. Multi-objective optimization employed NSGA-III in pymoo, minimizing negative yield, energy consumption, water intensity, and production cost [31]. We initially performed optimization using a simplified cost model; however, recognizing that this excluded waste treatment, quality control, contingency, and facility overhead (totaling 11.20/kg), were optimized using a full cost function incorporating all components. There optimization shifted the Pareto front upward by 8–12/kg across all operating points, with the knee-point solution moving from (9,150 IU/L, 124/kg) to (9,020 IU/L,132/kg), representing a 1.4% decrease in predicted yield and a 6.5% increase in cost. Critically, the qualitative conclusions regarding the superiority of the integrated platform remained unchanged. The Pareto front for the four-dimensional optimization contained 47 non-dominated solutions. This Pareto front is visualized for all six pairwise objective combinations in Supplementary Figure S9, with the pure cost-minimizing and pure yield-maximizing solutions indicated for reference. The selected operating point was identified using knee-point detection by maximum distance from the line connecting individual minima, representing the solution where marginal improvements in any objective require disproportionate sacrifices in others. After full-cost re-optimization, compared to the pure cost-minimizing solution ($118/kg, 7,240 IU/L), our selection sacrifices 10.7% cost advantage for 26.4% higher productivity; compared to pure yield maximization (9,620 IU/L, $156/kg), it sacrifices 4.9% yield for 15.4% cost reduction, representing the most balanced compromise under equal-weight multi-criteria decision analysis [31, 32]. Life cycle assessment followed ISO 14040/44 standards using SimaPro with the Ecoinvent database [33]. Techno-economic analysis considered a 500 kg/year production facility [34].

The selected operating point predicted a yield of 9,150 IU/L, specific energy consumption of 0.13 kWh/g, and production cost of $132/kg. Experimental validation confirmed performance at 8,980 ± 310 IU/L, 0.14 ± 0.03 kWh/g, and $132 ± 8/kg, representing 52–58% cost reduction compared to conventional enzyme production [2, 3]. The 52–58% range represents the 95% confidence interval from Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis (n = 10,000 iterations) incorporating uncertainty in enzyme titer (9.32 ± 0.29 IU/mL), PEF energy consumption (0.11 ± 0.02 kWh/g), and facility utilization rate (85 ± 5%), with the mean reduction of 57% corresponding to a conventional baseline cost of 308/kg derived from literature and our control experiments. Synergistic mechanisms and process integration

The superior performance of the SynBio-DSP platform stems from three synergistic mechanisms: (1) Genetic engineering via the QLM system coordinates high-yield production with enhanced cell fragility [35]; (2) Machine learning optimization identifies optimal dynamic fermentation conditions that traditional methods would overlook [36]; (3) The continuous PEF-ASL system combines mild physical treatments for efficient enzyme release while preserving activity [37]. This integrated approach breaks traditional trade-offs between yield, cost, and environmental impact, demonstrating that sustainable biomanufacturing can be both environmentally responsible and commercially competitive [14]. Discussion

The SynBio-DSP platform demonstrates that simultaneous co-optimization of cellular design and process configuration can overcome traditional trade-offs in biocatalyst manufacturing. The 3.6-fold improvement in enzyme titer achieved by the QLM system exceeds previously reported engineered B. subtilis cellulase production systems, which typically achieve titers of 0.5–7.4 IU/mL for recombinant cellulases [38, 39]. Industrial Trichoderma reesei strains produce 10–40 IU/mL but require 5–7-day fermentations with complex carbon sources [40], whereas our system achieves 9.32 IU/mL in 24 h using defined medium. This productivity compares favorably with recent advances in bacterial cellulase secretion engineering where titers of 4.8–6.2 IU/mL were reported using optimized signal peptides and promoter engineering in B. subtilis [38].

The temporal coordination strategy underlying the QLM system exploits B. subtilis native quorum-sensing regulatory logic, which evolved to coordinate population-level responses such as sporulation and competence development [20]. By linking enzyme production to the ComQXPA circuit, we impose production timing that is physiologically appropriate rather than externally dictated by chemical inducers. This approach builds upon established principles of auto-induction systems that have demonstrated superior performance over IPTG-inducible systems by separating growth and production phases without manual intervention [41]. The 37% growth rate reduction observed in the fully induced QLM strain is consistent with resource allocation models of recombinant protein production, where ribosomal capacity and amino acid pools are diverted from biomass synthesis to heterologous protein accumulation [23]. Despite this burden, the 4.3-fold increase in specific productivity (qp) demonstrates effective redirection of cellular resources, consistent with metabolic models predicting that B. subtilis can allocate up to 40% of its proteome to heterologous protein production before growth arrest occurs [24]. The genetic stability of 93.4% plasmid retention after 60 generations is acceptable for the intended repeated fed-batch operation with fresh inoculum preparation every 40–60 generations; however, chromosomal integration would be necessary for true continuous fermentation exceeding 100 generations, as demonstrated by recent studies achieving stable integrated expression in B. subtilis using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing [19].

Regulatory module cross-talk and orthogonality assessment

Given the complexity of the QLM system with four simultaneous regulatory layers (ComQXPA quorum sensing, CRISPRi, cI857-pR temperature-sensitive lysis, and P104 constitutive chaperone expression), we assessed potential cross-talk among modules. Experimental evidence confirmed module orthogonality: (i) The 32 °C Phase II temperature maintains cI857 in a partially active state but does not interfere with ComX pheromone stability (ComX half-life: 45 min at 32 °C vs. 38 min at 37 °C; p > 0.05). (ii) RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Table S7) confirmed no significant off-target effects of CRISPRi on comQ, comX, or comA expression (fold-change < 1.2, p > 0.05 for all targets), consistent with the orthogonality of dCas9 and quorum-sensing machinery. (iii) The P104 promoter uses sigma factor σ^A, while ComQXPA uses σ^H, minimizing direct promoter interference; however, indirect effects via shared RNA polymerase pools are possible and were observed as the 8% growth reduction in uninduced QLM strains. (iv) No detectable lysis was observed at 32 °C (Phase II conditions), confirming tight cI857 repression. Nevertheless, transient temperature spikes during scale-up could cause premature lysis, a limitation we acknowledge for industrial implementation.

Metabolic burden context and secretome analysis

The 8% growth reduction from constitutive chaperone overexpression under the P104 promoter requires contextualization. This reduction is consistent with reported costs of constitutive protein overexpression in B. subtilis, where each 1% of total cellular protein dedicated to recombinant product imposes approximately 0.5–1.0% growth penalty [23]. Our chaperone set (GroEL/GroES, DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE, Tig) represents approximately 2–3% of total cellular protein under P104 control, predicting a 1.5–3% penalty; the observed 8% suggests additional indirect effects on cellular resource allocation. To assess impact on native secretory proteins, we analyzed the extracellular proteome via LC–MS/MS (Supplementary Table S8). Key findings: native amylase (AmyE) decreased by 42 ± 5%, native protease (AprE) decreased by 38 ± 4%, and total extracellular protein decreased by 31 ± 3% compared to wild-type. However, CtCel9A represented 28.7% of total extracellular protein versus < 1% for any single native protein. This proteome reallocation is typical of high-level recombinant production systems and does not negatively impact process performance because CtCel9A is the product of interest. However, strains designed for multiple enzyme production would require more balanced expression strategies.

The 68% reduction in specific energy consumption achieved by PEF-ASL compared to bead milling results from the synergistic combination of genetic cell wall weakening and mild physical treatment. The 41% reduction in cell wall thickness from CRISPRi targeting of pbpA, dacA, and mreB decreases the critical electric field strength required for membrane permeabilization according to established electroporation models, where the critical transmembrane potential is proportional to cell radius and applied field strength [42]. The PBSX pre-weakening step further reduces the mechanical and electrical energy barrier, enabling efficient lysis at 4.2 kV/cm rather than the 6–8 kV/cm typically required for intact B. subtilis, consistent with reports that PEF treatment of Gram-positive bacteria typically requires higher field strengths (5–15 kV/cm) than Gram-negative bacteria (2–5 kV/cm) due to the thicker peptidoglycan layer [28]. The brief 68 °C thermolysis simultaneously releases enzyme and precipitates mesophilic host proteins, providing a partial purification effect that reduces downstream IMAC column loading. This integrated lysis approach represents a significant advance over conventional methods, as demonstrated by the comparative data in (Table 2), and aligns with recent trends toward continuous processing in industrial biotechnology [30].

Comparison of optimization approaches (Table 1) reveals that GPR achieved the highest titer with the fewest experimental runs among machine learning methods and substantially outperformed traditional statistical approaches. The superior performance of Bayesian optimization with GPR stems from principled uncertainty quantification, the Matern 5/2 kernel captures the smoothness properties of biological response surfaces while the acquisition function efficiently balances exploration of uncertain regions with exploitation of high-performing areas [16, 25]. This finding is consistent with recent applications of Bayesian optimization in bioprocess development, where GPR-based approaches have demonstrated 30–50% reduction in experimental burden compared to traditional design-of-experiments methods while achieving equivalent or superior optima [12, 43]. The expanded induction OD600 range proved essential for capturing environmentally induced shifts in quorum-sensing behavior, as the auto-induction threshold shifts from 18.3 to approximately 20–22 under the cooler Phase II conditions, highlighting the importance of exploring parameter spaces beyond initial physiological characterizations when using data-driven optimization approaches.

The Pareto front analysis reveals that different stakeholders might select alternative operating points: electricity-intensive regions might prefer lower energy points, while capital-constrained facilities might maximize yield, highlighting the flexibility of our optimization framework for context-specific implementation [31].

Several limitations merit consideration. The genetic stability data extend only to 60 generations, and chromosomal integration studies are needed for extended continuous operation, building on established protocols for stable genomic integration in B. subtilis [19, 45]. Scale-up of the PEF system from our 2.8 mL chamber to industrial volumes (liters to cubic meters) will require addressing electrode fouling, thermal management in larger chambers, and maintaining field uniformity, challenges that have been partially addressed in food processing applications of PEF technology but remain to be demonstrated for intracellular product release from microbial systems [28, 46]. The sfGFP fusion, while enabling crucial real-time monitoring, reduces specific activity by 8.3%, and applications requiring tag-free enzyme would incur additional processing costs (estimated $148/kg with TEV cleavage), consistent with the well-known trade-offs between fusion tag utility and catalytic efficiency [22]. The current study used a single model enzyme (CtCel9A) in a single host (B. subtilis), and broader applicability to other enzyme classes and production hosts remains to be demonstrated, though the modular nature of the QLM system and the general applicability of GPR optimization and PEF-ASL processing suggest potential transferability with appropriate modifications [18, 37]. Furthermore, our multi-objective optimization initially used a simplified cost model excluding waste treatment, quality control, contingency, and facility overhead. While re-optimization with the full cost function confirmed the qualitative conclusions and shifted the knee-point only modestly (1.4% yield decrease, 6.5% cost increase), we acknowledge that future optimization studies should incorporate all cost components from the outset. Future work should explore digital twin development for real-time adaptive control, integration of renewable energy sources to further improve sustainability metrics, and application of the platform to other industrially relevant enzymes such as hemicellulases, lignin-modifying enzymes, and lipases [36].

Conclusion

This study establishes SynBio-DSP as a transformative platform for sustainable biofuel enzyme production. By integrating synthetic biology, machine learning optimization, and continuous processing with systematic RSM optimization, we simultaneously break traditional trade-offs between yield, cost, and environmental impact. Our integrated approach achieves substantial improvements in productivity (3.6-fold increase), energy efficiency (68% reduction), production cost (52–58% reduction), and environmental footprint (57% carbon reduction). The response surface methodology provided robust optimization of the multi-parameter PEF-ASL system, revealing synergistic interactions between process variables. The Pareto-optimal solution identified through NSGA-III represents a balanced compromise among competing objectives, with the decision-making framework adaptable to different stakeholder priorities. This work demonstrates that sustainable biomanufacturing can be both environmentally friendly and commercially competitive, offering a scalable pathway for producing enzymes essential to the bioeconomy transition.

Materials and methods

Strain construction and engineering

Bacillus subtilis 168 was engineered with a Quorum-Lysis-Modulation system using CRISPR-Cas9 [15, 19]. The system comprised: (i) ctcel9A under ComQXPA quorum-sensing control with C-terminal 6xHis-tag and sfGFP reporter [20]; (ii) CRISPRi targeting pbpA, dacA, and mreB under IPTG-inducible Pspank promoter [15]; (iii) PBSX lysis cassette under temperature-sensitive cI857-pR promoter; (iv) chaperone operon (groES-groEL, dnaK-dnaJ-grpE, tig) under strong synthetic promoter P104 [21]. Transformants were selected and verified by sequencing and Western blot. Primer sequences and genetic parts used for strain construction are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Machine learning-guided fermentation optimization

Cultivations were performed in BioLector XT microbioreactors with online monitoring. A sequential experimental strategy combined Latin Hypercube Sampling (24 runs) with Bayesian optimization over five iterations [25]. A Gaussian Process Regression model with Matern 5/2 kernel was implemented using GPyTorch, trained with enzyme titer as response [16]. Parameters included: glucose (5–30 g/L), temperature (28–42 °C), pH (6.0–8.0), agitation (800–1200 rpm), induction OD600 (10–30), and IPTG concentration (0–0.8 mM). Each parameter was selected for its direct relevance to QLM system function: glucose influences ComX pheromone kinetics, temperature modulates quorum sensing, cI857 repression, and protein folding, pH affects ComX stability, and agitation influences pheromone accumulation [14, 20]. The induction OD600 range was expanded beyond the 18.3 threshold to capture environmentally induced shifts, and the temperature range spans complete cI857 repression (28 °C) to full activation (42 °C) to explore partial lysis gene expression. Cellulase activity was measured using 4-methylumbelliferyl-β-D-cellobioside fluorescence assay [47]. A representative fermentation time-course profile with online measurements (DO, pH, temperature, agitation, off-gas CO₂/O₂) and offline measurements (OD600, glucose, CtCel9A activity, sfGFP fluorescence) is provided in Supplementary Figure S8. The complete training dataset (39 runs) is provided in Supplementary Table S5. Model performance metrics and optimization convergence plots are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Continuous PEF-ASL system optimization using response surface methodology

The PEF-ASL system was optimized using Box-Behnken Design within Response Surface Methodology [26, 27]. Four independent variables were selected: PEF field strength (2–6 kV/cm), enzyme residence time (2–10 min), thermolysis temperature (60–75 °C), and stabilizer concentration (2–12%). A total of 46 experimental runs were conducted, including 5 center points. The PEF system (HVP-830, Suematsu Electronics, Japan) employed monopolar exponential decay pulses generated by 0.5 µF capacitor discharge, with pulse width 30 ± 2 µs, pulse frequency 100 Hz, and coaxial stainless steel 316L electrodes (gap 2 mm, treatment volume 2.8 mL) [28]. Specific energy input was calculated as W = (0.5 × C × V2 × f × t) / (V_chamber × flow rate) [17]. The integrated system comprised: PEF chamber (coaxial electrodes, 2 mm gap), packed-bed enzyme reactor (lysozyme and PlyBs immobilized on chitosan beads), counter-current thermolysis unit, microfluidic stabilization mixer, and tangential flow filtration. Complete cell lysis for establishing 100% recovery reference was performed using combined lysozyme (1 mg/mL, 37 °C, 60 min), sonication (20 kHz, 40% amplitude, 10 cycles of 30 s on/off), and French press (20,000 psi, three passages), confirmed by phase-contrast microscopy and protein release plateau. The system schematic is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1, and model validation data are provided in the Supporting Information.

Multi-objective optimization employed NSGA-III in pymoo, minimizing negative yield, energy consumption, water intensity, and production cost [31].
