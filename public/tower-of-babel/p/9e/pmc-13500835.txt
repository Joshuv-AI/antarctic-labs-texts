Chromosome-level genome assembly and annotation of the porcupine fish (Diodon hystrix)
Authors: Jun Ma, Liu Cao, Yanfeng Yue, Yatong Wu, Yan Lu, Pan Chen, Lei Tang, Jinxiang Liu, Hai Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The porcupinefish (Diodon hystrix), a coral reef teleost, is widely distributed in tropical/subtropical waters of the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian Oceans, and Mediterranean Sea. It shares easily recognizable features with pufferfish, such as body inflation and spines. Additionally, its culinary value makes D. hystrix a highly desirable species in many tropical coastal regions, with considerable market potential. However, lack of a high-quality genome hindered further studies on its reproduction, molecular biology, and genomic improvement. Here, we assembled the chromosome-scale genome using PacBio HiFi, ultra-long reads, and Hi-C. Of the 713.62 Mb genome, 98.63% anchored to 23 chromosomes (scaffold N50: 31.52 Mb) with 39.82% repetitive sequences. The assembled genome achieved a BUSCO completeness score of 97.7%, with 23,171 protein-coding genes predicted, 22,221 of which were functionally annotated. Phylogenetic analysis identified D. hystrix’s evolutionary relationships with other species in the Tetraodontiformes. In summary, the high-quality genome of D. hystrix sheds light on valuable insights into genome size evolution, and provides a valuable resource for exploiting genomic study and breeding applications in this species.

Background & Summary

The porcupine fish is a common name for the Diodontidae within the Tetraodontiformes, primarily referring to species within the genus Diodon. These fish are widely distributed in tropical and subtropical waters of the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian Ocean, and Mediterranean Sea, and often inhabit shallow coral reefs or rocky reef environments1. Tetraodontiformes represent one of the most morphologically and ecologically diverse radiations of spiny-rayed fishes2, with particularly prominent diversity in defense mechanisms. The spines of porcupine fishes are longer and cover the entire body, while those of pufferfishes are smaller, ranging from complete coverage to specific ventral areas3. Interestingly, box pufferfish have not evolved sharp spines but instead developed solid shield-like scales4. Because of their fixed shield-like armor scales, box pufferfish have lost the ability to expand. Previous research has indicated that the development of spines or shield-like dermal armor correlates with scale morphology and evolutionary plasticity3,5. However, this evolutionary divergence may be attributed to inherent genetic variations within species6–8, it remains to be further explored.

Tetraodontiformes serves as a particularly valuable object for investigating biodiversity and the patterns of genomic evolution in teleost. To date, published genomes of Tetraodontiformes available in public databases are predominantly from Tetraodontidae, such as Takifugu rubripes9, Takifugu flavidus10, Takifugu bimaculatus11, Takifugu obscurus12 and Tetraodon nigroviridis13. The genomes of other families have also been released, such as mola mola14, Pao palembangensis15, and Thamnaconus modestus16. However, only one genome of Diodontidae (a sister family to Tetraodontidae)—D. holocanthus—has been assembled using BAC technology17. The quality of genome assembled by BAC library is poor, making it difficult to meet the requirements of genomic research. Remarkably, pufferfishes (Tetraodontidae) possess the smallest known vertebrate genomes (with a genome size of 365 Mb)9. In contrast, the genomes of porcupine fishes (Diodontidae) are approximately twice the size of those in pufferfishes17–19, yet this discrepance does not arise from differences in chromosomal ploidy. Through BAC cloning and sequencing, it has been demonstrated that the gene density of porcupine fishes (D. holocanthus) is significantly lower than that of pufferfishes (T. rubripes and T. viridis). The accumulation of distinct repetitive elements within introns drives genome size variation between porcupine fish and pufferfish17. However, differences in repetitive elements alone cannot fully explain the two-fold disparity in genome size. Early karyotype analyses revealed that the chromosome number (2n) of Diodontidae is notably higher than that of Tetraodontidae, while certain genera (e.g., Takifugu sp.) exhibit chromosome miniaturization and karyotypic diversity20,21. This suggests that frequent chromosomal fusions and fissions may have occurred during the evolution of Tetraodontiformes. Additionally, chromosomes in Tetraodontiformes contain limited heterochromatin, primarily localized to centromeric regions22, it may compromise chromosome integrity. The loss of heterochromatin could also contribute to genomic divergence23. Thus, unsolved mysteries likely persist regarding the genome size differences between porcupine fishes and pufferfishes, necessitating more high-quality genomic datasets for resolution.

In this study, we assembled a chromosome-scale genome of Diodon hystrix (Fig. 1) and annotated the functional genes through PacBio HiFi, Ultra-long reads, and Hi-C sequencing technologies. The genome size is 713.62 Mb, with an N50 of 31.52 Mb, and a total of 22,221 protein-coding genes were predicted. This work provides a chromosome-level genome for the Diodontidae family, offering essential data for comparative analyses of diverse Tetraodontiformes species and contributing significantly to the understanding of genome evolution within this order.Fig. 1The D. hystrix used in the genome sequencing and assembly.

Methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures strictly adhered to the guidelines stipulated by Administration of Affairs Concerning Animal Experimentation of China and were approved by the Academic Committee of Hainan Tropical Ocean University. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal research (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Sample collection and genome sequencing

A male D. hystrix was collected from the Qionghai Research Center of East China Sea Fisheries Research Institute. The fish was anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222), then the tissues were sampled and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissue by the QIAGEN DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, USA) following recommended protocols of the kit, and the high-quality genomic DNA was subjected to Illunima short-read, PacBio long-read, Hi-C and Nanopore ultra-long sequencing. All sequencing was carried out by Novogene company (Tianjin, China). First, short-read libraries were prepared and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with 150 bp paired-end reads, a total of 34.34 Gb of raw data was generated for the genome survey. Second, a PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) library was constructed and sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform. The 46.7 Gb (65.81 × coverage with N50 read length of 17.68 kb) of HiFi reads were retained for genome assembly. Third, ultra-long reads was sequenced on a PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies), yielding 35.34 Gb of ultralong-read data at 49.93 × coverage. To enable chromosome-level assembly, a Hi-C library was constructed through chromatin digestion, proximity ligation, and biotin pulldown following the protocol. RNA sequencing

To better support gene annotation, seven tissues (spleen, brain, gill, liver, heart, kidney and gonad) were collected from the same individual used for genome sequencing. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), and was divided into two portions: one for mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and the other for full-length cDNA sequencing (Iso-Seq). RNA-seq was performed using the Illunima NovaSeq 6000 platform with a 150 bp paired-end strategy, yielding the average of 8.16 Gb clean data per tissue. Iso-Seq was conducted on the PacBio Sequel II platform with mixed RNA of all tissue, generating 109.42 Gb of high-quality SMRT sequences (Table 1).

Genome survey and de novo assembly

To estimate genome size of D.hystrix, 34.34 Gb of genome sequencing data generated from the Illumina platform was analyzed using the software Jellyfish (v2.2.7)24 for genome survey. Based on the 17-mer frequency distribution, the genome size of D.hystrix was estimated to be approximately 722.37 Mb, and adjusted to 709.58 Mb, with a heterozygous ratio of 0.21%, a duplication rate of 39.50%, and a GC content of 42.41%.

A total of 46.7 Gb of cleaned HiFi reads (PacBio) and 35.43 Gb of cleaned Ultra-long reads (ONT) were de novo assembled using HiFiasm (v0.19.9-r616)25 with default parameters. The draft genome of 713.62 Mb comprising 30 scaffolds (Scaffold N50 = 31.52 Mb) was obtained. Hi-C data were used to cluster, orient, and sort the scaffolds by the ALLHiC (v0.9.8) pipeline26 with parameters of “RE = GATC, minREs = 50, maxlinkdensity = 3, NonInformativeRabio = 0”. Subsequently, Juicebox (v1.11.08)27 was used for manual correction based on interaction intensity, and generated a chromosome-level assembly. Finally, we obtained a nearly complete genome with 23 chromosomal-scale scaffolds (Fig. 2A). Additionally, seven scaffolds remained unanchored at the chromosome level. Detailed statistical information (including chromosome length, GC content, and gene density) is provided in Table 2. The total genome length was 713,62 Mb, of which 703.84 Mb were anchored to chromosomes. The anchoring rate of the genome was 98.63% (Fig. 2B). Repeat and noncoding RNA annotation

For the ab initio prediction, RepeatModeler (v2.0.3)28, RepeatScout (v1.0.5)29, and LTR_finder (v1.0.6)30 were jointly used to predict the repetitive sequences of D. hystrix genome. The combined results, together with the Repbase (v19.06)31 database, were used to construct the final repetitive sequence libraries for subsequent analysis with RepeatMasker (v4.1.2)32. The total length of the repetitive sequences was 284,19 Mb, accounting for 39.82% of the D. hystrix genome. Among these repetitive sequences, the DNA transposons were the most abundant (accounting for 14.72% of the entire genome), followed by long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs, 11.91%), long terminal repeats (LTRs, 8.47%), and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs, 0.32%).

Noncoding RNAs were also annotated, including transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), microRNA (miRNA), and small nuclear RNA (snRNA). Based on the structural characteristics of tRNA, the tRNAscan-SE33 was utilized to identity tRNA in the genome. The rRNA sequences from a closely related species (T. rubripes) was selected as reference sequences to search for rRNA through BLASTN alignment (E-value ≤ 1e−5)34. The INFERNAL (v1.1.2)35, in conjunction with Rfam database (release 13.0) was used to predict miRNA and snRNA in the genome. Gene structure prediction and annotation

Gene structure prediction was conducted using a three-pronged gene strategy that integrated de novo, homology-based, and RNA-seq-based. Firstly, transcript reads generated by the Illumina platform were assembled using Trinity (v2.8.5)36. The assembled transcripts were subsequently integrated with long-reads transcripts generated from the PacBio platform. To eliminate redundancy, the merged transcripts were processed using CD-HIT (v4.8.1)37. PASA (v2.4.1)38 was used to correct these transcripts by mapping them to the D. hystrix genome. Open reading frames (ORFs) were then extracted from the corrected transcripts to serve as training set for de novo gene structure prediction, which was performed using Augustus (v3.5)39 and SNAP (v2013.1.29)40; Secondly, protein sequences from six homologous species (T. rubripes, Danio rerio, T. bimaculatus, Thamnaconus septentrionalis, Lates calcarifer, and Oryzias latipes) obtained from the NCBI database were aligned to the D. hystrix genome to predict homologous gene structures using Genewise (v2.4.1)41 and TBLASTN (E-value ≤ 1e-5); Thirdly, transcript reads generated by Illumina platform were mapped to the D. hystrix genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)42, while long-read transcripts generated from the PacBio platform were mapped to the same reference genome using minimap2 (v2.28)43. Subsequently, transcript structures derived from both alignment methods were reconstructed using StringTie (v2.2.1)44; Finally, EvidenceModeler (EVM, v1.1.0)45 with parameter “segmentSize = 200000, overlapSize = 20000, min_intron_length = 20” was employed to integrate the results of three prediction strategy and construct a highly reliable gene set. A total of 23,171 protein-coding genes were predicted. Phylogeny

To investigate the evolutionary relationships among D. hystrix and several Tetraodontiformes species, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis using the predicted proteins from the 9 genomes: T. rubripes9, T. flavidus10, T. bimaculatus11, T. nigroviridis13, M. mola14, T. septentrionalis16, L. calcarifer50, O. latipes51, and D. rerio52. The orthologous groups were inferred using the OrthoFinder program (v2.5.450) with default parameters53. The 2,726 single-copy genes were identified from all 10 species, and these sequences were used to construct a multiple sequence for alignment using the Muscle (v5.1.0) with default parameters54. Multiple sequence alignments for each single-copy orthologous gene were trimmed using Gblock (v0.91b)55. These trimmed alignments were then concatenated end-to-end, resulting in an ultra-long multiple sequence alignment of 2,726 single-copy orthologous genes was formed. The prottest356 was used to detect the best substitution model for the ultra-long multiple sequence alignment. Then, RaxML (v8.1.12)57 was used to infer the maximum likelihood tree with the optimal model “JTT + I + G + F” setting 1000 bootstraps and D. rerio as an outgroup. Divergence times were estimated using MCMCtree implemented in PAML (v4.10.5)58. The divergent ages were calibrated using three time points from TimeTree (http://www.timetree.org/): the divergent time between T. flavidus and T. bimaculatus at 0.89~7.56 million years ago (MYA), T. nigroviridis and the ancestor of Takifugu sp. at 35.6~49.1 MYA, and the ancestor of D. rerio at 180.0~251.5 MYA. Within the Tetraodontoidei, D. hystrix (family Diodontidae) and the ancestor of the family Tetraodontidae diverged around 55.0052 MYA (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Phylogenetic relationships across several species in Tetraodontiformes. Technical Validation

Evaluation of the genome assembly and annotation

The completeness of the final genome assembly was assessed using BUSCO (v 5.6.1)62 with the actinopterygii_odb10 database. The result showed that 97.7% of complete BUSCOs (C) were identified in the D. hystrix genome, comprising 96.2% single-copy BUSCOs (S) and 1.5% duplicated BUSCOs (D), respectively.

To evaluate the accuracy of the genome assembly, the clean Illumina sequencing reads were aligned to the genome assembly using BWA (v0.7.17)63. Both the reads mapping rate and genome coverage rate reached 99.74%. Additionally, Merqury (v1.3)64 was employed to assess the accuracy of the genome assembly through k-mer analysis of Illumina sequencing reads. The quality value (Qv) was 50.46, indicating >99.99% assembly accuracy.

The integrity of the annotated gene set was verified by BUSCO analysis using the same lineage database (actinopterygii_odb10). Results showed that 92.5% of complete BUSCOs were present in the annotated gene set, including 90.6% single-copy BUSCOs and 1.9% duplicated BUSCOs.
