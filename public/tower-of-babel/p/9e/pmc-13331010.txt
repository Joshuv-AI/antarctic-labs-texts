‘Gambling is an escape’: a qualitative study of problematic gambling among sexually diverse adults in Canada
Authors: Magaly Brodeur, Mélanie Rose Dixon, Julie-Christine Cotton, Natalia Muñoz Gómez, Anne-Marie Auger, Christine Loignon, Pasquale Roberge, Adèle Morvannou, Olivier Simon, Marie-Eve Poitras, Yves Couturier, Catherine Hudon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProblem gambling among sexual and gender diversity (SGD) populations has received increasing attention in research. While the literature shows that these populations are more likely than others to experience addiction and mental health issues, little is known about gambling among them. In our online survey (n=1519), a group of SGD participants reported problematic gambling practices.ObjectiveThe present study, conducted as part of a broader mixed-methods study on gambling among SGD populations, aimed to gather qualitative data to gain insight into why problem gambling seemed high in our online survey.Design, setting and participantsThis qualitative descriptive study was conducted via semi-structured individual interviews with voluntary participants who gamble and were from sexually diverse populations across Canada (n=23). A primarily inductive approach with a deductive component was employed to conduct a thematic analysis of the collected data.ResultsParticipants shared their thoughts on specific difficulties faced by SGD populations and gambling. These included themes such as gambling as a coping mechanism for SGD-related struggles, a lack of visibility and awareness of gambling issues and help resources, seeking a sense of community and belonging through gambling and other addictive behaviours and a minor theme regarding perspectives on risk-taking. Other contextual influences included the COVID-19 pandemic, online gambling and financial needs.ConclusionThis study adds to the existing knowledge of problem gambling among SGD populations by providing a qualitative perspective of sexually diverse participants on the issue. It also provides support for the minority stress framework as a potential reason why people from SGD populations might be at a higher risk of problem gambling.

Introduction

Gambling is a form of entertainment that over half (64.5%) of Canadians aged 15 or older engage in.1 While most people who participate in gambling activities do not experience any harm, gambling-related harm can affect individuals, families and communities across multiple dimensions, such as finances, relationships, emotional and psychological well-being, health, work, studies and criminality.2 3 Gambling is considered problematic when it gives rise to multiple gambling-related harms. An estimated 1.6% of Canadians are at moderate to high risk of problem gambling.1

Validated psychometric instruments, such as the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI),4 exist to assess and classify the severity of problem gambling. Furthermore, the extraordinary context of the COVID-19 pandemic, starting in 2020, raised great concern on the impact of the pandemic on mental health and addiction in the general population as well as for minoritised groups.58 Previous studies have documented the changes in gambling rates during the pandemic, revealing that while gambling remained the same or decreased for some, an increase in gambling practices and gambling-related harms was also widely reported.9 10

While some characteristics are associated with higher vulnerability to problem gambling in the general population (ie, being a male, being single, being of a younger age, having financial problems and having a lower education),11 the empirical attention given to gambling issues among sexual and gender diversity (SGD) is much more recent. ‘Sexual and gender diversity’ is an umbrella term used to describe individuals who identify in a way that is not exclusively heterosexual and/or cisgender.12 Most studies having focused on this subject have used a quantitative design and have been conducted in countries other than Canada.13 Yet, scientific literature has shown that SGD populations could have a higher likelihood of presenting with addictive behaviours1418 and mental health difficulties, such as depression and anxiety.1921 Researchers have proposed the conceptual model of minority stress to explain higher prevalences of mental health problems among sexual diversity populations, starting with Meyer22 and followed by Hendricks and Testa.23 In essence, Minority Stress Theory posits that SGD populations’ higher vulnerability to mental health problems could be significantly related to recurrent exposure as a member of a minority group to increased environmental stressors in the forms of stigma, marginalisation and discrimination. Some studies to date have also shown associations between the effects of minority stress and addictive behaviours, including gambling.1419 2427

Despite the growing body of scientific literature, little is known about gambling among SGD populations or its prevalence. Four existing literature reviews on the subject132830 included quantitative studies almost exclusively and came to similar conclusions regarding a lack of consistency in the literature and the impossibility of establishing well-grounded claims regarding the associations between SGD status and gambling measures across the reviewed studies. Furthermore, information is even more scant regarding gender minorities, showing associations highly driven by sex-at-birth over gender identity.15 Regarding qualitative evidence, both Bush et al 31 and Bush-Evans et al,32 discussed the lived experiences of SGD individuals regarding gambling and its associated harms. Both studies highlighted themes such as gambling as a form of escapism from identity-related stressors, identity concealment or experiences of discrimination in gambling spaces and barriers to help-seeking for problem gambling.3032

Current study

While quantitative data are essential in determining the prevalence of an issue among a given population, it does not always allow for exploring individuals’ experiences to understand a phenomenon thoroughly. Qualitative research methods are therefore crucial, as they enable a nuanced exploration of the lived experiences, attitudes and motivations underlying behaviours such as gambling within SGD populations. By delving into personal narratives and contexts, qualitative approaches allow for a deeper, more multi-faceted view of a phenomenon by exploring its meaning through opinions and experiences.33 To our knowledge, very few studies have conducted a qualitative investigation of SGD populations’ views on gambling within SGD populations.30 32 Therefore, the aim of the present study was to better understand the perspectives of SGD individuals at moderate-to-high risk of problem gambling regarding problem gambling within SGD populations.

Method

Context of the study

This study is part of a larger sequential mixed-methods research project aiming to describe the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on gambling habits among SGD populations (see online supplemental material for research protocol).34 The project’s first phase was a cross-sectional correlational study conducted in a pan-Canadian survey among SGD adults who gamble.35 The quantitative phase enabled us to examine the gambling habits of SGD populations and identify factors associated with moderate-to-high-risk gambling in Canada during the COVID-19 pandemic context.35 Data collection was conducted via an online survey in June 2023, which led to certain key observations, such as a relatively high rate (9.9%) of high-risk gambling among the sample (n=1519), as determined by scores of 8 or more on the PGSI.4 The prevalence of moderate-risk gambling habits (PGSI scores of 3–7) among the sample was estimated at 9.7%. The second phase of the project, in which the present study took place, was of a qualitative descriptive nature.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria to participate in this study were: (1) to be 18 years of age or older, (2) to have gambled at least once in the last 12 months, (3) to have participated in phase one of the study and agreed to be recontacted for phase two and (4) to have scored 3 or more on the PGSI, a 9-item scale that screens for gambling problems among the general population.4 Its cut-off scores determine gambling severity: non-problem (0), low-risk gambling (1–2), moderate-risk gambling (3–7) and high-risk gambling (8 or more).

Study design

The present study employed a qualitative descriptive design. A thematic analysis according to the methodological guidelines proposed by Braun and Clarke36 and following a primarily inductive approach (with a deductive component) was applied to meet the study’s objective of exploring the opinions and experiences of people who gamble among SGD populations. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) quality appraisal tool37 was used to ensure a rigorous and transparent reporting of this qualitative study (see online supplemental material for completed checklist).

Patient and public involvement

To ensure that the research project is relevant to the needs of SGD populations and stakeholders in the field, an advisory committee composed of two lived-experience partners and organisations representing practice settings was formed. The committee met approximately three times per year while the project was active, and partners with lived experience contributed to the review and validation of recruitment materials as well as the interview guide used in this study.

Participants

An email invitation to participate in this study was sent to potential participants that fulfilled eligibility criteria. In total, 88 potential participants were contacted. Of these, 83 identified as sexually diverse, meaning their sexual orientation was reported as not exclusively heterosexual, and 5 identified as gender diverse, meaning they did not identify exclusively as a cisgender man or woman.12 Following the email invitation, 37 individuals replied to the invitation (42% response rate). According to a review by Hennik and Kaiser,38 saturation in qualitative research may be achieved with a sample of 25 or under. Our final sample (n=23) was composed of 22 individuals identifying as sexually diverse and one other individual also identifying as gender diverse. Therefore, the results of the present study are mainly based on the perspectives of sexually diverse individuals regarding problematic gambling among both SGD populations as a whole.

Data collection

An interview guide was created for the larger qualitative study to explore gambling habits among SGD populations. It included questions that explored four main topics, both in general and during the COVID-19 pandemic: gambling habits, mental health, experiences with health and social services and opinions on interventions to prevent or treat problem gambling among SGD populations. Among these questions, the current paper focuses on participants’ responses to the following formulation: “I would like to ask for your opinion regarding the survey [of the previous research phase] results. The proportion of [SGD] individuals reporting excessive gambling habits seems rather high. Does this surprise you? If so, why?”

Invitations to take part in this study were sent out in November 2023. The individual interviews were conducted virtually from November 2023 to January 2024 by MRD, a female doctoral (PhD) research professional trained in psychology and qualitative research methods. No relationship was developed between the participants and the facilitator prior to data collection; the facilitator introduced herself as a research professional working for the primary investigator’s research chair based at the Université de Sherbrooke, and she reported no bias or personal interests in the study to the participants. Interviews lasted 48 min on average (ranging between 22 and 87 min) and were recorded in audio-video format via Microsoft Teams. Interview files were securely saved in a private folder onto the university’s cloud server. As compensation for their participation, participants received a $50 Amazon gift card.

The recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriptionist for analysis. Interviews ceased when authors (MB, MRD, NMG) felt that saturation had been reached, which in the context of this study was guided by a data saturation model39 centred on the question of the number of interviews needed until no new themes are apparent, as “new data repeats what was expressed in previously collected data” (p. 1897). No data transcripts were returned to participants for comment or correction, and the participants did not provide feedback on the findings. No field notes were taken during the interviews, and no repeat interviews were conducted.

Data analysis

MRD carried out a thematic analysis of the transcribed interviews, following the steps described by Braun and Clarke.36 After becoming familiar with the interview data by listening to all the interview recordings once and reading through the interview transcripts multiple times, MRD then generated the initial codes across the entire data set. Next, codes were collated into potential themes, and definitions for the emerging themes were formulated. The elaboration of the codebook was an iterative process, during which MRD and NMG reviewed and discussed the emerging codes and identified the final main themes. Disagreements between coders (MRD and NMG) were resolved by a third member of the team (MB), who validated the final thematic tree. Minor revisions to codes were also made following suggestions from the rest of the authors. The research team carried out the analysis with the understanding that our output may be indirectly influenced by our individual experiences and subjectivities. Our research team is composed of diverse profiles, allowing for variety and nuances in our interpretations. The present work’s analytic approach was primarily inductive, since codes and themes emerged from the data rather than from a predetermined framework or model; however, a deductive component was present, given it was carried out by collecting and analysing the answers offered in response to a specific interview question, based on the results of a previous phase of the study.35 NVivo (Software V.1.7.1) was used for the data analysis.

Results

Participants were aged between 20 and 79 years, mainly identified themselves as cisgender males (57%), were of bisexual (39%) or homosexual orientation (43% gay and 9% lesbian for a total of 52%), employed (65%), married or in a common law partnership (48%) and had a university education (61%). Furthermore, 65% of participants were at moderate risk of problem gambling, while 35% were at high risk, according to their scores on the PGSI (3 to 7 and 8 and above, respectively). The participants in this study were directly asked to share their reactions and thoughts on problem gambling among SGD populations. The responses were classified into two broad themes. The first theme pertained to influences specific to SGD populations, and the second theme addressed contextual influences such as the COVID-19 pandemic and individuals’ financial needs. Influences specific to SGD populations

Participants offered diverse perspectives on why gambling habits might be prevalent within SGD communities. These perspectives encompassed four sub-themes: gambling as a coping mechanism for SGD-related struggles, lack of visibility and awareness of gambling issues and help resources, seeking a sense of community and belonging through gambling, and perspectives surrounding the gay community and risk-taking as a minor theme.

Gambling as a coping mechanism for SGD struggles

The predominant theme identified was the use of gambling as a coping mechanism for struggles specific to SGD individuals. It was brought up by 11 interviewees. Participants highlighted feelings of marginalisation, isolation and the need to escape from societal pressures through gambling.

Gambling is an escape. And when you are going through certain trials and tribulations of your life, or it depends on the community that you’re in, you could be going through stressful times. You may not be accepted by your family or may not be accepted by your work colleagues, and you’re suppressing stuff. (Jacob, male, gay, PGSI score = 5)

Anthony (male, bisexual, PGSI score=6) described boredom and social dynamics within the community as factors contributing to gambling habits. Logan (male, gay, PGSI score=14) spoke about how gambling and other forms of addictive behaviour may offer people a certain ‘high’ that may help numb pain from traumatic experiences. Lucy (female, bisexual, PGSI score=4) elaborated on the role of gambling and other forms of addiction as a form of escape while emphasising experiences of discrimination and bullying:

[…] LGBTQ people are also overrepresented in unhealthy gambling behaviors or excessive gambling behaviors on top of being vulnerable or more at risk of developing other types of addictions. So like there’s a root problem here, right? Like people don’t just do these things for no reason, and if you’re studying a population that has a history of being marginalized or a history of being more likely to be stigmatized and abused, it makes sense why, as a coping mechanism, you have like these unhealthy, maladaptive coping mechanisms, whether it’s drinking or smoking or gambling, to deal with the trauma of whether it’s homophobic abuse or the trauma of bullying, or even the trauma of feeling isolated […].

Lack of visibility and awareness of gambling issues and help resources

Four participants expressed a lack of awareness of gambling problems among the SGD populations or available help for such problems. Some participants spoke about how alcohol- and drug-related problems appear to take centre stage far more compared with gambling because they can be less visible and are less talked about than substance use issues.

You can see that with drugs again, [it’s] out and about, in the streets or in the bars, the clubs, whatever, whatever. But gambling is done a lot behind closed doors. They just don’t consider it an issue within the community because alcoholism and drug addiction are taking [those] sort of areas. It’s so up there, and it’s always relevant. […]. (Jacob, male, gay, PGSI score = 5)

Charles (male, gay/queer, PGSI score=8) elaborated that gambling problems are not as visible because of the shame and social stigma attached to them, which may deter people from being open about their struggles with gambling. In this same light, Anthony (male, bisexual, PGSI score=6) expressed:

[…] it’s so personal, nobody knows. So, it’s clear that with a survey, it’s easier to be honest than to say at a party – Hey, yesterday I lost my home because I’ve been gambling for two months, and I didn’t win.

Finally, Elisabeth (female, bisexual, PGSI score=9) voiced concern regarding how gambling is being openly promoted online by content creators and personalities, yet the issues that may arise from it are taken rather lightly. She elaborated on the lack of education regarding gambling, the harms associated with it and their perceived influence on people’s gambling behaviour.

Gambling as a way to gain a sense of community and belonging

Three participants spoke about how gambling and other addictive behaviours may be a way for SGD individuals to feel a sense of community and belonging among peers who engage in the same behaviours.

So, like in some of the studies on smoking and drinking in the LGBTQ, […] you will find for some people that’s their only community. They just go to the bar, they go to the gay bar, the lesbian bar, and they sit down, and they all have a drink, and everyone’s talking, and they’re all drinking. And that’s basically the equivalent of community for them […]. So, if that’s the only way to alleviate your community isolation or to cope with your trauma from the homophobic bullying and harassment stuff, I’m not completely surprised, [gambling] it’s another addiction behavior that’s correlating with the LGBTQ. It’s sad but not completely surprising. (Lucy, female, bisexual, PGSI score=4)

Moreover, William (male, pansexual/queer, PGSI score=5) mentioned that, particularly in SGD populations, a sense of community and belonging is important, which may have made the pandemic even more difficult to bear since it did not allow for communities to meet in person:

[…] LGBTQ people are used to having [a community], it may be a small community, but their community means everything to them. So being, you know, not being able to be with your community, I guess, would give rise to, like, that escapism that I was referring to and leading you to, you know, go gamble, and do things to kind of take your mind off the current state of affairs or whatever, i.e., the pandemic. So, yeah, losing your community in a sense that alters your behaviors to gamble or other maybe unhealthier habits […].

Minor theme

Perspectives surrounding the gay community and risk-taking

A less commonly endorsed theme emerged surrounding gay individuals specifically. Two participants shared their perceptions regarding risk-taking and excessive behaviours among gay communities.

[…] I think on the gay community [there is] a perspective of sort of like life is short, so just live it while you still can, so they can be a little bit more reckless with finances. So, I think they could be like me, so they would withdraw to gambling as a way to get out of financial issues. (Samuel, male, gay, PGSI score = 11)

[…] So, you know, whether that be drug use, gambling, sex addiction, there seems to be the spectrum that everything is more intense in our community [referring to the gay community]. (Logan, male, gay, PGSI score = 14)

Contextual influences on gambling among SGD individuals

Participants proposed that some contextual influences may explain the reported high prevalence of problem gambling among SGD populations. These mainly revolved around the extraordinary context and stressors of the COVID-19 pandemic, including gambling accessibility during this time. A smaller proportion of participants expressed that general financial needs might explain the prevalence of problem gambling among SGD individuals.

COVID-19 pandemic

The stressful context of the COVID-19 pandemic, and how it was linked to distress, boredom and higher accessibility in online gambling, was a recurring theme in participants’ responses. Some participants shared the impression that during the pandemic, gambling may have become a route to escape uncomfortable emotional states or manage feelings of isolation during this time.

[…] I’m going from a zero to doing gambling for a year. […] But you know, even if people who just have a little bit of gambling habits, that roughly exaggerate[ed] or accentuat[ed] whatever they had […] before. So, totally not surprised because I’d say like during the pandemic, it was so stressful, people were looking for a way out and gambling is a way out of it. (Jim, male, gay, PGSI score=5)

[…] I think everybody did everything in excess. If you were a drug addict, you were doing drugs in excess. If you were gambling, you were doing gambling in excess, if you were a sex addict, you were having more sex. I feel like everybody was doing more of whatever their problem thing was, they were trying to do more of it to compensate for this isolation that basically we were in. (Alice, female, bisexual, PGSI score=11)

Other participants spoke about how the pandemic brought feelings of boredom and diminished socialisation due to restrictive public health measures, which may have led some people to turn to gambling to have something to do.

[…] during the pandemic especially, I don’t think that there was a lot of those social things that happened before suddenly weren’t happening, so people needed other things to do. And I mean, I’m not sure it would be an assumption, but you could probably assume that there are more like within the LGBT+ populations, there’s fewer people with children and other like things to occupy their life, I guess you would say, so maybe they would be seeking other opportunities to occupy their time like at least for me […]. James (male, gay/queer/omnisexual, PGSI score = 10)

Subtheme: gambling accessibility

A less commonly endorsed subtopic related to the COVID-19 pandemic brought up by two participants was gambling accessibility during this period. Public health measures and regulations differed from one province to another in Canada during the COVID-19 pandemic, giving rise to two perspectives on this matter. One participant mentioned that gambling venues were among the few establishments permitted to continue to operate in their province during the pandemic, when several other businesses were forced to close. In this sense, gambling was more accessible than other in-person entertainment opportunities (eg, cinemas, indoor dining, gyms and shopping centres).

[…] during the pandemic, like casinos, like one of the very few businesses that are allowed to open and actually, like allow people to come in, so I think some people could go to [the] casino just for a sense of normalcy and then because like gambling games or did they just gamble just because they were there [already] […]. So, so, yeah, like I think sort of makes it worse because it was one of the very few places where you can actually come in and sit down during the pandemic. (Samuel, male, gay, PGSI score = 11)

The second perspective, voiced by a participant living in a province that did suspend the operation of gambling venues, concerned the increased accessibility of and transition to online gambling. In this context, online gambling was a direct alternative to in-person gambling, as well as to other entertainment opportunities.

I also think that it combined with, at least in my province, like I said, I think there was a general rise in online gambling at that time, which I presume is what most people would have been doing at that time, because if casinos were closed and I guess you could still go buy lottery tickets at like gas stations and stuff, but a lot of stores were closed also, people weren’t going out as much, so I would assume that it was mostly online for most people as well. James (male, gay/queer/omnisexual, PGSI score = 10)

Financial need

Finally, three participants, Rachel (female, bisexual, PGSI score=6), Samuel (male, gay, PGSI score=11) and Olivia (female, Two-Spirited, PGSI score=5), shared their impression that general financial need might explain why problem gambling was high in the online survey sample:

I don’t know, maybe people are a lot like me, and they feel like it’s really hard financially to get ahead right now. It almost feels hopeless. You know, bills paid, and it just is not like, I think a lot of people maybe are just hopeful. They’re struggling to get by financially, and I mean, that’s why we all gamble, [it] is that hope to win. (Olivia, female, Two-Spirited, PGSI score = 5)

Discussion

This study examined the perspectives of individuals from SGD populations who were at moderate-to-high risk of problem gambling in order to better understand their perspectives on problem gambling within SGD communities. Although the interview question invited participants to comment on problematic gambling across SGD populations broadly, the final sample consisted almost entirely of sexually diverse participants. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect sexually diverse participants’ perspectives and should be interpreted with caution in relation to gender-diverse populations. While our main objective was not to examine gambling specifically during the pandemic, given data collection took place shortly after the pandemic, many of the participants’ responses reflected the pandemic’s extraordinary context. A key finding was the mention of gambling as a coping mechanism for dealing with the stress emerging from experiences of stigma, marginalisation and discrimination due to their identity. Similar themes have been reported by Bush-Evans et al,32 who explained many SGD individuals in their study described gambling as a response to managing rejection, concealment and identity-related stress, and Bush et al,31 whose participants mentioned gambling and/or using substances to cope with the stress of ‘coming out’ on top of other general life stressors. Although stress is one of the universal causes of gambling problems,40 in line with previous studies carried out among SGD populations and with the minority stress framework,22 our results propose that additional stressors which are specific to their non-normative sexual and gender identity are present for these groups. In turn, these stressors represent an important burden for their psychological well-being, which may contribute to the adoption of maladaptive coping strategies.22 32 While the quantitative literature on the subject does not allow for a clear link to be made between problem gambling and minority stress due to the nature of the studies conducted,13 in the present study, this link was proposed by people from SGD populations (mainly sexually diverse) themselves. Thus, understanding how SGD populations’ experience of gambling and gambling-related harm are grounded in minority stress remains important to address the social determinants of the inequalities faced by these populations at individual and systemic levels.

The participants also highlighted a lack of visibility and awareness of gambling issues within SGD communities, contrasting it with more prominent prevention efforts aimed at substance use disorders. The presence of shame and stigma related to gambling emerged as significant barriers to openly discussing gambling problems and seeking help, consistent with general reports in the literature.41 42 Nevertheless, an added layer of reluctance, specifically for SGD individuals, to seek help could be associated with a history of negative experiences in broader service utilisation, as has been evidenced in previous research.4346 This is especially important to consider in light of evidence that addiction treatment services may not always be designed to be inclusive and may not be tailored to the diverse realities of SGD populations.47 48 In fact, burdens to help-seeking for problem gambling were also discussed by the participants in Bush-Evans et al’s32 study, who identified internalised stigma, lack of tailored services and inadequate staff training as barriers that led them to feel excluded from gambling support services. Once again, these experiences of stigma and exclusion in service utilisation may be contributing factors to minority stress, reinforcing maladaptive behaviours over treatment seeking.

Although this issue was not a dominant theme in participants’ responses, at least one participant raised concerns regarding the visibility of online gambling promotion and the limited awareness of its associated harms. This points to the value of examining more critically how gambling promotion may shape gambling-related harms among SGD populations, especially those already facing minority stress and other forms of vulnerability. Furthermore, a minor theme regarding risk-taking among the gay community was endorsed by two individuals. It is rather unclear whether the two participants’ self-perception is that risk-taking and excess are more prevalent among the gay community or if this is how they think others tend to perceive this community. Hence, efforts to destigmatise community-specific beliefs and problem gambling while enhancing awareness and support options among SGD communities are crucial and should be prioritised by policymakers and service providers.

Finally, the extraordinary context of the COVID-19 pandemic was also discussed as an explanation for why problem gambling rates appear to be high among SGD populations. Added to general stressors and lifestyle adaptations to the public health measures of the pandemic, SGD individuals may face additional challenges in being isolated from their community.49 For example, previous studies have described the worsening of mental health in individuals during the closure of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersexual, asexual, and two-spirit (LGBTQIA2S+) safe spaces, and even more so for those in unsupportive households.50 51 These negative experiences during a period of isolation and general boredom could exacerbate maladaptive coping behaviours, such as gambling and substance use, as expressed by the participants. This aligns with a growing body of literature documenting the pandemic’s impacts on substance use and gambling habits.5 9 52 Since society has resumed most of its usual activities in Canada as the COVID-19 health crisis has been contained, future studies should investigate SGD populations’ gambling habits under more typical circumstances to assess the lasting effects of pandemic-related stressors.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first qualitative explorations of the perspectives of SGD individuals regarding problematic gambling among SGD populations (in our case, mainly from sexually diverse participants). This approach allowed us to provide additional empirical support for the relationship between the concept of minority stress and gambling behaviours among SGD populations through a new perspective while providing individuals from some of these populations with an opportunity to voice their experiences regarding an issue that impacts them directly. Another strength of this study was the presence of an advisory committee to ensure the project’s relevance to the needs of the target population and stakeholders in the field. This committee involved partner organisations with experience in the field and partners with lived experience, who provided support in reviewing the interview guide used for this study (ie, ensured the terminology, phrasing and pertinence of the questions were adequate). Lastly, our sample size allowed us to obtain rich data and diversity in the responses and is consistent with standards observed in other qualitative studies in the gambling literature.32 53 54

This study is not exempt from limitations. Our sample (n=23) involved voluntary participants who all identified as sexually diverse, which entails important implications. First, participation implicated access to a device connected to the Internet for the online interview and the willingness to discuss their gambling experiences. Therefore, while the response rate for our study was adequate, our results may not reflect experiences of individuals facing deeper challenges of exclusion and marginalisation. Second, only one participant identified as gender diverse, which is an important limitation of our study. Given that the pool of potential participants was derived from a previous phase of this study,35 in which about 7% of the sample identified as being gender diverse (for reference, 0.3% of people aged 15 and older in Canada identify as gender diverse and 4% as sexually diverse),55 and given the additional two criteria for participation in the present study (ie, agreeing to be recontacted for participation after the first phase and scoring 3 or above on the PGSI), the number of potential participants from gender diversity populations for this study was very limited (ie, only five participants, all of whom received an invitation to participate in this phase of the project, but only one accepted following through with their participation). This may have introduced some selection bias to our recruitment process. Thus, these results must be interpreted carefully. While the interview question asked participants’ opinions regarding gambling among the broad SGD umbrella, responses reflect the views of sexual diversity individuals and do not necessarily represent the perspective of gender-diverse populations (eg, transgender, intersex, gender-fluid and other identities). Lastly, given the data collected for this study constitutes retrospective accounts, it is possible that participants’ responses are subjected to a degree of recollection bias, and the absence of field notes and participant validation may have limited the depth and the confirmability of the findings.

Future directions

Future research should focus on gaining further insight into sexual diversity and gender diversity groups by studying them separately, while also acknowledging the interconnectedness between sexual orientation and gender identity and expression. Existing studies that include both groups in a single sample tend to have a majority of sexual diversity participants,31 32 35 56 which may lead to the perspectives of gender diversity individuals being less well represented or analysed in depth. This is crucial to consider, given some research has identified that differences may exist within SGD groups in their experience of sexuality and identification with the LGBTQIA2S+ community,57 as well as uniquely complex trajectories among transgender and non-binary individuals regarding the fluidity of sexual orientation and gender identity and expression.58 These differences may lead to unique experiences of minority stress (eg, transphobia) that could indirectly influence individuals’ relationships to gambling. It remains important to broaden the exploration of gambling experiences, from both qualitative and longitudinal perspectives, among various populations for which data are currently scarce (such as among transgender, non-binary and gender non-conforming people), and to do so through intersectionality-informed approaches.32 This would allow to better understand how overlapping characteristics such as race, age and socioeconomic status could contribute to minority stress and to engagement in addictive behaviours.59 60 Finally, regarding gambling treatment and intervention services, our results underscore the need for less stigmatisation towards gambling in general, more visibility of prevention efforts and tailored services for vulnerable clienteles, including identity-affirming care.

Conclusions

The results of this study allowed us to complement the emerging qualitative evidence regarding the gambling experiences of SGD groups. While sharing similar experiences with other populations regarding gambling during the COVID-19 pandemic, such as the perspective that boredom, isolation and financial need may have led to an increase in their gambling practices (including online gambling), our results indicate certain challenges are unique to the SGD population. Support for the minority stress model was observed through themes describing gambling as a result of an undeserved and unique distress among SGD communities that emerged regarding identity-related marginalisation. The present stigma still associated with problem gambling may layer identity-related stigma, which all in all deters individuals from seeking support to reduce gambling-related harm. Future efforts to acknowledge the plurality of experiences, by exploring intersectionality and recognising the needs of specific groups regarding gambling within the SGD umbrella, could contribute to reducing gambling-related harms by improving the accessibility and relevance of support services for SGD populations.
