Accurate skin lesion classification on imbalanced dermoscopic images with high variance via the SCTFD framework
Authors: Xianjun Li, Junlin Ouyang, Yuxiang Chen, Zulong Diao, Kuanching Li, Dacheng He, Wei Liang, Aneta Poniszewska-Marańda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate skin lesion classification algorithms play a crucial role in improving patient survival rates by enabling early detection and timely treatment. However, current methods struggle with limited feature extraction capabilities, which are further compounded by challenges such as data imbalance and high intra-class variance, making precise diagnosis particularly challenging. To overcome these hurdles, this investigation proposes SCTFD (Synthetic Classification Transformer Framework for Dermoscopy), a novel dermoscopic image classification framework designed to enhance classification accuracy. First, the SCTFD generates minority class samples using a nearest sampling synthesis approach based on an encoder-decoder structure (CN-SMOTE). Subsequently, it extracts features using MARD-Net (Multi-head Attention Residual Dilated Network), which integrates spatial-channel attention to enhance CNN performance and global sliding window attention to reduce the computational complexity of the Transformer. Finally, the loss is computed using FDLoss, specifically designed to address data imbalance and high intra-class variance. To validate the proposed method, experiments are conducted on the ISIC 2018 and ISIC 2019 public datasets. Experimental results show that SCTFD achieved an accuracy of 92.81% and an F1 score of 0.93 on ISIC 2018, and an accuracy of 91.33% and an F1 score of 0.88 on ISIC 2019, significantly lowering the classification barriers for critical diagnostic tasks.

Introduction

Skin cancer diagnoses have steadily risen over recent decades. The 2023 United States Cancer Statistics Report1 estimates around 104,930 new cases annually, with 12,470 deaths in the U.S. Accurate skin lesion classification algorithms are essential for enhancing patient survival rates through early detection and intervention2. Currently, the primary method for diagnosing skin cancer relies on dermatoscopy. Dermoscopic examination utilizes an optical magnification device to capture images of the affected area, followed by medical experts employing visual observation-based techniques3 to determine the presence of skin cancer. However, manual visual inspections are often intricate, prone to errors, and subjective. Therefore, employing computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) techniques to automate the classification of dermatoscopy images holds substantial practical significance in furnishing dermatologists with accurate information. However, this approach requires complex prior knowledge, and traditional machine learning algorithms have limited capability in feature extraction, making it challenging to perform complex lesion image classification tasks. Deep learning advancements4,6,8,57 have facilitated end-to-end CAD model5,9,56,59 training, greatly improving classification accuracy. Despite the substantial progress in CAD, three major challenges still hinder the achievement of high precision:

(1) Limited sample sizes in publicly available skin lesion image datasets hinder models from effectively learning intrinsic lesion features, leading to overfitting. A common solution to this problem is data synthesis. SMOTE10 is commonly used for oversampling minority classes, as it effectively increases the number of samples in these classes. However, SMOTE-based methods, which synthesize samples through interpolation, cannot provide rich information and hinder the model from effectively learning intrinsic lesion features. Another approach for data synthesis is the use of generative adversarial networks (GAN)11. GAN learns a high-dimensional representation of the data distribution and uses a generator network to generate samples that resemble real ones. However, GANs involve dual networks, are challenging to train, and are prone to mode collapse12. To address the issue mentioned above, this paper proposes a sample synthesis method that integrates CNN with the nearest-neighbor SMOTE. This method can produce high-quality dermoscopic images rich in information and highly similar to real ones. Compared to popular generative models like StyleGAN13 and Stable Diffusion14, our method offers a simpler encoder-decoder structure that avoids the training instability of GANs and is computationally efficient for medical image synthesis. While StyleGAN generates high-quality images, it requires large datasets and extensive training, which may not be feasible for imbalanced medical data. Our method prioritizes distribution alignment with real samples through nearest-neighbor interpolation, making it more suitable for augmenting minority classes in skin lesion datasets.

(2) The current dermoscopic image classification networks face challenges in extracting global features and suffer from low computational efficiency. Existing models are broadly categorized as CNN-based15,16 and Transformer-based17–19 approaches. CNN may experience reduced accuracy with complex skin lesion images due to limitations in capturing local features20. Their sensitivity to positional information can also affect diagnostic accuracy when small variations are present in the image. On the other hand, Transformers exhibit high computational complexity when calculating the self-attention matrix, resulting in excessive resource consumption. This paper proposes a network framework that integrates spatial-channel attention mechanisms, CNN, and a global sliding attention mechanism to overcome the local feature limitations of CNN and the computational inefficiency of the Transformer.Fig. 1Sample distribution across classes in the ISIC 2018 and ISIC 2019 datasets.

(3) Almost all public skin disease image datasets exhibit the problems of data imbalance and high intra-class variance. For instance, as shown in Fig. 1, the ISIC 2018 and ISIC 2019 datasets exhibit significant data imbalance issues. Currently, data imbalance is primarily addressed through data-level or algorithm-level approaches. Data-level methods oversample the minority class to expand its sample size21–23. However, this approach may lead to excessive duplication of minority class samples, increasing the risk of overfitting and noise. Algorithm-level methods address data imbalance by improving the loss function, such as adjusting the loss weights for different classes24 or for samples of varying difficulty25, making the model more attentive to minority class samples. Nevertheless, these methods require careful weight tuning to avoid bias toward the majority class or excessive focus on the minority class, and they cannot solve the problem of high intra-class variance. To address these issues, this paper introduces an innovative loss function that combines FocalLoss and DiceLoss with an intra-class variance loss term to tackle the problems of data imbalance and high intra-class variance.

In summary, this work proposes a framework named SCTFD (Synthetic Classification Transformer Framework for Dermoscopy). SCTFD consists of three main components: CN-SMOTE for synthesizing dermoscopic images, MARD-Net, which combines CNN with a global dilated sliding window attention mechanism, and FDLoss, designed to address data imbalance and high intra-class variance. Related work

This section provides a brief review of three key aspects: skin lesion classification methods, Data-Imbalanced Loss Functions, and the Transformer.

Skin lesion classification methods

Expert-driven dermoscopic lesion classification methods3 are subject to bias, while feature-engineering-based approaches26–28 heavily rely on the prior knowledge of researchers. To facilitate secure data exchange, privacy-preserving federated learning frameworks 42 and fine-grained blockchain-based medical record sharing systems 58 have recently been proposed to enable collaborative training across medical institutions without compromising raw data privacy. Romero et al.29 utilized a CNN for dermoscopic image classification, achieving high accuracy in an end-to-end model. However, due to the limited sample size, the model’s accuracy could not be further improved. The emergence of IoT-based wearable dermatoscopic devices further necessitates secure and efficient on-device processing strategies 43. To address this issue, Shetty et al.21 and Yao et al.24 introduced CNN-based models for dermoscopic image classification, using data augmentation to expand the minority class samples. However, the generated samples were too similar to the original, failing to provide the model with valuable information. Zhou et al.22 and Peng et al.23 used semi-supervised learning to convert unlabeled dermoscopic images into labeled data, generating more informative image data. However, semi-supervised learning often generates incorrectly labeled data, which degrades model performance. Dermoscopic image classification usually faces challenges such as data imbalance and high intra-class variance. To address these issues, Wang et al.30 improved intra-class consistency and inter-class separability by optimizing the distance between class activation maps for similar samples. Yue et al.31 introduced Adaptive Weighted Balanced Loss to handle data imbalance. Yap et al.9 used CNN ensembles with weighted predictions to leverage the strengths of multiple models. However, these methods are based on CNN architectures32,33 in information sciences, which have limited receptive fields, making it challenging to capture global image structures and impacting classification performance. To overcome this limitation, Transformer-based architectures17–19 have been introduced and demonstrated strong performance. However, due to the high computational burden of the Transformer, they are not suitable for resource-constrained environments. To address this, Wang et al.34 utilized knowledge distillation to enhance the performance of lightweight models specialized detectors for capturing subtle pathological features60.

Nevertheless, existing network architectures struggle to simultaneously address the small receptive field of CNN and the high computational complexity of Transformer. To address these challenges, this study introduces a new network architecture that combines spatial-channel attention, CNN, and Transformer encoders. By introducing a global sliding window attention mechanism, the computational complexity of the Transformer encoder is lowered, enabling effective global feature capture while facilitating efficient model training.

Transformer

In 2017, Vaswani et al.38 introduced the Transformer model, initially applying it to machine translation tasks. In 2018, Parmar et al.39 were the first to use the Transformer model for computer vision, extending the Image Transformer to tackle image transformation and generation tasks. In 2020, Lanchantin et al.7,40 utilized the Transformer for multi-label image classification, achieving a significant improvement in classification accuracy. The introduction of DETR41 in object detection challenged the dominance of CNN, while mmTransformer46 achieved SOTA results in vehicle trajectory prediction. However, at this stage, CNN continued to serve as the backbone for feature extraction. To tackle this problem, Dosovitskiy et al.47 proposed the Vision Transformer, composed entirely of Transformers, which achieved SOTA performance on the ImageNet dataset. To address the issue of quick saturation within the model, Zhou et al.48 proposed DeepViT, utilizing re-attention mechanisms to enable communication between multiple attention heads and reconstruct the attention maps. Neural architecture search algorithms offer automated optimization of transformer structures for resource-constrained medical imaging applications 45.

However, the quadratic complexity of attention computation in Transformers poses challenges for deployment in edge computing environments 6. Therefore, this study employs a global expansion sliding window attention mechanism49 to enhance the computational efficiency of the attention matrix, thereby reducing computational costs.

CN-SMOTE for skin lesion image synthesis

DeepSMOTE50 integrates CNNs with SMOTE to generate high-quality synthetic images; however, the generated samples may deviate from the original data distribution. To overcome this limitation, we propose CN-SMOTE, which combines CNNs with nearest-neighbor–based SMOTE and nearest-neighbor sampling to reduce the discrepancy between synthetic and real samples, thereby producing dermoscopic images that more closely follow the original distribution.

CN-SMOTE adopts an encoder–decoder architecture, incorporates nearest-neighbor SMOTE strategies, and employs a composite loss function consisting of reconstruction and penalty terms. Fig. 3 illustrates the overall workflow of CN-SMOTE for dermoscopic image synthesis.Fig. 3The overall process of synthesizing dermoscopic images using CN-SMOTE.

The overall process of synthesizing dermoscopic images using CN-SMOTE.

As shown in Fig. 4, the encoder–decoder architecture comprises an encoding path and a decoding path. In the encoder, the input dermoscopic image is processed through four identical blocks, each consisting of a convolutional layer (Conv), batch normalization (BN), ReLU activation, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP), to extract and compress high-level features into a latent representation. The decoder reconstructs the image by first transforming the latent representation via an MLP, followed by four identical de-residual blocks, each including a transposed convolutional layer (DeConv), BN, ReLU, and an MLP, to progressively recover spatial details and generate the final output image. This architecture enables accurate image reconstruction and serves as the backbone of the proposed synthetic image generation framework.Fig. 4Encoder-decoder architecture of CN-SMOTE.

Encoder-decoder architecture of CN-SMOTE.

During the training phase of CN-SMOTE (Algorithm 1), the imbalanced training dataset is processed in batches through the encoder-decoder structure, and the reconstruction loss is computed (Lines 4-5). Specifically, each mini-batch is independently forwarded through the encoder to obtain a latent feature representation, which is then reconstructed by the decoder. The reconstruction loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_L$$\end{document} enforces pixel-level fidelity between the input images and their reconstructions, ensuring that the encoder-decoder learns a stable and class-agnostic embedding space.

In addition to the reconstruction loss, the CN-SMOTE loss function incorporates a penalty term based on the reconstruction of embedded images. This penalty term is designed to regularize the latent space by explicitly encouraging intra-class feature consistency while allowing controlled variability. This penalty is calculated as follows: during training, a class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document} is randomly selected from the available skin lesion categories, and a batch of examples is randomly sampled from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document} (Lines 6-7). The batch size of the sampled class-consistent images is kept identical to that of the reconstruction batch to ensure balanced gradient contributions during optimization. The number of sampled examples matches the batch size used for reconstruction loss, with all images originating from the same class.

The encoder compresses these sampled dermoscopic images into a compact feature representation. During decoding, the decoder reconstructs the images in a shuffled order (Line 8). This permutation operation is applied in the latent space rather than the image space, which prevents trivial identity mappings and forces the decoder to learn more robust and discriminative class-level feature structures. This shuffling introduces variability into the encoded feature space, causing the decoded outputs to deviate from the original inputs while still conforming to their structural constraints.

The shuffled encoded features are then reconstructed, and the penalty loss is computed between the reconstructed images and the original sampled images. The total loss is obtained by summing the reconstruction loss and the penalty loss (Lines 9-10). Both loss terms are jointly optimized using standard backpropagation with a learning rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, without requiring adversarial training or additional discriminators. Finally, back propagation is applied to update the model parameters (Line 11).

During the sample synthesis phase, a subset of samples from the minority class is selected and fed into the encoder, which compresses the input into a compact feature representation (Lines 16-17). Unlike GAN-based approaches, CN-SMOTE performs sample generation entirely in the learned latent space, avoiding instability caused by adversarial objectives. MARD-Net

In this paper, the MARD-Net (Multi-head Attention Residual Dilated Network) architecture was designed using a multi-stage methodology, as depicted in Fig. 5. The MARD-Net consists of a residual convolutional projection module (ResBlock), an attention module (RCBAM), and a Transformer encoder module (TFM-Enc). We first employ a preliminary convolutional module to capture low-level features, thereby enhancing the representation of lesion characteristics. However, since lesions typically cover a small part of the image, uniform focus on all areas may result in interference from irrelevant information. To address this, we incorporate an RCBAM (Residual Channel-wise and Spatial Attention Module) attention module into MARD-Net to enhance focus on key information within the image while suppressing irrelevant features, thereby improving task performance effectively. Additionally, lesions in medical images may present complex distributions, requiring a holistic analysis of multiple regions. While convolution is proficient at extracting local details, it lacks the capacity to capture long-range dependencies essential for a comprehensive structural understanding of the image. As lightweight Transformers exist, they often sacrifice global context for efficiency. Our global dilated sliding window attention mechanism maintains global receptive fields while reducing computational complexity from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^2)$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n \times w)$$\end{document}, where w is the window size. This is particularly beneficial for high-resolution dermoscopic images, where full self-attention is prohibitive. We propose a Transformer encoder employing a dilated global attention mechanism to extract global features. The green circle in the figure represents the identity ResBlock, and the red circle represents the projection ResBlock, which reduces the image size by half. The architectural design of MARD-Net is motivated by the complementary strengths of CNNs and Transformers in addressing the unique challenges of dermoscopic image analysis. Convolutional layers excel at capturing fine-grained local textures and hierarchical patterns crucial for identifying subtle lesion boundaries and morphological structures. However, their inherent locality limits the capture of long-range dependencies between distant lesion regions, which is essential for holistic diagnosis. Conversely, Transformers provide global receptive fields but at prohibitive computational costs for high-resolution medical images and risk overlooking fine local details. MARD-Net bridges this gap through a deliberate three-stage arrangement:

First, ResBlocks establish a robust foundation by extracting multi-scale local features while preserving spatial details through residual connections. This is critical for dermoscopic images where lesion characteristics manifest at various scales. Second, the RCBAM module strategically refines these features by adaptively recalibrating spatial and channel responses, suppressing irrelevant background regions (which dominate dermoscopic images) while amplifying discriminative lesion-specific patterns. This attention mechanism serves as a critical bridge, ensuring that only salient features propagate to the global modeling stage. Third, the Transformer encoder with global dilated sliding window attention captures long-range contextual relationships between potentially disconnected lesion regions, enabling holistic understanding without the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^2)$$\end{document} complexity of full self-attention. The dilated sliding window specifically balances computational efficiency with the need to model both local neighborhood interactions and global image structure.

This arrangement is not arbitrary: the progressive transition from local to global modeling mirrors the clinical diagnostic process—dermatologists first identify local lesion features (local patterns), focus attention on relevant areas (attention), then integrate these observations into a global assessment (global context). Moreover, the bottleneck design of RCBAM reduces the feature dimensionality before the Transformer stage, which not only decreases computational load but also concentrates semantic information, making global attention more efficient. Ablation studies (Section Ablation experiment) confirm that removing any component degrades performance, validating that each module provides distinct, non-redundant contributions that synergistically enhance feature representation for imbalanced, high-variance dermoscopic datasets.

Given the indistinct boundaries between lesion and non-lesion areas in the images, as well as the relatively vague features of lesion regions, we employ the ResBlock to extract fine-grained local features from the image. Our model includes multiple ResBlocks. The residual structure is divided into two types: identity projection (in green) and residual projection (in red). Identity projection directly transfers the output of the previous layer to the next, while residual projection applies a nonlinear transformation to the input features before forwarding them. To align the feature channels, we utilize a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document} convolution layer. This method may result in the loss of specific information; therefore, residual connections are used to bridge shallow and deeper layers, thereby enhancing inter-layer information exchange and alleviating this issue. Additionally, we apply Batch Normalization (BN) after each convolution operation to accelerate training.Fig. 6The detailed structure of the ResBlock.

In medical images, lesion areas usually cover a minor fraction of the image, and applying equal attention to all areas can lead to interference from irrelevant information. Therefore, this study introduces the RCBAM to address this issue, with its detailed structure shown in Fig. 7. When feature maps are input into RCBAM, they are first compressed through average pooling and max pooling along the spatial dimension. The combination of these pooling methods refines feature weight learning, thereby enhancing the network’s ability to represent features. The aggregated features are then passed through a multi-layer perceptron with a dimensionally reduced hidden layer to lower computational costs. Finally, the attention map is generated via an activation function. Additionally, to prevent gradient vanishing during training and enhance model stability, residual connections are integrated into the RCBAM.Fig. 7The detailed structure of the RCBAM.

The detailed structure of the RCBAM.

In images of skin lesions, lesion regions are often not isolated; the presence of multiple complex regions may require integrated assessment for a more comprehensive understanding. Although convolution is adept at capturing local details, it cannot model long-range dependencies between regions, which hinders the effective construction of overall structural information within the image. To improve the network’s grasp of overall structural information, we utilize a Transformer encoder equipped with an enhanced attention mechanism to extract global features from the feature maps. The structure is detailed in Fig. 8, with GSW-Attention denoting the global sliding window attention mechanism.Fig. 8The detailed structure of GSW-encoder.

The essence of the Transformer is in calculating the attention matrix between vectors, as expressed in the following formula:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}})V \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_k$$\end{document} represents the dimension of the input vectors, and Q, K, and V are obtained by multiplying the input matrix X with designated weight matrices. The computational complexity can be derived as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^2)$$\end{document}, where n denotes the number of vectors. To mitigate the computational cost of self-attention, we adopt a global sliding window attention mechanism in place of traditional self-attention. The core idea is that each vector observes only its neighboring w vectors, while certain key vectors can access all vectors. FDLoss

The currently available dermoscopic image datasets generally suffer from severe data imbalance, with a few categories dominating the datasets, resulting in an excessive proportion of easy samples that restricts the model’s ability to learn from challenging samples. In addition, substantial differences among samples within the same class make the decision boundaries between categories ambiguous, thereby reducing the model’s generalization capability. As a result, skin lesion classification encounters two major challenges: data imbalance and significant intra-class variance. As previously mentioned, FocalLoss and DiceLoss can effectively handle the problem of sample difficulty imbalance. However, neither of these loss functions can simultaneously resolve the issues of data imbalance and high intra-class variance. Therefore, this paper proposes a novel combined loss function, FDLoss, which integrates FocalLoss and Dice Loss and introduces an Intra-class variance loss to address both sample difficulty imbalance and high intra-class variance effectively.

As shown in Eq. 2, the modulation factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-p_{ic})^\gamma$$\end{document} is crucial for FocalLoss in addressing hard-to-classify samples. Therefore, this modulation factor is incorporated into Eq. 3 in this study. Additionally, to prevent division by zero, a constant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} is added to both the numerator and denominator, as indicated in Eq. 5:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{1}=1-2 \cdot \frac{\sum _{i=1}^{N}\sum _{c=1}^{M}(1-p_{ic})^\gamma \cdot p_{ic}\cdot y_{ic}+\epsilon }{\sum _{i=1}^{N}\sum _{c=1}^{M}(1-p_{ic})^{2\gamma }\cdot p_{ic}^2+\sum _{i=1}^{N}\sum _{c=1}^{M}y_{ic}^2+\epsilon } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is the adjustment factor constant, with a larger value diminishing the impact of easily classified samples on the loss, thus enabling the model to concentrate more on challenging samples.

On the other hand, due to the high intra-class variance in dermoscopic datasets, enhancing the inter-class difference while minimizing the intra-class variance involves reducing the variance of probabilities among samples of the same class while simultaneously increasing their mean probability, as shown in Eq. 6.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{2}=\sum _{c=1}^{M}\frac{1/p_c^\sigma + p_c^\mu }{1/p_c^\sigma \cdot p_c^\mu } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_c^\mu$$\end{document} denotes the average probability of class c obtained from the model, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_c^\sigma$$\end{document} denotes the variance of probabilities for class c computed by the model. Eq. 6 defines the reciprocal of the harmonic mean of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{p_c^\sigma }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_c^\mu$$\end{document}. By minimizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{2}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_c^\sigma$$\end{document} is reduced while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_c^\mu$$\end{document} is increased. Combining Eq. 5 and Eq. 6, the final loss function is obtained as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{FD}=\mathscr {L}_{1}+\mathscr {L}_{2} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The above process shows that this work addresses the issue of high intra-class variance in dermoscopic image datasets by designing a loss function. This method can be easily implemented in various deep networks without requiring any redesign of modules or frameworks.

Experiments

This section provides a detailed introduction to the datasets, evaluation metrics, and experimental particulars employed in the paper. We then perform a set of comparative experiments and ablation studies to validate the effectiveness of our approach.

Datasets

This work focuses on experiments with the ISIC 2018 and ISIC 2019 datasets to thoroughly assess the performance of our method in comparison to other approaches. Both are publicly available benchmark datasets containing only real clinical dermoscopic images captured by dermatologists in clinical settings; no generated or augmented data are included in the original datasets. An overview of these datasets is given below:

ISIC 2018: The ISIC 2018 dataset comprises a total of 10,015 labeled dermoscopy images, primarily sourced from the HAM10000 dataset51. The images are RGB color JPEG files with varying original resolutions ranging from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$540\times 720$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4499\times 6748$$\end{document} pixels, reflecting real-world clinical acquisition conditions. The dataset covers seven categories of skin lesions: dermatofibroma (DF), vascular lesions (VASC), actinic keratosis (AKIEC), basal cell carcinoma (BCC), benign keratosis-like lesions (BKL), melanoma (MEL), and melanocytic nevi (NV). The class distribution is highly imbalanced, with NV being the majority class (6,705 images) and DF being the minority class (115 images).

ISIC 2019: In contrast to the ISIC 2018 dataset, the ISIC 2019 dataset contains more images (25,331 dermoscopy images) and adds a new lesion type, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). The images are sourced from the HAM10000 dataset51 and the BCN20000 dataset52. Similar to ISIC 2018, these are RGB color JPEG images with heterogeneous resolutions between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1024\times 1024$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4499\times 6748$$\end{document} pixels. The dataset includes eight lesion categories with even more severe imbalance: NV dominates with 12,875 images, while DF remains the rarest class with only 239 images. All images are acquired using standard dermoscopic equipment and have been validated by expert dermatologists for diagnostic labels.

We provide a comprehensive computational cost analysis of the entire SCTFD framework, including training time, inference time, and memory requirements. All experiments were conducted on a single GeForce GTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB memory.

Training time: The SCTFD framework involves a two-stage training process. Stage 1 trains CN-SMOTE for 50 epochs on the imbalanced dataset, requiring approximately 4,200 seconds. Stage 2 trains MARD-Net for 300 epochs on the balanced dataset. Concluding remarks and future work

In this work, we introduce SCTFD, a novel framework for dermoscopic image classification, designed to address challenges such as limited sample size, small receptive fields in CNN-based approaches, high intra-class variance, and class imbalance in dermoscopic image datasets. SCTFD comprises three main components: CN-SMOTE for synthesizing dermoscopic images, MARD-Net, which combines a CNN with a global dilated sliding-window attention mechanism, and FDLoss to address data imbalance and high intra-class variance. Specifically, to augment the dermoscopic dataset, we introduce CN-SMOTE, a method that synthesizes high-quality dermoscopic images without the need for a discriminator, making it easier to train than GAN-based approaches. Additionally, we integrate the RCBAM attention module and a dilated sliding global attention Transformer encoder into ResNet18 to enhance focus on lesion areas and capture the global structure of dermoscopic images. Finally, we combine FocalLoss and DiceLoss with an intra-class variance loss term to address the challenges of data imbalance and high intra-class variance.

Experiments conducted on the publicly available ISIC 2018 and ISIC 2019 datasets validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, with a detailed comparison to several SOTA approaches. We visualize the model’s activation maps using the Grad-CAM method and the extracted image features via t-SNE. Results show that SCTFD achieves SOTA performance in dermoscopic image classification, providing valuable support for dermatologists in diagnosing skin lesions. While this study focuses on skin lesion classification, the proposed methods (CN-SMOTE, MARD-Net, and FDLoss) have potential applicability to other medical image domains with similar challenges. However, application to other modalities may require adjustments: CN-SMOTE’s encoder-decoder architecture should be adapted to the target image resolution and anatomical structures; MARD-Net’s attention window size and dilation rates may need tuning based on object scales; and FDLoss hyperparameters might require recalibration for different imbalance ratios.

Current limitations include performance on cases with subtle or ambiguous features, where the RCBAM attention mechanism may struggle to localize faint lesions, and CN-SMOTE’s synthesis quality may degrade for extremely rare pathologies with limited reference samples. Additionally, the global dilated attention might miss fine-grained details critical for early-stage disease detection.

Future work will involve testing SCTFD on additional datasets with varying imbalance ratios and intra-class variance to further validate its generalizability, and addressing these limitations through enhanced attention mechanisms and multi-scale feature fusion strategies.
