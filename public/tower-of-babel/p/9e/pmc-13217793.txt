Hysteroscopic fundal fixation of LNG-IUS in adenomyosis: a prospective feasibility and safety study
Authors: Congqing Li, Long Wang, Youjiang Xu, Liwei Zhang, Fei Song, Wenyan Wang, Bing Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExpulsion of the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) limits its effectiveness in adenomyosis management, particularly in patients with anatomical risk factors. This study aimed to assess the feasibility and short-term safety of a novel hysteroscopic fixation technique designed to improve device retention.MethodsIn this single-center prospective feasibility study, 52 patients with adenomyosis underwent placement of a modified LNG-IUS (integrated with a GyneFix anchor) using a customized delivery cannula under hysteroscopic guidance. Feasibility outcomes included successful device deployment and procedural time. Safety outcomes included intraoperative complications, device expulsion rate at 6 months (exploratory), and adverse events. Symptom changes (pain VAS, menstrual PBAC scores) were assessed as exploratory endpoints.ResultsThe procedure was successfully completed in all 52 patients (100%) with a mean operative time of 23.9 ± 8.1 min. No intraoperative complications occurred. During the 6-month follow-up, no device expulsions were observed (0%; 95% CI: 0–6.8%). Symptom assessments indicated reductions in median pain VAS scores (from 7.2 to 2.8) and PBAC scores. Irregular bleeding was common initially, persisting as light spotting in 18 patients (34.6%) at 6 months.ConclusionsThis feasibility study suggests that the novel fundal fixation technique is technically achievable and appears safe in the short term for patients with adenomyosis who have completed childbearing. The observed device retention rate is encouraging, but requires confirmation in larger comparative studies with longer follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04438-7.

Introduction

Uterine adenomyosis, a common gynecological condition characterized by the presence of ectopic endometrial tissue within the myometrium, affects a significant number of reproductive-aged women, with reported prevalence rates of 7–23% in the general population and up to 70% in high-risk groups [1–3]. Clinical manifestations such as dysmenorrhea, menorrhagia, and infertility substantially impair quality of life [4]. Current management strategies include NSAIDs for symptomatic relief, hormonal therapies such as GnRH agonists or progestins, and surgical interventions. However, these approaches are often limited by side effects, recurrence risks, or irreversibility [5–7].

The levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) offers a well-established, conservative treatment option by inducing local endometrial suppression. Nonetheless, its clinical utility in adenomyosis is compromised by a high expulsion rate (5–20% in the first year) and potential displacement, leading to symptom recurrence and suboptimal therapeutic outcomes [8, 9]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in patients with enlarged uteri or distorted anatomy. Current evidence indicates that uterine volume > 200 cm³ or anterior-posterior diameter > 5 cm increases expulsion risk by 2.3-fold [10], with meta-analyses reporting 12-month expulsion rates of 16.8% [11]. Recent data from Türkiye have further highlighted the clinical challenges associated with intrauterine device applications in complex gynecological conditions and the importance of optimized placement techniques to improve treatment outcomes [12].

It is important to distinguish the mechanism of action: device fixation itself does not directly alleviate symptoms. Rather, secure device retention ensures continuous local levonorgestrel delivery, which in turn suppresses endometrial and ectopic lesion activity, leading to symptomatic improvement [13]. Existing fixation methods, such as hysteroscopic suture fixation, though effective, demand specialized expertise and carry inherent risks including uterine perforation [14]. Therefore, there is a need for a more reproducible and simplified fixation technique.

To address this clinical challenge, we developed and evaluated a novel hysteroscopic fixation technique designed to secure the LNG-IUS within the uterine fundus. This report presents the feasibility and short-term safety outcomes of this approach.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-arm, single-center prospective feasibility study enrolled 52 patients diagnosed with adenomyosis at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between November 2023 and May 2025. Adenomyosis was diagnosed based on standardized transvaginal ultrasonography criteria according to the Morphological Uterus Sonographic Assessment (MUSA) consensus [15], including direct features such as myometrial cysts and hyperechogenic islands, and indirect features such as irregular junctional zone and asymmetric wall thickening.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 30–50 years and had completed childbearing or had no fertility desire; (2) desired uterine preservation; (3) concurrent presence of uterine fibroids or adenomyomas was permitted. The exclusion criteria included: (1) age < 30 or > 50 years; (2) current fertility desire or request for hysterectomy; (3) intolerance or unwillingness to comply with the study protocol; (4) active pelvic infection or genital malignancy; and (5) underlying medical conditions that could interfere with the study intervention.

Patient selection for operative hysteroscopy

All patients underwent the procedure in an operating room under general anesthesia based on the following criteria: (1) anticipated technical difficulty due to deep uterine cavity (> 9 cm) or distorted anatomy from concomitant fibroids/adenomyomas; (2) history of prior LNG-IUS expulsion, suggesting anatomical factors that might complicate blind placement; (3) need for concurrent operative hysteroscopy to treat associated pathology (e.g., polypectomy, resection of focal endometrial thickening); and (4) patient preference or anxiety regarding office-based procedures. Patients without these characteristics who would be candidates for office-based placement were not included, as the technique was specifically developed for complex cases.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. SL-XJS2024-043). Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All patients provided written informed consent, specifically regarding the off-label modification and use of the devices and the investigational nature of the technique.

Device modification, preparation, and regulatory considerations

The following devices were used: LNG-IUS (Bayer HealthCare Inc., Germany); GyneFix IUD (Tianjin HeJie Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China), a frameless, fixed intrauterine device; and a customized suction cannula (Nantong AoHua Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China), φ9.3 mm (28 F). The LNG-IUS was modified by removing its transverse arms. It was then aseptically connected to the anchoring thread of the GyneFix IUD in the operating room immediately prior to insertion. The customized cannula was single-use and sterilized by ethylene oxide. This composite use constituted an off-label procedure performed under the aforementioned ethics approval. Surgical procedure

Operative Technique

All procedures were performed by two senior gynecological surgeons, each with over 10 years of experience in operative hysteroscopy and more than 100 successful LNG-IUS placement procedures prior to the study. Under general anesthesia, patients were positioned in lithotomy. Uterine position was assessed, and cavity depth was measured prior to cervical dilation to Hegar No. 9.5. A STORZ monopolar resectoscope was introduced, and concomitant pathologies including endometrial polyps or hyperplasia—particularly focal thickening at the fundus—were resected. Uterine distension was maintained using 5% mannitol at 90–110 mmHg.

The core fixation procedure is as follows: A customized placement cannula, pre-loaded with a Hegar dilator, was advanced to the uterine fundus. After removing the dilator, the modified LNG-IUS–GyneFix composite was deployed through the cannula into the fundal myometrium; proper anchorage was confirmed by a distinct loss of resistance. The GyneFix anchor is designed to be embedded within the myometrium to a predetermined depth of approximately 1.0–1.2 cm, minimizing the risk of transmural penetration or perforation. The cannula was then withdrawn and the suspension thread trimmed at the external os. A schematic of this key procedure is provided in Fig. 2, and the surgical maneuver is demonstrated in Video 1.

Final hysteroscopic evaluation confirmed successful fixation: the anchor arms were securely embedded in the fundal myometrium, and the LNG-IUS body was positioned centrally along the uterine axis in close apposition to the endometrium, with no active bleeding or tissue injury observed—representing a key intraoperative criterion for procedural success.

Data collection and follow-up

Clinical information, including demographic characteristics, preoperative clinical profiles (uterine volume, hemoglobin concentration, disease type), and perioperative parameters, was extracted from the electronic medical record system. Postoperative transvaginal ultrasound was performed at each follow-up visit (1, 3, and 6 months) to confirm correct anchor position and assess the depth of myometrial embedding. All follow-up assessments were conducted in person.

Outcome measures

Primary feasibility outcomes

Successful device deployment (defined as correct fundal anchor placement confirmed hysteroscopically).

Operative time (from cannula insertion to final fixation).

Primary safety outcomes

Intraoperative complications (e.g., uterine perforation, significant hemorrhage).

Postoperative adverse events (categorized by type and severity).

Exploratory outcomes

Device expulsion rate at 6 months (with 95% confidence interval).

Pain intensity (visual analogue scale, VAS 0–10) at 1, 3, and 6 months.

Menstrual blood loss (Pictorial Blood Loss Assessment Chart, PBAC) at 1, 3, and 6 months.

Hemoglobin levels at 1 month.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As this was an exploratory feasibility study with no comparator group, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. The sample size of 52 patients was chosen based on feasibility considerations and to provide preliminary estimates for planning future trials.

For exploratory longitudinal outcomes (VAS and PBAC scores), which were non-normally distributed, the Friedman test was used to assess overall differences across the four time points. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. The device expulsion rate is presented with its exact 95% confidence interval calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. All tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Patients (n = 52)

The mean age was 41.84 ± 4.97 years, and the mean uterine cavity depth was 10.52 ± 0.89 cm (range: 8.9–12.4 cm). Uterine volume, calculated using the prolate ellipsoid formula (length × width × height × 0.52), was available for 45 patients (86.5%), with a mean volume of 186.4 ± 42.7 cm³ (range: 112–298 cm³). Twenty-three patients (44.2%) had uterine volume > 200 cm³, a threshold previously associated with increased expulsion risk [10].

Regarding uterine anatomy distortion, 31 patients (59.6%) had concomitant pathologies that altered the uterine cavity morphology: 11 (21.2%) had endometrial polyps, 9 (17.3%) had adenomyomas, and 11 (21.2%) had uterine leiomyomas (with 2 patients having both leiomyomas and polyps). These conditions resulted in varying degrees of cavity distortion, including focal protrusions, irregular contour, or asymmetric cavity enlargement.

In terms of treatment history, 15 patients (28.85%) had a history of prior LNG-IUS expulsion. Preoperatively, 22 patients (42.30%) with menorrhagia and dysmenorrhea received gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRH-a) therapy.

Feasibility outcomes

The procedure was successfully completed in all 52 patients (100%) with a mean operative time of 23.88 ± 8.10 min. Final hysteroscopic evaluation confirmed correct fundal anchor placement in all cases.

Safety outcomes

No intraoperative complications (e.g., uterine perforation, significant hemorrhage) occurred. Postoperative transvaginal ultrasound at each follow-up visit confirmed correct fundal positioning of the device in all 52 patients. No cases of partial expulsion, downward displacement (defined as > 1 cm migration from fundal position), or malrotation were observed during the study period.

No device expulsions were observed during the 6-month follow-up period (0/52; 95% confidence interval: 0–6.8%). No devices required removal during the 6-month follow-up.

Adverse events

The most common adverse effect was irregular vaginal bleeding, which was universal within the first 3 months postoperatively. By 6 months, irregular bleeding persisted as light spotting in 18 patients (34.6%); of these, 15 (28.8%) reported mild spotting requiring no intervention, and 3 (5.8%) reported moderate spotting that was managed with reassurance only. No patients required additional hormonal treatment or requested device removal due to bleeding.

Other adverse events included breast tenderness in 5 patients (9.6%) and mild systemic symptoms (e.g., fatigue, headache) in 6 patients (11.5%). All were transient and self-limited.

Exploratory symptom outcomes

Significant reductions in pain VAS scores and PBAC scores were observed across all follow-up time points (Friedman test, p < 0.001 for both). Post-hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction demonstrated significant reductions at 1, 3, and 6 months compared to preoperative baseline (all p < 0.001). Hemoglobin levels increased significantly at the 1-month follow-up (p < 0.05). Detailed symptom outcomes are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This prospective feasibility study evaluated a novel hysteroscopic technique for securing the LNG-IUS in patients with adenomyosis. The primary finding is that the procedure is technically achievable, with successful fundal anchor placement in all 52 cases and a mean operative time of 23.9 min, comparable to standard hysteroscopic procedures. The absence of intraoperative complications suggests an acceptable short-term safety profile in this cohort.

Device retention

The observed zero expulsion rate at 6 months (0%; 95% CI: 0–6.8%) is encouraging and compares favorably with published rates of 15–20% for conventional LNG-IUS placement in similar populations [16]. However, this finding must be interpreted with caution. As noted by the “rule of three,” with 52 patients and zero events, the true expulsion rate could be as high as 6.8%. Furthermore, the absence of a comparator group precludes any definitive conclusion of superiority over standard techniques. The study was not powered to detect differences in expulsion rates, and the results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Mechanism of action

It is important to emphasize that the symptom improvements observed (reductions in VAS and PBAC scores) are attributable to the pharmacological effect of continuous levonorgestrel delivery enabled by device retention, rather than to the fixation procedure itself. The stable positioning achieved through fundal anchoring likely ensures uninterrupted hormone exposure, which is consistent with the established efficacy of a retained LNG-IUS [17, 18]. This causal chain—fixation enabling retention, retention enabling continuous drug delivery, and continuous delivery enabling symptom control—should be clearly distinguished.

Comparison with existing techniques

Compared to existing fixation methods, such as hysteroscopic suture fixation [19], our technique offers potential advantages in terms of procedural simplicity. The customized placement cannula standardizes fundal delivery and may reduce the learning curve. Both surgeons in this study, with extensive prior hysteroscopic experience, successfully performed the procedure without a discernible learning curve effect (no failed deployments in early cases). However, the learning curve for surgeons with less experience remains to be determined.

Recent data from Türkiye have further highlighted that technical considerations during intrauterine procedures significantly influence postoperative outcomes in patients with challenging uterine pathologies [12]. These findings support the rationale for developing optimized fixation approaches.

Safety considerations

While no intraoperative or short-term complications were observed in this cohort, fundal fixation of an intrauterine device carries theoretical risks that warrant careful consideration:

Uterine perforation

The fixed-length anchor design (approximately 1.0–1.2 cm) and controlled deployment mechanism are intended to minimize perforation risk. The customized cannula is calibrated to stop at the predetermined fundal position, and a distinct “loss of resistance” signals proper deployment. However, improper technique, unrecognized uterine anomalies, or excessive force could theoretically result in transmural penetration. Surgeons must be thoroughly trained and should discontinue deployment if unusual resistance is encountered.

Infection

As with any intrauterine procedure, there is a risk of ascending infection or endometritis. Standard aseptic precautions were observed, and no infections occurred in this cohort.

Future pregnancy

The safety of this technique in women with future fertility potential remains unknown. All participants had completed childbearing. The anchor’s presence within the myometrium could theoretically affect implantation, placentation, or uterine distensibility during pregnancy. Should pregnancy occur, management would require ultrasound localization of the device; removal in early pregnancy, if feasible and desired, should follow established guidelines. This technique should currently be reserved for women who have completed childbearing until safety data in fertile populations become available.

Difficulty of removal

While no removals occurred during this short-term study, the presence of a myometrial anchor could theoretically complicate future device removal. If the anchor becomes embedded or fibrosed, hysteroscopic guidance might be required. Patients should be counseled regarding this possibility.

Long-term endometrial effects

The potential for chronic mechanical irritation or inflammatory changes at the anchor site over years of use has not been systematically studied and warrants investigation in long-term follow-up studies.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged:

Single-arm, non-comparative design

The absence of a control group precludes any claims of superiority over standard techniques. The observed 0% expulsion rate, while encouraging, may reflect patient selection, chance variation, or unmeasured confounders rather than a true treatment effect.

Modest sample size (n = 52)

This results in a wide confidence interval for the expulsion rate estimate (0–6.8%). The study was not powered to detect clinically meaningful differences.

Short follow-up duration (6 months)

Although most expulsions occur within the first 3 months [12], this timeframe is insufficient to assess long-term device durability, anchor stability, or late-onset complications. With longer follow-up (2–5 years), additional expulsions or device migrations could occur due to uterine remodeling, myometrial contractility, or anchor degradation.

Restricted study population

All patients had completed childbearing; therefore, the safety and implications of this technique in women with future fertility potential remain unknown. Additionally, all procedures were performed by highly experienced surgeons, which may limit generalizability.

Lack of validated patient-reported outcome measures

The absence of standardized measures for treatment satisfaction limits the assessment of overall clinical benefit.

No data on device removal

As no devices were removed during follow-up, information on removal difficulty, anchor integrity at removal, and associated complications is unavailable.

Future directions

Several key questions remain to be addressed:

Long-term outcomes

Larger prospective cohort studies with extended follow-up (2–5 years) are needed to confirm the durability of device retention, monitor for late-onset complications, and assess outcomes at device removal.

Comparative effectiveness

Randomized controlled trials comparing this technique to standard LNG-IUS placement are necessary to establish comparative efficacy and safety.

Ultrasound-guided placement

Studies evaluating the feasibility of ultrasound-guided placement (without hysteroscopy) could enhance accessibility in resource-limited settings.

Fertility populations

The safety and implications of this technique in women with future fertility desires should be systematically evaluated before broader clinical application.

Learning curve analysis

Formal assessment of the learning curve for surgeons with varying levels of experience would inform training requirements and implementation strategies.

Cost-effectiveness

Health economic analyses comparing this technique to standard placement and alternative fixation methods would inform resource allocation decisions.

Conclusion

This feasibility study demonstrates that the novel hysteroscopic fundal fixation technique using a modified LNG-IUS and dedicated placement cannula is technically achievable and appears safe in the short term for patients with adenomyosis who have completed childbearing. The observed device retention rate (0% expulsion; 95% CI: 0–6.8%) is promising, but these findings are preliminary. The technique’s comparative efficacy, long-term safety, applicability to broader populations, and implications for future fertility require validation in adequately powered randomized controlled trials with extended follow-up. While the results are encouraging, they should be interpreted in light of the study’s exploratory nature, modest sample size, and absence of a comparator group.
