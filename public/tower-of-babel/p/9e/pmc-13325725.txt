Association of lipoprotein(a) and peripheral artery disease: Findings from The hispanic community health study/study of latinos (HCHS/SOL)
Authors: Toshiki Kuno, Priscilla Duran-Luciano, Priyanka Gianchandani, Katrina Swett, Robert Kaplan, Martha Daviglus, Gregory A Talavera, Kunihiro Matsushita, Santica Marcovina, Matthew A Allison, Ayana K April-Sanders, Leandro Slipczuk, Mario J Garcia, Jianwen Cai, Olga Garcia-Bedoya, Daniela Sotres-Alvarez, Parag H. Joshi, Carlos J. Rodriguez
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a recognized risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), but equipoise exists regarding its association with PAD [1]. While a prior study reported a positive association between elevated Lp(a) and PAD [1], other studies did not [2,3]. The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) identified racial and ethnic differences in this relationship, with elevated Lp(a) significantly linked to PAD among Hispanic/Latino individuals, but borderline or no association among European American, African American/Black and Chinese American groups [3]. The relationship between Lp(a) and PAD warrants further investigation. Therefore, we leveraged data from the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) to examine the association between Lp(a) and prevalent PAD in the largest population-based cohort of Hispanic/Latino adult individuals.

Methods

A total of 16,415 self-identified Hispanic/Latino participants aged 18–74 years were recruited from four US field centers (Bronx, NY; Chicago, IL; Miami, FL; and San Diego, CA) from 2008–2011. We included participants with baseline measurements of Lp(a) [nmol/L] and data on PAD, defined as self-reported PAD (based on answers to the question, “Has a doctor ever said that you have peripheral arterial disease (problems with circulation, blocked arteries to the legs)?” and/or an ankle-brachial index (ABI) <1.0. A prior study from the MESA showed that a borderline ABI (ABI <1.0) is associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes compared to a normal ABI range (1.0–1.3) [4]. HCHS/SOL was approved by institutional review boards; all participants provided written informed consent.

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of HCHS/SOL, including clustering, stratification and sampling weights [5]. Lp(a), as a predictor, was modeled three ways based on its distribution: (1) continuously per 10 nmol/L increment; (2) log-transformed (per 1 unit increment) to correct for right skewness; and (3) categorically by quintiles (Q): Q1 (0 to <6 nmol/L), Q2 (6 to <13 nmol/L), Q3 (13 to <32 nmol/L), Q4 (32 to ≤77 nmol/L), and Q5 (>77 nmol/L). Weighted proportions of prevalent PAD were examined across Lp(a) quintiles. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the association between Lp(a) and prevalent PAD, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, smoking, and diabetes. Restricted cubic splines were used to model the continuous association between Lp(a) levels and prevalent PAD. Analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4.

Results

In the HCHS/SOL target population, the overall prevalence of PAD was 13.4 % (95 % CI: 12.5–14.3). The distribution of ABI was centered around 1.1, with 87.8 % ± 0.9 (SE) of individuals having an ABI >1.0. Cardiometabolic risk factors including obesity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CKD were all significantly more prevalent among individuals with PAD, (p < 0.01), Table 1. A significant association between Lp(a) levels and PAD prevalence was observed (Fig. 1A). Individuals with PAD had significantly higher mean Lp(a) levels (Fig. 1B), were older and more likely to be of Cuban descent. In the multivariable model, each 1-unit increase in log-transformed Lp(a) was independently associated with 8 % higher odds of prevalent PAD (Fig. 1C). Each 10 nmol/L increase in Lp(a) was associated with increased odds of PAD (OR [95 % CI]: 1.01 [1.00–1.02]). Lp(a) >77 nmol/L (Q5) was associated with 45 % greater odds of prevalent PAD compared to the lowest quintile (Q1) after adjustments for age, sex, diabetes, BMI, hypertension, and smoking (Fig. 1C). Discussion

We demonstrated a significant association between PAD and Lp(a), with 45 % increased odds of PAD when Lp(a) >77 nmol/L among Hispanic/Latino adults compared with the lowest quintile of Lp(a). Using similar ABI criteria and methods, MESA reported this association only among Hispanics/Latinos, with no statistically significant findings among European, Chinese American or African American participants [3]. Our study replicated this association in a Hispanic/Latino population more than ten times larger than MESA, lending support to prior hypotheses suggesting that the highly admixed nature and ancestral variations among Hispanics/Latinos may underlie ethnic differences in Lp(a) levels [5]. As Hispanics/Latinos represent a rapidly growing segment of the US population, now nearing 20 %, evaluating early screening and prevention strategies for PAD and its complications in this high-risk group, particularly through targeted Lp(a) treatment approaches, could be an important focus for future studies.

Limitations of the study include its cross-sectional design, potential recall or misclassification bias from self-reported PAD, and limited generalizability beyond US Hispanic/Latino populations. However, key strengths include its multicenter design, large, representative Hispanic/Latino sample, rigorous PAD phenotyping using objective ABI data, and use of Lp(a) molar concentrations which reflect particle number and are less influenced by apo(a) isoform size.

Conclusion

Greater levels of Lp(a) were associated with increased PAD prevalence in this large cohort of US Hispanic/Latino adults. These findings highlight a potential role for Lp(a) in PAD risk stratification and potential prevention target in this high-risk population.
