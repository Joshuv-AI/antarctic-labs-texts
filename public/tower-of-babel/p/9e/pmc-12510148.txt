Delayed Inflammation Occurred Only to VYC-25L After Coinjection of VYC-20L and VYC-25L in an Asian Patient
Authors: Haibo Xiang, Muqian Wei, Pala Yi, Rigele Ao, Yimou Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Delayed inflammatory reactions (DIRs) following hyaluronic acid filler injections are uncommon but pose significant clinical challenges. We report a 23‑year‑old Asian woman who developed a selective DIR confined to her chin after receiving a 4-mL VYC‑25L injection. Mild redness on the chin emerged 1 month postinjection, resolving after botulinum toxin treatment for mentalis hyperactivity. Seventy days postinjection, following dental procedures, the patient developed acute chin edema, pain, and redness. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated localized inflammation without abscess formation. Emergency hyaluronidase administration resulted in rapid symptom resolution. This case highlighted selective DIR to VYC-25L in an Asian patient, potentially associated with a large-volume, single-plane injection into an area of elevated tissue tension. Preventative strategies, including pretreatment muscle relaxation, staged injections, volume limitation, and dual-plane placement, are proposed in this case report.

Due to the relatively shorter mandibular bone in Asian patients compared with Western individuals, there has been a growing demand in recent years for correcting mandibular retrusion using hyaluronic acid (HA) fillers. Common complications of HA injection include local redness, swelling, pain at the injection site, and more severe events such as infection and embolism.1 Delayed inflammatory reactions (DIRs) are relatively rare. A study involving 4500 patients reported a 0.98% incidence of all delayed adverse events following HA injection,2 with the incidence of DIR being even lower. Here, we present a representative case that exhibited a DIR, secondary to HA injection.

CASE REPORT

A 23-year-old woman sought treatment for a short and retruded chin and reported no history of autoimmune disorders. Her only previous cosmetic procedure was Botox injections for glabellar and crow’s feet lines, with no complications. This time, she received 2 mL of VYC-20L (Voluma, Allergan) in the cheeks (0.6 mL supraperiosteal, 1.4 mL superficial subcutaneous), 1 mL of VYC-25L (Volux, Allergan) in the mandibular angle (superficial subcutaneous), and 4 mL of VYC-25L (Volux, Allergan) in the chin (0.3 mL supraperiosteal, 3.7 mL subcutaneous). After the treatment, the retrusion and shortness of the chin improved significantly, with the facial convexity angle increasing from 147.6 to 153.1 degrees (Fig. 1).

Approximately 1 month postinjection, the patient observed mild redness localized to the chin, which was not apparent under natural light and not accompanied by any pain. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no obvious signs of local inflammation or soft tissue edema. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the MRI examination performed 1 month postinjection. HA was visible in the chin region, with no significant signs of inflammation or edema, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E413.) We suspected it was caused by excessive skin tension in the chin resulted from hyperactivity of the mentalis muscle. Botox was injected into the mentalis muscle (3 U per side) to induce relaxation, along with 20 U per side into the platysma to elevate the jawline. The redness gradually subsided during the subsequent month.

On day 70 postinjection, the patient underwent dental restoration and periodontal scaling at the department of stomatology. The next day, she reported redness, swelling, and increased skin temperature in the chin area (Fig. 1C). On day 73 postinjection, blood tests and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were obtained, revealing normal white blood cell counts and CRP values. She took cephalosporin antibiotics and tinidazole prophylactically, but the chin inflammation intensified over the following week, with the onset of pain and mouth-opening limitation. Ten days after the dental procedure (day 80 postinjection), the patient returned due to aggravated swelling and pain. MRI imaging showed significant chin edema and signs of inflammation compared with the prior one, with no adverse findings in the cheek region. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the MRI examination performed on day 80 postinjection. It revealed marked inflammation and edema in the chin region, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E414.) We performed emergency injection of hyaluronidase (450 U) into the subcutaneous region of the chin. Within 24 hours of hyaluronidase administration, her pain, swelling, and mouth-opening limitation were significantly relieved. Seven days later, the swelling had nearly disappeared, and pain and mouth-opening limitation had completely resolved (Fig. 1D). The chin was smooth with no palpable nodules. Additionally, the projection of the chin remained improved compared with the baseline.

DISCUSSION

We determined that the clinical manifestations in this case were best explained by DIR, rather than by infection, embolism, or other causes. Several lines of evidence support this conclusion: (1) The initial mild redness appeared 1 month postinjection, was painless, had no mottling or ulceration, and lasted for at least a month. (2) Despite undergoing dental treatment, the blood tests and CRP on day 3 postprocedure showed no signs of infection, and symptoms worsened after a week of antibiotic therapy. (3) MRI scans at both 1 month and day 80 postinjection revealed no distinct infectious foci, with only marked edema observed on day 80. (4) After administration of hyaluronidase, the adverse reactions resolved rapidly.

Nowadays, the etiology of DIR remains unclear. Some studies suggest that DIR is mediated by delayed-type (type IV) hypersensitivity.3,4 However, a recent study indicates that DIR is not associated with the adaptive immune system.5 Regardless of the mechanism, various triggers for DIR have been widely reported, including vaccination and dental treatments.6,7 Our patient had previously received a COVID-19 vaccination and underwent dental treatment before the onset of swelling and pain, which is consistent with known DIR triggers. The patient developed mild redness in the chin 1 month after injection, which resolved after mentalis muscle relaxation. Such signs may represent impaired circulation caused by localized mechanical tension or serve as a harbinger of DIR, and merit closer observation in future cases.

This is the first reported case in an Asian patient of DIR occurring exclusively after VYC-25L injection, despite simultaneous administration of both VYC-20L and VYC-25L. A similar case was previously reported in a Canadian patient.8 Both products are manufactured using Vycross technology, with VYC-25L exhibiting a higher elastic modulus and longer duration.9 We believe that a large-volume, single-plane injection is a major contributing factor to DIR, particularly in patients with pronounced chin retrusion and elevated tissue tension. For patients with elevated chin tension, we suggest pretreatment with botulinum toxin for mentalis relaxation 2 weeks before performing staged filler injections. The injection volume in a single session should ideally not exceed 2 mL, and a dual-plane approach (supraperiosteal and subcutaneous) is recommended to minimize the risk of DIR.

CONCLUSIONS

This case highlights the risk of DIR with high-modulus HA fillers (VYC‑25L) for chin augmentation, particularly when large volumes are injected in a single plane into high-tension areas and subsequent risk factors are present. Although effective for mandibular retrusion, caution is warranted regarding injection techniques and volumes in the chin. Preventative strategies—including muscle relaxation, staged lower volume, and dual-plane injections—could minimize DIR risk in such sensitive, high-tension zones.
