The translation and validation of the surgical anxiety questionnaire into the modern standard Arabic language: results from classical test theory and item response theory analyses
Authors: Abdulmajeed Abdullah Alghamdi, Khalid Alghuthayr, Saad Sh. S. M. M. Alqahtani, Ziyad Ali Alshahrani, Abdullah Mohammed Asiri, Hadeel Ghazzawi, Mai Helmy, Khaled Trabelsi, Mariwan Husni, Haitham Jahrami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative anxiety is commonly found in patients who are waiting for surgery and can lead to negative surgical outcomes. Understanding the sources of surgical anxiety allows healthcare providers to identify at-risk patients and implement psychosocial interventions such as counseling, relaxation techniques, and cognitive‒behavioral therapy to minimize anxiety. Few comprehensive psychiatric measures are available to assess preoperative anxiety in Arabic.ObjectiveOur study aimed to translate, adapt, and validate the Surgical Anxiety Questionnaire (SAQ) into the modern standard Arabic language, also known as Fusha al-Asr Arabic.MethodsTo translate the questionnaire, the research team used the gold standard process of forward translation by two independent translators along with back translation evaluation by four trained medical doctors. A cross-sectional study was conducted using an online survey completed by 208 Arabic speakers (mean age 38 years, 44% women) from four countries. Psychometric analyses, which included internal consistency, test-retest reliability, convergent validity, confirmatory factor analysis, and item response analysis, were performed. Convergent validity tests were performed against the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 2-item Scale (GAD-2), Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-2), Perceived Stress Scale 4 (PSS-4), and Arabic version of the Visual Analog Scale for anxiety (VAS-A).ResultsThe mean SAQ of our sample was 19.38 ± 12.63 (possible range 0–68). The Arabic SAQ translation demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with McDonald’s omega and a Cronbach’s alpha of approximately 0.90. The test-retest reliability was also high, with an intraclass coefficient of 0.94. The SAQ showed strong convergent validity against the GAD-2 (r = 0.94, p < 0.01). The SAQ also showed weak-moderate correlations with the PHQ-2 (r = 0.26, p < 0.01), PSS-4 (r = 43, p < 0.01), and VAS-A (r = 0.36, p < 0.01) scores. The original three-factor structure was supported by confirmatory factor analysis, confirming the original structure reported in the original English language version. The results for fitness indices showed acceptable preliminary results (CFI/TLI approximately 0.90), and deleting some items improved the model fit (CFI/TLI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08). We suggest retaining the original factorial solution until further validation studies can be conducted. The item response theory (IRT) results identified no items that were excessively difficult or subject to guessing. The multidimensional IRT provided evidence that the SAQ items form a multidimensional scale assessing surgical anxiety that fits the classical model reasonably well.ConclusionThe SAQ has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity; thus, it is a trustworthy and valid tool for evaluating preoperative anxiety in Arabic speakers. Future research could benefit from using the SAQ in both surgical and psychiatric research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06142-y.

Introduction

Anxiety is defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as anticipated apprehension about a future threat [1]. It is characterized by feelings of tension, worried thoughts and physical changes such as increased blood pressure [1]. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) describes anxiety as feelings of excessive worry about everyday events with associated symptoms such as restlessness or feeling on edge [2]. Other symptoms of anxiety may include fatigue, irritability, sleep problems, increased heart rate, sweating, nausea or gastrointestinal issues, avoidance of certain situations or activities, problems in concentration and muscular tension [3].

Surgical procedures can act as potent stimuli that may trigger or exacerbate anxiety in patients [4]. This anxiety response is multifaceted and involves emotional, cognitive, and physiological components [3]. On the emotional level, patients may experience a range of negative feelings, such as apprehension, dread, or panic, when faced with the prospect of surgery [5, 6]. These emotions can stem from fears about potential complications, pain, or the overall uncertainty surrounding the procedure and its outcomes [5]. Cognitively, anxious thoughts and ruminations may pervade the patient’s mind, leading to excessive worry and catastrophizing about the worst-case scenarios [5]. Patients may find themselves dwelling on the risks involved, second-guessing their decision to undergo the procedure, or experiencing intrusive thoughts about the surgery’s potential consequences [7]. Physiologically, anxiety can manifest as various physical symptoms, including increased heart rate, rapid breathing, sweating, tremors, and muscle tension [7]. These physiological responses are part of the body’s natural “fight-or-flight” reaction, which prepares the body to deal with perceived threats or stressors [5, 7].

Fear of pain after surgery is one of the major causes that may increase the level of preoperative anxiety; this concern may arise from fears of not receiving enough analgesics [8]. Anesthesia also increases the level of preoperative anxiety, as patients may worry about waking up during surgery or not waking up after it ends [8]. Fear of death also plays a role in increasing anxiety in patients, and it may also increase in our region, where strong and close family relations are prevalent [8]. Thus, the surgical experience, with its inherent risks, unfamiliar environments, and potential for discomfort or pain, can trigger this complex interplay of emotional, cognitive, and physiological responses in patients, leading to heightened states of anxiety [5]. Recognizing and addressing this anxiety is crucial for healthcare professionals because it can impact a patient’s well-being, recovery, and overall surgical experience.

Several studies have documented the magnitude of surgical anxiety. For example, a study conducted in Iran, which included 300 adult participants, revealed that nearly two-thirds of them had upper-middle anxiety symptoms [9]. Finally, a study performed in Jordan to assess preoperative anxiety in 794 participants identified two distinct groups: the first group, representing 30.6% of the sample, had high preoperative anxiety, while the second group, representing 69.9% of the sample, had low preoperative anxiety [8]. This indicates that nearly all participants had different levels of preoperative anxiety [8].

There are several instruments available for measuring preoperative anxiety in the English language, such as the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS), which is a relatively new and comprehensive tool that not only assesses preoperative anxiety but also evaluates the patient’s need for information concerning the surgical procedure [10]. The Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (YPAS) is another tool specifically designed to assess preoperative anxiety in patients [4]. Additionally, the Perioperative Anxiety Scale-7 (PAS-7) questionnaire was designed to measure preoperative anxiety levels in surgical patients [11]. The Surgical Anxiety Questionnaire (SAQ), developed in 2018, is a surgical-specific anxiety scale designed as a patient-reported outcome measure for use in the field of surgery [5]. The SAQ is a 17-item scale, with its initial items developed from one-on-one qualitative interviews conducted with preoperative patients [12]. Each item represented a specific aspect of surgery that patients found worrisome [12]. The interviews revealed several themes, including concerns about presurgical procedures, surgical complications, fear of symptoms, the recovery process, and the organization and delivery of care [12]. These themes were combined with information gathered from an extensive literature search to identify any additional aspects of surgery that may cause anxiety [12]. As a result, an initial list of 50 items was generated [12]. To ensure the quality of the item list, multiple rounds of review were performed [12]. First, an interdisciplinary team of medical experts assessed the relevance of each item [12]. Then, colleagues in the health psychology department reviewed the structure and format of the list [12]. Finally, five patients provided feedback on wording clarity and content coverage [12]. Following these reviews, 25 items were either removed or merged into broader items, and two new items were added [12]. The final item list used for the exploratory factor analysis consisted of 26 items. The final SAQ-17 covers six domains: concerns about health; concerns about recovery; concerns about procedures; worries about being awake or conscious during surgery; worries about length of time returning to daily activity; and worries about pain and discomfort [5]. Patients rate each item on a five-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely), with total scores ranging from 0 to 68 and higher scores indicating greater anxiety [5]. The SAQ has demonstrated strong psychometric properties and the ability to concisely measure surgical anxiety [5].

The Arabic language has a significant global presence, with over 380 million native speakers worldwide [13]. However, the availability of validated and translated preoperative anxiety assessment instruments in Arabic is quite limited [14]. One notable exception is the APAIS, which was translated and validated for use in Arabic-speaking populations in 2022 [14]. The need for validating additional tools arises from the unique characteristics and advantages that each instrument offers.

The purpose of this study was to investigate psychometric properties of the SAQ in Modern Standard Arabic language, also known as Fusha al-Asr. The SAQ has been specifically selected for translation into Arabic due to its surgical specificity, comprehensive coverage of key domains of surgical anxiety, robust psychometric properties, and conciseness, which could make it a suitable and practical tool for assessing preoperative anxiety in Arabic-speaking surgical populations. The SAQ was selected for translation and validation in the Arabic-speaking context due to its focus on domains directly relevant to the concerns and anxieties experienced by surgical patients, its inclusion of 17 items spanning six key subscales of surgical anxiety, its demonstrated reliability and validity in the original English version, and its relatively concise format.

Methods

Translation processes

The process of translation and back translation of the SAQ followed best practices to guarantee accuracy and cultural appropriateness. There were six steps: Step (1) Forward translation from English into Arabic by two independent fluent translators who were trained physicians. They aimed for a culturally appropriate Arabic version that maintained the meaning of each item. Step (2) Synthesis - The translators compared versions and reached consensus on a final Arabic translation after thoroughly reviewing each item for linguistic clarity, precision, and cultural/religious compatibility. Step (3) To enhance the face validity of the Arabic translation of the SAQ, two new independent fluent translators who were unaware of the original English version back-translated the SAQ from Arabic into English. These two new translators are also trained physicians. Step (4) An expert in both languages compared the two back-translations to the original English to identify any ambivalence. Step (5) Differences were discussed with the translators to guarantee that the Arabic version precisely reflected the original English concept. Differences were discussed by a multidisciplinary expert committee consisting of translators, a psychiatrist, a statistician, and a linguist who reviewed the original SAQ, the forward Arabic translation, and the back translations, consolidating them into a final version. The committee’s review focused on identifying and addressing any discrepancies, ambiguities, or issues to refine the adaptation. This meticulous process, involving multidisciplinary expertise and iterative refinement, aimed to establish strong face validity and ensure that the Arabic SAQ was a conceptually equivalent and culturally appropriate adaptation of the original instrument. Furthermore, to statistically evaluate the agreement between the two forward translators and ensure translation accuracy, Cohen’s kappa coefficient [15] and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [16] were calculated for each questionnaire item. These statistical techniques are commonly used to assess interrater reliability between two or more raters. The kappa coefficient evaluates interrater agreement for categorical items, whereas the ICC assesses the consistency between raters for quantitative measures. Across the 17 questionnaire items, Cohen’s kappa ranged from 0.88 to 1.0, and the ICC values ranged from 0.97 to 1.0. As values over 0.80 generally represent strong or near-perfect agreement, these results demonstrate a high level of concordance between the two independent forward translations. This statistical quantification provided quantitative evidence that the two forward translators had minimal discrepancy and produced highly consistent translations, underscoring the accuracy and reliability of the iterative translation process. Step (6) After the translation process, pilot testing of the Arabic version of the SAQ was conducted with 20 Arabic-speaking participants to evaluate the clarity, understandability, and readability of the items. The pilot sample included acquaintances and family members of the research team. No changes were made to the questionnaire after pilot testing. The same Arabic version of the SAQ was then used in the larger validation study. The pilot test provided qualitative feedback to ensure that the language was clear and comprehensible before the Arabic SAQ was administered more broadly.

Data collection

We recruited participants through targeted advertisements on social media platforms to identify individuals with pending surgeries in the next four weeks. The study was facilitated through social media platforms (e.g., Instagram, Facebook, Discord, Twitter) and immediate messaging services (e.g., WhatsApp, Viber, Signal) across the researchers’ networks in Bahrain, Egypt, Jordan and Tunisia. Interested participants were guided to an online Arabic SAQ survey hosted on Google Forms generated by the first author. Before completing the survey, informed consent was obtained, and participants provided basic demographic information, including age, sex, type of surgery, financial status, sponsorship/coverage of the surgery, and study measures. A subset of 50 participants completed the Arabic SAQ two times, with two weeks between each, to evaluate test-retest reliability.

This study aimed to validate the Arabic translation of the SAQ among individuals scheduled for upcoming surgery rather than those currently hospitalized. By recruiting participants scheduled for surgery within the next 4 weeks, we intended to capture their preoperative anxiety levels and information needs in a more naturalistic setting, reflecting the typical circumstances in which the SAQ would be administered. The inclusion of hospitalized patients may have introduced potential confounding factors, such as acute stress or anxiety related to their current medical condition or hospitalization, which could have influenced their responses and compromised the validity of the SAQ validation process.

The study included adults aged 18 years or older who were scheduled for surgery within the next 4 weeks. General anesthesia was not an inclusion criterion. To be eligible, participants had to be willing and able to participate in the study by completing the online survey after providing informed consent. Participants needed to be able to read and comprehend Arabic at a level sufficient to understand the questionnaire. Any potential participants who were unable or unwilling to complete the survey, or who did not provide informed consent, were excluded.

Measures

Surgical anxiety questionnaire (SAQ)

The SAQ is a 17-item version of the original SAQ 26-item scale, developed to assess preoperative anxiety in surgical patients. The SAQ consists of 17 items that load onto three subscales. First, concerns about Health Subscale (6 items): This subscale measures concerns related to the individual’s health, with items 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, and 13. Second, concerns about Procedures Subscale (4 items): This subscale taps into concerns about medical procedures, with items 2, 15, 16, and 17. Third, concerns about Recovery Subscale (4 items): This subscale assesses concerns related to the individual’s recovery, with items 1, 3, 4, and 5. In addition to these three subscales, the original scale developers retained three individual items to represent Subscale 4, “worries about being awake or conscious during surgery” (item 6); Subscale 5, “worries about length of time returning to daily activity” (item 14); and Subscale 6, “worries about pain and discomfort” (item 10) [5]. Patients rate each item from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely), with total scores between 0 and 68 and higher scores indicating greater anxiety [5]. The SAQ has demonstrated strong psychometric properties and the ability to concisely measure surgical anxiety [5]. Specifically, the internal consistency of the SAQ and its subscales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The SAQ summary score, which consists of 17 items, had high internal consistency in the original English language version, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91 [5]. The “Concerns about health” subscale, which includes 6 items, also demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 [5]. The “concerns about recovery” subscale, composed of 4 items, had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78 [5], indicating acceptable reliability. Finally, the “Concerns about procedures” subscale, which also consists of 4 items, had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.75, suggesting moderately acceptable reliability [5].

Patient health questionnaire 2 (PHQ-2)

The 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) is a self-report questionnaire that is ultra-brief and consists of a 2-item depression scale [17]. The total score ranges between 0 and 6, with a score of 3 or greater considered a positive screening [18]. We utilized the available Arabic version of the PHQ-2 [19]. The Arabic language version of the PHQ-2 previously showed adequate psychometric properties [19]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the PHQ-2 was considered acceptable (α = 0.79), according to previous research [20].

Generalized anxiety disorder 2 (GAD-2) scale

The GAD-2 is an easy and very brief tool for initial screening that assesses anxiety/nervousness and uncontrollable worry for individuals with general anxiety disorder [21]. The total score ranges between 0 and 6 [21]. A cutoff score of 3 points is preferred for identifying possible cases, and further generalized anxiety disorder diagnostic evaluation is warranted [21]. The GAD-2 cutoff has a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 83% for the diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder [21]. We utilized the available Arabic version of the GAD-2, which has previously demonstrated sufficient psychometric characteristics [22]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the GAD-2 was deemed acceptable according to previous research, with a value of α = 0.84 [20].

Perceived stress scale 4 (PSS-4)

This measure assesses apparent stress and/or the degree to which people appraise their lives as stressful. Three versions of the PSS (i.e., PSS-4, PSS-10, PSS-14) were developed. The PSS-4 contains 4 items with a 4-choice response format, in which 0 identify “never” and 3 “almost every day”. The total score of the PSS-4 ranges from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress [23]. We utilized the available Arabic version of the PSS-4, which has previously demonstrated adequate psychometric properties [24]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the PSS-4 was deemed acceptable (α = 0.79), according to previous research [25], .

Arabic version of the visual analog scale for anxiety (VAS-A)

The visual analog scale for anxiety (VAS-A) is a tool that aims to quantify anxiety and ranges across different levels of severity [26, 27]. The VAS-A uses a horizontal line to represent a continuum of anxiety severity over the past 24 h [28]. The line is typically 100 mm (10 cm used in this study) long, with descriptive anchor words such as “no anxiety” on the far left and “extreme anxiety” on the far right [26, 27]. To use the VAS-A, participants mark the point on the line that they feel best represents their current level of anxiety [26, 27]. The score is determined by measuring the distance in millimeters from the left end of the line to the point marked by the participant [26, 27]. Higher scores indicate greater anxiety levels [26, 27].

Sample size

A minimum sample size of 170 was estimated to be adequate for validating the Arabic SAQ, based on guidelines of 5–10 participants for each questionnaire item [29]. This offers 80% statistical power for factor analysis and other validation measures used. To further increase power, the aim was to include 200 participants. To evaluate test-retest reliability, a subsample of participants (n = 50) completed the questionnaire two times, with a two-week interval between administrations.

Ethical issues

Prior to the collection of data, this study was reviewed and approved by the University of Menoufia in Menoufia, Egypt (2023-MH-1018). All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study procedures adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments (1975, 1983, 1989, and 1996). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects. Participants in this study did not receive any monetary or nonmonetary incentives for their participation. This study was conducted solely for research purposes, and participants volunteered for their time and information without compensation and were free to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

Statistical analyses

Basic statistics

The statistical analyses performed in this study were comprehensive and appropriate for evaluating the psychometric properties of the Arabic version of the SAQ. To summarize the sample characteristics and questionnaire results, descriptive statistics consisting of means, standard deviations, and frequencies were used. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare SAQ scores between males and females.

Reliability analyses

Internal consistency was evaluated using McDonald’s omega and Cronbach’s alpha, with values above 0.70 considered acceptable [30]. The item-total correlations were used to assess the relationship between each item and the overall scale.

Validity analyses

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using structural equation modeling was conducted to test the hypothesized three-factor structure of the Arabic SAQ [31]. The maximum likelihood estimation method was used for the CFA [31]. Model fit was assessed using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker‒Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) [31]. Additional fit indices were also reported, including the Bentler-Bonett Nonnormed Fit Index (NNFI), Bentler-Bonett Normed Fit Index (NFI), Parsimony Normed Fit Index (PNFI), Bollen’s Relative Fit Index (RFI), Bollen’s Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Relative Noncentrality Index (RNI), Hoelter’s critical N (α = 0.05), Hoelter’s critical N (α = 0.01), Goodness-of-fit Index (GFI), McDonald Fit Index (MFI), Expected Cross Validation Index (ECVI), log-likelihood, number of free parameters, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-Size Adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SSABIC), and log-likelihood ratio chi-square value (χ2) [32]. CFA model fit was evaluated using three categories: absolute, incremental, and parsimonious fit. Values above 0.90 for CFI, TLI, NNFI, NFI, PNFI, IFI, RNI, GFI and MFI indicate an acceptable fit, while values below 0.08 for RMSEA and SRMR suggest an acceptable fit [31]. Modification indices were used to refine the model and improve the factorial fitness of the obtained solutions.

Convergent validity was examined through Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients between the SAQ and other well-established measures of anxiety, depression, stress, and overall health status (PHQ-2, GAD-2, PSS-4, and VAS-A).

Item-response theory

To further assess the psychometric properties of the SAQ, we employed item response theory (IRT) using graded response model (GRM) analysis [33]. Specifically, a graded multidimensional IRT (MIRT) model was tested. This approach was chosen due to its ability to provide detailed information about item performance and to model the potentially complex structure of the questionnaire [34]. A three-dimensional graded response model was fitted to the data, as preliminary analyses suggested the presence of three distinct but related dimensions. The model estimates discrimination parameters (a1, a2, a3) for each dimension and four intercept parameters (d1, d2, d3, d4) corresponding to the thresholds between the response options for each item [34]. These parameters represent the discrimination power of each item for each latent trait dimension [34]. Higher values indicate that the item is better at distinguishing between individuals with different levels of the latent trait [34]. Discrimination parameters indicate how well an item differentiates between respondents with different levels of the latent trait, while difficulty parameters represent the level of the latent trait required to have a 50% probability of endorsing a particular response option or higher [34]. These parameters represent the difficulty level of each item. In dichotomous items, a single difficulty parameter is often used, but in polytomous items (e.g., graded responses), multiple thresholds correspond to different response categories [34]. Model fit was assessed using measures such as CFI, TLI and RMSEA. This IRT analysis allows for a nuanced understanding of how each SAQ item functions across the continuum of the measured constructs.

All analyses were conducted in R Statistical Software version 4.3.1 (Beagle Scouts) with a significance level of p < 0.05. The analyses utilized the ‘lavaan’ package [35] for CFA and the ‘mirt’ [34] package for MIRT modeling.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 208 participants (44% females, mean age 38 ± 12 years) were involved in this research. The mean SAQ score was 19.38 ± 12.63 (possible range 0–68) for the whole sample. Compared with males, females had a significantly greater total SAQ (22.44 ± 11.61 vs. 17.00 ± 12.92, p = 0.002; Cohen’s d = 0.44). There was no significant correlation between age and SAQ-17 score (r = 0.014, p = 0.840), indicating that surgical anxiety, as measured by the SAQ-17, was not associated with patient age in this sample.

Reliability analyses

McDonald’s omega was calculated to determine the reliability of the scale. The result was 0.90 (95% CI 0.91–0.87), which indicates an acceptable level of internal consistency. In addition, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated, and the outcome value was 0.89 (95% CI 0.87–0.91), further confirming the scale’s acceptable level of internal consistency. No improvement in McDonald’s omega or Cronbach’s alpha was detected by deletion of any item. The first subscale demonstrated good reliability, with the “Concerns about health” (six-item) subscale having a McDonald’s ω of 0.83 and a Cronbach’s α of 0.83. The second and third subscales demonstrated poor reliability, with the “concerns about recovery” (four-item) subscale having a McDonald’s ω of 0.61 and a Cronbach’s α of 0.60 and the “concerns about procedures” (four-item) subscale having a McDonald’s ω of 0.69 and a Cronbach’s α of 0.68. Validity analyses

The CFA of the SAQ was conducted using two different solutions, both of which employed the three-factor structure described in the original English SAQ-17. Both solutions were evaluated for goodness of fit using various indices, as summarized in Table 2. In Solution 1, which is based on the original factorial structure of the SAQ, Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded a Χ² of 1031 (df = 91) (p < 0.001). The KMO test produced a value of 0.90. The CFI was found to be 0.86. Additionally, the TLI and the NNFI both had values of 0.83. The NFI was 0.80, and the PNFI was 0.65. RFI and IFI both yielded values of 0.76 and 0.86, respectively. The RNI was also 0.86. The RMSEA was 0.09, with a 90% CI ranging from 0.08 to 0.11 (p < 0.001). The SRMR was 0.07. Discussion

This study aimed to thoroughly evaluate the psychometric properties of the SAQ questionnaire. The results provide strong evidence supporting the reliability and validity of the SAQ as a valuable tool for measuring preoperative anxiety across different domains. Our analyses showed that the SAQ has high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding the recommended 0.70 threshold. Examining the subscales reveals a more nuanced picture. The “Concerns about health” subscale demonstrated good reliability, with a McDonald’s omega of 0.83 and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83. These values suggest that the items within this subscale are coherent and consistently measure the specific domain of health-related concerns. However, the “Concerns about recovery” and “Concerns about procedures” subscales exhibited relatively poor reliability, with McDonald’s omega values of 0.61 and 0.69, respectively, and Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.60 and 0.68, respectively. These coefficients fall below the generally accepted threshold, indicating that the items within these subscales may not be as consistently interrelated or measuring their intended domains as precisely as desired. The lower reliability of the “Concerns about Recovery” and “Concerns about Procedures” subscales could be due to several factors, such as the smaller number of items within each subscale or potential ambiguity or heterogeneity in the item wording or interpretations.

In addition to demonstrating reliability through internal consistency, our findings also supported the construct validity of the SAQ. CFA verified that the hypothesized factor structure (construct validity) adequately fit the collected data, with fit indices (i.e., CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) meeting or surpassing established benchmarks. This suggests that the SAQ effectively measures a meaningful and well-defined construct of preoperative anxiety. Two solutions were tested, with Solution 2 emerging as the superior model, and thus should be adopted. The improved fit indices in Solution 2, such as a higher CFI and lower RMSEA, indicate that refining the factor structure by eliminating low-loading items enhances the questionnaire’s psychometric properties. This refined model, which still captures the primary dimensions of surgical anxiety—concerns about health, procedures, and recovery—offers a more parsimonious and reliable measure. These findings align with previous studies emphasizing the importance of precise and concise instruments in clinical settings [5]. The retention of key single-item subscales further ensures comprehensive coverage of specific anxiety dimensions relevant to surgical patients. Solution 2 demonstrated superior fit indices and should be adopted due to its better fit. While Solution 1 provided a borderline acceptable fit with a CFI of 0.86, which is close to the commonly accepted threshold of 0.90, Solution 2’s stronger performance supports its use. To ensure the Arabic SAQ remains comprehensive, we recommend retaining all original items until further validation is conducted. Future studies should employ larger, more diverse samples, covering a wider range of surgical procedures, to robustly assess and refine the factor structure. This approach ensures the tool’s comprehensiveness while laying the groundwork for ongoing psychometric evaluation and optimization. Additionally, future research should replicate these findings across diverse surgical populations and settings to confirm the generalizability and robustness of the refined SAQ model. Longitudinal studies could also explore the predictive validity of the SAQ for postoperative outcomes, further establishing its utility in preoperative assessments.

MIRT analysis of the SAQ revealed a complex three-dimensional structure, indicating that the instrument assesses multiple related constructs rather than a single unidimensional trait. This finding has significant implications for both researchers and clinicians using the SAQ. The varying levels of item difficulty across the scale suggest that the questionnaire can differentiate between respondents at different levels of the latent traits being measured. However, the complexity of the scale structure also highlights the need for caution when interpreting SAQ scores. Simple sum scores may not fully capture the nuanced information provided by the multidimensional structure, potentially leading to oversimplification of respondents’ profiles. Future research should explore the development of scoring methods that account for this multidimensionality, such as factor scores or MIRT scoring. Additionally, the items that did not show substantial discrimination on any dimension may need refinement or reconsideration in future iterations of the SAQ. These findings highlight the importance of considering the multidimensional nature of the SAQ in research designs, clinical assessments, and when making inferences about individuals or groups based on SAQ scores.

We conducted the IRT analysis using Solution 1 (original SAQ) instead of Solution 2 (modified SAQ for Arabic language) because Solution 1 represents the original structure of the SAQ. Since this is the first evaluation of the SAQ using MIRT, testing the original model is prudent to establish a foundational understanding of its psychometric properties. This approach facilitates more informed decision-making regarding future assessments and potential modifications to the SAQ. By focusing on the original structure, we ensure that our findings are grounded in the previously validated model, providing a clearer basis for evaluating its performance and implications going forward.

The psychometric validation of the SAQ has multiple important implications for both researchers and practitioners. Our research findings support the reliability and validity of the Arabic version of the SAQ. Overall, the psychometric analyses conducted on the Arabic version of the SAQ showed that it had a one-factor structure, good convergent validity, high internal consistency, and acceptable test-retest reliability. These results confirm that the Arabic translation of the questionnaire is a valid and trustworthy tool for evaluating surgical anxiety symptoms in Arabic-speaking people.

We initially hypothesized that the SAQ would demonstrate convergent validity with the GAD-2 (anxiety), PSS-4 (stress), and VAS-A (anxiety) measures while showing divergent validity from the PHQ-2 (depression). However, our results revealed a different pattern. Contrary to our expectations, we found that the SAQ score was significantly correlated not only with measures of anxiety and stress but also with depression. This unexpected finding may be attributed to the high comorbidity and overlap between depressive and anxiety symptoms, which is well documented in the psychological literature [36]. The observed pattern of correlations suggested that surgical anxiety, as measured by the SAQ, may be part of a broader construct of psychological distress that includes elements of anxiety, stress, and depression. This finding highlights the complex interplay between these psychological factors in the context of surgical experiences and underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions of mental health when assessing patients’ preoperative psychological states.

The original scale had a mean of 13.56 and a standard deviation of 10.93, with an overall reliability of 0.91 [5]. The mean for the Arabic participants was 19.38, with a standard deviation of 12.63 and an overall reliability of 0.89. The overall reliability of the Persian version of the scale was similar to that of the original scale at 0.91 [37]. The Turkish scale was tested three times; for the first time, a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 was measured in November 2021 [38]. Later, in September 2022, there was another study with a mean of 19.07, a standard deviation of 13.15, and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 [39]. Finally, in January 2023, there was a mean of 16.25, a standard deviation of 13.79, and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 [40]. It is obvious that the mean score for the SAQ was greater in our region than in the original study and the last Turkish study and similar to the second Turkish study by Topcu [39]. Several factors may contribute to increased preoperative anxiety levels. One of the causes that may increase anxiety in patients undergoing surgery is choosing the surgeon, especially in our region, where family and cultural background play a major role in manipulating the choice. As a result, his or her choice will be affected, which may make him or her worried about making the wrong choice. Additionally, the patient has to make a final choice, and knowing that it may not change may increase it further [41].

Worrying about complications, such as fear of surgeon mistakes, infection, bleeding or adverse reactions during surgery, may contribute to an increase in the level of anxiety [8]. Poor counseling is responsible for increasing the level of preoperative anxiety, clarifying the situation and reassuring the patient of concerns and worries, which may reduce preoperative anxiety and lead to better outcomes [8]. Some beliefs that are related to some religions, such as Islam, could be reasons for the increasing anxiety level in Islamic communities. For example, the belief in the existence of the “Last Day” that starts after death and that there is an eternal life either in the heaven or in the hell [42]. The mean of the Turkish version of the SAQ is approximately equal to that of the Arabic version [39].

The psychometric strengths of the Arabic SAQ highlighted in this study have important clinical implications. By providing a rigorously validated Arabic instrument, healthcare providers can reliably identify patients experiencing elevated preoperative anxiety levels and initiate appropriate interventions or counseling. Routine screening with the Arabic SAQ could facilitate early detection of surgical fears and information needs, allowing preventive measures to be implemented before anxiety becomes severe. This proactive approach aligns with enhanced recovery principles and may improve patient experiences, reduce cancellations, and optimize surgical outcomes.

Furthermore, the availability of a linguistically and culturally adapted SAQ enables meaningful cross-cultural research collaboration and data pooling from Arabic-speaking populations. Ultimately, such cross-cultural insights could inform tailored preoperative interventions and shared decision-making processes that account for cultural nuances in addressing surgical anxiety.

Strengths and limitations

The Arabic translation and validation of the SAQ have several notable strengths. First, a rigorous translation process was implemented following best practices to ensure the accuracy and cultural appropriateness of the questionnaire. The developed Arabic version was distributed across three different countries with varying dialects, helping to ensure that it accurately reflected the original English version. Second, comprehensive psychometric evaluations were conducted, including assessments of internal consistency, convergent validity, test-retest reliability, and confirmatory factor analysis. These thorough evaluations provided robust evidence for the validity and reliability of the Arabic version.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged regarding Arabic SAQ translation and validation. First, convenience sampling using social media and instant messaging introduced potential selection bias and limited generalizability. Second, the use of self-report measures may be prone to social desirability and response biases. Third, the lack of clinical diagnosis of preoperative anxiety symptoms restricts generalizability to clinical populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the modified Arabic version of the SAQ (offered by Solution 2) has acceptable validity and reliability for assessing preoperative anxiety symptoms in Arabic-speaking individuals. Our findings indicate good internal consistency, very good test-retest reliability, and good convergent validity, although this conclusion is restricted due to the lack of other validated measures of preoperative anxiety symptoms in the Arabic language. Further research is needed to explore the cultural variations in preoperative anxiety and behavior in Arabic-speaking populations. Understanding these cultural factors will result in better detection and management of preoperative anxiety in the Arabic-speaking world. Fundamentally, the SAQ is a practical tool for evaluating preoperative anxiety symptoms in Arabic-speaking individuals, serving valuable purposes in both research and clinical settings.
