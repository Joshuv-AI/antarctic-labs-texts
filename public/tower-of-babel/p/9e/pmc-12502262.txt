FRIGIDA-like gene family in rapeseed: genome-wide analysis and expression analysis to exogenous phytohormones
Authors: Sana Basharat, Wajid Saeed, Pingwu Liu, Muhammad Waseem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Frigida (FRI) plays diverse roles in regulating plant flowering and drought tolerance. The biological importance of FRIGIDA-like proteins (FRL) has been well established in Arabidopsis and soybean; however, systemic analysis of the FRL gene in rapeseed in response to phytohormones is still in its infancy. The present study predicted 28 FRL proteins in rapeseed that were unevenly distributed across the entire chromosome set of rapeseeds. Phylogenetically, all BnaFRL proteins were clustered into four distinct clades along with Arabidopsis, rice, and soybean. Our study indicated that rapeseed BnaFRL arose from segmental duplication alongside single tandem duplication events. Moreover, gene structure analysis, such as Frigida domain presence, intron-exon distribution, and conserved domain analysis, validated the phylogenetic conservation of all the BnaFRL genes. Additionally, promoter analysis revealed several cis-regulatory elements related to hormones, suggesting their significant role in rapeseed hormone response mechanisms. Spatial expression across different tissues exhibited variable expression, whereas subcellular localization and temporal expression of selected BnaFRLs to exogenous hormone application (IAA, ABA, and GA) further supported their role in hormone responsiveness in rapeseed. In summary, our comprehensive analysis offers valuable insights into the evolution and potential functions of BnaFRL genes, emphasizing their significance in phytohormone responses, and establishing a foundation for further research on the BnaFRL family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07336-6.

Introduction

Plants are sessile organisms that have evolved many complex regulatory networks that play critical roles in diverse biological processes, including growth, development, and responses to environmental constraints [1]. One such regulatory network layer consists of phytohormones. Phytohormones are small chemical molecules that are pivotal for plant development and growth and are critical for tolerance to diverse environmental conditions. Phytohormones are categorized into stress response hormones (ethylene, jasmonic acid, abscisic acid (ABA), salicylic acid (SA)) and growth-promoting hormones (cytokinins (CK), strigolactones (SL), gibberellins (GA), and brassinosteroids (BR)) [2]. Frigida-like protein (FRL) is a key regulator of flowering in plants and acts as an activator of the floral repressor FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) [3]. FRL proteins have been identified in Arabidopsis [4], Medicago sativa [5], Oryza sativa [6] and soybean [7]. In plants, FRL genes regulate flowering and other biological processes, including responses to drought stress [8]. For instance, AtFRL regulates flowering in Arabidopsis [9]. Li et al. [10] PmFRL is potentially associated with ABA and GA signaling pathways, playing a role in regulating dormancy and flowering in Prunus mume. Similarly, several GmFRL genes may be linked to IAA, GA, and ABA signal regulators, thereby implying their biological functions during photoperiodic flowering [7]. These studies reveal the potentially important functions of FRL family genes with hormone cross-talk in plant responses to regulate flowering.

Brassica napus (rapeseed) is a versatile crop used as a source of edible vegetable oil, condiments, and fodder because of its high protein content. Moreover, B. napus (AACC), an allopolyploid, and the release of the entire B. napus genome sequence offers an opportunity for comparative genomic research. The FRL gene family is a key regulatory factor controlling flowering time in plants. It is essential to identify and analyze FRLs on a genomic scale to uncover their molecular functions, which may provide deeper insights into plant development. Currently, there is no systematic research on the BnaFRL gene family in rapeseed. Therefore, the functional roles of its members in the rapeseed genome require further investigation. In this study, we analyzed BnaFRL genes in rapeseed, focusing on genome-scale identification, chromosomal location, and phylogeny. Gene structure features such as intron-exon distribution, conserved motif analysis, cis-acting regulatory elements in promoters, and gene duplication were examined. Additionally, the expression profile reveals the role of BnaFRL genes in different developmental tissues and responses to phytohormones, such as IAA, ABA, and GA. Our findings offer new perspectives on the evolutionary and functional diversification of BnaFRLs with potential applications in rapeseed genetic improvement.

Materials and methods

Mining of rapeseed FRIGIDA proteins

Arabidopsis FRIGIDA protein was retrieved from the TAIR genome (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The FRIGIDA domain pattern (PF07899) was obtained from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org/). The hmmsearch (E-value: 1 × 10−5) algorithm was used to identify putative FRIGIDA genes in the B. napus proteome and only non-redundant FRIGIDA peptides were retained. The NCBI conserved domain database (NCBI CDD, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) and Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool (SMART) (https://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/smart/batch.pl) were used to further validate the presence of the FRIGIDA domain in each deduced sequence. The FRIGIDA proteins were named BnaFRL (further used in subsequence analysis) and numbered in ascending order on the chromosomes. Moreover, to further assess the physiochemical features of FRIGIDA proteins, peptide length (aa, amino acid), grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY), molecular weight (MW, Kda), and isoelectric point (pI) of each BnaFRL protein were calculated using the Sequence Manipulation Suite (https://www.bioinformatics.org/sms2/). Lastly, in-silico subcellular localization of each BnaFRL protein was predicted in WoLF–PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) and further validated in BUSCA (https://busca.biocomp.unibo.it/).

Phylogeny of rapeseed BnaFRL genes

To elucidate the phylogenetic relationship and potential function of the B. napus FRL protein, peptide sequences of BnaFRL from Arabidopsis, soybean, and rice were retrieved from the Phytozome database (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/pz/portal.html). An unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using the IQ-TREE program (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) using the maximum likelihood (ML) algorithm with a Poisson model and bootstrap set at 1000 replicates. The FRL genes were clustered according to Yu et al. [7] with slight modifications as required.

Structural characterization of BnaFRL

The position of the BnaFRL domain in the peptide sequences was predicted using the NCBI CDD database. Rapeseed genome annotation was used to retrieve the intron-exon distribution of all the B. napus FRL genes. To explore conserved motif sequences in the BnaFRL protein, the MEME suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used as follows: (i) maximum number of motifs, 10; (ii) number of repetitions, any; and (iii) optimum motif width set to ≥ 10 and ≤ 50. The output from the MEME tools was submitted to the gene structure view, tbtools, for visualization [11]. The PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was used to systematically predict cis-acting regulatory elements (CAREs) in the promoter sequences of all the BnaFRL genes. The TBtools software was used to visualize the predicted CAREs in the BnaFRLs. Lastly, NetPhos 3.1 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?NetPhos-3.1) waa s used toof predict phosphorylation sites with potential more than 0.09.

FRIGIDA gene chromosomal location and duplication analysis in rapeseed genome

The chromosome size and chromosomal location of each BnaFRL gene were obtained from rapeseed genome annotation, and the precise chromosomal location of each BnaFRL gene was determined using the Tbtools program. Gene duplication events, such as segmental and tandem duplications, were analyzed using the MCScanX program (http://chibba.pgml.uga.edu/mcscan2/). The syntenic map with chromosome location and duplications was visualized by the CIRCOS plot in Tbtools. The Ks_Ka calculator was (https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2/) used to calculate Ka/Ks ratios of each BnaFRL paralogous gene pair. The divergence time (T, MYA; millions of years ago) was calculated as follows: T = Ks/2R (where R = 1.5 × 10 − 8 and is synonymous with the number of substitutions per site per year) [12].

BnFRL transcriptional regulation analysis

The transcriptional regulatory network in the promoter region of BnaFRL (2000 bp) was predicted using the Plant Transcriptional Regulatory Map (PTRM) (https://plantregmap.gao-lab.org/). The BnFRL gene (P ≤ 0.05) was selected for visualization using Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/).

Plant materials

The plant material used in this study was B. napus 383-5, which is an inbred line developed by our group at the School of Breeding and Multiplication (Sanya Institute of Breeding and Multiplication), School of Tropical Agriculture and Forestry, Hainan University, Sanya, China, and preserved in our laboratory. The seeds were surface-sterilized, grown in a growth room under controlled conditions, and maintained at 24 °C under a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod. To investigate the expression of BnaFRL genes, different rapeseed tissues, such as stems, roots, leaves, flowers, seeds, and siliques were collected. For hormone stress experiment, one month old rapeseed plants were treated with 100 µM ABA, 100 µM GA, and 100 µM IAA, as described by Raza et al. [13]. The samples were collected at 0 h (CK; control), 1 h, 3 h, and 6 h after the treatment. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. All the samples were collected from five biological replicates, each with three technical replicates.

Transient expression analysis of BnaFRL in tobacco leaves

The full-length sequences of BnaFRL1, BnaFRL14, BnaFRL15, and BnaFRL21 without the stop codon were amplified and cloned into the pGreen0029 vector. The recombinant plasmids containing the BnaFRL1::GFP, BnaFRL14::GFP, BnaFRL15::GFP, and BnaFRL21::GFP fusion genes. A plasmid containing GFP alone was used as a control. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into tobacco leaf epidermal cells using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The fluorescence in the tobacco leaves was observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV10I, Leica, Germany), and photographs were taken.

Nucleic acid extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from all collected samples using TRIZOL reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was qualified using a Nanodrop (Thermo USA), and quality was assessed by 2% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis. To verify the expression of BnaFRL genes in B. napus, cDNA was synthesized using the Prime Script ™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, JAPAN). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq-II (TliRNaseH Plus) with the supplied protocol, and the reaction was run on a CFX96 Touch ™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). BnACTIN7 was used as an internal reference gene. The relative expression of each BnaFRL was assessed using the 2−ΔΔCt method [14]. The reactions were performed with three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Results

In-silico identification of BnaFRL genes in rapeseed

To identify the BnaFRL gene family in rapeseed, the hmm profile (PF07899) of the FRIGIDA family was used to study the rapeseed proteome (ZS11). Secondly, Arabidopsis FRL protein sequences were BLASTP against whole proteome of rapeseed. The deduced peptide sequences were deduplicated, and the conserved FRIGIDA domain was validated using NCBI CDD. A total of 28 FRL genes were obtained and renamed in ascending order of position on chromosomes BnaFRL1 to BnaFRL28. Detailed characteristic features of BnaFRL proteins are presented in Table 1, including gene name, peptide length, MW, GRAVY, pI, and in silico subcellular localization prediction. Briefly, the peptide length and molecular weight of BnaFRL varies considerable among all the BnaFRLs, ranging from 434 amino acids/48.08 Kda to 1217 amino acids/13.80 Kda, with BnaFRL9 to BnaFRL22 representing the extremes. The predicted pI ranges from 9.25 (BnaFRL13) to 5.12 (BnaFRL26), respectively. Overall, the pI of all the BnaFRL genes showed considerable variation, and 64% of the BnaFRL proteins had a pI of less than 7. Therefore, most BnaFRLs are rich in acidic amino acids. The GRAVY values of all BnaFRLs reflected the hydrophilic nature of all the proteins, with values less than 0. The in-silico subcellular localization analysis revealed varying degrees of compartmentation in the chloroplast, cytoplasm, nucleus, and plasma membrane. Most BnaFRL proteins are associated with the nucleus and cytoplasm, while others are located in the chloroplast and plasma membrane (Table 1).

Phylogeny of BnaFRLs

To elucidate the evolutionary relationship and potential biological functions of all FRL genes, an unrooted maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed with an IQ-Tree using FRL proteins from rapeseed, Arabidopsis, rice, and soybean. All the FRL proteins were clustered into four phylogenetic clusters (I ~ IV) based on Yu et al. [7] with rapeseed BnaFRL proteins scattered in each group. For instance, group IV had the highest number of FRLs in cluster IV, followed by cluster II. In general, members within the same cluster may have similar functions. In cluster I, rapeseed had BnaFRL1, BnaFRL14, and BnaFRL18 clusters, with four GmFRL, OsFRL, and a single AtFRL. Group II contained seven BnaFRL proteins, along with a single AtFRL, three OsFRL, and five GmFRLs. BnaFRL12 and BnaFRL17 clustered in group III with two Arabidopsis AtFRLs, three GmFRLs, and three OsFRLs. Gene structure analysis

To investigate the functional characteristics of BnaFRLs, protein domain, intron-exon distribution, and organization of conserved motifs by the MEME suite were analyzed together with phylogeny (Fig. 2). All BnaFRL proteins contained a single characteristic FRL domain, with the exception of a few in cluster IVb that had two FRL domains, BnaFRL5, BnaFRL6, BnaFRL11, BnaFRL22, BnaFRL23, and BnaFRL25. The exon-intron organization was conserved within the same phylogenetic clades, with exceptions. For example, BnaFRL genes in cluster I have three introns, with the exception of BnaFRL18, which has four introns. All the BnaFRLs in cluster II had three introns, whereas cluster II had only a single intron. However, in cluster IVa, three introns were present in all BnaFRLs, whereas IVb had to 3–6 introns. For example, BnaFRL5 and BnaFRL23 have six introns, and BnaFRL27, BnaFRL11, and BnaFRL20 have three introns. BnaFRL19, BnaFRL4, BnaFRL22, BnaFRL6, BnaFRL26, BnaFRL10, and BnaFRL25 had four introns. To further investigate the structural diversity of BnaFRL, ten conserved motifs were predicted. As shown in Fig. 2, all the BnaFRL peptides exhibited motif-3 and motif-7. CAREs in the promoter and phosphorylation site analysis in BnaFRLs

It is well established that phosphorylation alterations play a critical role in subcellular localization, stability, and interactions with other compatible proteins. To estimate the possibility of BnaFRL protein phosphorylation, we analyzed serine, tyrosine, and threonine peptide residues. The number of phosphorylation sites in different BnaFRL proteins varied widely from 717 (BnaFRL2) to 2191 (BnaFRL22), with an average of 1238.57 sites per protein (Fig. 3a, Table S2). These findings lay the foundation for the potential post-translational modifications of BnaFRLs in rapeseed, providing valuable insights for future research aimed at elucidating the functional mechanisms underlying these modifications and their impact on BnaFRL activity.

To understand the regulation and function of BnaFRL genes in rapeseed, promoter sequences were analyzed. The identified CAREs are involved in a variety of biological processes that can be broadly grouped into hormone-related, growth- and development-related, and stress response-related. Over 405 CAREs belonging to 19 types were identified, with an average of approximately 20 CAREs in the promoter of each BnaFRL gene. A large number of CAREs are hormone-and growth regulator-related CAREs, including ABA responsiveness (ABRE, ACGTG), auxin responsiveness (AuxRR-core, GGTCCAT; TGA-element, AACGAC), gibberellin-responsive element (GARE-motif, TCTGTTG), zein metabolism regulation (O2-site, GATGATGTGG), gibberellin-responsiveness (TATC-box, TATCCCA), salicylic acid responsiveness (TCA-element, CCATCTTTTT), and MeJA-responsiveness (CGTCA-motif, CGTCA). Moreover, BnaFRL gene promoters contained different CAREs related to abiotic stress responses, such as the MYB binding site involved in drought inducibility (MBS, CAACTG), low-temperature responsiveness (LTR, CCGAAA), and wound-responsive element (WUN-motif, AAATTTCCT). Notably, the BnaFRL promoter contains CAREs related to plant growth and development. Among these CAREs, light responsiveness (ACE, CTAACGTATT; AE-box, AGAAACAA; Box 4, ATTAAT; G-box, CACGTC), circadian control (circadian, CAAAGATATC), meristem development (CAT-box, GCCACT), and endosperm development (GCN4_motif, TGAGTCA) (Fig. 3b, Table S3). Gene duplication analysis in BnaFRL in rapeseed whole genome

All BnaFRL genes were uniformly distributed on all rapeseed chromosomes. Sub-genome A contained 13 BnaFRL genes on all chromosomes, whereas sub-genome C contained 15 BnaFRL genes. Notably, no genes were present on chromosomes A04, A07, and A08 from the sub-genome, and chromosomes of sub-genome C, such as C04, C06, and C08, had no genes. The highest number of genes were present in A09 and C09. Detailed information on the BnaFRL genes is presented in Table 1; Fig. 4a.

Collinear pair genes in rapeseed were analyzed to explore the syntenic relationships among BnaFRLs. This study identified 29 pairs of BnaFRL genes, including 25 BnaFRL genes, including 28 segmental duplications and a single pair of tandem duplications (BnaFRL25/BnaFRL26) (Fig. 4a). Moreover, the selection pressure on duplicated BnaFRL genes was assessed by calculating the ratios of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous substitutions (Ks) for paralogous gene pairs. The Ka/Ks ratios of gene pairs ranged from 0.05 to 0.69 for duplication pairs (Table S4). Additionally, the Ka/Ks ratios for all paralogous genes were less than 1, indicating purifying selection acting on the BnaFRL genes.

To elucidate the evolution of the BnaFRL genes, a comparative interspecific synteny was generated. Inter-specific collinearity analysis revealed a strong orthologous relationship among the FRL members, and 14, 30, and 28 collinear pairs were identified between Arabidopsis, B. rapa, and B. oleracea, respectively (Fig. 4b, Table S5). Transcription factor regulatory network analysis of BnaFRL genes

Exploration of the potential upstream TF regulatory network of all BnaFRl genes, among 28 BnaFRL genes, 283 were identified, belonging to different TF families such as TCP, LBD, Dof, NAC, MYB, bZIP, ERF, and WRKY (Fig. 5 and Table S6). The predicted TF families showed that WRKY (37 members) was highly enriched, followed by MYB (32 members), NAC (20 members), and ERF (19 members). The least enriched members were also predicted to have only a few members, including TCP (nine members), LBD (five members), bHLH (six members), NYF_B (one member), and MADS (three members). Among all BnaFRL genes, BnaFRl21 was the most targeted by 66 TFs followed by BnaFRL2 (60 TFs), BnaFRL22 and BnaFRL5 (55 TFs), BnaFRL25 (41 TFs), and BnaFRL1 (35 TFs). However, BnaFRL26 was targeted the least by only three TFs.

The BnaFRL genes were targeted by different types of TFs; for instance, BnaFRL1 was enriched in BnaFRL2 and WRKY (26 members), DoF (09 members), MYB (02 members), bHLH (06 members), and MADS (1 member), and BnaFRL21 was enriched in bZIP (11 members), DoF (7 members), WRKY (30 members), and MADS (1 member). Moreover, TFs related to plant development, growth, and responses to environmental stress were also predicted in BnaFRL genes, such as TCP, bHLH, WRKY, bZIP, MADS, and MYB.

Spatial expression profiling of BnaFRL genes

To explore the potential function of BnaFRL in rapeseed, spatial expression patterns were analyzed across different tissues, such as roots, stems, leaves, flowers, siliques, and seeds, using RT-qPCR. The results showed diverse expression levels across all tissues. For instance, BnaFRL01, BnaFRL2, BnaFRL9, BnaFRL7, BnaFRL8, BnaFRL9, BnaFRL14, and BnaFRL22 were expressed at high levels, followed by BnaFRL3 and BnaFRL16 in flowers; BnaFRL5, BnaFRL12, BnaFRL23, and BnaFRL26 in seeds; and BnaFRL25 had the highest expression in siliques (Fig. 6). Furthermore, BnaFRL13, BnaFRL14, BnaFRL18, and BnaFRL24 showed the highest expression in the stem but decreased expression in flowers, siliques, and seeds, whereas BnaFRL20 had high comparative expression compared with other tissues (Fig. 6). However, expression of BnaFRL11, BnaFRL19, and BnaFRL27 was not detected in any tissue. The differential expression of the BnaFRL genes exhibited diverse roles in the growth and development of rapeseed.

Discussion

FRIGIDA proteins play a pivotal role in plant growth, development, and response to abiotic stresses such as drought [8]. To date FRL protein has been identified in different plant species including Arabidopsis (8 members), soybean (16 members), rice (13 members), maize (33 members), P. mume (12 members), and tomato (14 members) [7], present study identified 28 BnFRL genes in while genome of rapeseed (Table 1). A comparative analysis of FRL genes from different plants indicated a significantly greater number of FRL genes than in Arabidopsis, soybean, rice, tomato, and P. mume. This indicated that the most pronounced expansion was observed in the lineages of maize, soybean, and rapeseed, where the number of FRLs was three or four times that of Arabidopsis.

To predict the possible function of FRL proteins in rapeseed, evolutionary and phylogenetic analyses of FRL proteins in rice, Arabidopsis, and soybean showed that this gene family is clustered into four phylogenetic clades. Generally, members of the same subgroup may have similar functions, which aids in our understanding of the potential biological functions of BnaFRLs. In subgroup III, two members of the BnaFRL family (BnaFRL12 and BnaFRL17) clustered with three members of the GmFRL family (GmFRL1/2/3) and two members of the AtFRL family (At5g16320 (AtFRL1) and At1g31814 (AtFRL2)). AtFRL1 and AtFRL2 play crucial roles in regulating FLC expression in Arabidopsis, and their functions depend on genes that affect the chromatin structure of the FLC locus [15]. In subgroup II, seven members of the BnaFRL family clustered with five members of the GmFRL and single AtFRL family, AtFRL3, which can regulate the photoperiod pathway in Arabidopsis [16]. In a previous study, BrFRIb functioned as an activator of BrFLC in B. rapa [17].

Gene duplication, together with polyploidization, plays a critical role in the expansion and diversification of biological functions and is considered the major driving force for the evolution of novelties [18, 19]. For instance, single segmental and tandem duplications (PmFRL02/PmFRL03, PmFRL03/PmFRL04, and PmFRL04/PmFRL05) have been identified [10]. On contrary, in soybean single tandem duplication pair GmFRL04/GmFRL05 and six segmental duplication gene pairs GmFRL01/02/03, GmFRL07/08, GmFRL04/06, GmFRL09/10, GmFRL11/12, GmFRL13/14, and GmFRL15/16 [7]. Rapeseed has a highly repetitive, allotetraploid genome, with approximately 37.5% of its sequence composed of repetitive elements and many genes present in two to eight duplicated copies”—a result of multiple whole-genome duplication and hybridization events [20]. These results indicated that the gene pairs BnaFRL25 and BnaFRL26 expanded through tandem duplication. In contrast, 28 gene pairs underwent expansion through segmental duplications. These findings suggest that segmental duplication is the primary driving force in conjunction with tandem duplication events among the BnaFRL members, thereby facilitating the expansion of the BnaFRL gene family.

The Ka/Ks ratio is commonly used to assess the evolutionary dynamics of gene duplication following divergence from ancestral genes [21]. This ratio reveals the type of selection pressure on amino acid substitutions: a Ka/Ks value less than 1 indicates purifying (negative) selection, a value of approximately 1 suggests neutral evolution, and a value greater than 1 reflects positive selection. In the case of tandemly duplicated GmFRL genes, the Ka/Ks values were consistently below 1, indicating that these genes have experienced strong purifying selection. Consistent with this study, the present study indicated that the Ka/Ks ratio is less than 1, implying that rapeseed BnaFRL genes undergo purifying selection. These results imply a slow rate of evolution and highlight the functional conservation within the FRL gene family.

The gene structure plays an essential role in the evolution of gene families. The gene structure analysis in terms of exon-intron distribution and conserved motif differences was notably consistent among all BnaFRL genes, similar to the same phylogenetic clades with the exception that variations existed among different phylogenetic clades. CAREs play a critical role in the transcriptional regulation of diverse biological processes, from development to responses to abiotic stresses. Consistent with previous studies, for instance, Li et al. [10] and Yu et al. [7] FRL genes also contain gibberellin-responsive and ABA-responsive elements and their expression is regulated to a certain extent by GA and ABA. BnaFRL genes have many CAREs involved in hormone signaling, including ABA, IAA, and GA. Expression profiling under exogenous phytohormone application showed significant expression and promoter analysis of BnaFRL, indicating that the promoter sequences of BnaFRL2, BnaFRL7, BnaFRL15, BnaFRL18, BnaFRL21, and BnaFRL24 contained CAREs related GA, IAA, and GA. Functional analysis of the BnaFRL family is still in its infancy, and these results need to be verified by gene cloning and expression analyses in the future.

Conclusion

The present study provides an in-depth systematic analysis of the BnaFRL gene family in rapeseed using various bioinformatic approaches. The present study identified 28 BnaFRL genes that were unevenly distributed across all chromosomes. Whole-genome duplication analysis revealed 29 pairs of segmental duplications with single tandem duplications, revealing that segmental duplication played a major role in the expansion and evolution of BnaFRL genes. All identified BnaFRL genes clustered into four phylogenetic clades, along with Arabidopsis, rice, and maize FRL orthologs. Exon-intron distribution and conserved motif sequence analysis revealed phylogenetic conservation within the group, with a few exceptions. Tissue-specific expression patterns showed that a few BnaFRL genes showed higher expression in specific plant parts, such as flowers, leaves, and stems. However, RT-qPCR analysis of BnaFRL genes after exogenous application of IAA, ABA, and GA indicated their significance in the application of hormones. The subcellular localization of the selected BnaFRLs validated their location in the nucleus. Briefly, the present genome0scale study provides a foundation for further functional characterization of BnaFRL genes in rapeseed growth, development, and response to phytohormones.
