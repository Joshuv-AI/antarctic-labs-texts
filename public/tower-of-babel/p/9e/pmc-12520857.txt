Genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors influencing dog lifespan: a systematic review
Authors: Janice O'Brien, Aliya McCullough, Courtney Sexton, Audrey Ruple
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ImportanceUnderstanding animal life histories is important for delivering timely and appropriate veterinary care and ensuring high quality of life standards throughout different life stages of an individual.ObjectiveTo determine the factors affecting companion dog lifespan and the certainty with which those factors are known.Evidence ReviewFactors influencing dog lifespan were searched for in three large databases according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. The resulting papers were reviewed according to Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation criteria for epidemiological evidence.FindingsAdult size of dog is inversely correlated with lifespan. Neutering dogs has a larger beneficial effect on lifespan in female dogs than male dogs, but the reasons for this discrepancy between sexes are still unclear. Diet restriction increases lifespan, and overweight dogs have shorter lifespans than those of their normal body condition counterparts.Conclusions and RelevanceAmong well-studied and proven factors, like body size, mechanism research is still necessary to understand the underlying reasons for these correlations. While neutering is one of the most well-studied modifiable factors on longevity, more research should be added to the field of work. Other modifiable factors such as household and outdoor environmental exposures and dietary exposures require much more research before any definitive conclusions can be drawn about their effect on longevity in pet dogs. Researchers and practitioners can use this information in their daily work.

INTRODUCTION

As the poet Mary Oliver once said, “And it is exceedingly short, his galloping life. Dogs die so soon… We would do anything to keep them with us, and to keep them young. The one gift we cannot give.” Unfortunately, companion dogs’ lives are shorter than those of humans by a factor of between six and twelve [1]. There is a wide difference in longevity among individual dogs, and determining which factors may contribute to lengthening or shortening their lives is of utmost interest to dog owners, veterinary professionals, researchers, and pet industries, alike. Indeed, a US veterinarian made headlines when he claimed that dogs of today are actually living shorter lives than decades previous [2]. However, a review of scientific studies in multiple countries has proven that if anything, dogs' lives are getting longer (although the tools used to measure longevity in dogs are imperfect) [3].

Perhaps motivated by different agendas, both academia and industry are compelled to determine factors which are associated with longevity in companion dogs—whether they are factors which are non-modifiable (i.e., those like breed or sex which are fixed at the time of birth) or modifiable (i.e., can change depending on owner decisions, veterinary recommendations, etc.). There is much speculation about both of these types of factors. Perspective owners face conflicting recommendations about whether to acquire a pet from a breeder, or from a shelter/rescue and what that means for the health of their potential pet. Some speculate that single-breed dogs are inbred, and therefore are less healthy than their mixed-breed counterparts—so owners desiring for a long lived pet dog should opt for mixed-breed dogs from shelters or rescue organizations. Others assert that responsible breeders work hard to ensure the genetic health of their pedigrees are maintained through rigorous testing, and therefore a longer lived dog should come from a breeder. A white paper produced by a US-based pet insurer found a reduced risk of cancer in labradoodle and goldendoodle crosses compared to their parent breeds [4], which may support the theory that crosses and mixed-breeds are healthier, but longevity was not studied in this analysis.

A specific consideration in discussions of breed is distinguishing between breed and phylogeny when conducting analyses and attempting to control for genetic factors. Some breeds which appear similar or which are grouped into the same breed category by kennel club organizations may not be genetically similar at all. A pug, for example, is more genetically similar to a mastiff than it is to a pomeranian, even though both pugs and pomeranians are included in the toy category by the American Kennel Club (AKC) [5]. In another example, while basset hounds and Pembroke Welsh corgis both have the FGF4 mutation which causes their chondrodystrophic skeletal changes, basset hounds descend from scent hound lineages and corgis descend from herding lineages according to both the AKC and United Kennel Club [56]. Aside from the shared specific mutation which gives both breeds their squat stature, they are genetically relatively dissimilar among dog breeds. Thus, in large study analyses which consider the effect of breeds on health and longevity, phylogeny would be the preferred method to grouping breeds. Furthermore, the variation in body size in most dogs is attributable to mutations in only a handful of genes: IGSF1, IGF1R, ASCL4, IGF1, LCORL, HMGA2, GHR, CD36, SMAD2, IGF2BP2, and ESR1 [7].

Among modifiable risk factors, whether to neuter animals is frequently discussed in relation to cancer, obesity, joint health, behavior, and other health outcomes such as morbidity related to trauma. While neutering appears to decrease the risk of certain cancers (like prostate cancer in males or mammary tumors in females), it seems to increase the risk of others (like lymphoma, mast cell tumors, hemangiosarcoma, and osteosarcoma) [8]. However, aging is the primary risk factor for cancer, and dogs that live longer are more likely to develop cancer [9]. Therefore, understanding the effect of neutering on longevity is an important consideration in the discussion of neutering and its health effects.

Another modifiable risk factor which is common to all dogs is diet choice, and in this area, separating marketing claims from evidence-based statements can be difficult. Examining specifically what evidence-based research has found about the effect of diet or nutrition on dog longevity is necessary to determine what clinicians can reasonably state in what can be emotionally charged discussions with clients about the best diet choice for their pet dog.

Pharmaceutical companies and academics are conducting randomized clinical trials to test certain medications to see if they will lengthen the life of older dogs [1011]. These studies are underway but do not yet have any published results regarding the efficacy of their medications on the lifespan of companion dogs.

Given that this is such an important question at this moment in time, conducting a review of the scientific literature regarding what factors are known to shorten or extend companion dogs’ lives is pertinent. The goal of this review is to determine what are the genetic factors, environmental exposures, lifestyle and diet choices that are known to impact longevity in companion dogs, and the strength of the scientific evidence that supports each of these risk factors.

METHODS

A search of three major large databases (Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed) of scientific manuscripts was conducted in May 2025. Each of the databases was queried using the following search terms: (dog* OR "companion animal*" OR "domestic dog*") AND (lifespan OR longevity OR aging OR "life span" OR "life expectancy" OR mortality OR survival) AND (genetic* OR genomic* OR heredity OR "breed difference*" OR "single nucleotide polymorphism*" OR SNP*) AND (environment* OR "environmental exposure*" OR lifestyle OR diet OR nutrition OR "living condition*" OR "household factor*" OR "air pollution" OR "built environment" OR "socioeconomic status" OR "owner behavior" OR neuter OR spay OR castrated).

The manuscripts fitting the results of the search terms were downloaded as BibTeX documents and the R package “revtools” [12] was used to de-duplicate references. This list of papers was then reviewed individually and screened for applicability within the review study. Papers were excluded if they were a) not about domestic dogs, b) were review articles about the topics, and/or c) did not measure longevity (Fig. 1). Survival analyses, which were conducted on animals with particular diagnosed conditions, were not counted as longevity studies for this review and were therefore excluded. Studies that were conducted using domestic dogs housed in kennels or other research environments (as opposed to pets in homes) were included because even though these dogs are not companion dogs, they are the same species, and the well-controlled study designs that are possible from research populations can still inform knowledge about companion dogs. Studies which examined factors that differ based on age (e.g., metabolomics of young dog muscle and old dog muscle) were not included unless they were explicitly doing so to determine factors associated with longevity.

PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses.

The studies that met the inclusion criteria were then reviewed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria for assessing certainty of evidence in epidemiological studies [13]. Per the criteria, observational studies of any kind started with a score of “Low” and randomized controlled trials of any kind started with a score of “High.” Each study was then evaluated across the following domains: Risk of Bias, Inconsistency, Indirectness, Imprecision, and Publication Bias. For each domain, each study could receive a score of: Downgrade, Upgrade, or No change based on GRADE criteria recommendations. After assessing and downgrading or upgrading across all the domains, each study could receive an Overall Certainty of Evidence of Very Low, Low, Moderate, High.

Large datasets such as Vetcompass, Veterinary Medical Database (VMDB), or insurance datasets originating in countries from which over 50% of the pet population is insured received mild risk of bias ratings and no change score. When study designs or statistical analyses are carefully considered to account for confounding factors, the study may receive an upgrade recommendation. Necropsy datasets received high risk of bias scores and recommendations to downgrade because necropsy data are overly-representative of unexpected and early deaths in animals. Sample sizes fell into the following categories: n < 100 - small, 100 < n < 1,000 - moderate, 1,000 < n < 10,000 - large, n > 10,000 - very large. Small sample sizes received a downgrade recommendation, moderate received no change, and large and very large sample sizes received upgrade recommendations. Academic studies received no change recommendations. If research was industry-funded or provided a dataset to an academic group, no change was recommended. If research was entirely conducted by industry to include analysis and writing of the publication, a downgrade was recommended as is consistent with GRADE criteria recommendations.

If a research study examined multiple exposures (e.g., both sex and neuter status were analyzed independently), each exposure was considered within the context of the paper. For example, many papers examined both sex status and breed effect on longevity—where sex typically had a much larger number of individuals considered compared to the total number of individual dogs belonging to a single breed. Individual breed scores would likely receive a lower overall quality of evidence based on the sample size compared to the sex calculations even if determined from the same population of dogs.

The studies were read and are summarized in reverse alphabetical order in GRADE criteria, then sorted according to GRADE criteria (highest to lowest) within factor categories. Please note that there is variation across paper titles in the rules used for capitalization of breeds and some manuscripts employ terminology (e.g., “mongrel,” “mutt,” etc.) that has been simplified for consistency in the text herein. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram was created using an R package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria) and shiny app available online [14].

RESULTS

From the search, 55 manuscripts were identified. A further seven were identified through citations of either the 55 manuscripts, or citations within the nineteen factor-specific review articles which were not included in this study. Sixty-two manuscripts which included non-modifiable factors (sex, breed, body size, brain characteristics, and drooling), modifiable life factors (neuter status, rabies vaccination status, behavior, body weight, reproductive investment, radiation, dental scaling frequency, and diet), and epigenetic factors (gene expression levels, telomere length, and methylation) that are correlated with longevity in pet dogs were included in this systematic review. Sex

Most studies determined that female dogs tend to live longer than male dogs (n = 15 for females living longer, n = 2 for males living longer, n = 5 for no difference). This appeared to be true even when different analytic or statistical methods were used: mortality, predicted lifespan at different ages, Gompertz slope, and mean/median age at death were all methods which found that female dogs tended to live longer than male dogs. However, this was typically a small effect, and in small studies the sample size was typically not large enough to find a statistically significant difference given the small magnitude of the effect. Indeed, one study found that females lived longer than males, and it was statistically significant, but the authors rounded all ages presented in the study to the nearest half-year, so both male and female median ages at death rounded to 10 years [15]. Another study found that when adjusting for weight, there was no difference between female and male life expectancy [16]. Yet another study found that neuter status was likely a confounding variable in the VMDB and Vetcompass datasets: female dogs are much more likely to be spayed, and neutering had a much larger effect on longevity than sex in cox proportional hazards models [17] (Table 1).

Neuter status

Neutering has a statistically significant positive effect on female longevity, but its effect on longevity in males is less clear. When neuter status is examined at the population level without considering sex, the positive effect of spaying females appears to predominate in the overall results: when comparing neutered vs. intact animals, the lifespan of neutered animals is longer than that of intact ones. However, when this is stratified by sex, the effect of neutering in the males becomes less clear. Some studies find that intact males live longer than neutered ones, and vice versa; however, the magnitude of the effect is usually small in large population studies. There may, however, be breed-specific variation to the magnitude of this effect. In two breeds, for example, rottweilers and bulldogs, the magnitude of this effect is greater than 1 year (note the direction of this magnitude is inverse in these examples: intact male rottweilers, and neutered male bulldogs live longer than their respective counterparts) [1819]. The benefit of neutering seems to predominate in decreased mortality in early life—with the cause of death for intact animals at this life stage more likely to be from infectious and traumatic causes [17]. In several studies, when mortality rates for older animals were compared, neuter status became less significant [1720]. However, an important consideration which was only taken into account in one study is the inherent issue of immortal time bias—where neutered animals by definition must survive to the time of their procedure. Because of this bias, neutered animals may indeed appear to survive longer [18] (Table 2).

Breed

Mixed-breed dogs on average live longer than single-breed dogs. This is true even when accounting for body size—that is, small single-breed dogs live shorter lives than their small mixed-breed counterparts. One study compared single-breeds, mixed-breeds, and F1 hybrids (the first filial generation produced by mating two single-breed parents); in that analysis, F1 hybrids outlived all others [16].

Longest lived breeds frequently identified are toy poodles, collies (of many varieties), dachshunds, and West Highland white terriers. Shortest lived breeds frequently identified are: great dane, dogue de Bordeaux, Bernese mountain dog, St. Bernard, and bulldogs (of many varieties). Toy poodles were found to be one of the shortest lived breeds (with a life expectancy at age 0 of 12.7 years in Japan) in one publication [21], but were also reported as being one of the longest lived breeds (with a median age at death of 9.3 years, 14.63 years, 14.4 years, and 14.2 years in Swedish, UK, and US datasets) in several other publications [22232425].

When phylogenetic analysis is used to determine which clades have the most effect on lifespan, the clades which include the Caucasian shepherd dog, the mastiffs and bulldogs, and the presa Canario and cane corso have the shortest lives [26].

More genetic heterozygosity within a breed correlates with a longer lifespan [272829]. Within doberman pinschers specifically, European dobermans have a shorter life expectancy than dobermans in other countries, most likely due to inbreeding depression within their lineage [28]. Within golden retrievers, the coefficient of inbreeding negatively correlates with lifespan [29].

Individual genes which have been correlated with longevity in dogs include: PRDX1, IGF1, GHR, CD36, SMAD2, IGF2BP2. IGF1 and SMAD2 are likely implicated in growth early in life [30]. PRDX1 appears to be the only gene so far identified which correlates with longevity independently of body size. Among golden retrievers, a specific mutation of the 5’UTR region of ERBB4 (this region does not code for any protein but instead is responsible for regulating expression) is associated with longevity [31] (Tables 1 and 3).

Body size

Body size and its correlation with lifespan in companion dogs has been extensively studied, and increasing body size is repeatedly correlated with decreasing lifespan. This holds true even when accounting for phylogeny [3233], so, for example, among poodle breeds, toy poodles live longer than do standard poodles [22]. This is also true even when study design properly accounts for right-censoring bias [27], which is a type of bias which can disproportionately under-estimate the lifespans of groups of dogs which are still alive when a study occurred. That size inversely correlates with lifespan is consistently true at all life stages—giant breed dogs consistently have a higher mortality at all ages compared to other body sizes, while small breed dogs consistently have the lowest mortality rates [20]. Da Silva and Cross [33] suggest that the increased mortality in large dogs breeds may be related to increased incidence of cancer outcomes in large dogs (Table 1).

Brain-related differences

Brain size, Havers-Halberg oscillation periodicities, and epilepsy do not correlate with lifespan in dogs, in spite of the fact that brain size correlates with body size, and although each of the studies included in this analysis that examine neuroanatomical differences have low or very low certainties of evidence (Table 1).

Other non-modifiable factors

Age-related cataracts appear to correlate with life expectancy and may be directly related to oxidative stress accumulated in the individual dog. Among beagles who were part of radiation exposure experiments, the development of palpable neoplasms was associated with longer lifespans. There also appears to be a strong negative association between lifespan and drooling, and this association persists even when adjusting for body size (Table 1).

Reproductive investment

While there appears to be evidence that high reproductive investment (which is defined and measured in different manners in different studies, but attempts to examine the amount of fetal biomass that a female dog produces through either litter size, neonate weight, number of litters, or total number of offspring) has a negative effect on lifespan, the effects of moderate and low reproductive investment are less clear. Only one study investigated the individual effect of reproductive investment on lifespan (total number of offspring) [34], while the other studies examined the effects at a breed level (average litter size and neonate weights) [3235] (Table 2).

Factors related to veterinary care

While access to veterinary care, or frequency of veterinary care is often listed as a potential confounder in many studies, especially those considering the effect of neutering, few address the specific factors that veterinary care offers and whether those factors are associated with longevity. Rabies vaccination status is associated with lower all-cause mortality in free-roaming owned dogs [36]. When controlling for veterinary visit frequency, dental scaling frequency is also associated with a reduced hazard ratio for mortality—more dental cleanings at the veterinarian decrease mortality [20] (Table 2).

Body condition

Dogs maintaining a leaner body condition have longer lifespans and lower instantaneous risk of death compared to overweight dogs [3738] (Table 2).

Diet

The only study which directly examined the effect of dietary factors on longevity compared the effect of dietary restriction on dogs fed the same diet over their lives [39]. No study has compared the effects of different diet types or dietary ingredients on longevity itself. One study compared the effect of different diets fed to older dogs on telomere length [40], which is a biomarker for aging, but is at best a proxy for longevity (Tables 2 and 3).

DISCUSSION

The majority of research on factors which modify longevity in pet dogs has included non-modifiable factors such as sex, breed, and body size. This appears to mostly be due to the fact that these factors are studied frequently as part of the demography of any study. However there are specific investigations which do address these factors individually and provide meaningful insights into interactions between them (i.e., mixed-breed vs. single-breed status does still have an effect on longevity, even when accounting for body size, and female dogs do tend to live longer than males, but this is mostly because gonadectomy is more commonly performed on female dogs than male dogs).

This is useful information to both prospective pet owners and veterinary professionals when it comes to decision-making in the process of obtaining a dog (e.g., selecting breed and sex of dog), and to clinicians in determining courses of treatment. However, the research about how to best extend the lifespan of pet dogs with changes to their lifestyle is less robust. Of these modifiable factors, neutering is the most studied. In female dogs, neutering has a statistically significantly positive effect on longevity.

Neutered dogs are also reportedly less at risk for early developmental and congenital conditions like aortic stenosis, patent ductus arteriosus, portosystemic shunt, and ventricular septal defect [41]. However, information regarding differences in early life causes of mortality and all studies which examine the effect of neutering on longevity must be examined with nuance. In the context of survival analyses, immortal time bias [42] applies to the group of neutered dogs before they were neutered: the dogs had to survive, by definition, from birth to neutering to become classified as neutered—which will lead to early survival time being misattributed. In cross-sectional or retrospective designs, this is very related to the immortal time concept, however it is a form of survivorship or selection bias, where the entirety of the neutered dog population will be by definition healthier than the entire population of the intact dogs because the intact population of dogs will contain puppies which died from infectious or severe congenital conditions. Dogs must by definition survive until after their neutering surgery to be counted in the neutered group in these cross-sectional and retrospective designs. The fact that neutered dogs are less at risk for early developmental conditions that are mostly cardiovascular defects may be less an effect that neutering has on the cardiovascular system, and more that dogs who are neutered must survive to the age of sterilization and through the procedure itself. In study designs which attempt to account for these related biases, the results are rather middling. Intact rottweilers do appear to live longer [18], but this is very breed-specific. In a large population of many breeds which examined age-specific mortality, there was no effect of neutering on longevity amongst older animals—only that early-life deaths are more common in intact animals [17], which is likely consistent with the immortal time bias or survivorship bias effect.

However, it would be expected that these biases would not be different between males and females. So the marked differences reported in the effect of neutering on longevity between males and females (where spaying females has a significantly larger effect on longevity than neutering males) demonstrates that there likely still is a physiological effect beyond a design bias, and that this effect is more beneficial in female dogs than for male dogs.

The significant positive effect that spaying female dogs has on their longevity is frequently repeated in many observational studies with high sample sizes and many breeds. However the mechanisms to understand why are less clear. Intact female dogs are more likely to die at a younger age from traumatic and infectious causes of death—however whether those traumatic causes are dystocia or hit-by-car due to roaming or dog fights is not categorized in the current literature. Whether the infectious causes of death are brucellosis, pyometra, or other causes are also not broken down. Reproductive investment itself is not very well studied—there seems to be preliminary evidence for the fact that high reproductive investment has a negative impact on lifespan, but more studies which focus on individual dogs (number of litters), and which account for breed size as a confounder need to be conducted before determining whether limiting reproductive investment appropriately accounts for all the longevity benefits that spayed female dogs have.

What does seem to be clear is that living longer allows the causes of death for spayed female dogs to be different than those of their intact counterparts, as older dogs are more likely to have a cause of death attributed to neoplasia. As Urfer and Kaeberlein state, “given that lifespan is finite in both intact and desexed dogs, we argue that more emphasis should be placed on lifespan as a relevant measure of positive or negative health outcomes associated with desexing dogs, regardless of ultimate cause of death” [43]. If neutered animals are more likely to die from cancer, it is due to their longevity which has allowed this cause of death to become a possibility. Mathematical modeling has demonstrated that the increased risk of cancer in companion dogs is due to increases in longevity [44].

The inverse correlation of body size and longevity in companion dogs has been well proven, although the mechanisms for the cause of this are still being studied. Growth may compromise longevity in dogs since the change in breed sizes is mostly due to changes in early growth rate, and the main cause of the increased mortality in large breed dogs is cancer [32]. Whether this is due to up-regulation in growth genes like IGF1 [7] which can also promote cancer [45], or whether this is because allocating resources to growth early in life comes at the expense of somatic maintenance [33] has not been truly definitively determined in scientific literature. Preliminary work with IGF-1 has determined that larger dogs have higher levels of serum IGF1, but this work has yet to be connected with lifespan [46]. P53 and SIRT1 pathways have also been possibly implicated as acting differently between large breed dogs and small breed dogs as they age, but this field needs further study [47]. Other studies have determined that body size correlates with differences in diseases—with larger dogs getting more orthopedic, gastrointestinal, ear/nose/throat, cancer, neurological, endocrine, and infectious diseases and smaller dogs getting more ocular, cardiac, liver/pancreas, and respiratory diseases [48].

While breed and body size are interrelated since many breed standards include body size, there are also breed-specific factors that influence lifespan which are independent of body size. Brachycephalic breeds have shorter lives compared to those of their same-body-size counterparts, which is likely attributed to their worse overall health [49]. While breed personality was potentially implicated in changes in longevity, this work seems questionable when considering the fact that breed only explains 9% of the variation in behavior in individual dogs, and indeed modern breeds are primarily distinguished by aesthetic DNA traits [50].

Breed status does appear to have an effect on the longevity of companion dogs—with mixed-breed dogs living on average longer than their single-breed counterparts (although in the one study which accounted for it, F1 hybrids of single-breed dogs outlived mixed-breed dogs). The reasons often cited for this are hybrid vigor and inbreeding depression. Among single-breed dogs, inbreeding depression is associated with decreased lifespan and heterozygosity is associated with increased lifespan, but proving that it is directly responsible for the increased longevity of mixed-breed dogs has not yet been done.

Studies related to the direct effect of diet on longevity are sparse. Among dogs eating the same diet, caloric restriction leads to an increase in lifespan. While the quality of this evidence was low, this is consistent with findings in other species including humans [51]. However, no studies comparing different dietary exposures and their direct effect on longevity have been conducted. Feeding older dogs a diet enriched with supplements thought to benefit health did result in changes to minimum telomere lengths, but no changes to mean telomere lengths. While telomere length is a biomarker for aging, it does not directly correlate with longevity [52]. Because diet is one of the most commonly modified exposures that owners can easily control, determining the magnitude of the effect of dietary exposures on longevity remains an important and under-studied topic. Related to diet, one study has proven that overweight dogs live shorter lives than their normal body condition counterparts. While dietary modifications can help with the maintenance of a healthy body condition, activity levels and exercise are also important to consider.

Overall, the scientific burden of evidence about what factors most influence longevity in pet dogs focuses most on the factors which are non-modifiable. Even the research field surrounding the most extensively studied modifiable factor, neutering, could benefit from more studies which control for access to veterinary care and which account for immortal time bias in the neuter group. Other factors which are modifiable—such as diet—have very little scientific evidence to support what exposures benefit longevity in pet dogs.
