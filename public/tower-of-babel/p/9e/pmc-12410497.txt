Atypical electrical injury leading to chest wall necrosis, multi-organ failure and terminal gastrointestinal bleed: A case report
Authors: Syed Ibrahim, Ahmed Reaan, Ashja Syed, Safdar Shaikh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHigh-voltage electrical injuries (HVI) are usually life-threatening and are commonly caused by contact with high voltage power lines. They can lead to deep burns and multi-system trauma.Case presentationThis report presents an atypical case of a 21-year-old male who had an accidental electrocution with high voltage power lines during a religious event, leading to a 4th-degree burn of the chest wall, right neck, pelvis, bilateral anterior thighs, perineum and both arms; the total body surface area (TBSA) was approximately 37 %. Deep chest wounds exposed the ribs, muscles and fascia. During his hospital stay, he developed several complications. The management of the wound included multiple debridements, reconstruction of the chest wall using a latissimus dorsi muscle flap, and split-thickness skin grafting from the left thigh. Initially the patient recovered but later developed lower gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding and expired due to hypovolemic shock.DiscussionThis case highlights the complex management of HVIs involving extensive TBSA. Prompt surgical intervention is essential and includes coverage of deep wounds using a muscle flap, along with skin grafting for protection of the underlying structures. Due to the risk of systemic complications with such injuries, multidisciplinary care is required. In this case, the sudden onset of GI bleeding shows the unpredictable trajectory of such injuries.ConclusionHVIs involving extensive TBSA can result in rare but fatal complications. Prompt multidisciplinary management is crucial, and there is need for further research to improve outcomes and prevent complications in such complex cases.Keywords: Case report, High voltage injury, Latissimus dorsi flap, Fourth-degree burn, Chest wall reconstruction, Multi-organ failure

Introduction

Electrical injuries can be classified into high voltage (>1000 V) and low voltage (<1000 V) injuries [1]. Low voltage injuries (LVI) are usually caused by household currents, while HVIs are most often caused by contact with high voltage power lines [2]. In developing countries, 27 % of admissions to burn units are due to electrical injuries, most of which are men [1]. The average TBSA of the burn is 13.5 % [1]. Such injuries have the potential of causing multisystem trauma [3]. The mortality rate ranges from 3.75 % to 58.8 % [4].

Electrical injuries cause destruction at the points of entrance and exit of the electric current in the body [5]. During contact the victim is enclosed in an electrical circuit; this means that visceral burns are also produced which may be very deep, depending on the duration, intensity and pathway of the current [6,7].

Electrical injuries are mostly life threatening, requiring various surgical procedures such as decompression, split skin grafting and debridement [6,8]. Decompression is required for tight compartments with neurovascular compromise, followed by debridement and wound closure, while excision and grafting are needed for burn-related contractures [9]. Outcomes of electrical injuries include, but are not limited to, amputation, compartment syndrome, arrhythmia, and renal failure [10]. Burns that involve more than 30 % of the TBSA lead to a systemic effect due to considerable hypovolemia and the formation and release of inflammatory mediators [11]. This is why the prognosis of a burn patient is influenced by the TBSA [12].

This report at a tertiary care center, presents an atypical case of accidental electrocution with high voltage power lines leading to a 4th-degree burn with the TBSA approximately 37 %. Written informed consent was taken from the family of the patient.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [13].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old male of average height, weight, and build, with no known comorbidities, sustained a high-voltage electrical injury at approximately 4:30 PM. In a religious event, he was holding a pole, that came into contact with overhead high-voltage power lines, with the current passing through his trunk, groin and thighs.

1st week after accident

He was initially taken to a regional hospital and transferred to our facility at 12:24 AM the following day. Upon arrival at the Emergency Room, he was hypotensive (BP 97/50 mmHg), had a pulse of 85 bpm, and oxygen saturation of 95 %. He appeared agitated with a GCS score of 12/15. Physical examination revealed a fourth degree burn on the chest wall, with additional involvement of the right neck (refer to Fig. 1), pelvis, bilateral anterior thighs, perineum, and both arms, involving approximately 37 % of TBSA (refer to Fig. 2). Deep chest wounds exposed ribs, muscle, and fascia, with rib fractures noted. Subcutaneous emphysema of the chest and neck was worsening; hence intubation was done due to impending airway compromise. He was started on a nasogastric diet, which was later optimized.

Involvement of the right neck.

Extent of the burn.

The patient was shifted to special care the same day. Initial investigations included a CT scan which revealed pneumomediastinum and pneumopericardium. Bilateral tube thoracostomies were performed to remove the trapped air. Cardiac workup showed elevated troponin-I (0.261 ng/mL), suggesting myocardial contusion. The patient remained hemodynamically unstable with repeated episodes of hypotension and tachycardia.

The patient's creatine phosphokinase was markedly elevated (15,942 U/L), consistent with rhabdomyolysis. Multiple areas of deep burns were noted, with chest and neck wounds involving full muscular thickness and abdominal wounds involving full skin thickness.

Early wound management included debridement of the scrotum, chest, and lower abdomen, followed by dressing and vacuum-assisted closure therapy to the left chest.

On day 5, blood cultures grew multiple drug-resistant organisms, and the patient developed septic shock secondary to burn-related sepsis. Blood cultures remained positive throughout most of the stay, and he was kept on multiple antibiotics.

2nd week after accident

Multiple serial debridements were performed, including removal of necrotic ribs. At one point, the heart was exposed through the chest wound (refer to Fig. 3).

The wound after debridements (the ribs can be seen).

3rd week after accident

On day 15, a portion of the pericardium that had necrosed was replaced with bovine pericardial tissue. A LD flap and STSG from the left thigh were used to reconstruct the left chest wall (refer to Fig. 4). The LD flap was harvested using an oblique skin paddle to maximize skin coverage while also allowing for primary closure. An orchidectomy was also performed to remove the necrosed left testis.

Reconstruction of the chest wall.

On day 18, the patient underwent tracheostomy. He was also diagnosed with secondary acute kidney injury. Lab findings included creatinine 4.0 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 93 mg/dL, estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate 16.4 mL/min/1.73 m2, and deranged electrolytes with hypernatremia. Arterial blood gas showed normal anion gap metabolic acidosis with concomitant respiratory acidosis.

4th week after accident

Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a moderate-to-large pericardial effusion with early signs of tamponade. Hence, a high-risk pericardiocentesis was performed.

5th week after accident

The patient's GCS had improved to 15/15.

6th week after accident

Due to exposure and suspected infection, the bovine pericardium was removed, and further debridement of the chest, abdomen, and left thigh was performed. A new STSG was harvested from the right thigh, and the LD flap was advanced further to close the chest defect.

8th week after accident

Additional debridements and STSG from the left thigh were carried out to cover the residual wounds. Around this time, the patient became tachypneic and developed respiratory alkalosis with an elevated A-a gradient and rising troponin-I, leading to transfer to the Surgical ICU.

Throughout his hospital course, the patient required multiple blood transfusions due to persistent anemia. Labs consistently showed elevated white blood cell count with neutrophilia and monocytosis, and thrombocytopenia.

10th week after accident

The patient had recovered and was undergoing physiotherapy. However, he suddenly developed lower GI bleeding and was transferred to the ICU. Upper GI endoscopy did not reveal an active bleeding source. Hypovolemic shock ensued due to blood loss. A subsequent colonoscopy showed blood throughout the colon but no identifiable source. A radionuclide scan with tagged red blood cells and a contrast-free CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis were both negative for active bleeding.

Despite these efforts, the patient suffered a massive rectal bleed and rapidly deteriorated. He became hypotensive and bradycardic (32–44 bpm), with a drop in GCS. Epinephrine, norepinephrine, and atropine were administered, but he remained unresponsive. The family was counseled and opted for Do Not Resuscitate. The patient was taken to the ICU, and death was declared following asystole on electrocardiogram.

Discussion

The case presented above was of an electrical burn with TBSA approximately 37 %. A study reported that in patients with TBSA above 20 % the mortality rate was 85.4 % [14].

In such a case of extensive electrical burn over the chest, reconstruction of the chest wall poses a challenge due to the presence of two important closed cavities—the pleural and pericardial cavities—each containing vital organs. So, whenever they are exposed due to trauma, they need to be reconstructed instantly. [15] In our patient, the chest wall, ribs and the pericardium were necrosed, and after multiple debridements, the heart was also exposed. In such cases, debridement alone can increase the chance of complications; instead, coverage using a well-vascularized tissue, such as a muscle flap, improves prognosis [16,17].

The commonly used muscles for reconstruction of the chest wall are the latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major and vertical rectus abdominus [18]. In our patient, the flap was taken from the latissimus dorsi muscle because both the pectoralis major and vertical rectus abdominus had been affected by the electrical burn. The latissimus dorsi flap has numerous advantages such as good perfusion, a large surface coverage, unconstrained freedom of placement, and a well-vascularized muscular coverage [19].

Apart from the muscle flap, an intact skin barrier is also necessary to avoid infections, maintain function, and minimize disfigurement [20]. In our patient STSGs from his bilateral thighs were used to cover the chest wall. The advantages of this are easy accessibility and relatively concealed anatomical location of the thigh [21].

Alongside the wound and its coverage, our patient also had multi-organ failure. The visceral complications that can arise in such injuries are related to the skin, heart (arrhythmia and cardiac arrest), respiratory system (respiratory arrest), vascular system, neurological system (impairment of consciousness, paralysis and paresthesia, peripheral neuropathy and spinal cord injury), musculoskeletal system (muscle necrosis, compartment syndrome and fracture/dislocation), and kidney (renal failure) [9]. For such damage it is vital to involve the relevant department immediately so that the damage is minimized to prevent further complications. For our patient, multiple departments were referred, including but not limited to, cardiothoracic surgery, cardiology, nephrology, infectious diseases and gastroenterology.

The treatment of our patient was successful, and the patient had started rehabilitation through physiotherapy. However, a GI complication occurred suddenly, and the patient relapsed. Although GI bleeding is a well-known complication of burn injuries, the presentation was atypical in this case because patients are expected to develop stress gastritis or Curling's ulcers within 72 h of the burn injury. This leads to bleeding due to acid-peptic digestion of the gastric mucosa. Hence, ulcer prophylaxis with antacids or H2-receptor antagonists is recommended in burn patients. In the case of this patient, the source could not be determined despite endoscopy, and he expired due to hypovolemic shock. [22,23]

Conclusion

This case highlights the atypical presentation and effects of HVIs, especially with a large TBSA, including the vital organs. To improve the patient's condition, immediate and efficient management is crucial. This includes debridement, flap reconstruction, skin grafting and supportive. Despite these interventions, the prognosis is uncertain, and unexpected complications like sepsis and GI bleeding can significantly affect it. This case shows the need for further research into the treatment of electrical burns covering a large body area, with multi-organ failure as well as the prevention of complications associated with it.
