Sleep duration variations across weekdays and weekends and their associations with generalised anxiety disorder and depressive symptoms in Nigerian adults
Authors: Adekola B. Ademoyegun, Niyi Akintayo, Atinuke Ogunmola, Oluwafunmilayo A. Adeniyi, David O. Fabuluje, Mosimiloluwa C. Akinsole, Olumide A. Olaoye, Taofeek O. Awotidebe, Chidozie E. Mbada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep duration and weekday–weekend sleep variability are increasingly recognised as important determinants of mental health. However, evidence from African populations on the relationship between sleep patterns and symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) and depression remains limited. This study examined the associations of sleep duration and multidimensional sleep patterns with GAD and depressive symptoms among Nigerian adults.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1,223 adults with complete data. Weekday and weekend sleep duration were categorised as short (< 7 h), normal (7–9 h), or long (> 9 h). Sleep patterns were classified as: (1) optimally based (optimal, suboptimal weekday, suboptimal weekend, mixed) and (2) consistency based (consistent normal, consistent short, consistent long, inconsistent). Anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using the GAD-7 and PHQ-9. Multivariable logistic regression examined associations between sleep variables and mental health outcomes.ResultsOverall, 55.8%, 36.4%, and 7.8% of participants reported short, normal, and long weekday sleep duration, respectively, compared with 36.7%, 47.8%, and 15.5% during weekends. The prevalence of GAD and depressive symptoms was 12.8% and 31.4%. Compared with normal sleep duration, short and long weekday sleep were associated with higher odds of GAD (AOR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.89–4.63 and AOR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.68–7.10, respectively). Similarly, short and long weekend sleep were associated with higher odds of GAD (AOR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.44–3.25 and AOR = 2.92, 95% CI: 1.75–4.87, respectively). Suboptimal weekday, mixed, consistent short, consistent long, and inconsistent sleep patterns were also significantly associated with GAD (AORs = 2.72–4.08). For depressive symptoms, long weekday sleep (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.01–2.75), long weekend sleep (AOR = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.81–3.73), suboptimal weekday sleep (AOR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.02–1.94), mixed sleep patterns (AOR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.43–3.43), and inconsistent sleep patterns (AOR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.26–2.48) were associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms.ConclusionBoth sleep duration and sleep regularity were associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms among Nigerian adults. Background

Depressive symptoms and generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) are among the most common and disabling psychiatric conditions globally. Together, they contribute substantially to the global burden of disease, affecting millions of individuals across diverse populations [1, 2]. These conditions are associated with significant impairments, including disruptions in vocational, social, and physical functioning, loss of human capital, elevated risk of suicide, increased mortality from chronic illnesses, and overall deterioration in psychological and physiological well-being [3–10]. Given their high prevalence and substantial impact, it is essential to identify modifiable behavioural factors that may influence the risk or severity of these disorders.

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process essential for optimal physical and mental functioning [11]. A growing body of evidence links sleep disturbances, including poor sleep quality, insufficient sleep, and irregular sleep patterns, to adverse mental health outcomes, including symptoms of depression and anxiety. Consistently achieving 7–9 h of sleep per night is associated with better mental health and overall well-being [5, 12–15]. Conversely, both short and long sleep durations have been associated with poor mental health outcomes [12–15]. For example, Wang et al. [12] reported that US adults sleeping 5 to < 7 h per night had higher odds of anxiety disorders compared to those with normal sleep duration (7–9 h). However, findings on long sleep duration and anxiety remain inconsistent: some studies report increased anxiety symptoms with ≥ 10 h of sleep [15–17], while others find no such association [12, 15, 18]. The links between sleep and depression are similarly complex, with insufficient sleep increasing the risk of developing or recurring depressive symptoms [5, 13, 14, 19, 20], and evidence also associating long sleep duration with greater depressive symptomatology [21].

Despite extensive research on average sleep duration and quality, less attention has been given to weekday–weekend differences in sleep duration, a pattern increasingly recognised as relevant to mental health. Emerging evidence suggests notable disparities: adults often obtain shorter sleep on weekdays due to occupational and social obligations, followed by longer recovery sleep on weekends to compensate [22, 23]. In the US, for instance, adults from 2017 to 2020 displayed longer sleep and delayed sleep–wake timing on free days, with widespread chronic sleep deprivation, social jetlag, and sleep disturbances [22]. Mireku and Rodriguez [23] similarly found that more individuals met the recommended sleep duration during weekdays (45%) compared with weekends (33%). Other reports also document weekday–weekend mismatches [24, 25]. However, evidence on these patterns in developing countries, including Nigeria, is scarce.

The potential importance of weekday–weekend sleep variability can be understood within established theories of sleep regulation. The Two-Process Model of Sleep Regulation proposes that sleep is governed by the interaction between a homeostatic process, reflecting the accumulation of sleep pressure during wakefulness, and a circadian process, which regulates the timing of sleep and wakefulness [26]. Modern occupational, academic, and social demands may disrupt these processes by restricting sleep during weekdays and encouraging compensatory sleep during weekends. Consequently, substantial variations in sleep duration across the week may contribute to circadian misalignment, social jetlag, emotional dysregulation, and increased vulnerability to anxiety and depressive symptoms [27, 28]. This framework suggests that both sleep duration and the consistency of sleep patterns may be important for mental health, and that average sleep duration alone may not fully capture the behavioural characteristics relevant to psychological well-being.

These weekday–weekend discrepancies are believed to have significant mental health implications. Inconsistent sleep patterns, such as shortened weekday sleep linked to work stress or prolonged weekend “catch-up” sleep, have been associated with depression and anxiety symptoms [29, 30]. Yet, many existing studies rely on average sleep duration measures that mask important differences between weekdays and weekends, potentially overlooking behavioural patterns shaped by modern lifestyles, work schedules, and social demands [23, 31]. Disrupted weekday–weekend sleep patterns may misalign circadian rhythms, while consistently short weekday sleep may reflect chronic stress exposure; both may interact with sleep disturbances to exacerbate mental health symptoms [32].

This study addresses these critical gaps by examining how weekday and weekend sleep durations, as well as composite sleep pattern profiles, predict symptoms of GAD and depressive symptoms among Nigerian adults. This is particularly pertinent because the burden of depression and anxiety is substantial in many African populations [33], and socio-economic, cultural, and environmental factors, such as rapid urbanisation, economic pressures, and cultural obligations, may shape sleep behaviours differently compared with high-income countries [33, 34]. To capture the multidimensional nature of sleep behaviour and its theoretical links to sleep homeostasis and circadian regulation, we adopted three complementary analytical approaches: (1) a separate-duration model, treating weekday and weekend sleep independently; (2) a health-impact model, categorising sleep as optimal, suboptimal weekday, suboptimal weekend, or mixed; and (3) a consistency model, classifying sleep as consistent normal, consistent short, consistent long, or inconsistent. The specific objectives were to examine the associations of weekday and weekend sleep duration with symptoms of GAD and depression, and to determine whether optimally-based and consistency-based sleep pattern profiles are associated with these mental health outcomes among Nigerian adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January and June 2025 among adults (≥ 18 years) residing in Nigeria. Participants were recruited using a mixed-mode approach. Both online (Google Forms) and paper-based questionnaires were disseminated across Osogbo, Osun State, through community gatherings, workplaces, religious centres, and social networks. Recruitment relied on voluntary participation, and no incentives were provided. Eligible participants were adults currently residing in Nigeria who provided informed consent. Individuals younger than 18 years, those with severe cognitive impairment, mental health conditions requiring active treatment, or those institutionalised (e.g., prisons or long-term psychiatric facilities) were excluded.

A minimum sample size of 1000 was estimated to obtain proportions within ± 2.5% and mean differences within ± 1%, yielding 99% power to detect a small effect size (Cohen’s d < 0.2) [35]. A total of 1248 questionnaires were returned, of which 1223 contained complete information and were included in the analysis. The study was approved by the Osun State Health Research Ethics Committee, Nigeria (OSHREC/PRS/569T/783). Written or electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measures

Sociodemographic and health-related characteristics

Data were obtained on age, gender, marital status (married/cohabiting vs. single/widowed/divorced/separated), employment status (full-time student, unemployed, employed, retired), and education (low: none/primary/secondary; high: tertiary). Monthly income was categorised relative to the Nigerian national minimum wage (₦70,000/month; approximately US$45/month). Participants earning less than ₦70,000 (< US$45) per month were classified as having low income, those earning ₦70,000 to <₦140,000 (approximately US$45–90) per month as having moderate income, and those earning ≥₦140,000 (≥ US$90) per month as having high income.

Lifestyle variables included a history of alcohol use (yes/no) and cigarette smoking (yes/no). Participants also reported diagnoses or current medication use for hypertension or diabetes mellitus (yes/no). Physical activity (PA) was assessed using the question: “In the past week, on how many days did you engage in moderate-to-vigorous physical activities (e.g., running, walking, aerobics, gardening) for at least 60 minutes?” Responses were categorised into low (0–2 days), moderate (3–4 days), and high (5–7 days) [36, 37]. Screen time (ST) was assessed by asking participants how many hours per day they spent on phones, computers, gaming, or television. ST was categorised as normal (≤ 3 h/day) or excessive (> 3 h/day). Perceived health status was assessed using a single self-rated health question: “In general, how would you rate your current health status?” Responses were rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (poor), 1 (fair), 2 (good), 3 (very good), to 4 (excellent), with higher scores indicating better perceived health.

Sleep duration

Participants reported their average nightly sleep duration on weekdays and weekends. Sleep duration was classified into: Short: <7 h, Normal: 7–9 h, and Long: >9 h.

Generalised anxiety disorder

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7). Items are scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score of 0–21. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 was used to classify participants as having probable GAD, consistent with established sensitivity (89%) and specificity (82%) [38]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the GAD-7 was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α = 0.89).

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), scored from 0 to 27. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 was used to identify clinically significant depressive symptoms, consistent with previous studies reporting a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 88% for detecting probable major depression [39]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the PHQ-9 was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α = 0.86).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarise the data. Group differences in weekday and weekend sleep duration, optimally based sleep patterns, consistency-based sleep patterns, GAD status, and depressive symptoms status were examined using Chi-square tests, independent t-tests, and one-way analysis of variance, with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons where appropriate. Potential covariates were identified based on previous literature and observed associations with the outcome variables. Variables associated with GAD or depressive symptoms at p ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analyses were considered candidate covariates for multivariable modelling to minimise the risk of excluding potentially important confounders [40]. Three logistic regression models were constructed for each outcome: an unadjusted model (Model 1), a model adjusted for age and gender (Model 2), and a fully adjusted model including age, gender, and candidate covariates identified from the bivariate analyses (Model 3). For logistic regression analyses, the presence of GAD and clinically significant depressive symptoms was coded as 1, while the absence of these conditions was coded as 0. Normal sleep duration, optimal sleep pattern, and consistent normal sleep pattern were used as the reference categories as applicable. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). The maximum VIF values were 2.4 and 2.5 for the GAD and depressive symptoms models, respectively, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0. Participants with missing data on key variables were excluded using listwise deletion.

Results

A total of 1,223 participants with complete data were included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 39.3 ± 17.2 years, and 50.3% were female (Table 1). On weekdays, 55.8%, 36.4%, and 7.8% of participants reported short, normal, and long sleep durations, respectively, whereas on weekends, 36.7%, 47.8%, and 15.5% reported short, normal, and long sleep durations, respectively. Only 25.3% of participants exhibited both optimally and consistently normal sleep patterns. The mean GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores were 4.56 ± 4.22 and 6.18 ± 5.69, respectively. Overall, 12.8% of participants had probable GAD and 31.4% had clinically significant depressive symptoms based on established screening thresholds.

Associations with participants’ characteristics and sleep durations/patterns, GAD, and depressive symptoms

Associations between participants’ characteristics and sleep duration/patterns, GAD, and depressive symptoms are presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S4. Weekday and weekend sleep duration, optimally-based sleep patterns, and consistency-based sleep patterns were significantly associated with several sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related characteristics, including age, gender, education, income, employment status, perceived health status, chronic conditions, screen time, and physical activity (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Furthermore, employment status, income, alcohol intake, cigarette smoking, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and perceived health status were associated with probable GAD, while age, education, employment status, perceived health status, and screen time were associated with depressive symptoms (Supplementary Table S4).

Association between sleep duration, sleep patterns and GAD

Table 2 summarises the associations between weekday–weekend sleep duration, sleep patterns, and GAD.

Weekday and weekend sleep duration

Compared with normal weekday sleep duration, both short and long weekday sleep durations were associated with significantly higher odds of GAD in the unadjusted model. The associations remained significant after adjustment for age and gender and after full adjustment. In the fully adjusted model, short weekday sleep duration was associated with 2.96-fold higher odds of GAD (Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.89–4.63; p < 0.001), while long weekday sleep duration was associated with 3.46-fold higher odds of GAD (AOR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.68–7.10; p = 0.001). Similarly, compared with normal weekend sleep duration, both short and long weekend sleep durations were significantly associated with higher odds of GAD across all models. In the fully adjusted model, short weekend sleep duration was associated with 2.17-fold higher odds of GAD (AOR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.44–3.25; p < 0.001), whereas long weekend sleep duration was associated with 2.92-fold higher odds of GAD (AOR = 2.92, 95% CI: 1.75–4.87; p < 0.001).

Optimally-based sleep pattern

Compared with an optimal sleep pattern, a suboptimal weekday sleep pattern was associated with significantly higher odds of GAD in all models. The association remained significant in the fully adjusted model (AOR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.69–5.17; p < 0.001). Similarly, participants with mixed sleep patterns had significantly higher odds of GAD than those with optimal sleep patterns (AOR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.37–5.41; p = 0.004). In contrast, a suboptimal weekend sleep pattern was not associated with GAD in any model.

Consistency-based sleep pattern

Compared with consistent normal sleep, consistent short sleep, consistent long sleep, and inconsistent sleep patterns were all associated with significantly higher odds of GAD. In the fully adjusted model, consistent short sleep was associated with a fourfold increase in the odds of GAD (AOR = 4.08, 95% CI: 2.27–7.32; p < 0.001), while consistent long sleep was associated with 3.69-fold higher odds (AOR = 3.69, 95% CI: 1.47–9.26; p = 0.005). Likewise, inconsistent sleep patterns were associated with 3.07-fold higher odds of GAD (AOR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.71–5.49; p < 0.001).

Association between sleep duration, sleep patterns and depressive symptoms

Table 3 presents the associations between weekday–weekend sleep duration, sleep patterns, and depressive symptoms.

Weekday and weekend sleep duration

Compared with normal weekday sleep duration, short weekday sleep duration was not associated with depressive symptoms in any model. Long weekday sleep duration was not associated with depressive symptoms in the unadjusted model or the model adjusted for age and gender. However, after full adjustment, long weekday sleep duration was associated with significantly higher odds of depressive symptoms (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.01–2.75; p = 0.045). For weekend sleep duration, short sleep duration was not associated with depressive symptoms in any model. In contrast, long weekend sleep duration was consistently associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms across all models. In the fully adjusted model, participants with long weekend sleep duration had 2.59-fold higher odds of depressive symptoms compared with those with normal weekend sleep duration (AOR = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.81–3.73; p < 0.001).

Optimally-based sleep pattern

Compared with an optimal sleep pattern, a suboptimal weekday sleep pattern was associated with significantly higher odds of depressive symptoms across all models. The association remained significant in the fully adjusted model (AOR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.02–1.94; p = 0.036). Similarly, mixed sleep patterns were significantly associated with depressive symptoms in all models, with participants exhibiting mixed sleep patterns having more than twice the odds of depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted model (AOR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.43–3.43; p < 0.001). Suboptimal weekend sleep patterns were not associated with depressive symptoms in any model.

Consistency-based sleep pattern

Compared with consistent normal sleep, neither consistent short sleep nor consistent long sleep was associated with depressive symptoms in any model. However, inconsistent sleep patterns were significantly associated with depressive symptoms across all models. In the fully adjusted model, participants with inconsistent sleep patterns had 1.77-fold higher odds of depressive symptoms compared with those with consistent normal sleep (AOR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.26–2.48; p = 0.001).

Discussion

This study examined weekday–weekend sleep duration trajectories, optimally-based and consistency-based sleep patterns, and their associations with symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) and depressive symptoms among Nigerian adults. Several important findings emerged, highlighting the relevance of both sleep duration and sleep regularity for mental health in this population. A substantial proportion of participants reported short sleep duration during weekdays and longer sleep duration during weekends. This pattern suggests the presence of weekday sleep restriction followed by compensatory weekend recovery sleep, commonly referred to as “catch-up sleep.” Similar weekday–weekend sleep disparities have been reported in other populations and are often attributed to occupational demands, academic commitments, lifestyle pressures, and social obligations that restrict sleep during the workweek [22–25, 27, 41]. These findings suggest that irregular sleep schedules may be common among Nigerian adults and may have important implications for psychological well-being. In the present study, several socio-demographic, lifestyle, and health-related factors, including age, gender, income, education, employment status, alcohol intake, chronic health conditions, perceived health status, screen time, and PA, were associated with sleep duration and sleep pattern trajectories. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that socioeconomic circumstances, health status, and lifestyle behaviours influence sleep habits and sleep regularity [42, 43].

This study found robust associations between sleep duration, sleep patterns, and symptoms of anxiety. Both short and long sleep durations during weekdays and weekends were associated with higher odds of GAD, suggesting a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and anxiety symptoms. These findings align with previous evidence indicating that both insufficient and excessive sleep may adversely affect emotional regulation, stress responsiveness, and psychological functioning [12, 15–18, 44, 45]. In addition, suboptimal weekday sleep patterns, mixed sleep patterns, consistent short sleep, consistent long sleep, and inconsistent sleep patterns were associated with higher odds of GAD. The strongest association was observed for consistent short sleep, suggesting that chronic sleep restriction may be particularly detrimental to anxiety-related outcomes. Collectively, these findings indicate that both the quantity and regularity of sleep are important correlates of anxiety symptoms.

With respect to depressive symptoms, long sleep duration emerged as a more important correlate than short sleep duration. Long weekday sleep and long weekend sleep were independently associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms, whereas short sleep duration was not associated with depressive symptoms after adjustment for potential confounders. These findings are consistent with studies reporting associations between prolonged sleep duration and depression, potentially reflecting underlying psychological distress, reduced daytime functioning, inflammatory processes, or other health-related factors [21, 46, 47]. Furthermore, suboptimal weekday sleep patterns, mixed sleep patterns, and inconsistent sleep patterns were associated with depressive symptoms, suggesting that sleep irregularity may be an important behavioural marker of poor mental health. These findings support growing evidence that variability in sleep schedules may disrupt circadian regulation and sleep homeostasis, thereby contributing to emotional disturbances and depressive symptomatology [29, 30, 48].

Interestingly, the pattern of associations differed between anxiety and depressive symptoms. Whereas both short and long sleep durations were associated with higher odds of GAD, depressive symptoms were primarily associated with long sleep duration and irregular sleep patterns. This suggests that deviations from normal sleep duration in either direction may be relevant to anxiety symptoms, whereas prolonged and irregular sleep behaviours may be more strongly linked to depressive symptoms. Taken together, these findings suggest that both sleep duration and sleep regularity are important correlates of mental health. Notably, sleep irregularity, particularly mixed and inconsistent sleep patterns, demonstrated consistent associations with both anxiety and depressive symptoms. These results reinforce emerging evidence that maintaining regular sleep schedules may be as important as obtaining adequate sleep duration for promoting psychological well-being.

Implications

The findings of this study have important public health and clinical implications. The strong associations observed between sleep duration, sleep irregularity, anxiety symptoms, and depressive symptoms suggest that sleep health should be considered an important component of mental health promotion strategies. Public health campaigns in Nigeria frequently emphasise diet, physical activity, and chronic disease prevention; however, the present findings indicate that sleep duration and sleep regularity are also important behavioural determinants of mental health. Incorporating sleep-health education into primary healthcare services, workplace wellness programmes, educational settings, and community outreach initiatives may help improve awareness of healthy sleep practices. Clinically, routine assessment of sleep duration and sleep variability may facilitate the early identification of individuals at elevated risk of anxiety or depressive symptoms. At a policy level, integrating sleep health into broader mental health and non-communicable disease prevention programmes may contribute to improving population well-being.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first to investigate weekday–weekend sleep duration patterns, optimally-based sleep patterns, and consistency-based sleep patterns in relation to anxiety and depressive symptoms among adults in Nigeria. The large sample size enhanced statistical power and precision of the estimates. Furthermore, the consideration of multiple socio-demographic, lifestyle, and health-related factors strengthened the adjusted analyses and enabled a more comprehensive evaluation of sleep–mental health associations. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and prevents the determination of the temporal direction of the observed associations. Second, although participants were recruited through both online and paper-based approaches, recruitment was predominantly concentrated in Osogbo, Osun State. Consequently, the findings may not fully reflect the demographic, cultural, and socioeconomic diversity of the wider Nigerian population. Third, sleep duration was assessed using self-report measures and may therefore be subject to recall bias or misclassification. Fourth, anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using validated screening instruments rather than clinical diagnostic interviews; consequently, the findings relate to probable GAD and clinically significant depressive symptoms rather than confirmed psychiatric diagnoses. Finally, although multiple potential confounders were considered, residual confounding arising from unmeasured factors, such as occupational stress, caregiving responsibilities, shift work, or undiagnosed sleep disorders, cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both sleep duration and sleep regularity are significantly associated with symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder and depressive symptoms among Nigerian adults. Both short and long sleep durations were associated with higher odds of anxiety symptoms, whereas long sleep duration was more strongly associated with depressive symptoms. In addition, suboptimal weekday sleep, mixed sleep patterns, and inconsistent sleep patterns were consistently associated with poorer mental health outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of promoting both adequate and regular sleep as part of mental health prevention and intervention strategies. Future longitudinal and nationally representative studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and evaluate the effectiveness of sleep-focused interventions for improving mental health outcomes.
