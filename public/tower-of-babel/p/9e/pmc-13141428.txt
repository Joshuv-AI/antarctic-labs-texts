MicroRNA-regulated signaling and tumor microenvironment modulation in lung cancer: mechanistic insights and translational opportunities
Authors: Chandani Shrestha, Sofia Brito, Barsha Deshar, Junyoung Cho, Gwangbin Lee, Dasom Jung, Junghyun Park, Kyung Seo Min, Yoo Kyung Chae, Jiyoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Its clinical complexity and resistance to therapy arise largely from the profound heterogeneity of its molecular signaling networks. Recent research has highlighted the significant role that microRNAs (miRNAs), small endogenous non-coding RNAs, play in the development and progression of lung cancer via diverse molecular mechanisms. miRNAs can act as either oncogenes, promoting cancer progression, or as tumor suppressors, inhibiting tumor growth. Their dysregulation has been closely linked to several fundamental characteristics of lung cancer, including sustained proliferation, immune evasion, resistance to programmed cell death, aggressive invasion and metastasis, angiogenesis, and metabolic reprogramming. Each of these features contributes significantly to the aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance of lung cancer. This review examines current insights into the roles of miRNAs in lung cancer, outlining the mechanisms by which miRNA-mediated signaling pathways drive tumorigenesis and influence the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, it explores the emerging potential of miRNAs in lung cancer diagnostics and therapeutics. By providing insights into the molecular functions of miRNAs, this review elucidates the promising prospects of miRNAs as biomarkers for early diagnosis and targets for novel therapeutic strategies in lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths globally in 2022 [2]. In Korea, lung cancer is consistently among the top causes of cancer mortality; projections for 2024 estimated it as the leading cause of cancer death, with 292,221 newly diagnosed cases and 83,770 related deaths overall nationwide [3]. Based on the microscopic appearance of tumor cells, lung cancer is classified into two major histological subtypes: non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). NSCLC is the most common type of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all cases, and is subdivided into adenocarcinoma (40%), squamous carcinoma (25%), and large-cell carcinoma (10%) [4]. Lung cancer has a complex and multifactorial etiology, and environmental factors such as exposure to tobacco smoke, radon, and other hazardous substances are commonly associated with its development [5]. Current diagnostic techniques include computed tomography (CT) scans and sputum cytology; however, despite advances in therapeutic strategies, the 5-year survival rate of patients with lung cancer remains poor at approximately 15% [1], primarily due to intratumoral heterogeneity, drug resistance, and limitations in early detection.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a central driver of lung cancer progression and therapeutic resistance. It is composed of stromal fibroblasts, endothelial and immune cells, extracellular-matrix (ECM) components, and soluble mediators that form a dynamic network that interacts with tumor cells [6]. Within that ecosystem, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) promote the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, and immune evasion [7]. Persistent inflammation, metabolic reprogramming, and hypoxia further reshape the TME in ways that promote tumor aggressiveness and reduce therapeutic efficiency [8–10]. Dysregulated TME tumor signaling has increasingly been linked to altered miRNA networks that fine-tune gene expression and modulate the hallmarks of malignancy. These interconnected processes of lung cancer complexity, TME composition, and aberrant miRNA expression provide a conceptual framework for understanding this disease (Fig. 1).

Emerging evidence suggests that miRNAs might be useful as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for lung cancer [11]. miRNAs are endogenous non-coding RNA molecules that are approximately 22 nucleotides in length and involved in metabolic activities such as cell growth, development, differentiation, and apoptosis. They function by forming complementary base pairs with mRNA molecules in the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) [12]. Aberrant miRNA expression levels have been reported in several malignancies, including lung cancer. miRNA dysregulation is widely implicated in lung cancer pathogenesis because it influences cell-cycle progression, apoptosis, migration, angiogenesis, and immune modulation [13]. Beyond their canonical regulatory roles, miRNAs also mediate intercellular communication within the TME via exosomes, orchestrating fibroblast activation, immune-cell polarization and ECM remodeling [14, 15]. Together, their dual functions position miRNAs as key molecular links between tumor-intrinsic signaling and microenvironmental adaptation.

In this review, we provide an integrative synthesis of miRNA-mediated regulation across key oncogenic signaling pathways in lung cancer, with emphasis on their roles in proliferation, apoptosis, EMT/metastasis, stress responses, and therapy resistance. We also highlight emerging translational opportunities, including their diagnostic potential, delivery strategies for miRNA-based therapeutics, and relevant clinical trials.

miRNAs in Lung Cancer Biology

Aberrant expression of miRNAs is closely linked to lung cancer development and progression [16]. By simultaneously regulating multiple oncogenes and tumor suppressors, miRNAs influence key biological processes such as uncontrolled proliferation, apoptosis evasion, the EMT, angiogenesis, and metastasis [17]. In NSCLC, oncogenic miRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-155 activate growth and survival pathways by inhibiting key tumor suppressors such as phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 [18, 19]. At the same time, tumor-suppressive miRNAs such as the let-7, miR-34a, and miR-200 families suppress critical oncogenic drivers such as Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2), and zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1/2 (ZEB1/2), thereby limiting invasion and metastasis [17, 20, 21]. Dysregulated miRNA networks also mediate communication between tumor cells and the microenvironment through exosomal transfer that promotes immune evasion and therapy resistance [22]. Collectively, these findings establish miRNAs as central regulators of lung cancer biology and promising diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Dual Roles of miRNAs in Lung Cancer

In cancer biology, miRNAs can act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors, depending on the molecular and cellular context. Their dual roles are governed by the cellular environment, oncogenic mutations, and interactions within the TME. For instance, miR-200c, classically an EMT suppressor through ZEB1/2 targeting, also interacts with KRAS-associated pathways and shows context-dependent effects on cell proliferation when its tumor-suppressive function is attenuated [23, 24]. The let-7 family, a prototypical tumor suppressor group, becomes functionally inert in LIN28-overexpressing cells due to suppression of its maturation, and that loss of function is reinforced in MYC-overexpressing cells through additional repression of let-7 biogenesis [25]. Likewise, miR-125b acts as a tumor suppressor by activating p53 signaling in certain NSCLC contexts and promotes cell survival via TP53INP1 downregulation in others [26, 27]. Recognizing these context-specific determinants explains why the same miRNA can appear to play opposing roles and is thus essential for pathway-based interpretation and rational therapeutic design in NSCLC.

miRNAs in Lung Cancer Signaling Pathways

miRNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and profoundly shape the intracellular signaling cascades that underlie the hallmarks of lung cancer [28]. By targeting kinases, phosphatases, transcription factors, and adaptor proteins, miRNAs can function as oncogenic drivers or tumor suppressors that modulate proliferation, survival, differentiation, and metastatic behavior in NSCLC [29, 30]. Dysregulated miRNA expression disturbs the balance of multiple signaling networks, often reprogramming cells toward malignant behavior and therapeutic resistance [31]. To clarify the functional landscape, these signaling networks can be broadly categorized into three major groups: (1) proliferation and survival pathways that promote tumor expansion and metabolic adaptation; (2) EMT, invasion, and metastasis pathways that confer invasive and self-renewal properties; and (3) apoptosis, stress, and differentiation pathways that regulate cell fate and responses to genotoxic or inflammatory stimuli. The following sections delineate how distinct miRNAs integrate within each of these networks to modulate lung cancer progression.

Pathways Regulating Proliferation and Survival

Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), particularly epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), sit at the apex of a signaling network that integrates extracellular growth cues into intracellular programs controlling proliferation, survival, and therapeutic response in NSCLC [32, 33]. Upon ligand binding, activated RTKs engage several major downstream cascades, most prominently the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [34], the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) pathway [35], and the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway [36]. miRNAs shape those signaling axes at multiple levels, either reinforcing oncogenic RTK output or restoring negative regulatory feedback [37, 38]. These interconnected RTK-driven pathways and their regulation by miRNAs are schematically summarized (Fig. 2). In the following subsections, we first outline the EGFR/RTK hub and then discuss how key downstream modules are regulated by miRNAs in NSCLC.

EGFR/RTK signaling hub

EGFR and RTKs function as upstream hubs that couple extracellular ligands to intracellular oncogenic cascades such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK/ERK, and JAK/STAT in NSCLC [35]. Upon ligand binding, EGFR dimerizes and undergoes autophosphorylation, triggering a network of downstream pathways that collectively promote proliferation, angiogenesis, survival, and therapeutic resistance.

miRNAs act as fine-tuning regulators of this axis by targeting EGFR itself, adaptor proteins, or downstream effectors [38]. Among tumor-suppressive miRNAs, miR-34a inhibits EGFR translation, thereby restraining proliferation and metastasis [39]. Likewise, miR-128b downregulates EGFR expression, and its loss correlates with a poor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) response [40]. miR-145 and miR-218-5p also suppress EGFR and inhibit PI3K/AKT/ERK signaling, reducing invasion and enhancing TKI sensitivity [38, 41].

Conversely, oncogenic miRNAs amplify EGFR-driven signaling. miR-21, which is induced by EGFR/STAT3 activity, promotes proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis [42]. miR-27a is upregulated in EGFR-mutant NSCLC and serves as a circulating biomarker of EGFR activity [43]. In addition, miR-200c mediates RTK/ALK/EGFR crosstalk, regulating the EMT and drug sensitivity [44].

Collectively, those studies reveal that dysregulated miRNA expression reshapes the EGFR/RTK signaling dynamics that control tumor growth, metastasis, and TKI resistance. However, the upstream mechanisms that regulate miRNA expression in response to EGFR activation or therapeutic pressure remain poorly characterized. Emerging evidence suggests that miRNAs such as miR-200c integrate EGFR with parallel RTK networks, modulating the EMT and therapy outcomes. Therapeutic strategies that restore tumor-suppressive miRNAs or inhibit oncomiRs could normalize EGFR-RTK signaling and improve outcomes in EGFR-driven lung cancer.

PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway

Downstream of activated RTKs such as EGFR, the PI3K/AKT axis plays a central role in controlling cell proliferation and survival in NSCLC [35]. When RTKs are activated by ligand binding, PI3K is recruited and catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol-(4,5)-bisphosphate (PIP₂) into phosphatidylinositol-(3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP₃), which leads to the activation of AKT at the plasma membrane [45]. Activated AKT phosphorylates a wide range of substrates that promote cell-cycle progression and anti-apoptotic signaling, including the glycogen synthase kinase-3β, mTOR, and Forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factors [45].

The tumor suppressor PTEN, a lipid phosphatase, counteracts AKT activation by dephosphorylating PIP₃, thereby restraining its proliferative and survival signaling [38]. However, PTEN is frequently downregulated by oncogenic miRNAs in NSCLC. miR-21 [46], miR-155 [19], miR-92a [47], miR-425-5p [48], miR-103a-3p [49], miR-200c-3p [50], miR-1297 [51], and miR-4286 [52] directly inhibit PTEN, leading to sustained AKT phosphorylation, enhanced proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis. Similarly, miR-196a [53] and miR-629 [54] suppress FOXO1 (a downstream effector of AKT that mediates apoptotic and cell-cycle checkpoint control), thereby promoting tumor cell survival. In addition, miR-382-3p downregulation increases SUMO1 activating enzyme subunit 1 expression, which promotes AKT SUMOylation and phosphorylation, further enhancing tumorigenic potential [55].

The AKT/FOXO axis plays a critical role in regulating apoptosis, oxidative stress defense, and metabolic adaptation. AKT phosphorylates FOXO1/FOXO3 and drives their nuclear export, which silences FOXO-dependent transcriptional programs such as apoptosis, antioxidant defense, and DNA repair [45]. The loss or inactivation of FOXO1/3 is associated with chemoresistance and poor prognosis in NSCLC [56, 57]. miR-629 and miR-31-3p both downregulate FOXO1 in NSCLC, promoting proliferation, invasion, and survival [58, 59], and miR-21-3p inhibits FOXO3a, thereby reducing apoptosis and promoting cisplatin and docetaxel resistance [60]. These findings indicate that miRNA-mediated repression of FOXO transcription factors amplifies the oncogenic output of the PI3K/AKT pathway and supports cellular stress adaptation in lung cancer.

In contrast, several miRNAs suppress AKT signaling and act as tumor suppressors. miR-448 targets EPHA7 [61] and miR-186-3p targets kinesin family member 2 C [62], and they thereby inhibit NSCLC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. miR-125b downregulation suppresses phosphorylated AKT expression, inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis [63]. Similarly, miR-20a-5p [64], miR-548 L [65], miR-181-5p [66], miR-126 [67], miR-30b-5p [68], miR-373-3P [69], and miR-494-3p [70] target key regulators of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade in ways that reduce tumor growth and metastasis.

In addition, several miRNAs link AKT signaling to cellular metabolism. miR-199a-5p inhibits the glucose transporter GLUT1 (SLC2A1) in NSCLC, thereby limiting glucose uptake and metabolic flux [71]. Similarly, miR-449a directly targets lactate dehydrogenase A and thus suppresses glycolysis [72]. Conversely, miR-214 is upregulated in EGFR-mutant NSCLC and promotes glycolysis via PTEN downregulation and AKT signaling activation, suggesting that it has a role in metabolic reprogramming and therapeutic resistance [73]. Altogether, many studies characterize miRNAs that activate AKT signaling as oncogenic; however, the cooperative or compensatory regulation of multiple miRNAs converging on the same nodes (such as PTEN, FOXO, and mTOR) remains poorly understood.

MAPK/ERK signaling

The MAPK/ERK (RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK) cascade is another major effector branch engaged by RTKs and oncogenic RAS/RAF alterations in NSCLC. It acts as a central driver of proliferation, survival, and transcriptional reprogramming, culminating in ERK-dependent gene expression linked to tumor progression and drug resistance [74–76].

Multiple miRNAs modulate this axis at distinct nodes. The tumor-suppressive miR-7 directly targets focal adhesion kinase (FAK), thereby attenuating EGFR/FAK–ERK signaling and inhibiting proliferation and migration in NSCLC models [77, 78]. miR-145-5p directly targets neuroblastoma RAS viral oncogene homolog (NRAS), reversing gefitinib resistance and dampening downstream ERK signaling in EGFR-TKI–resistant NSCLC cells [79]. miR-1258 inhibits the growth factor receptor–bound protein 2(GRB2)/RAS/ERK pathway, thereby reducing the growth and invasion of NSCLC cells [80]. miR-143-3p targets the KRAS network; restoration of miR-143 suppresses ERK signaling and cell proliferation in lung cancer models, supporting its relevance in KRAS-driven NSCLC [81, 82].

In addition to proliferation, ERK signaling regulates apoptosis and therapy response. For instance, miR-34a downregulates BCL-2 and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1, thereby inducing apoptosis and inhibiting proliferation in NSCLC cells [83]. Conversely, miR-21 enhances MEK/ERK signaling and promotes aggressive phenotypes and chemoresistance in NSCLC, complementing its oncogenic role in the PI3K/AKT pathway [42].

Those observations indicate that miRNAs within the ERK pathway have context-dependent functions that attenuate or amplify MAPK signaling, depending on the genetic background and therapeutic context. For example, KRAS-mutant NSCLC often displays suppression of the miR-143/145 cluster and overexpression of miR-21, which can collectively sustain ERK activation [84, 85]. However, the upstream regulatory triggers that control miRNA expression in response to MAPK feedback remain poorly understood.

JAK/STAT signaling pathway

The JAK/STAT cascade integrates cytokine and RTK inputs to regulate proliferation, survival, immune evasion, and therapy resistance in NSCLC; activated JAKs phosphorylate STATs, which dimerize and translocate to the nucleus to drive oncogenic programs (including MYC, BCL-XL, and CCND1) [86, 87].

Multiple miRNAs fine-tune that axis at distinct nodes. For instance, tumor-suppressive miR-618 directly restrains the Janus Kinase 2 (JAK2)/STAT3 pathway to inhibit proliferation, invasion, and migration, and it is downregulated in NSCLC [88]. Moreover, miR-449b-3p targets interleukin 6 (IL-6), blunting the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 loop and suppressing the EMT and invasion [89], and miR-548d-3p promotes pyroptosis via a JAK2/STAT3/NLRP3 inflammasome axis, linking STAT3 control to inflammatory cell death in NSCLC [90]. In addition, miR-153 downregulates PRDM2 and impairs NSCLC growth through JAK/STAT modulation [91], and miR-218 functions as a tumor suppressor by targeting IL-6/STAT3 components (IL6R, JAK3), so it correlates with improved prognosis [92]. miR-224-5p targets IL6ST to reduce JAK2/STAT3 activity and suppress NSCLC phenotypes, highlighting receptor-level control of the pathway [93], and miR-608 lowers BRD4 and decreases phosphorylated JAK2 and STAT3, limiting growth and invasion [94]. Furthermore, miR-196b-5p activates a FAS-NF-κB, IL-6-STAT3 feed-forward loop that drives NSCLC progression, and miR-135b forms a STAT3/miR-135b/NF-κB circuit that confers aggressiveness and poor prognosis [95, 96]. Collectively, these data define a miRNA layer that either restrains or amplifies JAK/STAT signaling in NSCLC, illustrating the context-dependent balance between cytokine-driven oncogenesis and immune regulation. However, the mechanisms by which external stresses modulate JAK/STAT-associated miRNA expression remain insufficiently understood.

Pathways Regulating the EMT, Invasion, and Metastasis

The transition from localized tumor growth to invasive and metastatic disease in NSCLC is driven by signaling pathways that reprogram epithelial cells toward mesenchymal, migratory, and stem-like phenotypes [97]. Core developmental regulators, including Notch, Wingless-related integration site (Wnt)/β-catenin, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/SMAD, Hedgehog, and Hippo, coordinate cell–cell communication, polarity, cytoskeletal dynamics, and ECM remodeling to promote the EMT and metastatic progression [98]. miRNAs fine-tune these pathways at multiple nodes, acting either to enforce EMT programs or preserve epithelial identity and suppress invasion [99]. The interplay between these EMT-associated pathways and their miRNA regulators is illustrated (Fig. 3). In the following subsections, we outline the major EMT-regulatory pathways and detail how miRNAs modulate their activity to shape metastatic behavior and therapeutic responsiveness in NSCLC.

Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway is a conserved cell–cell communication mechanism critical for tissue homeostasis, EMT regulation, and the maintenance of cancer stemness. Canonical activation occurs when ligands such as Jagged1/2 or DLL1/4 bind to Notch receptors (NOTCH1–4), triggering γ-secretase–mediated cleavage and release of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). The NICD then translocates to the nucleus to activate transcription of canonical downstream genes, including HES and HEY family members. Aberrant Notch activation has been detected in approximately one-third of NSCLC cases and is associated with poor prognosis, EMT induction, and metastasis [100–102].

Aberrant Notch activation promotes the EMT and metastasis in NSCLC by upregulating EMT-inducing transcription factors (e.g., SNAI1, ZEB1) and enhancing tumor stemness. miRNAs are key modulators of this pathway [103]. The miR-200 family, a well-known EMT suppressor, targets Jagged1 and thereby disrupts Notch signaling to inhibit invasion and migration [104]. miR-582-5p suppresses NSCLC proliferation by directly targeting NOTCH1, leading to reduced NICD levels and downstream signaling inhibition [105]. Conversely, oncogenic miR-1275 activates Notch signaling by targeting NUMB, a Notch antagonist that promotes NOTCH1 degradation [106]. Its upregulation stabilizes NICD and reinforces Notch-dependent stemness in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). miR-4428 enhances the expression of NOTCH2, further amplifying Notch signaling and driving NSCLC progression [107]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Notch-associated miRNAs orchestrate the EMT, invasion, and stem-like phenotypes in NSCLC. However, the upstream signals that regulate these Notch-modulating miRNAs remain poorly defined.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling

Canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling promotes the EMT, metastatic spread, and cancer stemness in NSCLC by stabilizing cytoplasmic β-catenin, enabling its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation of EMT- and stemness-associated genes [108]. As a developmental pathway frequently hijacked by lung tumors, Wnt/β-catenin integrates extracellular Wnt ligands, Frizzled (FZD)/LRP receptor activation, and Dishevelled (DVL)-mediated signaling to drive epithelial plasticity, invasion, and therapeutic resistance. At the post-transcriptional level, multiple miRNAs fine-tune this axis. The miR-200 family constrains the EMT and Wnt output in lung cancer by repressing ZEB1/2 and directly targeting CTNNB1 (β-catenin) [109]. miR-29b acts downstream of noncanonical Wnt7a/FZD9 signaling and mediates tumor-suppressive effects in NSCLC models [110]. miR-374a displays context-dependent behavior across NSCLC lines: it can activate β-catenin and RAS signaling via PTEN suppression or restrain tumor growth by targeting CCND1 in other contexts, demonstrating molecular background specificity [111]. miR-1 suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signaling by targeting FAM83A, thereby reducing β-catenin activity and lung cancer cell proliferation [112]. miR-214 directly binds the 3′-UTR of CTNNB1 and inhibits DVL1-mediated activation, thereby attenuating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in NSCLC [113]. Furthermore, miR-1275 enhances Wnt/β-catenin and Notch to increase LUAD stemness, and antagonizing miR-1275 or blocking Wnt/Notch reverses those effects [114]. However, how β-catenin regulates miRNAs during the EMT remains poorly understood. Together, these data define a miRNA circuitry that either amplifies or restrains Wnt/β-catenin-driven EMT and stemness in NSCLC, highlighting the tractability of these regulatory nodes for biomarker development and anti-metastatic strategies.

TGF-β/SMAD signaling

The TGF-β signaling pathway is a pivotal regulator of the EMT, invasion, and metastatic dissemination in NSCLC. In its canonical form, ligand binding to the TGF-β type II receptor (TGFBR2) recruits and phosphorylates the type I receptor (TGFBR1/ALK5), which in turn activates the receptor-regulated small mothers against decapentaplegic homolog (SMAD) 2 and SMAD3. These phosphorylated SMADs associate with the common mediator SMAD4 and translocate into the nucleus, where they orchestrate transcriptional programs that repress epithelial markers (e.g., CDH1) and induce mesenchymal genes (SNAI1, ZEB1/2, VIM, TWIST) [115], thereby driving the EMT and enhancing metastatic potential.

miRNAs act as potent modulators of TGF-β/SMAD signaling at multiple regulatory levels. The oncomiR miR-21-5p reinforces TGF-β/SMAD signaling by directly targeting SMAD7, a negative feedback regulator of the TGF-β receptor complex, thereby promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion in NSCLC cells [116]. In contrast, the miR-200 family establishes a feedback loop with the TGF-β–ZEB axis to preserve epithelial identity; TGF-β-driven EMT suppresses miR-200 expression, unleashing ZEB1/2 and reinforcing mesenchymal traits in lung cancer [109, 117]. Additional tumor-suppressive miRNAs restrain TGF-β responses in NSCLC: miR-145 and miR-497 block TGF-β-induced EMT by targeting metadherin and attenuating SMAD signaling, and miR-145 has also been reported to repress SMAD3/TGFBR2, limiting invasion [118, 119]. miR-940 directly targets SNAI1, inhibiting TGF-β-induced EMT and invasion in NSCLC cells [120]. The miR-134/487b/655 cluster counteracts TGF-β-induced EMT and gefitinib resistance in LUAD by targeting MAGI2, highlighting a miRNA strategy for resensitizing EGFR-mutant tumors [121]. Likewise, miR-17 family members can suppress TGFBR2, mitigating TGF-β signaling and reversing cisplatin resistance in NSCLC models [122]. Recently, miR-422a has emerged as another suppressor; it attenuates TGF-β/SMAD signaling by downregulating SULF2, thereby impairing proliferation, invasion, and the EMT of NSCLC cells [123]. In contrast, miR-520e defines a negative feedback loop in which TGF-β/SMAD activation induces miR-520e, which in turn suppresses TGFBR2, thus limiting metastatic potential [124]. Overall, these miRNAs either reinforce or restrain TGF-β/SMAD-driven EMT; however, how TGF-β dynamically regulates miRNA expression and how these miRNAs interface with other EMT-associated pathways remains incompletely understood.

Hedgehog signaling pathway

The Hedgehog pathway is crucial for the development of organs, including the brain, gastrointestinal tract, and lungs [125–127]. This pathway is activated when Hedgehog proteins bind to the tumor suppressor Patched 1 (PTCH1), relieving its inhibition of Smoothened (SMO). Active SMO inhibits the Suppressor of fused, which normally sequesters the transcription factor glioma-associated oncogene homolog (GLI). Once released, GLI translocates into the nucleus and activates genes involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Abnormal activation of the Hedgehog pathway has been reported to contribute to NSCLC progression and is associated with poor prognosis [128]. In recent years, growing evidence has linked aberrant Hedgehog signaling to the EMT, metastasis, and the maintenance of cancer stem-like cells in NSCLC. The activation of GLI1 transcriptional activity enhances the expression of EMT markers such as N-cadherin, vimentin, and SNAIL and represses E-cadherin, thereby promoting invasion and metastatic dissemination [129].

Given the molecular significance of the Hedgehog pathway in lung cancer, the transcriptional regulation of miRNAs has been extensively explored. For example, miR-101-3p [130] and miR-212 [131] promote NSCLC cell growth via PTCH1 suppression. In addition, miR-520b directly targets speckle-type BTB/POZ protein, which stabilizes GLI2 and activates the Hedgehog pathway in NSCLC [132]. In contrast, miR-182-5p and miR-183-5p act as tumor suppressors by targeting GLI2 and GLI1, respectively, thereby inhibiting the Hedgehog pathway and reducing the EMT and metastatic potential [133]. Overall, miRNAs modulate Hedgehog signaling at both the receptor and GLI levels, thereby influencing the EMT and stemness; however, how TGF-β/Wnt crosstalk and GLI-dependent feedback loops shape miRNA expression remains unclear.

Hippo signaling pathway

The Hippo signaling pathway is a major oncogenic pathway that plays a critical role in the development and progression of various cancers [134]. When activated, the upstream mammalian sterile 20-related 1 and 2 kinases, large tumor suppressor 1 and 2 kinases (LATS1/LATS2), and Yes-associated protein/Transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (YAP/TAZ) proteins are sequentially phosphorylated. Phosphorylated YAP/TAZ recruit 14-3-3 proteins, which promote YAP/TAZ retention in the cytoplasm or their degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system [135]. Conversely, upon Hippo pathway inactivation, unphosphorylated YAP/TAZ localize to the nucleus, where they regulate target genes required for cell proliferation, migration, and survival [136].

Deregulation of the Hippo/YAP axis not only drives tumor initiation but also mediates chemoresistance, stemness, and immune evasion in lung cancer. Tumor-suppressive miRNAs such as miR-497 and miR-497-5p directly target YAP1 (and its transcription partner TEAD1), thereby inhibiting proliferation and reducing cisplatin resistance in NSCLC cells [137, 138]. In contrast, the oncogenic miR-130a forms a positive feedback loop with YAP (because YAP induces miR-130a expression) that represses the TEAD inhibitor Vestigial-like4 (VGLL4), further amplifying YAP-TEAD oncogenic transcription [139, 140]. Loss of VGLL4, a known tumor suppressor, unleashes YAP activity and upregulates programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), linking the Hippo pathway to immune evasion [141]. In contrast, miR-375 suppresses YAP1 and restrains tumor growth and neuroendocrine differentiation in specific lung cancer subtypes [142, 143]. Collectively, these examples illustrate a context-dependent balance between YAP-activating and YAP-suppressive miRNAs that is influenced by factors such as tumor subtype and VGLL4 expression status.

Beyond proliferation control, dysregulated Hippo signaling influences diverse malignant phenotypes in NSCLC, including the EMT, cancer stemness, metabolic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance. Aberrant YAP1/TAZ activation promotes nuclear localization and transcriptional upregulation of EMT-inducing transcription factors such as SLUG and ZEB1, leading to E-cadherin suppression and enhanced invasive potential [144, 145]. In addition, YAP1 enhances cancer stem-like traits by upregulating pluripotency factors, including SOX2 and OCT4, and thereby contributing to tumor recurrence and therapeutic resistance [146].

miRNA-mediated modulation further shapes these processes. miR-25 targets LATS2, promoting NSCLC migration and invasion, whereas miR-21 silences KIBRA, an upstream activator of LATS1/2 [147, 148]. miR-183-5p directly suppresses LATS1 [149], and reduced miR-103a upregulates the deubiquitinase OTUB1, stabilizing cytosolic YAP [150]. Although OTUB2 has also been recognized as a YAP/TAZ activator [151, 152], its regulation by miRNAs remains unknown. Additionally, miR-582-5p [153] and miR-214-3p [154] directly inhibit YAP, reducing NSCLC proliferation. Overall, miRNA dysregulation critically shapes YAP/TAZ-driven EMT, stemness, and therapy resistance, underscoring opportunities to restore Hippo pathway balance in NSCLC.

Pathways Regulating Apoptosis, Stress, and Differentiation

Cell-fate control in NSCLC is governed not only by proliferative signaling but also by pathways that regulate apoptosis, cellular stress responses, and cellular differentiation [155]. Key transcriptional nodes such as p53 and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) integrate genotoxic stress, inflammatory cues, and metabolic imbalance to determine whether a cell will repair its damage, undergo programmed death, or adopt a pro-survival or stem-like state [156, 157]. miRNAs intricately modulate these pathways by fine-tuning the expression of core regulators, amplifying oncogenic stress signals, or restoring tumor-suppressive responses [16, 158]. The p53 and NF-κB signaling pathways and their regulation by miRNAs are depicted (Fig. 4). In the following subsections, we highlight how miRNAs interface with the p53 and NF-κB pathways to shape apoptosis, stress adaptation, therapeutic response, and differentiation programs in NSCLC.

p53 signaling pathway

The p53 signaling pathway serves as the central guardian of genomic stability, orchestrating cellular responses to DNA damage, oncogene activation, and oxidative stress [159]. Upon activation by the upstream ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) and ATM- and Rad3-related (ATR) kinases, p53 induces the transcription of genes involved in cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, senescence, and apoptosis, thereby preventing the propagation of damaged DNA [160]. In NSCLC, frequent TP53 mutation or murine double mutant 2 (MDM2) amplification disrupts this protective mechanism, facilitating uncontrolled proliferation and resistance to apoptosis [161].

miRNAs impose an additional post-transcriptional layer of control on the p53 axis, functioning either as repressors or enhancers of its activity. Among oncogenic miRNAs, miR-125b directly binds to the 3′-UTR of TP53 mRNA, suppressing its translation and dampening apoptosis, which in turn promotes NSCLC growth and metastasis [162]. Similarly, miR-504 targets TP53, blunting p53-dependent transcription of p53-upregulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA) and Bcl-2–associated X (BAX) protein and reducing DNA-damage sensitivity [163]. miR-150 has also been reported to inhibit p53 expression and attenuate p53-mediated G1 arrest [164]. Another repressor, miR-122-5p, is markedly upregulated in A549 cells and correlates inversely with p53 levels, leading to diminished control of the p53-regulated mevalonate pathway and enhanced cell proliferation and migration [165].

Conversely, tumor-suppressive miRNAs reinforce p53 signaling. The miR-34 family, particularly miR-34a, is a direct transcriptional target of wild-type p53; its restoration induces apoptosis and senescence via the p53/miR-34a/MYCN axis and enhances cisplatin sensitivity in NSCLC [166, 167]. Likewise, miR-7 acts as a positive regulator of p53 by suppressing AKT3, which otherwise activates MDM2-mediated p53 degradation, thereby stabilizing p53 and promoting apoptosis [168]. On the other hand, miR-138 has been implicated in modulating the p53–growth arrest–DNA-damage-inducible protein 45 alpha (GADD45A) axis and thereby fine-tuning DNA-damage repair [169].

Overall, these findings reveal a bidirectional and context-dependent miRNA–p53 regulatory network, in which the same miRNA can act as either a suppressor or activator depending on TP53 status, stress intensity, and co-altered pathways such as PI3K/AKT. However, the upstream cues driving miRNA dysregulation in response to genotoxic stress and the feedback loops between mutant p53 and oncogenic miRNAs remain incompletely defined. Clarifying how p53-regulated miRNAs interact with parallel tumor suppressors (e.g., p63/p73) and DNA-repair networks is an important direction for future research. Restoring p53 activity through miRNA-based approaches, such as miRNA mimics (e.g., miR-34a) or inhibitors of p53-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., anti-miR-125b) might synergize with chemotherapy or MDM2 blockade, offering therapeutic potential for both TP53-wild-type and mutant NSCLC.

NF-κB signaling pathway

The NF-κB pathway is a master regulator of inflammatory and stress responses, linking chronic inflammation to tumor survival, angiogenesis, and metastasis in NSCLC [170]. In resting conditions, NF-κB dimers are sequestered in the cytoplasm by IκB proteins [171]. Upon stimulation by cytokines, growth factors, or oncogenic stress, activation of the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, particularly IKKβ, leads to IκB phosphorylation and degradation, and that allows p65/p50 nuclear translocation and the transcription of genes involved in cell survival, the EMT, and anti-apoptotic signaling [172, 173].

Aberrant NF-κB activation is frequently observed in NSCLC and correlates with resistance to chemotherapy and EGFR-TKIs. miRNAs have emerged as key post-transcriptional regulators of this pathway. Among them, miR-155, itself transcriptionally induced by NF-κB, forms a positive feedback loop by targeting FOXO3a, thereby sustaining NF-κB activity, promoting cancer stem-like properties, and inducing gefitinib resistance [174]. Similarly, miR-21, an NF-κB-responsive oncomiR, is upregulated in NSCLC, binds PTEN, and thereby activates AKT/NF-κB signaling, promoting proliferation, migration, and the EMT [175].

Other oncogenic miRNAs further amplify NF-κB signaling. miR-135b is significantly upregulated in NSCLC and directly targets the deubiquitinase CYLD, a negative regulator of NF-κB, thereby enhancing NF-κB activity and establishing a feed-forward loop with IL-6/STAT3 signaling [96]. Likewise, miR-1307-5p targets TRAF3, another inhibitory adaptor of NF-κB/MAPK, thus engaging the NF-κB/MAPK cascade to promote proliferation and invasion in LUAD [176]. In addition, miR-196b-5p directly represses NF-Kappa-B Inhibitor Alpha (NFKBIA), the gene encoding Inhibitor of κB alpha (IκBα), resulting in constitutive NF-κB activation, IL-6 secretion, and NF-κB/STAT3 cross-talk in NSCLC [177].

Other miRNAs act as negative regulators of the pathway. miR-146a-5p directly suppresses TRAF6, modulating NF-κB/p65 phosphorylation and cell migration [178], and miR-146b-5p targets IRAK1, attenuates NF-κB activation, and restores EGFR-TKI sensitivity [179]. Additional tumor-suppressive miRNAs, such as miR-148b and miR-218, further restrain NF-κB signaling: miR-148b represses activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule [180], limiting NF-κB-driven invasion, and miR-218 decreases p65 phosphorylation and glycolytic activity, thereby suppressing the EMT and metastatic behavior [181].

Collectively, miRNAs establish a dual regulatory network within the NF-κB pathway, where oncomiRs such as miR-155, miR-21, and miR-135b reinforce inflammatory and survival signaling, and suppressive miRNAs such as miR-146a/b and miR-218 attenuate that axis and restore drug sensitivity. The balance between these activities is shaped by the tumor subtype, cytokine environment, and TKI exposure, underscoring the complexity of NF-κB regulation in NSCLC. However, the upstream inflammatory and epigenetic cues that modulate NF-κB-responsive miRNAs and their reciprocal feedback with pathways such as STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK remain poorly understood. Restoring NF-κB-inhibitory miRNAs or blocking NF-κB-amplifying miRNAs might resensitize resistant NSCLC to EGFR-TKIs and immunotherapy.

miRNAs and the Tumor Microenvironment

The TME has been described as a central regulator of lung carcinogenesis, controlling the sequence of tumor progression and shaping the therapeutic response [182]. The TME consists of cancer cells and non-transformed stromal cells, including fibroblasts and endothelial cells, immune cells, the ECM, nerves, and vessels. Notably, stromal cells such as CAFs, TAMs, and TILs play key roles in orchestrating tumor growth, immune evasion, the EMT, and therapy resistance [183]. Key stromal components and their associated miRNAs within the TME are summarized (Fig. 5).

miRNAs and Cancer-associated Fibroblasts

CAFs are among the most common and important stromal cells in the TME [184]. They are also a key part of the tumor mesenchyme, providing structural support and actively regulating tumor behavior by promoting or restraining tumor growth. CAFs are involved in proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis [185]. Dysregulated miRNAs in CAFs are predictors of tumor progression [186].

For example, miR-21 upregulation drives the transdifferentiation of lung fibroblasts into CAFs, thereby enhancing NSCLC progression [187]. Tumor-derived exosomal miR-1247-3p activates CAFs, thereby increasing lung metastasis [188]. miR-210 functions as a proangiogenic factor in CAFs from patients with NSCLC [189]. Exosomal miR-1290 induced by COX-2 overexpression promotes CAF activation and tumor progression via the CUL3-NRF2 pathway in LUAD [190]. In NSCLC, CAF-derived exosomal miR-210 is taken up by tumor cells, targets the RNA surveillance factor UPF1, and suppresses PTEN, thereby activating the PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis and the EMT to drive migration and invasion [191]. Likewise, CAF-derived exosomal miR-20a directly represses PTEN in recipient NSCLC cells, enhancing PI3K/AKT signaling and cisplatin resistance both in vitro and in xenograft models [192]. During radiotherapy stress, CAF-derived exosomal miR-196a-5p targets NFKBIA (IκBα) to activate NF-κB signaling, leading to increased DNA damage repair and radioresistance [193]. Additionally, miR-196a activates CAFs that assist lung cancer cells in migrating and invading neighboring tissues [194]. Moreover, loss of miR-1 in CAFs elevates stromal cell–derived factor 1α secretion, which activates CXCR4, NF-κB, and Bcl-xL in tumor cells, fostering proliferation and chemoresistance.

Collectively, CAF-derived miRNAs remodel the NSCLC microenvironment by coordinating PI3K/AKT activation, relieving NF-κB repression, and driving the EMT, ultimately promoting invasion, therapy resistance, and aggressive tumor phenotypes.

miRNAs and Tumor-associated Macrophages

TAMs are a major component of the TME and play a pivotal role in lung cancer progression. They create a local milieu that facilitates cancer cell proliferation during tumor growth and metastasis [195]. TAMs are also key mediators of angiogenesis, metastasis, chemotherapy resistance, and immune evasion [196]. Increasing evidence shows that miRNAs regulate TAM phenotypes and functions in lung cancer.

For example, miR-17 and 20a were reported to target hypoxia-inducible factor 2α and thereby modulate the proangiogenic function of TAMs [197]. In high-risk heavy smokers, miR-320a promotes an M2-like pro-tumorigenic phenotype by downregulating STAT4 [198]. M2 macrophage–derived exosomal miR-501-3p promotes lung cancer by targeting WDR82 [199]. M2 macrophage–derived exosomal miR-942 targets FOXO1, promoting metastasis and angiogenesis [200]. Additionally, lung cancer cells have high levels of miR-375, which can regulate the expression of CCL2 by stimulating the recruitment of macrophages [201].

Exosomes from hypoxic LUAD cells are enriched in miR-1290, which induces M2 macrophage polarization by targeting suppressor of cytokine signaling 3(SOCS3) to enhance LUAD progression [202]. When those exosomes are transferred to macrophages, the miR-1290 upregulates SOCS3 expression, thereby driving polarization toward the M2 phenotype [203]. Likewise, elevated miR-21 levels in hypoxic environments promote M2 macrophage polarization and drive lung cancer progression by targeting interferon regulatory factor 1 [202]. Exosomes from irradiated lung cancer cells induce macrophage proliferation, activation, and M2 polarization, facilitated by miR-4655-5p, which targets MID1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase [204]. In addition, circPVT1 (an oncogenic non-coding RNA) in lung cancer–derived exosomes promotes M2 macrophage polarization through the miR-124-3p/enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) axis, thereby enhancing lung cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [205].

Overall, these miRNAs orchestrate TAM polarization toward a pro-tumorigenic M2 state, reinforcing inflammation, immune suppression, angiogenesis, and metastatic behavior in NSCLC.

miRNAs and Tumor-infiltrating Lymphocytes

TILs are a critical component of the adaptive immune response within the TME [206]. These immune cells, which are subdivided into CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, migrate into the tumor stroma and can have antitumor effects. Their presence often correlates with a favorable prognosis and improved response to immunotherapy. For instance, miR-142-5p inhibits the tumorigenic effects of CD4+ T cells on NSCLC cells by decreasing the expression of PTEN and PD-L1 [207]. An integrated analysis of lung cancer–related data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) revealed that miR-195 suppresses LUAD by regulating CD4+ T cell activation via coiled-coil domain containing 88 C, a member of the hook-related protein family involved in regulating the Wnt signaling pathway [208]. Similarly, miR-15b inhibits the activation of CD8+ T lymphocytes by suppressing the production of IL-2 and interferon-gamma in mice with Lewis lung carcinoma [209]. The miR-183/96/182 cluster regulates the expression of FOXF2 and ZEB1, thereby inducing an IL-2-mediated antitumor CD8+ cytotoxic T cell response that inhibits cancer progression and metastasis in LUAD [210]. LINC01798-mediated miR-17-5p downregulates ITGA8 in LUAD, resulting in reduced infiltration of both CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells [211], and miR-130a also enhances the cytotoxic ability of NK cells against NSCLC by targeting STAT3 [212]. However, whether these stromal miRNAs have cell-type-specific effects remains unclear. Further studies are needed to identify and evaluate cell-type-specific TME miRNAs and develop TME miRNA–mediated immunotherapy for patients with lung cancer.

miRNAs in the diagnosis of lung cancer

Early and accurate diagnosis is essential for improving the prognosis of lung cancer, which often remains asymptomatic until advanced stages [213, 214]. The tumor–node–metastasis classification system describes the anatomical extent of malignancy and categorizes NSCLC into four stages (stages I–IV) based on tumor size and invasion, regional lymph-node involvement, and the presence of distant metastasis. In stage I, cancer cells are confined to lung tissues, but in stage II, cancer cells can be found in the lymph nodes on the perimeter of the lung. By stage III, cancer cells can be found in lymph nodes farther away from the lung, possibly in the middle of the chest, and in stage IV, lung cancer is characterized by the spread of cancer to both lungs, the area around the lungs, and distal parts of the body.

According to findings from the National Lung Screening Trial, high-risk patients who underwent a CT screening showed a 20% lower mortality from lung cancer [215]. However, the most significant limitation of screening using CT scans is a high false-positive rate, which leads to unnecessary follow-up CT scans or invasive lung biopsies [216]. A conceptual comparison highlights the differences between CT-based screening and blood-based miRNA diagnostics (Fig. 6).

To address the limitations of CT, circulating miRNAs have emerged as promising minimally invasive biomarkers that can complement imaging-based screening. Several miRNAs have been evaluated as diagnostic tools for distinguishing early-stage from advanced-stage NSCLC (Table 1). Because no single miRNA panel currently offers both high sensitivity and specificity, a combined diagnostic strategy — using miRNA signatures as a first-line screen before CT — might improve accuracy and reduce unnecessary imaging. For example, integrating baseline blood miRNAs with LDCT has been investigated for its potential to predict lung-cancer risk and personalize screening intervals [217].

Recent research has unveiled a new diagnostic model for detecting fucosylation modifications of extracellular vesicle miRNAs, instead of using reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Aberrant glycosylation, particularly fucosylation modifications, has been observed in cancerous tissues and the extracellular vesicles of patients with malignancies. An electrochemical biosensor, which captures fucose using lentil lectin-coated magnetic beads, identified fucosylated extracellular vesicle miR-4732-5p as a promising biomarker for the early diagnosis of LUAD [218].

Notably, many circulating miRNAs are encapsulated within exosomes, which preserve their stability and enhance their diagnostic utility. These vesicle-bound miRNAs not only modulate the TME but also serve as stable, minimally invasive biomarkers. For instance, exosomal miR-378, miR-382, and miR-1246 have shown potential in distinguishing NSCLC patients from healthy controls, highlighting their dual diagnostic and functional relevance [219–221].

Role of miRNAs in lung cancer therapy

miRNAs play diverse and clinically significant roles in shaping therapeutic responses in NSCLC. They modulate drug sensitivity, regulate DNA damage repair, influence immune evasion, and help determine whether tumor cells undergo apoptosis or resist treatment. The involvement of miRNAs across chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and emerging delivery platforms is outlined (Fig. 7). Below, we summarize their involvement in major treatment modalities and highlight emerging therapeutic delivery systems.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy remains a first-line treatment for advanced or metastatic NSCLC; however, the development of chemoresistance significantly limits its efficacy [222]. miRNAs contribute to both resistance and sensitization by targeting genes involved in apoptosis, drug efflux, DNA repair, autophagy, and survival pathways [223].

For example, the loss of miR-424-3p promotes chemoresistance by targeting YAP in NSCLC [224], and miR-146a induces resistance by targeting C/EBP homologous protein in lung cancer [225]. Serum levels of miR-106a have also been proposed as predictive biomarkers of cisplatin resistance [226]. miR‑328 promotes cisplatin resistance in NSCLC by targeting PTEN [227], whereas miR-326 suppresses SIRT1, inhibiting proliferation and promoting apoptosis of chemotherapy-resistant NSCLC cells [228]. In LUAD, miR-1275 was found to target PROCA1, thereby inducing cell apoptosis and cisplatin chemosensitivity [229]. In addition to chemotherapy, miRNAs also contribute to drug resistance in targeted therapies. For instance, miR-21 promotes resistance to EGFR TKIs by downregulating PTEN and activating AKT signaling, and miR-125b suppresses the pro-apoptotic protein BAK1, thereby reducing chemotherapy-induced cell death [230]. On the other hand, it has been reported that miRNAs are not only able to induce, but also increase chemosensitivity. For example, miR-185-5p modulates the chemosensitivity of human NSCLC to cisplatin by targeting ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 1 [231]. miRNA-1 and miR-106b have been also reported to increase the chemosensitivity of NSCLC to cisplatin [232]. miR-503 makes cancer cells more sensitive to chemotherapy by targeting several proteins involved in drug resistance, such as multidrug resistance protein 1, multidrug resistance-associated protein 1, excision repair cross-complementation group 1, survivin, and Bcl-2 [233], and miR-26a-5p enhances NSCLC cell sensitivity to paclitaxel [234]. Furthermore, targeting of the Notch pathway by miR-140-3p enhanced the sensitivity of LUAD cells to multiple antitumor agents [235]. Moreover, miR-326 inhibits the proliferation of chemotherapy-resistant NSCLC cells and promotes their apoptosis by suppressing SIRT1 [228]. Recent evidence indicates that autophagy is essential to addressing chemoresistance. For example, overexpressing miR-873-5p enhanced NSCLC cell sensitivity to gefitinib by inducing autophagy [236]. On the other hand, autophagy regulation by miR-199a-5p was suggested as a potential mechanism of cisplatin resistance in SCLC [237]. Furthermore, combining miRNA mimics with ferroptosis inducers has enhanced antitumor efficacy in xenograft models [238].

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is a widely used cancer treatment that destroys tumor cells with ionizing radiation. This treatment modality is common for NSCLC, either as a primary option for early-stage, inoperable tumors or in combination with chemotherapy for locally advanced stages to enhance tumor control [239]. Several miRNAs have been shown to modulate radiosensitivity by regulating DNA damage response and repair pathways. The expression of miR-328-3p improves the radiosensitivity of cells by altering DNA damage/repair signaling pathways in NSCLC [240]. Similarly, impaired DNA damage response and repair signaling was also observed with miR-384-mediated radiosensitization of NSCLC [241]. miR-144-5p and miR-519a increase the radiosensitivity of NSCLC by targeting EPH receptor A2 and activating transcription factor 2, respectively [242, 243]. miR-27a enhances radiosensitivity by inhibiting ZEB1 expression and facilitating DNA damage repair signaling [244]. miR-125a-5p also improves radiosensitivity by targeting sirtuin 7 (SIRT7) in NSCLC cells [245]. Exosomal miR-101-3p from bone marrow stem cells promoted radiotherapy in NSCLC cancer through EZH2, which manages DNA repair and autophagy [246]. miR-145 modulates radiosensitivity in NSCLC cells by suppressing Tropomodulin 3 (TMOD3), a member of an actin filament pointed-end capping protein family [247]. Conversely, some miRNAs have been associated with radioresistance. miR-21 has been reported to trigger resistance to radiotherapy in NSCLC [248], and miR-181a has also been reported to reduce the radiosensitivity of NSCLC by targeting PTEN [249]. Moreover, several circulating miRNAs have been identified as potential predictive biomarkers for radiotherapy response in NSCLC. Plasma levels of miR-98-5p, miR-302e, miR-495-3p, and miR-613 have been associated with treatment outcomes [250]. In addition, baseline extracellular vesicle miR-30c, together with autophagy-activated circulating tumor cells, have demonstrated promising potential in predicting chemoradiotherapy resistance and outcomes in NSCLC patients [251]. miR-384 enhances the radiosensitivity of NSCLC cells by impairing DNA damage repair through direct targeting of ATM, Ku70, and Ku80, leading to increased cell death and reduced G2/M-phase arrest.

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy uses the body’s own immune system to recognize and eliminate cancer cells. In recent years, immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of lung cancer through the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors [252]. Among them, programmed death 1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 has become a popular target for immunotherapeutic blockade of co-inhibitory immune pathways. PD-1, a negative regulatory receptor expressed on activated T cell surfaces, interacts with PD-L1 or PD-L2, which dampens cytotoxic T cell responses and thus helps tumor cells to evade T cell cytolysis, promoting tumorigenesis [253]. Recent studies have unveiled the pivotal role of miRNAs as regulators and therapeutic modulators targeting PD-L1. For example, miR-140 [254], miR-155-5p [255], miR-138-5p, miR-200c [190], and miR-34a [256] directly target PD-L1, enhancing T cell function as tumor suppressors and inhibiting NSCLC proliferation. In addition, miRNAs have shown their potential as predictive biomarkers of immunotherapy efficacy in NSCLC patients with high PD-L1 expression [257]. Despite those advances, the extensive mechanistic and clinical roles of miRNAs in immunotherapy remain inadequately defined and require further investigation.

Delivery systems for miRNA therapeutics: current challenges and advances

Despite the promising efficacy of miRNA mimics and inhibitors, their clinical translation remains limited by delivery-related barriers, including rapid degradation, off-target effects, and immune activation. The first-in-human miRNA mimic, MRX34 (liposomal miR-34a), demonstrated systemic target engagement in patients with solid tumors, including NSCLC. However, its phase I trial was terminated due to immune-related adverse events, underscoring the limitations of lipid nanoparticle–based delivery systems [258, 259]. To improve safety and tumor selectivity, targeted bacterial minicells (TargomiRs) were developed to deliver a miR-16 mimic to EGFR-positive tumors; they have shown manageable toxicity and early clinical activity in mesothelioma and lung cancer [260, 261]. Exosome-based vectors have emerged as a biocompatible alternative. Engineered or mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes provide good stability and immune evasion and efficient gene silencing in NSCLC models, although large-scale manufacturing and precise biodistribution control remain major hurdles [262–264]. In parallel, inhalable formulations, including nano-in-microparticle aerosols and gold-nanoparticle carriers, enable localized pulmonary delivery of miRNA therapeutics, improving airway bioavailability while minimizing systemic toxicity [265, 266]. Nanocarrier design, exosomal delivery, and lung-targeted administration are pivotal steps toward meeting the current stability and immunogenicity challenges, and they could ultimately enhance the clinical feasibility of miRNA-based therapies for NSCLC.

miRNAs in lung cancer animal models, clinical trials, and genetic polymorphism

Understanding the clinical and translational relevance of miRNAs in lung cancer requires comprehensive evaluations across multiple research platforms. From in vivo animal models to early-phase clinical trials and population-level genetic studies, miRNAs have been investigated as both functional regulators of tumor biology and potential therapeutic tools. These diverse approaches help bridge the gap between mechanistic insights and practical applications in the diagnosis, prognostication, and treatment of lung cancer.

In preclinical studies exploring miRNA-mediated therapeutics, diverse lung cancer animal models have been used to evaluate therapeutic efficiency and molecular mechanisms. For instance, the intranasal administration of let-7 miRNAs reduced tumorigenesis in the lungs of mice carrying a G12D-activating mutation of the KRAS oncogene [267]. In addition to transgenic mouse models, 4-(N-methyl-N-nitrosamine)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (NNK), a tobacco smoke carcinogen, has also been used to induce lung cancer in mice [268]. The mice with NNK-induced lung cancer exhibited high expression of miRNAs, including miR-144-30, miR-20a-5p, miR-29c-3p, let-7d-3p, and miR-328-3p, that was reversed by licochalcone A treatment. In Lewis lung carcinoma, which spontaneously developed as an epidermoid carcinoma in the lungs of C57BL mice, miR-21 was highly expressed via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, compared with controls [269]. Environmental exposure also regulates miRNAs; for instance, mice exposed to particulate matter < 2.5 μm for 8 weeks demonstrated dysregulation of lung-cancer-associated miRNAs [270].

First-in-human testing has confirmed that systemically delivered miRNA therapeutics can reach tumors and modulate molecular targets in patients. The liposomal miR-34a mimic MRX34 demonstrated clear on-target activity, but its phase I trial was terminated due to immune-mediated adverse events, despite steroid premedication, highlighting the need for safer delivery platforms [258, 259]. In contrast, TargomiR, an EGFR-targeted bacterial minicell delivering a miR-16 mimic, showed manageable toxicity and preliminary activity in thoracic cancers, supporting receptor-guided delivery strategies applicable to NSCLC [271, 272]. Beyond those early efforts, a first-in-human study of INT-1B3, a lipid-nanoparticle-formulated miR-193a-3p mimic, is ongoing [273], with preclinical data demonstrating immunogenic-cell-death signatures and durable antitumor immunity [274].

Looking forward, three translational directions are most promising: (i) tumor-targeted or local pulmonary delivery to maximize intrapulmonary exposure while minimizing systemic toxicity; (ii) exosome-based or MSC-derived vesicles that improve stability and immune stealth but require scalable manufacturing; and (iii) rational miRNA drug combinations such as pairing miR-21 inhibitors with EGFR-TKIs or a PD-(L)1 blockade guided by circulating miRNA biomarkers to identify responsive patients [263, 274]. Together, these findings highlight how preclinical and clinical miRNA research is converging toward translational applications that range from biomarker discovery to therapeutic delivery.

Moreover, it has been reported that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of pre-miRNAs contribute to genetic susceptibility to lung cancer. For example, miR-146a rs2910164C > G and miR-23 rs6505162C > A polymorphisms correlate with the risk of lung cancer, whereas miR-196a2, miR-30c-1, miR-608, and miR-27a SNPs were not associated with lung cancer in female non-smokers [275]. The miR-605 rs2043556 polymorphism was also associated with the risk of lung squamous cell carcinoma, but not with LUAD, in female non-smokers [276]. Continued integration of preclinical, clinical, and genomic data will be pivotal for realizing the full therapeutic potential of miRNAs in lung cancer.

Conclusions: challenges and future perspectives

miRNAs play multifaceted roles in lung cancer, influencing key processes such as tumor progression, metastasis, therapy resistance, and diagnostic potential. Throughout this review, we have highlighted how miRNAs intersect with major signaling pathways, shape the tumor microenvironment, and contribute to both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive programs. Their ability to regulate numerous targets and participate in complex RNA–RNA regulatory networks emphasizes their importance and reveals the challenge of defining their functional specificity.

Despite growing insights, significant challenges remain. The heterogeneity of miRNA expression across patients and lung cancer subtypes, the context-dependency of miRNA activity, and the widespread presence of competing endogenous RNA interactions continue to complicate both mechanistic interpretation and therapeutic exploitation. Additionally, the dual roles exhibited by certain miRNAs and the broad spectrum of their targets present obstacles for developing selective and safe therapeutic strategies.

Future studies should focus on defining the molecular principles that govern miRNA target selection, clarifying how cellular and microenvironmental contexts influence miRNA function, and improving strategies for precise and context-dependent miRNA modulation. Notably, emerging single-cell and spatial omics technologies offer powerful platforms for resolving cell-type-specific and spatially restricted miRNA regulatory networks within heterogeneous lung tumors and their microenvironments. Integrating single-cell small RNA sequencing with transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic profiling will enable the dissection of context-dependent miRNA–target interactions across tumor, immune, and stromal compartments that cannot be captured by bulk analyses [277, 278]. Spatially resolved analyses further allow mapping of miRNA regulatory activity within distinct tumor niches, such as invasive fronts or immune-excluded regions, providing mechanistic insights into miRNA-driven tumor progression and therapy resistance. Coupling these technologies with functional perturbation platforms, including CRISPR-based miRNA modulation and patient-derived organoid or xenograft models, will be essential for validating causal miRNA functions and prioritizing clinically actionable targets. Advancing delivery systems, refining biomarker discovery, and integrating miRNA profiles with genomic and clinical data will further support the translation of miRNA research into diagnostic and therapeutic applications. Beyond continued efforts in mechanistic research, technological innovation, and translational studies will also be critical for advancing miRNA-based diagnostics and therapies for lung cancer (Fig. 8). For instance, future studies should prioritize the clinical translation of miRNA discoveries, with promising directions including the development of tumor-selective delivery systems (e.g., lipid nanoparticles or exosome-based carriers), validation of circulating miRNAs as minimally invasive biomarkers for early detection and therapy monitoring, and integration of miRNA signatures with genomic data to guide precision stratification of NSCLC patients. Moreover, combining miRNA modulation with existing therapies, such as miR-21 inhibition with EGFR-TKIs or a PD-(L)1 blockade, could overcome resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy.

In summary, understanding miRNA mechanisms in lung cancer is essential to unlocking their full clinical potential. These efforts will bridge mechanistic insight with clinical translation, accelerating the development of next-generation RNA-based therapeutics for lung cancer.
