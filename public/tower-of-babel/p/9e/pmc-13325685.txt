How to facilitate ultrasound examination of the fetal heart: the 5‐4‐3‐2‐1 method
Authors: M Levy, B Stos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe propose a new standardized, systematic method of fetal cardiac screening, the step‐by‐step ‘5‐4‐3‐2‐1’ method. This method is based on understanding the cardiac structures through a process of navigating between the different recommended views during an abdominothoracic sweep, following a user‐friendly checklist to identify the main ultrasound features associated with congenital heart defects (CHD). The components (or ‘points’) that form the checklist in each of the views have been selected in order to cover most CHD, with particular focus on abnormalities associated with genetic disorders and critical CHD. This easy‐to‐remember 5‐4‐3‐2‐1 method provides an overview of the fetal heart at the start of the abdominothoracic sweep by checking 5 points, then assesses the four‐chamber view checking 4 points, the three‐vessel and three‐vessel‐and‐trachea views checking 3 points and the two outflow tracts checking 2 points, and then uses color Doppler to check 1 final point. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Congenital heart defects (CHD) are the most common congenital malformations, with an incidence of about 8 per 1000 live births. After an initial improvement in the rate of prenatal detection of CHD following the introduction of fetal echocardiography in the 1980s 1 , 2 , 3 , the detection rate has now stagnated at around 50%, despite efforts to improve it 4 , 5 . Many infants are born with undiagnosed CHD, despite having undergone at least two screening ultrasound scans at around 22 and 32 weeks' gestation, and despite the evolution of ultrasound guidelines. This can have serious postnatal consequences, especially for infants whose cardiac defects require rapid postnatal management to minimize potential adverse hemodynamic and neurological sequelae. Several studies have demonstrated that a significant number of prenatally undiagnosed cases of CHD are due to one of two main issues: either the ultrasound images are of poor quality or the defect is not recognized by the sonographer, despite good ultrasound views 6 , 7 .

Herein, we propose a new standardized, systematic method of fetal cardiac screening, the step‐by‐step ‘5‐4‐3‐2‐1’ method. This method is based on a sequential process, navigating between the different recommended views during an abdominothoracic sweep, to assess the cardiac structures and to obtain a three‐dimensional appreciation of the heart and great vessels. Following an easy‐to‐remember checklist, it focuses on identifying the main ultrasound features associated with CHD, using the specific cardiac views that are recommended in the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) fetal cardiac screening guidelines 8 . The components (or ‘points’) that form the checklist in each of the views have been selected with the aim of screening for the majority of internationally classified heart abnormalities 9 , with particular focus on abnormalities associated with chromosomal or genetic disorders that indicate invasive testing for array comparative genomic hybridization, and on abnormalities that necessitate urgent cardiac management of the neonate at birth.

This easy‐to‐remember 5‐4‐3‐2‐1 method provides an overview of the fetal heart at the start of the abdominothoracic sweep by checking 5 points, then assesses the four‐chamber view (4CV) checking 4 points, the three‐vessel (3V) and three‐vessel‐and‐trachea (3VT) views checking 3 points and the two outflow tracts checking 2 points, and then uses color Doppler to check 1 final point.

PRACTICAL POINTS

Technique (Figure 1, Videoclip S1)

To obtain the recommended views for fetal cardiac screening using the 5‐4‐3‐2‐1 method, an abdominothoracic sweep is performed in a transverse plane, tilting the probe while holding it in the same position on the maternal abdomen. The sweep starts from the standard transverse abdominal view at the level of the abdominal‐circumference measurement and then continues towards the fetal head, passing through the four‐chamber view and then the three‐vessel (3V) and three‐vessel‐and‐trachea (3VT) views. Dotted lines in the 3V/3VT view show expected linear alignment of vessels (straight line) and of anteroposterior midline (from spine to sternum) (curved line). Starting from the four‐chamber view, the two outflow‐tract views are obtained by slight sliding and rotating of the probe 45° in one direction, depending on fetal position, to visualize the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), then back to the four‐chamber view, with rotation of the probe 45° in the opposite direction to visualize the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). The analysis of the fetal heart begins in a standard transverse abdominal view at the level of the abdominal‐circumference measurement. An abdominothoracic sweep towards the fetal head is then performed, by tilting the probe while holding it in the same position on the maternal abdomen to maintain the same transverse plane, passing through the 4CV and on to the 3V and 3VT views. In larger fetuses, it may be necessary to slide the transducer slightly towards the fetal head to accomplish this. Returning to the 4CV, the probe is then rotated 45° in one direction and then moved back through the 4CV and rotated 45° in the other direction, to obtain the two outflow‐tract views. Color Doppler is then used for one final check in the 3VT view. In each of the views, a number of different points are checked (Figure 1). The slightest anomaly in any of these points may indicate CHD (Table 1).

Overview of the heart: 5 points to check

The first point in the checklist, overview point 1, is assessed in a standard transverse abdominal view at the level of the abdominal‐circumference measurement, to visualize the position of the inferior vena cava (IVC). The probe is then swept towards the fetal head, maintaining the same transverse plane, until the 4CV is achieved, in order to assess the 4 remaining points in the overview.

Point 1. Situs: inferior vena cava situated in front of and to the right of the aorta

The IVC should be seen in front of and to the right of the aorta (Ao). Sweeping the probe towards the head of the fetus makes it possible to follow the IVC until it joins the atrium on the right‐hand side of the atrial septum. If the IVC is indeed situated to the right of and in front of the Ao in the abdomen, this indicates situs solitus, i.e. the situs is normal. If the IVC is located to the left of the Ao, this indicates either isolated situs inversus or heterotaxy. If there is a double‐vessel sign, with a binocular appearance of the IVC in front of the spine, it may be difficult to identify the Ao and the venous vessel; this can also be a sign of heterotaxy.

Point 2. Position of the aorta: situated to the left of the spine

In the transverse abdominal plane, the Ao should be the only vessel seen in front of the spine and it should be situated to the left of the spine. When sweeping towards the fetal head, into the thorax, the Ao should maintain this position. Once the 4CV has been visualized, the Ao should be seen situated behind the left atrium and in front of and to the left of the spine. If it is located to the right of the spine, this indicates a right aortic arch, which can be associated with conotruncal anomalies; this should prompt genetic analysis (for 22q11 deletion).

Point 3. Apical orientation and position of the heart in the thorax: to the left

In the 4CV, the position of the heart in the thorax should be one‐third on the right and two‐thirds on the left. If a line is drawn in the sagittal plane from the spine to the sternum, the apex of the heart should be oriented to the left side, at an angle of between 30° and 60° relative to this imaginary line. An angle > 60° could indicate a conotruncal anomaly or a deviation of the heart due to extracardiac anomalies. If the apical orientation and position of the heart is to the right, this indicates meso‐ or dextropositioning, which may be associated with situs inversus or heterotaxia.

Point 4. Rhythm of the heart: 110–180 bpm

The fetal heart should beat with a regular rhythm but a variable rate of around 150 bpm. Bradycardia may occur occasionally but should always be transient. A fetal heart rate above 180 bpm is a tachycardia and a heart rate below 110 bpm is a bradycardia, both of which should prompt referral to a fetal cardiologist.

Point 5. Thoracic and cardiac size: heart area 30% of thoracic area

In the 4CV, the heart should occupy about 30% of the thoracic cross‐sectional area on visual estimation. If needed, this can be confirmed by measurement. Enlargement of the heart is observed in various conditions, such as fetal hemodynamic disorders, valvular anomalies and myocardial dysfunction.

The mnemonic ‘SPORT’ can be used to remember the 5 points in the overview (Situs, Position, Orientation, Rhythm, Thoracic and cardiac size).

Four‐chamber view: 4 points to check

Maintaining the 4CV, the 4 points to be evaluated in this view can then be assessed.

Point 1. Similar size of right and left chambers

In the 4CV, all four chambers should be visualized. In the second trimester, the right and left chambers should be similar in size. In the third trimester, the chambers on the right side should be slightly larger than the corresponding chambers on the left side. Asymmetry of the chambers in the second trimester is always pathological.

Point 2. Offset tricuspid‐valve and mitral‐valve insertion

The opening of the mitral and tricuspid valves should be visualized. The valves should not be inserted at the same level on the septum. The tricuspid valve should follow on from the right atrium (RA) and should be situated closer to the apex of the heart, while the mitral valve, following on from the left atrium (LA), should be closer to the base of the heart. This offset of the valves may be subtle but its confirmation is important. The absence of offset of the atrioventricular valves can reveal an atrioventricular septal defect, often associated with aneuploidy. Inversion of the offset indicates an atrioventricular discordance or corrected transposition of the great arteries (TGA).

Point 3. Cardiac crux integrity

The septum should be continuous from the apex to the base of the heart, except at the level of the foramen ovale, which is located in the center of the interatrial septum. The foramen ovale flap (septum primum) should open into the LA (and close the foramen ovale after birth). A defect at the cardiac crux (ostium primum atrial septal defect and/or inlet ventricular septal defect) indicates an atrioventricular septal defect, often associated with aneuploidy.

Point 4. Pulmonary vein connection with left atrium: flattened contour

During fetal heart development, the pulmonary veins should be incorporated into the posterior wall of the LA, giving it a smooth, flattened or even mildly concave appearance. On ultrasound, this contour of the LA posterior wall may resemble the horns of a bull or a ‘mortarboard’ graduation hat. Assessment of this contour on ultrasound is more sensitive than is color Doppler imaging, which can be misleading as the color flow may appear normal even when the veins connect abnormally, or conversely the color flow may appear abnormal despite normal connections. A LA shape that is rounded, rather than flattened, can indicate a total anomalous pulmonary venous return (TAPVR); this is important to identify because of its severe neonatal impact. An increased distance between the Ao and the LA may also be a sign of TAPVR, owing to the presence of the abnormal confluence of the pulmonary veins, although this sign appears to be less specific compared with the contour at the connection of the pulmonary veins with the LA.

Three‐vessel and three‐vessel‐and‐trachea views: 3 points to check

From the 4CV, a continuation of the sweep towards the fetal head will allow visualization of the 3V and 3VT views.

Point 1. Number of vessels: three

In the 3V view, only three vessels should be visible: from left to right and from front to back, they are the pulmonary artery (PA), the Ao and the superior vena cava (SVC). The presence of fewer than three vessels is due to the absence or abnormal position of an artery. The presence of a fourth vessel on the left side may be due to isolated persistence of the left SVC or TAPVR.

Point 2. Size of vessels: pulmonary artery and aorta similar in second trimester and pulmonary artery slightly larger in third trimester

In the second trimester of pregnancy, the PA and Ao should be approximately equal in diameter. In the third trimester, the PA should be slightly larger than the Ao. The ratio of the diameter of the PA to that of the Ao should remain < 1.5. A larger discrepancy in size can suggest a future coarctation or aortic stenosis if Ao < PA, or pulmonary hypoplasia or tetralogy of Fallot if PA < Ao. The PA should never be smaller than the Ao.

Point 3. Position: vessels aligned linearly and V‐shape of aortic and ductal arch confluence when sweeping to three‐vessel‐and‐trachea view

The PA should originate anteriorly from the right ventricle (RV), the Ao should originate from the center of the heart from the left ventricle and the SVC should enter the RA posteriorly. This normal arrangement of the three vessels gives the appearance of a linear alignment from left to right and from front to back: the PA, then the Ao and then the SVC. In the 3V/3VT view sweep, ductal and aortic arches should converge, with a V‐shaped appearance at their confluence, situated to the left of the trachea. Absence of a linear alignment may indicate an anomaly of arterial position (TGA). A U‐shaped arch confluence indicates a right aortic arch, possibly associated with a conotruncal anomaly or vascular ring.

Two outflow tracts: 2 points to check

The views of the right and left ventricular outflow tracts are obtained by returning to the 4CV and sliding and rotating the probe depending on the fetal position. If the heart is viewed from an apical position, the outflow tracts are visualized by starting from the 4CV and rotating the probe 45° in one direction, and then returning to the 4CV and rotating the probe 45° in the opposite direction, as the two outflow tracts are orthogonal. When the 4CV is visualized with the apex positioned laterally, the outflow tracts are seen by sliding and tilting the probe towards the head of the fetus.

Point 1. Left ventricular outflow tract: subaortic septal continuity

The interventricular septum should be seen to be intact up to the level of the aortic valve (in a normal arrangement of the great vessels). An interventricular septal defect leading to septoaortic discontinuity indicates a conotruncal defect.

Point 2. Right ventricular outflow tract: bifurcation of pulmonary artery (right ventricle–pulmonary artery concordance)

The artery emerging anteriorly from the RV in the right‐ventricular‐outflow‐tract view can be confirmed as the PA if it bifurcates after a short distance. Generally, the PA can be visualized dividing into the right PA and the ductus arteriosus, which directs blood towards the Ao. The left PA is more difficult to visualize owing to its orientation, although, in certain cases, it is possible to see the trifurcation of the PA into the left and right pulmonary branches and ductus arteriosus. Absence of bifurcation in the right‐ventricular‐outflow‐tract view may indicate that the artery is instead an Ao, as in TGA.

Color Doppler: 1 final point to check

Point 1. Direction of arterial flow at level of ductal and aortic arches: antegrade

Color Doppler can be used in each view but its interpretation can be difficult, which may lead to misinterpretation. For this reason, we recommend its use at least in the 3VT view to check the direction of arterial flow through the ductal and aortic arches. There should be antegrade flow of blood in both the Ao and PA. Reversed flow in an artery indicates proximal obstruction and retrograde filling of the artery by the ductus arteriosus, which will need to be maintained open after birth to avoid neonatal compromise.

Conclusion

Herein, we have presented an easy‐to‐remember method to establish, quickly but thoroughly, whether the fetal heart appears normal. The slightest anomaly in any of the points described in this 5‐4‐3‐2‐1 method may indicate CHD and should lead to referral to a fetal cardiologist.
