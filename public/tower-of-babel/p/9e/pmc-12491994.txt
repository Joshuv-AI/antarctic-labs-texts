Validity and Reliability of Bengali Language Shoulder Pain and Disability Index Version on Patients With Frozen Shoulder at a Rehabilitation Centre in Bangladesh: A Validation Study
Authors: Abid Hasan Khan, Md Saruar Hossain Bhuiyan, H M Al‐Amin, Rajib Sutradhar, Md Sherajul Haque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe shoulder pain and disability index (SPADI) is a commonly utilized outcome measure. The study aimed to evaluate the reliability and validity of the Bengali version of the SPADI among patients with frozen shoulder.MethodsThe SPADI was administered to 103 patients with frozen shoulder receiving physiotherapy at Centre for the rehabilitation of the paralyzed (CRP), a well‐known rehabilitation hospital in Bangladesh. Among these patients, 31 underwent a retest after 15 days. To evaluate the construct validity of the SPADI, the scores were compared with other outcome measures, including the disabilities of the arm, shoulder, and hand questionnaire (DASH), the numerical pain rating scale (NPRS), and the 36‐item short form health survey (SF‐36). Structural validity was assessed using a two‐factor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Additionally, the study analyzed internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and measurement error.ResultsThis study validated the Bengali version of the SPADI for frozen shoulder (FS). It showed strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (pain α = 0.842, disability α = 0.878), and reliability (ICC > 0.98). A confirmatory factor analysis confirmed a two‐factor structure (CFI = 0.985). Findings highlight SPADI's value in FS assessment while emphasizing its limitations in capturing broader quality‐of‐life impacts.ConclusionThe Bengali version of SPADI showed good reliability and validity in a group of patients with frozen shoulder. This means that it is a dependable and accurate tool for measuring shoulder pain and disability in these patients.Keywords: Bengali SPADI, frozen shoulder, patient‐reported outcomes, shoulder pain, validation study

Introduction

Frozen shoulder (FS), also known as frozen shoulder, is typically marked by intense shoulder pain and limited movement in both active and passive shoulder motions [1]. These limitations are often not visible in X‐rays of the glenohumeral joint, and there is no definitive diagnostic test for this condition [2]. Clinically, diagnosis should include restricted range of motion (ROM) in different movement planes, particularly in external rotation at various degrees of shoulder abduction [3], and a normal shoulder X‐ray to rule out other pathologies [4]. Delays in diagnosing FS, unclear prognosis, and the absence of a standardized rehabilitation program can increase patient frustration and worsen the sociocultural impact of the condition [5]. FS affects many aspects of daily life, including leisure activities and work, requiring individuals to adapt their routines to manage the associated disabilities [6, 7].

To build an effective therapeutic alliance, it's crucial to use clear, evidence‐based information. This enables the creation of a personalized rehabilitation program that addresses the patient's specific needs and concerns. Health indicators can help bridge the gap between clinicians' and patients' perceptions of disabling conditions [8]. The patient reported outcome measures (PROM) system [9], which gauges patients' views on their health status, clinical outcomes, mobility, and quality of life [10], can systematically capture patients' perspectives, improving communication and patient management through a tailored care approach [11].

One study that evaluated the responsiveness of the shoulder pain and disability index (SPADI) among patients with FS found the SPADI to be a suitable PROM for this population [10]. The SPADI, a self‐administered tool, comprises 13 items divided into two subscales: five for pain and eight for disability, addressing the primary concerns of FS patients [8]. It is practical and quick to complete, taking less than 5 min, and is easy for clinicians to score [12]. SPADI has demonstrated excellent reliability and construct validity, especially in primary care settings for shoulder pain [13]. Despite being widely used to assess pain and disability in FS patients, lacks specific scientific validation in this group [14]. Previous research has shown that the original English SPADI is feasible for clinical use and highly responsive in assessing shoulder pain and function [15]. Furthermore, a clinical practice guideline linked to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health from the Orthopedic Section of the American Physical Therapy Association states that the use of SPADI as a PROM in patients with Frozen Shoulder has a Grade A recommendation [16]. Therefore, this study aims to examine the reliability and validity of the Bengali version of SPADI in a sample of Bengali patients with FS.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

It was a validation study where 103 patients were recruited through convenience sampling as recommended by Terwee et al. [17] at the Centre for the rehabilitation of the paralyzed (CRP) in Bangladesh over 5 months from October 2024 to May 2025. To be included in the study, participants had to be over 18‐years old and diagnosed with FS by orthopedic surgeons and physiotherapists [18, 19]. The diagnosis criteria included a gradual and progressive onset of shoulder pain, restricted active and passive ROM in multiple planes, sleep‐disturbing night pain, and/or a firm end‐feel at the end ranges of movements [13], particularly in external rotation at varying degrees of shoulder abduction, for at least 1 month [3] along with a normal shoulder radiograph [4, 20]. Exclusion criteria involved the presence of red flags (such as neoplasia, fractures, or glenohumeral dislocation) [21], secondary stiff shoulder (as per the ISAKOS 2015 classification) [22], history of trauma, cognitive impairments, or inability to understand the Bengali language.

Procedure

The names and contact details of patients diagnosed with FS were retrieved from outpatient department records using the patient data management system (PDMS) at the CRP. This dataset served as the population frame. Individuals identified as eligible were contacted by telephone to provide information about the study and were invited to participate. Each patient completed the SPADI alongside the DASH, NPRS, and SF‐36 questionnaires. Additionally, clinical and demographic information, including age and gender, was collected during the recruitment phase through structured interviews conducted by trained evaluators. A total of five (05) evaluators with previous experience working as outcome evaluators in various health projects were trained before the evaluation of the participants about the information collection methods and the structured questionnaire. During the recruitment period, patients did not receive any new or ongoing treatment interventions related to FS. This was confirmed through patient self‐report during the telephone screening, where participants were asked about recent physiotherapy, pharmacological management, or other therapeutic modalities. Only those who reported no active treatment were invited to participate.

Outcome Measures

The study included two measurement time points to assess test–retest reliability of the questionnaire. The first screening occurred during the initial screening after telephone invitation, while the second was conducted 15 days later. This interval served as the washout period, minimizing recall bias while preserving the clinical stability of symptoms, which is crucial when assessing reliability. The 15‐day duration was selected based on prior reliability studies [13] and was considered sufficient to avoid learning effects, without allowing for significant clinical change.

Shoulder and Pain Disability Index (SPADI)

The Bangla version of the SPADI used in this study was cross‐culturally adapted and validated by Rahman et al. [23]. The SPADI has been applied to assess shoulder dysfunction in various contexts, including patients with neck cancer [6], those who have undergone shoulder surgery for anterior instability [24], and individuals with nonspecific shoulder pain [25]. Before completing the questionnaire, patients were instructed to mark their responses on a visual analog scale (VAS) for each of the 13 items [26]. The scores for each subscale were summed and converted into a score out of 100, with higher scores indicating greater pain and disability [27]. Patients could mark only one item per subscale as “not applicable,” which would then be omitted from the total score. If more than two items were marked as “not applicable,” no score was calculated [15]. Previous studies have reported a minimal detectable change (MDC) of 17 points for the SPADI in patients with FS [13] and 18 points for the German version of SPADI in patients post shoulder arthroplasty [28].

Disability of Arm, Shoulder and Hand Questionnaire (DASH)

The DASH questionnaire is used to evaluate the ability of the upper extremities to perform daily activities, regardless of the specific musculoskeletal issue. It consists of 30 items that assess disability and symptoms, focusing on the difficulty in performing physical, social, and work‐related activities and the impact on sleep and the patient's perception of their upper extremity problems [29]. Patients rate their difficulty on a five‐point Likert scale, from “no difficulty or no symptoms” (score of 1) to “unable to perform the activity or severe symptoms” (score of 5). The DASH score ranges from 0 (no disability) to 100 (severe disability) and cannot be calculated if more than three items are missing [30].

The study utilized the English version of the DASH Questionnaire, which was translated into Bangla by an expert translator to ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness for the target population. This approach is supported by the Institute for Work & Health, which recommends that when a formally validated version is unavailable, translation by qualified professionals is a valid and acceptable method for maintaining the integrity of patient‐reported outcome measures [31].

Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NPRS)

The numerical pain rating scale (NPRS) is an 11‐point measure where patients rate their pain from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). The NPRS has demonstrated good responsiveness for shoulder pain and was used in this study to assess patients' pain perception over the past week [32].

36‑Item Short Form Health Survey Questionnaire (SF‑36)

The SF‐36 is a questionnaire used to evaluate health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) [33]. It measures eight domains: physical functioning (PF), role physical (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role emotional (RE), and mental health (MH). The SF‐36 has proven effective in assessing patients with various shoulder disorders [34]. In this study, the validated Bengali version was used [35].

The ceiling and floor effects of measurement tools were not assessed in our study because of the assumption that the selected outcome measures were adequately calibrated for the target population and study objectives. While we recognize that overlooking these effects may limit interpretability in certain contexts, our approach aligns with findings from Šimkovic and Träuble [36], who emphasized that the impact of ceiling and floor effects becomes critical primarily when measurement tools are poorly matched to the sample or when extreme clustering of scores is observed. In our study, no such clustering was evident during preliminary data checks, and the measurement tools demonstrated acceptable distribution characteristics.

Statistical Analysis

This study adhered to the COSMIN initiative's guidelines for evaluating the psychometric properties of SPADI [37]. Structural validity was assessed through a two‐factors confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [38] using three key indicators [39]: the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) (both ≥0.95), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (≤0.06), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) (≤0.08). Reliability was examined as internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and measurement error, analyzed separately for the Pain and Disability subscales.

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha (α), with recommended values between 0.70 and 0.95 [40], α if an item was deleted (expected to be below the total Cronbach's alpha), and items to total correlation (Spearman's ϱ, values ≥0.40 considered satisfactory) [41].

Test–retest reliability was measured using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2,1) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), requiring a minimal ICC value >0.75 for group reliability and >0.90 for excellent individual clinical measurement reliability [42]. Measurement error was assessed using the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the MDC [43]. The SEM was determined using the formula: SD × √(1 − ICC), where SD represents the pooled standard deviation of the measurements, and ICC refers to the test–retest reliability coefficient. The MDC was then calculated by multiplying the SEM by 1.96 (the z‐score for a 95% confidence level) and by the square root of 2 [44].

The outcome measures examined the validity of two constructs: pain (SPADI pain subscale score correlated with NPRS) and disability (SPADI disability subscale score correlated with DASH) [13]. Construct validity was tested through a‐priori hypothesis using Spearman's rank correlation (rs) coefficients between SPADI and DASH, NPRS, and SF‐36, with cut‐offs for strong (rs > 0.60), moderate (0.30 < rs < 0.60), and weak (rs < 0.30) correlations [45, 46]. Validity was considered satisfactory (≥75% of expected hypotheses fulfilled), moderate (<75% but ≥50%), or low (<50%) [47].

An a priori hypothesis testing was performed based on the anticipated correlations for construct validity, evaluating the following set of predefined assumptions [13]:

For pain subscale: 1. The SPADI Pain subscale showed a strong positive correlation (> 0.60) with NPRS scores, reflecting their shared focus on pain intensity;2. The SPADI Pain subscale showed a strong positive correlation (> 0.60) with the DASH total scores;3. The SPADI Pain subscale showed a negative correlation (> 0.30) with the physical functioning (PF) subscale of the SF‐36, consistent with the understanding that shoulder pain adversely affects overall physical health status;4. The SPADI Pain subscale exhibited a negative correlation (> 0.30 and < 0.60) with the bodily pain (BP) subscale of the SF‐36, reflecting the overlap in their pain‐related constructs. For disability subscale: 1. The SPADI Disability subscale validated a strong positive correlation (> 0.60) with the DASH total scores;2. The SPADI Disability subscale demonstrated a moderate positive correlation (> 0.30 and < 0.60) with NPRS scores;3. The SPADI Disability subscale showed a negative correlation (> 0.30) with the role physical (RP) subscale of the SF‐36;4. The SPADI Disability subscale proved a negative correlation (> 0.30) with the social functioning (SF) subscale of the SF‐36.

The R‐package lavaan was utilized to conduct the CFA, while all other statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL; 2004). A statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05 was established for all analyses.

Structural Validity

The results of the CFA conducted on the two subscales of the SPADI, namely Pain and Disability, showed satisfactory values for all indicators. The CFA indicated a two‐factor structure with the following results: comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.985, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) = 0.980, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.045, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) = 0.048.

Discussion

The results of this study provide valuable insights into the demographic, clinical, and psychometric characteristics of the Bengali version of the shoulder pain and disability index (SPADI for assessing FS. These findings offer a robust foundation for validating the application of SPADI as a reliable PROM within clinical practice in Bangladesh.

Psychometric Properties

The psychometric evaluation of SPADI was strongly supported by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results (CFI: 0.985, TLI: 0.980, RMSEA: 0.045, SRMR: 0.048), and its two‐factor structure was confirmed, maintaining consistency with its original design. High internal consistency was demonstrated by the pain (α = 0.842) and disability (α = 0.878) subscales, as indicated by Cronbach's alpha values. Reliability was reinforced through item‐to‐total correlation analyses, with all items showing correlations above 0.40, although item #9 (putting on a shirt with front buttons) was identified as having the lowest correlation, suggesting potential redundancy. Test–retest reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients exceeding 0.98 for both subscales, was observed to be exceptional. Minimal measurement error (SEM: 2.10–2.15; MDC: 6.00–6.10) was also noted, highlighting the stability of SPADI scores over time and ensuring its effectiveness in capturing changes in FS symptoms, particularly for longitudinal assessments.

Demographic and Clinical Observations

The demographic data show a near‐equal gender distribution (53.7% female, 46.3% male), with a mean age of 54.8 ± 8.2 years, aligning with the typical middle‐aged population affected by FS [1]. Diabetes (25.0%) emerged as the most common comorbidity, reinforcing its established link to FS [3, 50]. Clinically, FS was more prevalent in the left shoulder (55.0%), while 80.0% of participants had right‐hand dominance, suggesting a potential influence of functional patterns on disease manifestation [51]. Chronicity was notable, with 62.5% reporting symptoms beyond 3 months, emphasizing the need for sustained therapeutic approaches [52].

Range of Motion and Symptom Assessment

FS patients exhibited marked restrictions in shoulder ROM, particularly in external rotation (29.2 ± 13.7°), confirming this movement's role as a key diagnostic indicator [53]. Functional limitations were reflected in elevated SPADI pain (56.7 ± 23.0) and disability (50.9 ± 23.4) scores, supported by moderate‐to‐severe NPRS (7.1 ± 2.1) and DASH (39.2 ± 19.5) scores [54]. SF‐36 results revealed significant impairments in physical role functioning and bodily pain, while general health and mental well‐being were relatively preserved, suggesting AC's primary burden is localized to physical domains [55].

Structural Validity

Alignment with findings in other studies was observed, though differences were noted. Similar CFA values were reported for SPADI's structural validity (CFI: 0.999, TLI: 0.997, RMSEA: 0.030) by Venturin et al. [13] and the new version of the Turkish SPADI [56] had a better fit (CFI = 0.978, TLI = 0.971, RMSEA = 0.058, SRMR = 0.043). However, limitations in the Disability subscale were highlighted by Jerosch‐Herold et al. [57], where Rasch model analysis indicated differential item functioning (DIF) by age and gender. Internal consistency values comparable to those found in this study (pain: α = 0.859, disability: α = 0.895) were reported by Venturin et al. [13], though higher values were noted in the cross‐cultural adaptation studied by Membrilla‐Mesa et al. [58], where the Spanish version showed α = 0.916 for pain and α = 0.860 for disability. Reliability findings were consistent with ICC values reported by Venturin et al. (pain: 0.989; disability: 0.990) and overall SPADI exhibited good reliability and excellent responsiveness [59]. However, Membrilla‐Mesa et al. [58] observed slightly lower ICC values (0.89–0.93), indicating variability across cultural adaptations. Issues with certain items in the Disability subscale were further reinforced by Rasch model findings, emphasizing the need for context‐specific evaluations in diverse populations.

Construct Validity and Hypotheses Testing

Construct validity findings indicated strong correlations between SPADI and comparator instruments, with the pain subscale meeting all a priori hypotheses (100%) and the disability subscale meeting most (75%). The correlations with NPRS and DASH scores were particularly strong (≥0.60), confirming the relevance of SPADI in capturing pain and functional impairments. However, weaker correlations with SF‐36 domains highlight the need for complementary PROMs to address broader aspects such as social functioning and quality of life [60, 61].

Implications for Clinical Practice and Research

The findings validate the Bengali version of SPADI as a reliable and culturally adaptable tool for assessing FS. The robust psychometric properties underscore its suitability for clinical practice, enabling targeted interventions based on pain and disability scores. However, limitations such as potential item redundancy, small test–retest reliability samples, and the absence of advanced psychometric analyses like Rasch modeling warrant further research. We acknowledge the small sample size as a limitation, particularly in relation to reliability analysis. This constraint was primarily due to the patient availability, stringent inclusion criteria and resource limitations inherent to clinical settings in countries like Bangladesh. Despite this, we ensured methodological rigor by applying validated tools and standardized data collection procedures. Additionally, the observed correlations with SF‐36 domains suggest that SPADI alone may not fully capture the impact of FS on patients' lives, advocating for the use of complementary PROMs.

Future studies should focus on refining SPADI items, expanding sample sizes for reliability testing, and exploring modern psychometric approaches to enhance structural validity. Investigating central sensitization mechanisms in FS could also provide insights into novel therapeutic strategies targeting the central nervous system.

Conclusion

This study marks a significant step in validating SPADI for Bengali‐speaking FS patients, reinforcing its role as a reliable PROM for pain and disability assessment. Its application in clinical practice can facilitate more precise and personalized care, improving patient outcomes and advancing FS management in Bangladesh.
