Determinants of Hepatitis B vaccine uptake among health science students: A cross-sectional study in Burao, Somaliland
Authors: Hamda Jama Yousuf, Ahmed Abdirahman Farah, Ali Ahmed Hussein, Mohamed Hussein Egeh, Abdiasis Aden Omer, Bashiir Mohamed Mohamoud, Suhur A. Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a global public health concern, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Health science students are at high risk of exposure during clinical training. However, there is limited evidence on their vaccination coverage in Somaliland. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of Hepatitis B vaccination among health science students in Burao, Somaliland. A cross-sectional study was conducted from July to September 2025 among 332 health science students from seven universities in Burao. Participants were selected using a stratified random sampling method. Data were collected through a structured, self-administered questionnaire and analyzed using STATA. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics, while multivariable logistic regression analyses identified factors associated with Hepatitis B vaccination. Only 12.3% of students were vaccinated against hepatitis B. The main barriers to vaccination were cost (55.1%), lack of information (18.7%), and limited access (17.2%). Factors significantly associated with vaccination status included income, knowledge, attitude, and preventive practices. Students with medium income were eight times more likely to be vaccinated. Those with poor preventive practices had markedly lower odds of vaccination. The study revealed alarmingly low hepatitis B vaccination coverage among health science students in Burao, despite good knowledge and positive attitudes toward HBV prevention. Financial constraints, limited awareness, and accessibility barriers were major obstacles. Targeted interventions such as subsidized vaccines, awareness campaigns, and mandatory vaccination policies for students entering clinical practice are urgently needed to ensure protection against HBV.

Background

Hepatitis B is a serious viral infection that can cause significant damage to the liver. Hepatitis B is a serious viral infection that can lead to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. According to a study, Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a global health issue that affects 3.61% of the world’s population. The problem is most severe in the African region (8.8% prevalence) and the Western Pacific region (5.26% prevalence).1 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), two billion people have been infected with Hepatitis B at some point; of these, 257 million have a chronic (long-term) infection, which puts them at risk of developing serious liver disease.2 Hepatitis B represents a significant public health challenge in Ethiopia. Research indicates that the prevalence of the infection in the country ranges from 6% to 9%.3 In Uganda, Hepatitis B infection is considered highly endemic, with a national prevalence of 10%. The country exhibits significant geographical variation in infection rates, which range from 4% in the southwest to 5% in Kampala and its surrounding districts, with a peak of 25% in the northeast region.4 As Somalia recovers from a devastating civil war, it faces a major health threat from hepatitis infections. To combat this, the country has created national guidelines for patient care. The World Health Organization (WHO) confirms the severity of the problem, noting that the prevalence of Hepatitis B is very high, affecting more than 8% of the population.5 The prevalence of the Hepatitis B virus in Somaliland is classified as intermediate to high, with notable heterogeneity reported across different studies. While a localized study of pregnant women at Hargeisa’s Edna Adan Hospital identified a Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) prevalence of 4.4%, broader reviews of the Somali region indicate a much wider range, from 5% to over 20%. This discrepancy underscores the necessity for further research to accurately determine the specific prevalence rates among various regions and demographic groups within Somaliland.5,6 The Hepatitis B virus (HBV) causes 80% of all liver cancer cases. Because of this strong link, it may be the second biggest cancer-causing agent for humans, surpassed only by tobacco.7 Nosocomial (hospital-acquired) transmission of the virus can occur through exposure to contaminated biological materials, such as blood, blood products, and other body fluids. A significant risk is also posed by percutaneous injuries from sharp instruments, like needles or scalpels, that were previously used on an infected patient.8 Healthcare workers and medical students are more likely to get Hepatitis B because their work and training expose them to patients’ blood and body fluids.9 While trainees operate within the same healthcare delivery system as seasoned professionals, their risk of accidental exposure may be elevated. This increased vulnerability is often attributed to a combination of their relative inexperience and inadequate training in the correct utilization of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE).10 Multiple epidemiological studies conducted among medical and health science students at the University of Lomé, Togo, have reported varying hepatitis B prevalence rates of 4.6%, 11%, and 9% among students in different study periods.11 A study at the School of Medicine at Makerere University in Uganda showed that 11% of medical students were infected with Hepatitis B. The infection was confirmed by testing for the Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). In Somaliland, hepatitis B vaccination is included in the national immunization program primarily targeting infants; however, there is no comprehensive policy ensuring vaccination coverage for high-risk groups such as health science students and healthcare workers. Access to the hepatitis B vaccine is often limited to private health facilities, and the cost is typically borne by individuals, which may hinder uptake. In addition, there are no structured institutional vaccination programs or mandatory vaccination requirements for students before clinical training. This policy and implementation gap may contribute to the low vaccination coverage observed in this study. Despite the high risk of occupational exposure among health science students, there is limited evidence regarding hepatitis B vaccination coverage and its determinants in Somaliland. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of hepatitis B vaccination and identify factors associated with vaccine uptake among health science students in Burao, Somaliland. This research directly contributes to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by investigating the vaccination status of health science students in Burao. The study aligns with SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) by providing data crucial for the 2030 hepatitis elimination target. Furthermore, it supports SDG 4 (Quality Education) by promoting health awareness and occupational safety among future healthcare professionals. The findings will provide valuable insights for policymakers, universities, and health authorities to improve vaccination coverage and implement effective preventive strategies.

Methodology

Study design and study setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted to investigate the uptake of hepatitis B vaccination and its determinants among health science students in Burao, Somaliland. The study was carried out over a period of three months, from July to September 2025. Burao, the capital city of the Togdheer region, has a semi-arid climate and an estimated population of 406,866, according to the Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS) 2020. The city is one of the major educational and commercial centers in Somaliland, with a rapidly growing population and several higher education institutions offering health-related programs.12

Study population

The study population consisted of undergraduate health science students enrolled in universities in Burao, Somaliland. Inclusion criteria included all undergraduate health science students who consented to participate. Students who are absent during data collection or whose vaccination history or demographic information is incomplete or unverifiable will also be excluded.

Operational definition

Vaccination status

The study assessed hepatitis B vaccination status as the dependent variable, categorized as vaccinated (received full doses) and not vaccinated.

The main independent variables included socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, academic year, department, income, and occupation), as well as knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) related to hepatitis B infection and vaccination.

Knowledge was assessed using 11 dichotomous (Yes/No) questions. Each correct response was scored as 1, and incorrect responses as 0. A total knowledge score was calculated for each participant individually. Participants who scored ≥70% (i.e., at least 8 out of 11 correct responses) were classified as having “good knowledge,” while those scoring below 70% were categorized as having “poor knowledge.”

Attitude was assessed using 5 Likert-scale items (strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, strongly agree). Each item was scored on a scale from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude toward hepatitis B vaccination. A composite attitude score was calculated for each participant individually. Participants who scored ≥70% of the total possible score were classified as having a “positive attitude,” while those scoring below this threshold were categorized as having a “negative attitude.

Practice was assessed using 3 dichotomous (Yes/No) questions related to occupational exposure and preventive behaviors. For exposure-related items, responses indicating exposure (e.g., “Yes”) were considered indicative of poorer practice, while responses indicating no exposure (e.g., “No”) were considered indicative of better practice. Scores were assigned accordingly and summed to generate a composite practice score for each participant. Participants who scored ≥70% were classified as having “good practice,” while those scoring below 70% were categorized as having “poor practice.”

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a proportion of 50%. After applying a finite population correction for the total population, the final sample size was 332.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed by the research team based on a review of relevant literature. The questionnaire was distributed electronically using Kobo Toolbox, an open-source data collection platform commonly used for field-based research. To ensure clarity and consistency, the questionnaire was translated into Somali and back-translated into English. A pilot test was conducted among 10% of the sample (students not included in the main study). Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with a value of ≥0.7 considered acceptable. The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the questionnaire was 0.82, indicating good reliability.

Data analysis

All completed questionnaires were checked for accuracy and completeness before entry into a database. STATA version 17 was used. After data cleaning, descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize participants’ characteristics. Knowledge, attitude, and practice variables were computed as composite scores and categorized into dichotomous variables using a 70% threshold (≥70% = good/positive; <70% = poor/negative), as defined in the study variables section. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between independent variables and hepatitis B vaccination status. Both crude odds ratios (cORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Result

Prevalence of Hepatitis B vaccination among health science students in Burao, Somaliland

A total of 332 health science students participated in the study. Of these, 41 (12.3%) were vaccinated, while 291 (87.7%) were not vaccinated.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

The study included 332 health science students; Table 1 shows that the majority of whom were young adults aged 20–24 y (55.12%), followed by those under 20 y (37.05%). Female participants predominated (74.70%), reflecting the higher enrollment of women in health-related programs. Nursing students constituted the largest group (37.95%), followed by Midwifery (17.47%), Medicine (15.06%), Medical Laboratory Science (12.65%), Nutrition (10.84%), and other disciplines (6.02%). The primary barriers to receiving or completing the Hepatitis B vaccine were cost (62.9%), lack of information (21.3%), time or access constraints (19.6%), and fear or personal beliefs about vaccination (10.3%). The multivariable logistic regression analysis in Table 5 identifies factors independently associated with Hepatitis B vaccination status among health science students in Burao, Somaliland. Income was strongly associated with vaccination: students with medium income were significantly more likely to be vaccinated compared to those with low income (AOR = 8.27, 95% CI: 2.26–30.29), whereas high income was not significantly associated (AOR = 2.40, 95% CI: 0.59–9.76). A history of hepatitis B infection among family members or close contacts was also significantly associated with vaccination status. “I don’t know” had lower odds of vaccination (AOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.07–0.96), and those who responded “No” had even lower odds (AOR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07–0.54) compared to students who answered “Yes.” Attitude and practice were significant predictors as well: students with negative attitudes were less likely to be vaccinated (AOR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.60–1.40), while students with poor vaccination practices were strongly less likely to be vaccinated (AOR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01–0.27). Other variables, including academic year, field of study, marital status, occupation, age, sex, general knowledge, and reported barriers such as cost, lack of information, fear/beliefs, and access, were not statistically significant. Discussion

In Somaliland, hepatitis B vaccination is included in the national immunization program primarily targeting infants; however, there is no comprehensive policy ensuring vaccination coverage for high-risk groups such as health science students and healthcare workers. Access to the hepatitis B vaccine is often limited to private health facilities, and the cost is typically borne by individuals, which may hinder uptake. In addition, there are no structured institutional vaccination programs or mandatory vaccination requirements for students before clinical training. This policy and implementation gap may contribute to the low vaccination coverage observed in this study. This study examined the prevalence and determinants of hepatitis B vaccination among health science students in Burao, Somaliland. Only 12.3% of the students were vaccinated. This coverage is similar to reports from various countries that found HBV vaccination to be unavailable, expensive, and lacking in instruction on infection control, which contributed to medical students’ low or nonexistent vaccination rates. The current study shows a higher hepatitis B vaccination rate than a previous study conducted in Ethiopia4.7%.13 While it is lower than the studies carried out in Uganda44.3%,14 India57%,15 Nigeria39.2%,16 and Ethiopia43.4%.17 This variation may be attributed to differences in vaccine availability, affordability, institutional vaccination policies, and awareness programs. This indicates that, despite being aware of HBV transmission and its complications, a large gap exists between knowledge and prevention practices among health science students in Burao. The study also found the cost was the leading barrier to vaccination (62.9%), followed by lack of information (21.3%) and access constraints (19.6%). These findings are consistent with studies conducted.18–21 In this study, income was a significant determinant of vaccination. Students with a medium income level were over eight times more likely to be vaccinated compared to those with low income. This finding aligns with studies conducted.14,19,20,22 The economic constraint observed in Burao suggests that subsidized or free vaccination programs could substantially improve uptake. Additionally, awareness of hepatitis B of Hepatitis B was significantly associated with vaccination status. Students who lacked awareness or were uncertain about the importance of vaccination were significantly less likely to be vaccinated. Similar findings were reported in Ethiopia and Uganda,14,19 where inadequate knowledge about transmission and vaccine benefits negatively affected vaccination rates. Although most participants in this study had good theoretical knowledge about HBV transmission and prevention, their low vaccination rates imply a gap between knowledge and practice, which may stem from systemic or behavioral barriers. This finding is in line with a study in Kenya.20 It might also suggest that some students perceive vaccination as a professional obligation rather than a personal health priority. Future qualitative research could explore this relationship in more depth to clarify these dynamics. Moreover, practice showed a strong association with vaccination status. Students who demonstrated poor preventive practices were significantly less likely to be vaccinated. This is comparable to the results of an earlier study that showed a strong relationship between practice level and full-dose immunization,19 indicating that students who engage in preventive behaviors, such as screening and use of personal protective equipment, are more likely to complete their vaccination schedule. This trend reflects increased clinical exposure and awareness of occupational risks as students advance in their studies. Similar patterns have been reported in Uganda 14 and Ethiopia.19 All things considered, the results highlight how urgently institutional initiatives are needed to increase HBV vaccine coverage. Somaliland’s health officials and universities have to think about making vaccinations a requirement for health science students before clinical rotations. The main obstacles of cost and knowledge gaps found in this study might also be addressed by financial assistance programs and awareness efforts. This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, the data were self-reported, which may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Third, the study was conducted in a single city, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions of Somaliland. Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into hepatitis B vaccination uptake among health science students.

Conclusion

This study assessed the prevalence and determinants of Hepatitis B vaccination among health science students in Burao, Somaliland. The findings revealed that only 12.3% of the students were vaccinated against Hepatitis B, were unvaccinated. Despite showing good knowledge and generally positive attitudes toward Hepatitis B prevention, vaccination uptake remained very low. The major barriers identified were vaccine cost, lack of information, and limited access to vaccination services. Factors significantly associated with vaccination status included income, knowledge or perception about vaccination, attitude, and preventive practice. Students with medium income, good practice, and clear awareness about the importance of vaccination were more likely to be vaccinated. These findings highlight a significant gap between knowledge and practice among future healthcare professionals in Somaliland. The results call for urgent public health interventions to ensure that all health science students are protected before entering clinical practice.
