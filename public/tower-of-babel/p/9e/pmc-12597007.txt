Haemostasis alterations in immune thrombocytopenia and their clinical significance
Authors: Thomas Pincez, Natalie Mathews, Arnaud Bonnefoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is highly heterogeneous, and only a subset of patients with severe thrombocytopenia develops significant bleeding. ITP is also associated with a paradoxical increased risk of thrombosis. Here, we review the multiple haemostasis alterations reported in patients with ITP. Data show an increased platelet basal activation with lower platelet reactivity but with overall increased platelet function. The activated state of platelets and the endothelial dysfunction lead to an activation of coagulation. These alterations tend to counterbalance the bleeding tendency related to thrombocytopenia but have some variability among patients. Consequently, several functional assays have correlated the magnitude of haemostasis alterations to bleeding risk. These results suggest that ITP is a multifaceted bleeding disorder that goes beyond simple thrombocytopenia. The heterogeneity in the various alterations in haemostatic function is likely to drive the variable bleeding phenotypes observed. Haemostasis functional assays could thus serve as clinically relevant tools to individualize management.Keywords: blood coagulation, immune thrombocytopenia, platelet function tests

Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by an isolated reduction of platelet count due to the premature destruction of platelets and to some extent, megakaryocytes. The clinical presentation of ITP is highly heterogeneous. Although the most frequent presentation is a mild, cutaneous bleeding tendency, severe haemorrhage can occur. Meta-analysis of ITP patients found frequencies of severe haemorrhage of up to 20% in children and 10% in adults but with heterogeneous definitions for severe bleeding [1]. Indeed, only 0.5% to 1% of children and ∼1.5% of adults will develop intracranial haemorrhage [1]. Platelet count is a poor predictor of bleeding, and there is no tool to predict bleeding severity of ITP. Despite patients with platelets >30 × 109/L rarely experiencing bleeding, the correlation between platelet count and bleeding severity under this threshold is very low [2]. Moreover, there is also a paradoxical increased risk of thrombosis risk in ITP [3,4]. This suggests that, in addition to thrombocytopenia, other clinically significant alterations of haemostasis are at play.

Haemostasis alterations in ITP

Overview of the mechanisms of thrombocytopenia

Multiple mechanisms have been implicated in the decreased platelet production and increased platelet destruction observed in ITP. The increased platelet destruction is mainly due to immunoglobulins secreted by autoreactive B cells [5]. Platelet opsonization leads to antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis by macrophages expressing Fc γ receptor, mainly within the spleen. Some autoantibodies can mediate complement fixation, enhancing phagocytosis and leading to complement-dependent cytotoxicity. Platelet phagocytosis might also be increased by the increased exposure of P-selectin and phosphatidylserine seen in activated platelets. Platelets can be destroyed by hepatocytes through Ashwell-Morell receptors following platelet desialylation induced by some autoantibodies and T cells [6]. Additionally, cytotoxic T lymphocytes have been shown to directly lyse platelets, and NK cells may also mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [7]. Increased platelet apoptosis may also contribute to thrombocytopenia. Finally, insufficient platelet production has been identified as a contributing factor to the pathology of ITP. This mechanism is due to direct immunoglobulin-mediated and T-cell-mediated toxicity but also to reduced concentrations of thrombopoietin, which is consumed by young platelets that are rapidly destroyed after reaching circulation.

Quantitative and qualitative platelet alterations

Main platelets alterations are summarized in Figure A. Platelet aggregation studies have been difficult to conduct in patients with ITP given the low reliability of light transmission aggregometry in patients with reduced platelet count (<100 × 109/L) and have led to conflicting results. Light transmission aggregometry using adapted reference ranges for low platelet count samples, found functional abnormalities in a quarter of patients with ITP (using ristocetin, adenosine diphosphate, epinephrine, and collagen as agonists) [8]. One study used flow cytometry (FC)–based platelet aggregation assay analysis (also called microaggregation) and found equivalent (phorbol myristate acetate and adenosine diphosphate) or enhanced (protease-activated receptor 1-activating peptide) aggregation compared to controls [9]. Another study using the same approach found decreased aggregation in patients with severe (Buchanan bleeding score ≥ 4), but not mild, bleeding phenotypes (ristocetin and phorbol myristate acetate) [10]. One study found that the mean platelet function, as measured by a viscoelastic analyzer, was below normal; this study lacked a control group, however [11].

Basal platelet activation has been shown to be increased in most patients with ITP in almost all studies [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]], with only a few exceptions [10]. Activation has been mainly determined by FC through platelet P-selectin expression and PAC1 positivity, but the few studies analyzing other markers, such as soluble P-selectin, GPIb, GP53, resting cytosolic calcium, and procoagulant platelet level found similar results [14,16,17,21,22]. On the contrary, agonist-stimulated platelet reactivity assessed by FC have shown variable results, and discrepancies reported, notably depending on the agonist and the methodological approach used [10,[13], [14], [15],20,21,23,24]. While some studies found that reactivity was increased [14,15] or not present [10] (based on P-selectin [10,14,15], GPIb [14], and open GPIIbIIIa expression [10]) most reports found that it was impaired (mainly based on P-selectin) [13,15,20,21,23,24]. The increased platelet size observed in ITP may confound such results and size-adjusted analysis may in fact reveal reduced platelet reactivity in this population [9].

Shear-induced platelet adhesion assessed by the Impact-R Cone and Plate(let) Analyzer has been shown to be reduced in patients with ITP, but this is probably a direct effect of thrombocytopenia, rather than indicating a functional defect [12]. In fact, platelets have been shown to carry an increased capacity to form aggregates, especially in individuals with higher basal P-selectin platelet expression [12].

Platelet microparticles (PMP) are small vesicles originating from platelet membranes that contain cytoplasm components. Most of these particles have a higher procoagulant effect than platelets do. Several studies using FC have shown higher levels of PMP in ITP [20,23,[25], [26], [27]], which may be inversely correlated with platelet count [23]. Moreover, red cell microparticles may also be increased, possibly further enhancing the coagulation [23,26].

The specific mechanisms driving alterations of platelet function are unclear. The effect of the autoantibodies has been equivocal. Sera of patients with ITP had variable effects on donor platelets, some increasing while others impaired the aggregation response [28]. Most studies found that platelet basal activation [18] and agonist-induced activation were not linked to anti-GPIIb/IIIa or anti-GPIb/IX antibody presence or titre [13,21]. However, 2 studies found that anti-GPIIb/IIIa antibodies were associated with reduced aggregation [8,9]. Complement activation and the desialylation of platelets in ITP may also contribute to platelet dysfunction [29]. ITP may also be associated to increased neutrophil extracellular traps [30,31], which initiate platelet activation as well as coagulation via thrombin formation [32,33]. Finally, increased platelet apoptosis has been found in ITP and could contribute to the procoagulant state [34,35].

Coagulation, fibrinolysis, and endothelial alterations

Although platelet alterations have been the most studied in ITP, alterations pertaining to whole blood, plasma, and endothelium have also been reported (Figure B).

Studies implementing 2 global haemostasis assays, rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) and clot formation and lysis assay, have shown a pattern of reduced clot formation likely due to the thrombocytopenia, yet higher resistance to fibrinolysis, which was independent of platelet count [23,34,36]. This reduced fibrinolysis is concordant with findings of higher plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1) levels [23,34,37,38], reduced tissue plasminogen activator antigen and activity, and reduced plasmin generation [36]. Plasmin directly degrades fibrin, and PAI-1 inhibits tissue plasminogen activator, which activates plasmin generation.

Some studies assessed other changes in plasmatic factors. Prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time was found, but also an increased level of coagulation factors VIII, IX, XI, prothrombin fragment I+II and D-dimers, suggesting a low-grade coagulopathy [26,38]. Factors VIII and IX were negatively correlated with platelet count, which may counterbalance the bleeding risk induced by the thrombocytopenia [26]. Two studies also found elevated von Willebrand factor in some patients [39,40]. One study speculated that a procoagulant profile observed on a calibrated automated thrombogram could be attributed to increased resistance to protein C [23]; a similar profile was also observed in another report [23,24].

Endothelial dysfunction is also likely to be present in ITP [23], as suggested by higher levels of PAI-1 [23,34,37,38], von Willebrand factor [39,40], E-selectin [23], intercellular adhesion molecule 1 [31], vascular cell adhesion molecule [40], and thrombomodulin [31]. Such endothelial dysfunction may be triggered by autoantibodies, PMP, and proinflammatory cytokines known to be present in ITP [36,41]. Proinflammatory cytokines might also contribute to the observed procoagulant and hypofibrinolytic changes. Additionally, the basal activation of platelets and the increased amount of PMP found in patients with ITP lead to surface phosphatidylserine exposure and tissue factor activation. This process may contribute to the activation of coagulation.

Overall effect of haemostasis alterations on bleeding and thrombosis risk

Most studies are concordant with a procoagulant state in patients with ITP as well as a basal activation of platelets, which contributes to the coagulation activation. The coagulation activation is likely the main contributor to the venous thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and stroke risk associated with ITP [3,4]. The functionality of the platelets regarding primary haemostasis is less clear. The combination of high basal activation with possibly impaired reactivity is reminiscent of the phenomenon of platelet exhaustion, defined as the functional impairment of platelets following excessive activation. The overall combination of these alterations tends to limit the bleeding tendency associated with thrombocytopenia while increasing the risk of thrombosis. The variation between studies is partly attributed to differences in the assays used. However, it could also be due to differences in the patients’ characteristics and phenotypes. This variability may provide biomarkers in the prediction of bleeding risk amongst patients diagnosed with ITP.

Haemostasis alteration as a tool for prediction of bleeding in ITP

Bleeding prediction is a major need in ITP and several approaches have been investigated. The current approach is based on the haemorrhagic phenotype with which the patient presents [42]. Despite its relative efficacy (a low score is relatively predictive of a reduced risk of severe bleeding), the major limitation of this approach is that a sufficient preestablished bleeding tendency is required to consider the patient at high risk of severe bleeding. Thus, the clinical interpretation is made slightly too late, as the optimal timepoint to intervene is before significant bleeding. To overcome this obstacle, several functional tests have been investigated. It is important to note that the definition of bleeding or severe bleeding varies significantly between studies; this discrepancy limits comparisons.

Platelet-based tests have been the most frequently investigated. As mentioned, platelet count by itself is a poor predictor of bleeding, but studies suggest that immature platelet fraction or platelet size could be directly correlated with bleeding risk [43,44]. Additionally, higher platelet basal activation has been found in patients with higher bleeding scores, independently of platelet count [16,44,45]. This finding may depend on the protocol used, as another study using platelet-rich plasma rather than whole blood found no association [12]. Conversely to basal activation, higher agonist-stimulated platelet reactivity was found to be protective of bleeding in 2 studies [10,46]. In concordance with platelet exhaustion, these findings suggest that a higher basal activation impairs platelet reactivity and is associated with higher risk of bleeding. Platelet function seems to appropriately reflect disease activity, as it seems to vary with treatment response [[47], [48], [49]]. FC-based platelet aggregation assay analysis may be a useful tool, as one study found that patients with severe bleeding (Buchanan bleeding score ≥ 4), had a lower platelet aggregation [10]. Platelet adhesion using Impact-R Cone and Plate(let) Analyzer could be higher in patients with bleeding symptoms, independently of platelet count [12]. Nevertheless, this finding was not confirmed by further studies [21]. Despite their significant role in ITP haemostasis alteration, the place of PMP assessment in clinics is unclear. One study found that patients without bleeding had higher PMP levels than those with bleeding [50], but this observation was not confirmed in another study [25].

Research on whole blood and plasma-based tests, including viscoelastometry, is still relatively limited. One study assessed the sonoclot analyzer in ITP and found that a lower platelet function value and a prolonged activated clotting time were associated with increased bleeding risk [11]. Two studies using ROTEM found reduced parameters of clot formation and firmness in patients with increased bleeding [44,51]. The platelet-based parameter of ROTEM showed similar correlation [51]. ROTEM was more correlated with bleeding than platelet, especially in patients with platelet count <30 × 109/L [51]. However, the limited ability of viscoelastometry and platelet adhesion and function analyzers to identify individuals at risk of bleeding in settings other than ITP must be taken into account when interpreting studies in ITP [52,53].

Antibody and cytokine levels have also been investigated. One study found no correlation between platelet antibodies and bleeding [12]. Another study found a possible correlation between a model considering the level of 11 different cytokines and bleeding risk [54].

In summary, studies clearly suggest substantial interindividual variability of haemostasis alteration in patients with ITP. They also demonstrate that these alterations are directly implicated in the risk of bleeding. These 2 premises indicate that functional haemostasis testing may have a clinical role in the prognostication of bleeding and therefore the management of patients with ITP. The most studied tests are platelet-based and mainly FC-based. The optimal test to capture the complex haemostasis alterations seen in ITP remains unknown but should be easily implementable in care facilities and ideally not require a specialized haemostasis laboratory.

Conclusion

Historically, ITP has been considered an example of an antibody-mediated autoimmune disease. This disorder was found to be far more complex, involving numerous alterations in several immune cells and effectors. Such alterations affect both primary haemostasis and coagulation, yielding an imbalanced haemostatic system with alterations counterbalancing the bleeding risk associated with thrombocytopenia. The interindividual variability of these alterations seems to be the root of the heterogeneous bleeding phenotypes that have been observed clinically. While not yet standardized or widely accessible, the assessment of such haemostatic alterations represents a promising step towards individualized care in the management of ITP.
