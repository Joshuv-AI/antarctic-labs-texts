Small Business Grants Awarded by the National Institutes of Health for Rhinologic Diseases, 1985–2024
Authors: Jack Birkenbeuel, Mishek Thapa, Ericka L Erickson, Graham Pingree, Kai Zhao, Zachary Soler, Rodney J Schlosser, Vinay K Rathi
Published: 2026

Introduction

Premarket research and development of pharmaceuticals and devices requires substantial investment. For small firms, securing sufficient capital to finance early‐stage product development can be particularly challenging. To help mitigate these barriers and address unmet public health needs, National Institutes of Health (NIH) established the Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) programs (Table 1). These programs offer phased, non‐dilutive seed capital to US‐based, for‐profit small businesses conducting research with potential for commercialization; non‐dilutive seed capital permits founders of early stage firms to obtain funding without sacrificing a portion of their equity stakes [1]. The STTR program is specifically intended to promote academia–industry technology transfer; NIH requires small businesses to partner with non‐profit research institutions.

NIH SBIR and STTR programs collectively provide over $1.4 billion in annual funding [2]. SBIR/STTR funding has supported the commercialization of many clinically important drugs (e.g., parathyroid hormone) and devices (e.g., dural sealants for CSF leakage) [1]. Prior work has explored the impact of SBIR/STTR funding on fields such as cardiology, neurology, pediatrics, and basic science [3, 4, 5, 6]. We sought to investigate the extent and focus of NIH SBIR/STTR for rhinologic technologies.

Methods

We cross‐sectionally analyzed all NIH SBIR (R41/R42) and STTR (R43/R44) awards supporting projects related to diseases of the nasal airway/paranasal sinuses/anterior skull base. We identified relevant awards by querying the public NIH RePORTER database using predefined terms intended to maximize sensitivity (Table S1). Two authors (J.L.B. and M.T.) independently screened project titles and abstracts for eligibility; the senior author (V.K.R.) resolved any discrepancies between reviewers.

For each annual award, we extracted mechanism (SBIR/STTR), fiscal year, funding amount, disease category (cerebrospinal fluid leak/sinusitis/nasal airway obstruction/olfactory disorders/rhinitis/sinonasal tumors/all other), and parent project serial number. For each project, we extracted the initiation year (defined as the first year of SBIR/STTR support), funding NIH institute, calculated the total funding amount, and determined whether the project progressed to Phase II funding.

We used descriptive statistics to summarize award characteristics–adjusted funding for inflation using the Biomedical Research and Development Price Index. We performed all analyses using IBM SPSS and Microsoft Excel. This study analyzed public, non‐clinical data and therefore did not require exemption or review by the institutional review board at The Ohio State University College of Medicine.

Results

Between 1985 and 2024, the NIH awarded $53.6 million in small‐business grants supporting 46 projects targeting rhinologic disease (Table S2). Among these projects, $9.7 million (of $53.6 million; 18.1%) supported 11 (of 46; 23.9%) SBIR‐funded projects and $43.9 million (81.9%) supported 35 (76.1%) STTR‐funded projects. Of the 42 projects eligible for Phase II funding, 19 (45.2%) progressed from Phase I to Phase II. This included 3 (of 10 eligible; 30.0%) SBIR projects and 16 (of 32 eligible; 50.0%) STTR projects. Four (of 46; 8.7%) projects received direct Phase II funding. The median funding amount and duration per project was $873,383 (interquartile range [IQR]: $319,306–$1,687,779) over 2.0 years (IQR: 1.0–3.0 years).

Most awards supported development of diagnostic (n = 21; 45.7%) and therapeutic devices (n = 12; 26.1%). The most frequently targeted disease areas were olfactory disorders (n = 14; 30.4%), sinusitis (n = 8; 17.4%), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (n = 7; 15.2%), and other (n = 7; 15.2%) (Figure 1a,b); examples of projects categorized as “other” include devices intended for use during sinus surgery (e.g., novel endoscopes or aerosolized particle reduction technology).

Total funding increased over time (Figure 1a). Nearly half ($26.2 million/$53.6 million; 48.9%) of all funding was awarded by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) (Figure 1b); much of the remainder was awarded by the National Institute on Aging ($14.5 million; 27.1%) and National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders ($6.3 million; 11.8%) (Figure 1b).

Discussion

We found that NIH has granted nearly 50 SBIR/STTR awards supporting the commercialization of rhinologic products over the past four decades. Most awards funded medical device development and approximately half targeted olfactory disorders or sinusitis. Our analysis reveals two distinct features of SBIR/STTR funding for rhinologic products. First, Congress allocates substantially more funding to the SBIR program (86% of SBIR/STTR total funding; Table 1), but >75% of rhinologic project funding was provided through the STTR program for academia–industry collaborations. Second, Phase I to Phase II progression rate appears higher for rhinologic products (50% for STTR‐funded projects vs. <30% overall) [1]. These results underscore the vital and practical importance of academic research in advancing the diagnosis and treatment of rhinologic disease.

Our study has limitations. We may have failed to identify rhinologic products, despite using sensitive search terms and inclusion of products (e.g., Epstein–Barr virus detection assays) with abstracts listing both rhinologic (e.g., nasopharyngeal carcinoma) and non‐rhinologic (e.g., lymphoma) applications. Furthermore, we were unable to analyze rhinologic application volume levels or success rates because project‐level details are non‐public for rejected proposals.

Our findings suggest that NIH may be underfunding research and development for products targeting rhinologic diseases, which are increasing in prevalence and morbidity due to factors such as climate change and population aging [7, 8]. For example, in 2014, all rhinologic projects accounted for <0.2% of total SBIR/STTR funding, but two rhinologic conditions (chronic sinusitis and rhinitis) accounted for >0.4% of direct US healthcare spending [9, 10]. These results may reflect challenges firms face when navigating among NIH institutes with missions relevant to rhinologic disease, which have varying outreach strategies, review processes, participation requirements, and scientific priorities [1]. Lack of a principal institute for rhinologic disease may hinder grant targeting by firms and dilute funding responsibility among institutes. Such barriers to innovation merit attention as lawmakers reconsider NIH organization and funding priorities.
