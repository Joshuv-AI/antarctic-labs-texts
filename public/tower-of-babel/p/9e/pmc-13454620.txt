Effect of different washing and post-curing protocols on the fracture resistance of 3D-printed pediatric crowns after thermal cycling
Authors: Emre Serhan Alper, Huseyin Simsek, Suleyman Kutalmış Buyuk, Cigdem Guler, Mustafa Çadırcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) printing is increasingly used for pediatric crown restorations; however, post-processing procedures may critically influence their mechanical performance. This in vitro study evaluated the effects of washing and post-curing durations on the fracture resistance and fracture pattern distribution of 3D-printed pediatric crowns after thermal cycling. Ninety crowns fabricated using a digital light processing (DLP) printer were assigned to nine groups according to washing (0, 3, and 10 min of ultrasonic cleaning) and post-curing (0, 10, and 20 min) durations. After cementation onto standardized dies and 10,000 thermal cycles, fracture resistance was tested and fracture patterns were classified. Two-way analysis of variance revealed significant effects of washing and post-curing (p < 0.001), with post-curing demonstrating the strongest individual influence and a significant interaction between factors. The highest fracture resistance was observed with 10 min of ultrasonic washing followed by 10 min of post-curing, whereas prolonged post-curing reduced strength. Fracture patterns also differed significantly among groups. These findings indicate that mechanical performance depends on the coordinated interaction of post-processing steps. For the tested resin system, 10 min of ultrasonic washing followed by 10 min of post-curing appears to represent a clinically applicable protocol to maximize fracture resistance.Keywords: 3D printing, Pediatric crowns, Fracture resistance, Thermal cyclingSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

Crown restorations used in pediatric dentistry represent an important treatment option for managing extensive tooth structure loss associated with early childhood caries, enamel hypoplasia, and developmental defects, including dentinogenesis imperfecta. These restorations not only restore dental function but also contribute to improved nutrition, speech, and esthetics, thereby enhancing the overall quality of life of the child1. For many years, stainless steel crowns have been considered the “gold standard” due to their high durability, cost-effectiveness, and favorable handling characteristics. However, the metallic appearance of these restorations is often considered esthetically inadequate, especially in esthetically demanding situations, and may not fully meet the expectations of parents and children2,3.

In recent years, increasing esthetic awareness among children and parents, along with the development of cost-effective materials and the integration of digital technologies into clinical practice, has led to the emergence of restorative alternatives to stainless steel crowns. In particular, growing social and esthetic concerns, the impact of dental appearance on children’s school and social environments, and parental demand for more natural-looking solutions have directed clinicians toward esthetically satisfactory materials. This trend has not only expanded the range of available crown materials but has also driven the modernization of manufacturing techniques4,5.

Zirconia-based crowns have been regarded as a significant advancement due to their tooth-like color, biocompatibility, and high mechanical strength. Despite these advantages, the need for technical precision during placement, challenges in adapting to certain clinical situations, and the potential to cause wear on opposing teeth due to their relatively high stiffness may limit their widespread clinical use6,7. At this point, three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies have emerged as a modern manufacturing alternative to conventional subtractive milling systems. While traditional milling workflows rely on the removal of material from prefabricated blocks, 3D printing is based on the principle of additive manufacturing, in which restorations are fabricated through the layer-by-layer deposition of material. Compared with subtractive milling, this approach allows production with reduced material waste. In addition, the ability to easily reproduce crowns from digital data when needed represents a significant advantage of this technology. Furthermore, the capability of 3D printers to fabricate a single crown or multiple crowns simultaneously within the same production time stands out as an important benefit that enhances clinical efficiency. By comparison, milling systems require each restoration to be milled individually, resulting in a direct increase in production time as the number of restorations increases. Consequently, crown restorations used in pediatric dentistry have evolved to address not only functional demands but also esthetic and economic requirements, leading to a broader range of clinical applications3,8–11.

Recent studies have demonstrated that post-processing steps play a critical role in determining the mechanical properties of 3D-printed crowns. For example, Hwangbo et al. (2021) reported that different washing solutions and washing durations had significant effects on biocompatibility and flexural strength, with excessively prolonged washing times leading to a deterioration in mechanical strength12. Moreover, variations in post-curing duration have been reported to significantly influence mechanical performance; however, extending the curing time beyond a certain threshold may result in diminishing returns or even reductions in strength and hardness13–15. Taken together, these findings indicate that post-processing parameters should not be considered in isolation when evaluating the mechanical performance of 3D-printed materials. Consistent with this view, Finck et al. (2024) emphasized that washing and post-curing procedures jointly influence the mechanical properties of the material16.

Although numerous studies have focused on temporary or prosthetic restorations used in adult dentition, evidence regarding the effects of washing and post-curing durations on the fracture resistance of pediatric crowns applied to primary teeth remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the impact of different washing and post-curing protocols on the fracture resistance of 3D-printed pediatric crowns and to provide guidance for clinical practice16,17.

The null hypothesis was that different washing and post-curing durations would not significantly affect the fracture resistance or fracture pattern distribution of 3D-printed pediatric crowns after thermal cycling.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample preparation

This in vitro experimental study was conducted to compare the fracture resistance of 3D-printed pediatric crowns after thermal cycling under different washing and post-curing protocols. The study did not involve human participants, human-derived tissues, or animal specimens. All experiments were performed using typodont teeth and 3D-printed materials. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (v3.1, Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on the effect size estimated from pilot measurements conducted at the beginning of the study. The pilot analysis yielded a very large effect size (Cohen’s f = 1.42). Assuming an α level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, a minimum of eight specimens per group was determined to be sufficient. To increase statistical robustness, ten specimens were included in each group (total n = 90).

Initially, a mandibular second primary molar typodont tooth (PedoPrep Jaw Model; EduDent, Turkey) was prepared according to standard pediatric crown preparation principles. No digital scanning was performed after preparation. Instead, prefabricated crown designs of different sizes, created in Blender software (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, Netherlands) according to established anatomical guidelines, were used. The fabricated crowns were physically tried on the prepared tooth to determine the most appropriate size. Based on this selection, 90 crowns of the selected prefabricated design were printed using a digital light processing (DLP)-based 3D printer (SprintRay Pro 95 S; SprintRay Inc., USA) with a layer thickness of 50 μm, positioning the occlusal surface parallel to the build platform. Following printing, no standardized washing or post-curing procedures were applied prior to allocation into the experimental groups.

Washing and post-curing protocols

For post-processing procedures, the crowns were divided into a total of nine groups according to three different washing durations (0, 3, and 10 min) and three different post-curing durations (0, 10, and 20 min) (n = 10 per group) (Table 1).

In the 0-minute washing (0 W) groups, the specimens were not subjected to ultrasonic cleaning. Residual resin was manually removed for approximately 10 s using a soft nylon-bristled brush under light manual pressure by a single calibrated operator. Subsequently, 96% ethanol was sprayed onto the internal surface for 10 s. This was followed by an additional 10 s of brushing using a clean, soft nylon-bristled brush to ensure adequate solvent-assisted cleaning. Cleaning was considered complete when no visible resin residue remained under standardized illumination. All specimens were air-dried for 30 s prior to further processing.

In the 3- and 10-minute washing groups (3 W and 10 W), washing procedures were performed using an ultrasonic bath device at the predetermined durations for each group, with a 96% ethanol solution at room temperature. Ultrasonic washing was carried out using an Ultrasonic Cleaner device (Phrozen Ultrasonic Cleaner; Phrozen Inc., Taiwan).

After completion of all washing procedures, the specimens were air-dried for 5 min and then allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 min to facilitate evaporation of residual solvent from the surface.

Post-curing procedures were performed using a curing device (SprintRay ProCure 2; SprintRay Inc., USA). Post-curing durations were applied according to group allocation as 0, 10, or 20 min.

Design and fabrication of die models

The prepared tooth was digitally scanned, and a standardized die geometry was designed using Blender software based on the acquired digital data for subsequent crown cementation procedures. The die models were fabricated using a liquid crystal display (LCD)-based 3D printer (Phrozen Sonic Mini 8 K; Phrozen Inc., Taiwan) with a photopolymer resin (Saremco Crowntec; Saremco Dental AG, Switzerland). A resin system different from that used for crown fabrication was intentionally selected to isolate the mechanical behavior of the crown material during fracture resistance testing and to minimize potential interactions originating from the supporting structure (Fig. 1).

After fabrication, all die specimens were washed for 10 min using an Ultrasonic Cleaner device (Phrozen Wash; Phrozen Inc., Taiwan) and subsequently post-cured for 10 min in a curing unit (Phrozen Cure; Phrozen Inc., Taiwan).

Prior to fracture resistance testing, the fabricated crowns were cemented onto the 3D-printed die models using a light-cured resin-modified glass ionomer cement (Photac-Fil; 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure procedural standardization, all crowns were seated manually under consistent finger pressure by the same experienced operator. Excess cement was removed with a microbrush prior to light polymerization. Light curing was performed using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Valo Cordless; Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA) with a wavelength range of 385–515 nm and an output intensity of approximately 1000 mW/cm². Each specimen was light-cured for 20 s from the buccal, lingual, and occlusal surfaces, resulting in a total curing time of 60 s.

Thermal cycling

Prior to mechanical testing, all specimens, consisting of 3D-printed pediatric crowns cemented onto standardized die models, were subjected to a thermal cycling protocol designed to simulate the oral environment. The specimens were thermocycled for 10,000 cycles between water baths at 5 °C and 55 °C using an automated thermal cycling device (Lua TC-17 Thermocycler; Lua GmbH, Lübeck, Germany), with a dwell time of 30 s in each bath.

The selected thermocycling protocol was based on a previously published study evaluating 3D-printed resin crowns for primary teeth18. Furthermore, laboratory simulation studies have suggested that 10,000 thermal cycles may approximate one year of clinical service, although the precise in vivo correlation remains variable. Accordingly, this cycling regimen is widely accepted as an appropriate method for simulating short-term clinical performance in in vitro investigations19. Given that the present study focused on restorations for primary teeth, which have a limited functional lifespan, simulation of short-term clinical performance was considered sufficient for the purposes of this study.

Fracture resistance test

After thermal cycling, each crown was positioned directly on the testing device without the use of any intermediate material. Fracture resistance testing was performed using a universal testing platform (Shimadzu AGS-X; Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). A compressive load was applied perpendicular to the occlusal surface of the crown using a steel spherical indenter with a diameter of 1 mm at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min, and the maximum load at fracture was recorded in Newtons (N) (Fig. 2).

Experimental setup for the fracture resistance test. The crown specimen was positioned directly on the testing device and loaded perpendicular to the occlusal surface using a universal testing machine with a 1-mm-diameter steel spherical indenter.

Evaluation of fracture patterns

Fracture patterns observed after fracture resistance testing were evaluated according to the classification system defined by Oguz et al. Based on the extent of damage to the crown and the die, fractures were categorized into six distinct fracture types (Types I–VI)20. The definitions of the fracture types are summarized in Table 3.

Classification of fracture types.

Fracture pattern evaluation was performed in a blinded manner by two independent examiners, both of whom were unaware of the experimental group allocation of the specimens. The assessment was conducted using both naked-eye examination and stereomicroscopic evaluation. Each specimen was classified under a single fracture type only. Inter-observer agreement was calculated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Disagreement occurred in only 1 out of 90 specimens (percent agreement: 98.9%). The inter-observer agreement was therefore excellent (κ = 0.977), indicating almost perfect reliability.

SEM analysis

To qualitatively evaluate fracture surface characteristics in relation to the classified fracture types, representative fractured specimens were selected from each group. Samples were mounted on carbon adhesive tape and sputter-coated with gold (Au) for 1 min to enhance electrical conductivity. Imaging was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Hitachi SU1510; Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Japan).

Fracture surface topography, crack initiation and propagation patterns, and overall failure morphology were examined to characterize differences among fracture types. Images were obtained at magnifications of ×15, ×20, and ×27 to allow general morphological assessment of fracture features.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data satisfied the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances, fracture resistance values were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) to evaluate the main effects of washing time and post-curing time, as well as their interaction effect.

When statistically significant differences were detected, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test. Partial eta squared (ηp²) values were calculated to assess effect sizes.

The distribution of fracture patterns among the groups was evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate, depending on the expected cell counts. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Exact p-values are reported to three decimal places where applicable; values below 0.001 are reported as p < 0.001. Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

SEM evaluation of fracture types

SEM analyses were performed on representative specimens selected from the groups in which each fracture type was most frequently observed. Distinct fracture morphologies were observed among the tested groups (Fig. 3).

Type I, Type IV, and Type VI fractures exhibited progressively increasing failure severity, ranging from localized crown damage to catastrophic failure involving die fracture.

When evaluated in conjunction with the mechanical results, fracture mode distribution did not demonstrate a direct linear relationship with fracture resistance values. Although Type VI fractures were predominant overall, their frequency varied across post-processing protocols. Notably, the 10 W/10 C group exhibited the highest fracture resistance despite predominantly demonstrating Type VI fractures, whereas the 10 W/0 C group showed a higher proportion of Type I fractures with slightly lower fracture resistance values.

These observations indicate that maximum fracture load and fracture pattern distribution reflect complementary but independent aspects of failure behavior under different post-processing conditions.

Discussion

Additive manufacturing of dental restorations requires carefully controlled post-processing steps, as washing and post-curing directly influence polymer conversion, structural homogeneity, and long-term mechanical stability12. The present findings demonstrate that fracture resistance was influenced by both washing duration and post-curing time, with post-curing exhibiting a slightly stronger individual effect. More importantly, the significant interaction between these parameters indicates that mechanical performance was protocol-dependent rather than governed by either factor alone. This synergistic relationship underscores that post-processing parameters cannot be interpreted as isolated or linear contributors to mechanical behavior.

A key finding of the present study is that the influence of post-curing duration on fracture resistance was dependent on the applied washing protocol. Under conditions where ultrasonic washing was not performed (0 W), extending post-curing failed to enhance mechanical performance, indicating that curing alone cannot compensate for insufficient surface cleaning. In these groups, where surface cleaning was limited to manual procedures, heterogeneous removal of residual monomers and the formation of irregular polymerization regions during post-curing may have contributed to the accumulation of internal stresses within the polymer network. The findings reported by Xu et al. and Jang et al., indicating that inadequate washing conditions may lead to the development of heterogeneous microstructures in resin-based materials, are consistent with the results of the present study17,21.

In the group subjected to 3 min of ultrasonic washing (3 W), the mechanical response demonstrated a non-linear dependence on post-curing duration, suggesting that short-duration washing alone does not establish a stable structural condition. It is plausible that brief ultrasonic exposure partially removes surface resin without achieving complete monomer elimination, thereby generating a heterogeneous polymer network that remains mechanically unstable unless adequately stabilized by controlled post-curing. In this context, moderate post-curing may function as a necessary structural consolidation step, whereas insufficient or excessive curing appears unable to compensate for the intermediate washing condition. Similar threshold-dependent behavior has been reported in resin-based systems subjected to varying post-processing protocols12,22–25.

Groups subjected to 10 min of ultrasonic washing (10 W) demonstrated a more favorable mechanical profile, suggesting that adequate monomer removal plays a central role in establishing structural homogeneity within the polymer network. Effective ultrasonic cleaning likely facilitates more uniform polymerization by reducing residual surface and subsurface resin, thereby enhancing matrix integrity. However, extending post-curing beyond a moderate duration did not further improve mechanical performance and appears to have been detrimental under these conditions. This finding suggests that excessive post-curing may increase cross-link density, making the material stiffer and more brittle. As a result, stresses may accumulate more easily within the material under load. Under thermal cycling conditions, this increased rigidity may predispose the material to reduced resistance against repeated thermal expansion–contraction cycles16,23,26. Similar observations have been reported in resin-based systems, where higher degrees of polymerization did not consistently correspond to improved mechanical behavior13–15,27. From a clinical perspective, these findings indicate that optimal mechanical performance is achieved not by maximizing curing duration, but by balancing effective ultrasonic washing with controlled post-curing. For the resin system tested in the present study, 10 min of ultrasonic washing followed by 10 min of post-curing appears to represent a rational and clinically applicable protocol to maximize fracture resistance.

When considered more broadly, ultrasonic washing appears to contribute positively to mechanical stability when applied within controlled durations. Adequate ultrasonic cleaning likely enhances structural uniformity by facilitating more effective removal of residual resin prior to post-curing. However, the beneficial effect of washing is not unlimited, and excessively prolonged exposure may negatively affect material integrity, as reported in previous studies22,24,25. Importantly, the washing durations evaluated in the present study fall within clinically realistic timeframes, suggesting that moderate ultrasonic cleaning does not compromise fracture resistance. Variations in solvent composition, resin chemistry, and printing technology may further influence the threshold at which washing transitions from beneficial to detrimental.

In the absence of post-curing (0 C), fracture resistance did not follow a consistent improvement pattern with increasing washing duration, suggesting that washing alone is insufficient to ensure structural stabilization. The reduced mechanical stability observed after short-duration ultrasonic washing may reflect incomplete monomer removal combined with the development of localized structural heterogeneity within the resin matrix. More specifically, 3 min of ultrasonic washing may represent a structurally vulnerable intermediate condition. While such brief exposure may not be sufficient to eliminate uncured surface resin, it may still induce superficial solvent interaction, potentially leading to partial swelling or plasticization of the outer polymer layer. This partial modification could create a weak boundary region between the superficial and deeper polymer network, predisposing the material to structural discontinuity under load. This interpretation becomes more compelling when considered alongside fracture pattern distribution. The predominance of catastrophic Type VI fractures (90%) in the 3 W/0 C condition suggests that crack propagation occurred in an unstable and uncontrolled manner, consistent with localized structural weaknesses. In contrast, conditions associated with more limited fracture patterns indicate relatively better preservation of structural integrity12,20,21,26,28–30. From a clinical standpoint, these findings reinforce that washing procedures, when not followed by appropriate post-curing, may not provide sufficient mechanical reliability in 3D-printed pediatric crowns.

When washing and post-curing protocols are considered in combination, the findings reinforce that mechanical performance is determined by their coordinated interaction rather than by any single post-processing step. Adequate ultrasonic washing establishes a structurally uniform substrate, while controlled post-curing contributes to stabilization of the polymer network; together, these processes function synergistically to optimize mechanical integrity. Similar interactive effects between washing duration and post-curing protocols have been emphasized in previous investigations16,26. From a clinical standpoint, these results highlight that post-processing parameters should be optimized as an integrated protocol. For the resin system evaluated in the present study, a combination of 10 min of ultrasonic washing followed by 10 min of post-curing appears to represent a rational and clinically applicable approach to achieve optimal fracture resistance.

The present in vitro design does not fully replicate the complex biomechanical and environmental conditions of the oral cavity. The experimental protocol was limited to a single resin system and printing platform, and cementation was performed under manual seating pressure, which may have introduced minor variability in cement thickness. In addition, fracture resistance was evaluated under static loading, whereas intraoral forces are dynamic and multi-axial in nature. The potential influence of different cement types and cement thicknesses was not investigated and warrants further exploration.

Despite these limitations, the controlled experimental design allowed for a clear evaluation of the interaction between washing and post-curing protocols. Future research should extend these findings by incorporating different resin formulations, alternative post-curing systems, and dynamic fatigue models, as well as integrating mechanical testing with microstructural and chemical analyses to better simulate clinical conditions.
