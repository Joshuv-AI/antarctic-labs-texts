Insights into the antibacterial, antifungal, and antiparasitic activities of functionalized ZnO quantum dots
Authors: Shoaib Ashiq, Ghulam Mustafa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this article, we have synthesized mercaptoacetic acid (MAA), mercaptopropionic acid (MPA), ethylene glycol (EG), and citric acid (CA) functionalized ZnO quantum dots (QDs) and the role of these QDs as antibacterial, antifungal, and antiparasitic assays is investigated. In the antibacterial trial, it is observed that the zone of inhibition (ZOI) varies with the functionalized ligand of the ZnO QDs. The antifungal activity of these ligand-coated QDs using potato dextrose agar (PDA) containing petri plates has been inspected. The maximum antifungal activity of 78.3 % is explored against Aspergillus niger by MAA functionalized ZnO QDs at an optimized concentration of 2 mg/mL. During antiparasitic measurements, the promastigote and amastigote cell cultures of L. tropica are studied in this probe, by means of the MTT assay for ligand-coated these functionalized ZnO QDs at 20 µg/mL. The parasite’s promastigote and amastigote forms each have a highest death rate of 70.31±1.16 and 68.17±0.12, respectively, which is achieved through MAA functionalized ZnO QDs, while at the same time, the minimum activity of 34.44±0.12 and 32.24±0.33 is measured in the case of CA functionalized ZnO QDs.Keywords: ZnO, Quantum dots, Antibacterial, Antifungal, AntiparasiticSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Probably the quantum dots (QDs) are amongst the most explored nanomaterials that serve the necessities of contemporary applications, such as light-emitting diodes1, lasers2, sensors3, medicine4, as well as biolabels5. These semiconducting nanomaterials are fluorescent with a size analogous to Bohr’s excitonic radius of a material6. These semiconducting QDs have particle dimensions in the nanometer realm and have very state of the art properties. Quantum states7 and confinement of excitons (electron-hole pair)8 modify their optical emission and absorption energies9. In the last decade, significant focus has been placed on the optoelectronic properties of QDs10, as their numerous fundamental characteristics depend on size11. Among the typical luminescent QDs, CdTe12, CdS13 and CdSe14 retain the finest luminescent features, but the cadmium (Cd) content put the human health at risk15. Therefore, the researchers have been trying for years to discover alternate contenders with low noxiousness like dye-doped nanoparticles (NPs)16, carbon QDs17,18, metal NPs19, up-conversion luminescent nanoparticles20, and ZnO QDs21.

ZnO is a biologically safe22 semiconducting material, with an excellent potential to replace the traditional Cd-containing substances in the biological sciences. The defect-related luminescent properties of ZnO QDs can simply be destroyed by water molecules23. Therefore, surface modifications are mandatory to uphold luminescence charisma of ZnO QDs. The photophysical characteristics of ZnO NPs are excellent24, and their surfaces can be functionalized with ease. Upon considerate modification, these become extremely stable in water25, and the quantum yield of these materials can be enhanced to 30% (in some cases to 85%)26. Hence, ZnO QDs are found to be low-cost27, low toxicity28, and eco-friendly fabrication29, that are likely to be acted well in biological applications30.

Nowadays, through a variety of methods, including the release of antibacterial agents, the inclusion of antibacterial groups, and stimulus-responsive antibacterial properties, antibacterial materials can now achieve exceptional antibacterial effects. These mechanisms are the best alternatives to antibiotics31. Antibacterial materials can lead to the forfeiture of metabolites and nutrients, and ultimately obliterate cellular DNA32. As antibacterial activity and reliable mechanical properties are desirable for dental materials, ZnO QDs offer bioactivity33, biocompatibility34 and inhibition of metalloproteinases35. Furthermore, Zn is crucial to about 300 catalytic enzymes and is involved in cell differentiation36 and help wound healing37. Cytotoxicity of ZnO QDs is greater in the case of small sizes38, and these QDs are not agglomerated like other traditional NPs39, eluding the perturbation about the assimilation into resins40. The main factors for antibacterial activity of ZnO include phase, crystallite size41, lattice constants42, surface defects43 as well as crystal orientation44.

The US Foods and Drug Administration (FDA)45 has documented ZnO as safe when used as a nutrient, and regarded the use of ZnO as a filter that protects against UVB46 and UVA47. They are also reported to show extensive anti-Candidal activity through various cellular and molecular mechanisms48. Despite the marvelous antifungal activity of ZnO QDs, their mechanism of action in Candida cells is still under investigation. In last 10 years, nanotechnologists have efficiently applied QDs to cut down the growth of several fungal pathogens, such as Candida albicans, Aspergillus niger, and Fusarium graminearum49,50. A detailed analysis of the mechanism of antifungal activity of ZnO QDs can affect the cellular and molecular determinants of drug resistance51.

In this work, to the best of our knowledge, we report the first comprehensive investigation of the effects of four structurally different surface ligands on the biological efficacy of ZnO QDs. These ligands include mercaptoacetic acid (MAA), mercaptopropionic acid (MPA), ethylene glycol (EG), and citric acid (CA). In contrast to previous research that mostly focused on bare ZnO nanoparticles or single ligand functionalized systems. The distinctive and novel contribution of this work is the evaluation of antibacterial, antifungal, and antiparasitic (antileishmanial) activities in a single study, along with a multi-ligand comparative approach.

Material and methods

Chemicals

All the chemicals are used as obtained without any prior treatment, and deionized water is used to carry out all the aqueous phase measurements. The percentage purity of all the chemicals is as given: methanol (CH3OH, ≥99.7%; Sigma-Aldrich), zinc acetate (Zn(CH3COO)2, ≥99%; Sigma-Aldrich), potassium hydroxide (KOH, ≥85%; Sigma-Aldrich), mercaptoacetic acid (HSCH2COOH, 90%; Sigma-Aldrich), mercaptopropionic acid (HSCH2CH2COOH, ≥98%; Sigma-Aldrich), ethylene glycol ((CH2OH)2, ≥90%; Sigma-Aldrich), citric acid (C6H8O7, 99%; Sigma-Aldrich), ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥99.8%; Sigma-Aldrich), acetone (CH3COCH3, ≥99.5%; Sigma-Aldrich).

Synthesis of ZnO QDs

The synthesis method for ZnO QDs has been explained in detail in following the directions mentioned in previous studies52. Herein briefly, we are reporting the synthesis of ZnO QDs with four different functionalized ligands i.e., MAA, MPA, EG, and CA. Typically, synthesis of ZnO is carried out by first mixing aqueous zinc acetate (0.1 M) and mercaptoacetic acid (0.1 mM) in a flask and stir for 20 minutes at 60 ℃. Then the pH of the solution is settled to 11 by the dropwise addition of KOH solution in methanol (0.1 M). The subsequent mixture is refluxed for almost 2 hours, and the product is precipitated by using ethanol. After that, the final product is washed repeatedly, first with deionized water and then with acetone to completely remove the inorganic and organic impurities, respectively. The same practice is adopted for the synthesis of ZnO QDs, functionalized with other ligands. The synthesized ZnO QDs are dried at 70℃ in an oven, and the powdered form is stored in a plastic vial for further measurements.

Characterization

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopic study is accomplished by using a BRUKER TENSOR-Ⅱ FTIR spectrometer to approve the functionalization of the synthesized ZnO QDs in the range between 4000 and 400 cm−1. PANalytical X-ray diffractometer (XRD) model 3040/60 X’Pert PRO having CuKα (λ = 1.54 Å) is operated at 45 kV and 40 mA, over an angle range of 10–80° to verify the crystal structure of ZnO. The UV-Vis absorption measurements are performed by using a Shimadzu-1601 spectrophotometer in the wavelength region of 200–800 nm. Steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) measurements are noted by using the PicoQuant, Germany, FluoTime 300 (FT-300) time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) setup. Further aspects of the measurements of the SSPL spectra can be found in the reported work of Mubeen et al56,57. JSM-6460 LV energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) and high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM), Philips CM 30, JEOL, JEM-2100F are employed for elemental analysis and to investigate the particle size of the synthesised ZnO QDs, respectively.

Results and discussion

FTIR measurements of synthesized ZnO QDs using four separate functionalizing ligands, MAA, MPA, EG, and CA, are taken for each sample to detect functional groups and verify surface functionalization as depicted in all the panels of Figure 1. The major characteristic peaks in all the FTIR spectra fall in the region from 400 to 4000 cm−1. More precisely, the broadened peaks lying between 3200-3500 cm−1 are assigned to O-H bond stretching58 in all the four spectra displayed in Figure 1 panel (a), (b), (c), and (d). The C-H vibrational mode of the methylene (-CH2) group is responsible for the small and less intense peaks in the region 2900-3000 cm−1.

FTIR spectra of ZnO QDs with MAA (a), MPA (b), EG (c), and CA (d) as functionalizing ligands, along with bare (e) ZnO QDs.

The vibrational modes of carbonyl groups, C=O, are displayed by the peaks in the 1600–1800 cm−1 region for MAA (Figure 1 (a)), MPA (Figure 1 (b)), and CA (Figure 1 (d))52. There is no carbonyl bond stretching peak in the ascribed region for EG (Figure 1 (c)) as EG doesn’t have any carbonyl functionality in its structure. In all these spectra, the distinctive peak at about 840 cm−1 is attributed to the bending vibrational mode of C-H bonds of functionalizing ligands attached to the surface of ZnO QDs. The characteristic ZnO vibrational peak has been distinctly identified in the region between 430–570 cm⁻1 with center at 500 cm−1 which is very well in accordance with the reported literature59,60. However, the absence of the S-H bond peak in the area between 2500-2650 cm−1 for MAA, and MPA fortifies the bonding interaction between ZnO QDs and these functionalizing ligands. In the matter of EG and CA, the interacting site is the hydroxyl group (-OH). The absence of any stretching vibrational peak in the functional group region of FTIR spectrum of bare ZnO QDs (Figure 1 (e)) indicates the absence of anchoring of any capping ligand to the surface of QDs. The broad peak centered at 3400 cm−1 is due to adsorption of water molecules at the surface of bare ZnO QDs. These observations verify the successful surface functionalization of ZnO QDs by MAA molecules.

XRD analysis is performed to ascertain the average crystallite size and crystal phase purity of the QDs. The XRD pattern of these synthesized ZnO QDs reveals the formation of hexagonal (Zincite) crystal structure at room temperature, as depicted in Figure 2.

XRD patterns of hexagonal (JCPDS: 01–079-0208) ZnO QDs with MAA (a), MPA (b), EG (c), and CA (d) as functionalizing ligands.

The absence of any impurity peak confirms the purity of synthesized ZnO QDs. Moreover, the broadening of peaks in the XRD pattern endorses nanoscale size, and a minor shift in peaks proves surface functionalization effects. These XRD patterns of each QDs exhibit seven prominent peaks observed at 2θ values of 31.6°, 34.4°, 36.1°, 47.4°, 56.3°,62.7°, and 68.3°. These peaks can be indexed with the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), and (201) diffraction peaks of the hexagonal structure for the crystalline phase of the ZnO QDs (JCPDS reference card no. 01–079-0208)52. The crystalline nature and ‘hkl’ parameters are well corresponding to the standard values and well matched with the previous studies61. With functionalizing ligands comprising MAA, MPA, EG, and CA, no significant difference in the XRD pattern is noticed except for the minor fluctuations in the broadening and intensity of the peaks. The formulation of ultra-small-sized QDs is confirmed by the peak broadening. Moreover, the assessed crystallite sizes (in nm) estimated from Scherrer’s formula62 are 4.30 nm, 4.77 nm, 5.13 nm, and 6.64 nm with functionalizing ligands MAA, MPA, EG, and CA, respectively.

To further strengthen the synthesis and functionalization, the absorption spectra of these QDs are measured at room temperature, by using water as the reference solvent as displayed in Figure 3 (a). The UV–visible absorption spectra of QDs change noticeably after surface capping with a ligand because the ligand modifies the surface states, particle growth, dispersion stability, and electronic environment of the QDs. Bare ZnO QDs exhibited a broad absorption profile with noticeable tailing toward the visible region, indicating surface defects and particle agglomeration. After surface functionalization, the absorption edge became sharper and slightly blue-shifted, suggesting improved surface passivation, reduced defect density, and enhanced quantum confinement due to ligand-controlled particle growth. With each functionalizing ligand, absorption-excitonic peak for ZnO QDs has appeared in 330 nm to 335 nm region, corresponding to the bandgap energy value extending from 3.90 to 4.09 eV whereas bare ZnO QDs display absorption edge at 350 nm with corresponding bandgap energy of 3.50 eV, as depicted in Figure 3 (b).

(a) UV-Vis absorption spectra, and (b) Tauc’s plots of bare ZnO QDs along with MAA, MPA, EG, and CA as functionalizing ligands.

With four different functionalizing ligands, the observation of distinct and sharp absorption peaks proposes that these synthesized QDs exhibit stable and monodispersed character in aqueous medium. The bulk ZnO has a bandgap energy of 3.37 eV or less, consistent to an absorption wavelength of 368 nm63,64. A noticeable blue shift in the absorption edge compared to bulk is detected with various functionalizing ligands, clearly indicating the quantum confinement effect. It also demonstrates the functionalizing aptitude of different ligands with ZnO QDs. Due to quantum confinement, the dimensions of QDs are reduced, and the bandgap energy rises. The quantum confinement effect dominates when the size of the particle becomes equivalent to or even less than the Bohr’s excitonic radius.

TEM and HRTEM micrographs of synthesized MAA functionalized ZnO QDs displayed in Figure 4 (a) and (b), respectively, reveal the particle morphology, synthesis of ultra small sized QDs, dispersion behavior, and confirms the surface functionalization. The aggregation of these QDs appeared in TEM micrograph is attributed to the existence of highly polar carboxylic acid group (-COOH) on the surface of these QDs. This confirms the successful functionalization of synthesized ZnO QDs. The average particle size of MAA functionalized ZnO QDs estimated from the TEM micrograph is assessed to be 4.26 nm, which is in good agreement with the size anticipated by aforementioned XRD measurements, and also the previously reported work52,61,65.

(a) TEM micrograph, (b) HRTEM micrograph, and (c) EDX analysis of MAA functionalized ZnO QDs.

The lattice structure of MAA functionalized ZnO QDs is exposed by the HRTEM micrograph (Figure. 4 (b)), that matches well to a d-spacing of about 0.22 nm of the (101) crystal plane. EDX analysis (Figure 4 (c)) verifies the successful synthesizes of MAA functionalized ZnO QDs in an extremely pure form.

When molecules are excited by using a constant source of electromagnetic radiation, they exhibit photoluminescence upon recombination of electron-hole pairs. The emitted photon intensity is detected as a function of wavelength, subsequently, a steady-state fluorescence spectrum is obtained.

Following the excitation at 306 nm, the ZnO QDs exhibit a strong PL emission at 520 nm due to the radiative recombination of electron and hole pair from conduction and valance bands, respectively. The PL intensity of synthesized ZnO QDs gradually moves from 495 nm to 525 nm as depicted in Figure 5, which is in accordance with the previously reported literature52. The PL spectrum of bare ZnO QDs exhibit strong broad visible emission attributed to surface defects and trap-state recombination. After capping with the ligand, the defect-related emission intensity significantly decreased, while the near-band-edge emission became more pronounced. This behavior confirms effective surface passivation by the capping ligand, which suppresses non-radiative recombination pathways and enhances the optical quality of the QDs.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial potential of the synthesized ZnO QDs against Escherichia coli is assessed using the agar well diffusion method. This test measures the zone of inhibition (ZOI) surrounding a test material-containing well, which indicates the QDs’ capacity to inhibit bacterial growth. The antibacterial potential of the synthesized QDs is revealed in Figure 6. Differently functionalized ZnO QDs are used to treat Escherichia coli, and the ZOI is determined. The maximum ZOI is 12.3 mm at 500 µg/mL of MAA, trailed 11.1 mm by MPA, 10.1 mm by EG, and 7.2 mm by CA functionalized ZnO QDs against Escherichia coli (Table 1). In all cases, functionalized ZnO QDs exhibit significantly enhanced biological activities compared to bare ZnO QDs supporting the conclusion that surface functionalization is the key driver of improved activity. It has been observed from the results that ZOI varies with functionalized ligand of the ZnO QDs. In antibacterial trial, the first wall of resistance is the membrane to defend the bacteria from the foreign agent.

The ligands perform a crucial role in augmenting the antibacterial activity of QDs by improving their stability, dispersibility, and interaction with bacterial cell membranes66. The greater surface to volume ratio of QDs is responsible for the creation of more reactive species. Functional groups on the ligand surface further facilitate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or interrupt membrane integrity, increasing the QDs’ bactericidal effectiveness67. The antimicrobial activity of ZnO QDs is primarily attributed to ROS generation, including hydroxyl radicals and superoxide ions, which induce oxidative stress in microbial cells. Additionally, electrostatic interactions between positively charged ZnO surfaces and negatively charged microbial membranes lead to membrane disruption. To strength these mechanistic claims, previous studies have used ROS detection assays (like the DCFH-DA fluorescence probe) and zeta potential measurements to illustrate that ROS results in oxidative stress and that the surface charge of ZnO nanoparticles causes membrane disruption68,69. These reactive ions are causing the DNA damaging, lysis of protein, and denaturation of enzymes and ultimately cause the bacterial death70. In our findings the concentration based antibacterial activities of ZnO QDs are examined which are allied to the earlier studies71,72. Notably, bare (non-functionalized) ZnO QDs demonstrate lower antibacterial activity, with a ZOI of about 6.5 mm against E. coli at 500 µg/mL. This indicates that ligand functionalization greatly improves the biological effectiveness of ZnO QDs. This finding is consistent with earlier studies revealing that the surface functionalization of ZnO nanostructures significantly enhances their interaction with bacterial membranes, thereby increasing their antimicrobial efficacy73,74.

Antifungal activity

Potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates are used to measure the antifungal activity against Aspergillus niger. This test provides a quantitative assessment of each functionalized QD’s antifungal potency by calculating the percentage growth inhibition of the fungal colony over successive time intervals as displayed in Figure 7. The maximum antifungal activity of 78.3% is observed with MAA-functionalized ZnO QDs at an optimized concentration of 2 mg/mL, followed by 69.1% with MPA, 55.0% with EG, and 48.0% with CA, as presented in table 2. QDs are extra ordinary reactive and stable species due to their larger surface area and smaller size49.

The accretion may also be affected by the physiochemical features, such as surface charge, size, and Brownian movement. The antifungal properties of QDs stem from the creation of ROS. These QDs cause holes in the wall and alter the structure of the plasma membrane, including depolarization, fluidity, and permeability. As a result, they get entry into the cytoplasm unhindered and cause the disruption in the structure of DNA, RNA, and enzymes. These antifungal effects are in line with how ZnO-based nanomaterials work. For example, ROS-induced oxidative stress damages fungal membrane lipids, and direct electrostatic interactions between the nanoparticle surface and the negatively charged fungal cell wall make the membrane more permeable and cause intracellular leakage75,76. The ligand functionalization of the current ZnO quantum dots probably changes these effects by changing the surface charge, making them easier to spread out, and making them better able to stick to microbial cell surfaces. These mechanistic pathways align with previously documented experimental findings, including ROS detection assays and membrane integrity assessments conducted on ZnO-based nanomaterials targeting fungal pathogens77–79.

Antileishmanial activity

The MTT cell viability assay was used to assess the functionalized ZnO QDs’ antiparasitic potential against Leishmania tropica. By using metabolic activity as a stand-in for cell viability, this colorimetric assay makes it possible to calculate the inhibition concentration (IC50) for both promastigote and amastigote forms of the parasite. Leishmaniasis is considered to be an exceptionally rare, contagious tropical and subtropical disease parasite, primarily Leishmania species. According to the report of the World Health Organization, there are 1.6 to 3.0 million new cases worldwide reported every year due to this parasite. Phlebotomus and Lutzomyia sandflies bite human beings and transfer an infectious intracellular parasite, responsible for disease. The uncontrollable spreading of this disease is probably by means of an inappropriate vector, and both reasonable and ineffective therapy. As mentioned in Table 2, the promastigote and amastigote cell cultures of L. tropica are studied in this probe, by means of the MTT assay for various ligand-coated ZnO QDs at 20 µg/mL. The promastigote and amastigote forms of the parasite, each have a highest death rate of 70.31±1.16 and 68.17±0.12 respectively is achieved through MAA functionalized ZnO QDs, while the lowest activity of 34.44±0.12 and 32.24±0.33 against promastigote and amastigote is performed by CA functionalized ZnO QDs. Functionalized ligands can enhance the anti-leishmanial activity of ZnO QDs by improving their stability, cellular uptake, and controlled Zn2⁺ ion release, in accordance with the previously reported work by Mahmoud et al80. They also reduce toxicity to host cells, increasing selectivity toward the parasite.

This comparison shows that the MAA-functionalized ZnO QDs made in this study have biological activity that is either competitive or better than that of several reported literature as depicted in table 3. This is especially true for antifungal (78.3% inhibition against Aspergillus niger) and antileishmanial (70.31% promastigote inhibition) activities. The multi-ligand comparative approach exhibited in this study also adds a new dimension that hasn’t been reported in previous work.

Conclusion

MAA, MPA, EG, and CA functionalized ZnO QDs were successfully synthesized and identified by various spectroscopic methods such as FTIR, XRD, UV-Visible, TEM, HRTEM, EDX, and SSPL. While measuring the antibacterial activities, the maximum ZOI was 12.3 mm at 500 µg/mL of MAA, trailed 11.1 mm by MPA, 10.1 mm by EG, and 7.2 mm by CA functionalized ZnO QDs against E. coli. It was observed that ZOI varies with the nature of the functionalized ligand of the ZnO QDs. This could be because of the fact that the ligand offered a crucial role in enhancing the antibacterial activity of these QDs by improving their stability, dispersibility, and interaction with the bacterial cell membrane. The maximum antifungal assay of 78.3% was examined against Aspergillus niger by MAA functionalized ZnO QDs at an optimized concentration of 2 mg/mL, followed by 69.1% by MPA, 55.0% by EG and 48.0% by CA functionalized ZnO QDs. Because of their smaller size and greater surface area, these QDs instigated holes in the wall and altered the structure of the plasma membrane, and consequently, they entered the cytoplasm and triggered the disruption in the structure of DNA, RNA, and enzymes. The parasite’s promastigote and amastigote forms each had a highest death rate of 70.31±1.16 and 68.17±0.12, respectively, is achieved through MAA functionalized ZnO QDs, while the lowest activity of 34.44±0.12 and 32.24±0.33 against promastigote and amastigote was performed by CA functionalized ZnO QDs. These functionalized ligands assisted the enhancement in the anti-leishmanial activity of ZnO QDs by improving their stability, cellular uptake, and controlled Zn2⁺ ion release. They also reduced toxicity to host cells, increasing selectivity toward the parasite. Among all these functionalizing ligands, MAA proved to be the most suitable functionalizing ligand of ZnO QDs for the measurement of biological activities. In future, this work can be designed to prove that ligand-functionalized ZnO QDs can be used against a wide range of pathogens, including drug-resistant bacterial strains, pathogenic fungi, and other Leishmania species. This framework needs to be expanded to include these pathogens. Additionally, integrating ZnO quantum dots with targeted drug delivery systems or photocatalytic activation methods may reveal synergistic mechanisms, signifying a promising avenue for the advancement of next-generation nanomedicines founded on functionalized QDs systems.
