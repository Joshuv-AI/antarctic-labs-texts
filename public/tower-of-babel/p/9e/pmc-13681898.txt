Early detection of myocardial fibrosis in patients with coronary microvascular dysfunction in the absence of late enhancement: role of T1 mapping and extracellular volume analysis
Authors: Runze Wen, Qiang Xie, Bo Pan, Xingxing Zhu, Dan Li, Xuemei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) can exacerbate myocardial fibrosis, which may further impair microvascular function. However, the relationship between CMD and the extracellular volume fraction (ECV) in patients with ischemia with no obstructive coronary artery disease (INOCA) who are late gadolinium enhancement (LGE)-negative remains undetermined. We used T1 mapping and ECV to assess diffuse myocardial tissue abnormalities and extracellular compartment expansion in LGE-negative patients with CMD. We then investigated how these markers correlate with global myocardial flow reserve (MFR).MethodsBetween August 2024 and May 2026, we prospectively enrolled 86 LGE-negative patients with INOCA from a single center who underwent a positron emission tomography−magnetic resonance imaging (PET-MRI) protocol. Patients with significant epicardial stenosis (stenosis on coronary angiogram or computed tomography ≥ 50%), prior history of revascularization, known myocardial infarction, or heart failure were excluded. MFR and ECV were derived from PET perfusion imaging and pre- and post-T1 mapping, respectively. CMD was defined as PET-derived MFR of < 2.0 in at least one major coronary artery territory.ResultsAmong the 86 patients included, 39 (45.3%) were diagnosed with CMD. ECV was substantially higher in the CMD group (31.22 [29.93, 33.25]% vs. 26.66 [25.85, 27.55]%, P < 0.001), with a more modest but statistically significant elevation in native T1 (1225 ± 25 ms vs. 1212 ± 21 ms, P = 0.012). Multivariable linear regression revealed that only global MFR (β [95% confidence interval] = − 1.905 [− 2.489, − 1.321], P < 0.001) was an independent predictor of ECV in LGE-negative CMD patients. In the overall cohort, resting myocardial blood flow (MBF) (r = 0.539, P < 0.001) and global MFR (r = − 0.741, P < 0.001) were positively and negatively correlated with ECV, respectively. When participants were stratified into ECV tertiles, global MFR showed a progressive decrease (2.92 vs. 2.48 vs. 1.78; P < 0.001 for trend) with increasing ECV values.ConclusionsIn patients with INOCA with CMD, prolonged native T1 and elevated ECV indicate diffuse interstitial expansion, consistent with early tissue remodeling, even when LGE is absent on CMR. These findings suggest that native T1 and ECV may hold promise as adjunctive tissue markers to support risk stratification, though their role in clinical management requires further validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02597-5.Keywords: Coronary microvascular dysfunction, Late gadolinium enhancement, Extracellular volume fraction, Cardiac magnetic resonance, Myocardial flow reserve

Introduction

The coronary circulation comprises two main components: the epicardial coronary arteries and the coronary microcirculation. Although epicardial coronary artery disease (CAD) has long been regarded as the primary cause of myocardial ischemia, no obstructive CAD is detected in more than 50% of patients with stable angina who undergo elective invasive coronary angiography [1]. Among these patients presenting with ischemic symptoms and no obstructive coronary artery disease (INOCA) [2], a heterogeneous population with multiple underlying etiologies, approximately 50–65% are reported to have coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) [3, 4]. Invasive physiological assessment remains the reference standard for subtyping CMD. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) reflects global vasodilator capacity by integrating resting and hyperemic flow, while the index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) specifically quantifies distal microvascular resistance. Together they distinguish flow-predominant abnormalities from resistance-driven dysfunction, forming the basis of formal pathophysiological classification. The coronary microcirculation plays a critical role in regulating myocardial blood flow, and its dysfunction has been implicated in a wide range of cardiovascular disorders, including heart failure with preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (HFpEF) and ischemic heart disease [5, 6]. CMD disrupts the transport of oxygen and nutrients to the myocardium, resulting in tissue hypoxia, cellular injury, and ultimately cardiomyocyte death [7]. Furthermore, CMD can exacerbate myocardial fibrosis, which in turn creates a vicious cycle by further compromising microvascular function. Myocardial fibrosis is closely linked to impaired ventricular function, adverse cardiac remodeling, and increased myocardial stiffness. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is currently considered the gold-standard non-invasive imaging modality for the detection of focal myocardial fibrosis because of its superior contrast resolution, high spatial resolution, and complete myocardial coverage [8]. However, LGE has limited sensitivity for diffuse myocardial fibrosis, as the relative differences in gadolinium concentration between fibrotic and normal myocardium become less distinguishable in diffuse disease processes. Consequently, early pathological alterations may remain undetected using conventional LGE imaging.

More recently developed techniques, including native T1 mapping and extracellular volume (ECV) quantification, may offer distinct advantages over conventional LGE imaging by allowing for more accurate quantification of diffuse myocardial fibrosis and facilitating the detection of early fibrosis-related myocardial changes [9, 10]. These modalities demonstrate excellent agreement with histopathological findings obtained from endomyocardial biopsy and, unlike LGE, are not dependent on regional variations in image contrast [11, 12]. Elevated myocardial ECV values measured by CMR have also been associated with increased all-cause mortality and may possess prognostic value comparable to that of LV ejection fraction [13]. Histologically, myocardial ECV reflects the fractional volume of the myocardial extracellular matrix, particularly collagen deposition, and may therefore serve as a promising non-invasive biomarker of diffuse extracellular matrix fibrosis. Myocardial fibrosis and INOCA-related cardiac dysfunction arise from multiple diverse etiologies. Prior respiratory viral infection is one underappreciated trigger. Acute viral exposure may induce mild myocardial inflammation, which can slowly progress to interstitial fibrosis and raise long-term heart failure risk [14]. This multi-source pathological background further underscores the utility of sensitive CMR tissue markers for early detection of subtle myocardial remodeling. Despite these advances, the relationship between CMD and myocardial ECV in patients with INOCA remains incompletely understood.

To date, few studies have systematically evaluated native myocardial T1 values and ECV in patients with and without CMD, particularly among individuals without detectable LGE on CMR. Accordingly, the present study aimed to compare myocardial native T1 values and ECV between LGE-negative patients with and without CMD, to investigate whether CMD is associated with diffuse myocardial tissue abnormalities and extracellular compartment expansion, and to examine the relationships between myocardial tissue characteristics and positron emission tomography (PET) perfusion parameters in this population. We hypothesized that native T1 values and ECV would be significantly elevated in LGE-negative patients with CMD compared with those without CMD, reflecting early subclinical expansion of the myocardial extracellular compartment and underlying interstitial tissue changes.

Methods

Study population

Consecutive patients aged 18 to 75 years who presented with chest pain or exertional dyspnea suspected of myocardial ischemia with no obstructive epicardial CAD on invasive or computed tomographic coronary angiography within the past year were enrolled between August 2024 and May 2026. Obstructive epicardial CAD was defined as ≥50% luminal stenosis or fractional flow reserve ≤0.8. Supplementary Fig. 1 outlines the in- and exclusion criteria.

Prior to the PET-MRI, participants were instructed to abstain for 24 h from caffeine or food containing significant amounts of methylxanthines (coffee, tea, chocolate, cola and bananas) as confirmed by the study. Medications containing dipyridamole were held for 48 h; long-lasting nitroglycerines, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers (CCB), and diuretics for 24 h; and short-acting nitrates for 1 h before the examination. This was done to prevent confounding effects on pharmacologically induced hyperemia. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (IRB No. 2024KYER-232). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Baseline data collection

Baseline data for all participants, including demographic characteristics, laboratory test results, medical history, and relevant clinical data, was obtained from the electronic medical record system of the hospital. Prior to the PET-MRI examination, blood samples were collected from participants for routine laboratory testing, including HbA1c, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and blood lipids. Blood samples were processed according to standard operating procedures of the central clinical laboratory of the hospital. All concurrent medications were documented, including antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers, CCB, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, nitrates, statins, and oral hypoglycemic agents.

Cardiac PET‑MRI protocol

All imaging was performed on an integrated 3.0 T PET-MRI system (Biograph mMR, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Each participant underwent both rest and regadenoson-induced hyperemia stress myocardial perfusion imaging.

PET protocol

Prior to perfusion imaging, scout images were obtained, followed by attenuation correction scans using a two-point Dixon MRI sequence. 13N-ammonia PET myocardial perfusion imaging was performed using the following methods. For rest PET imaging, 370 MBq (10 mCi) of 13N-ammonia was administered intravenously into an antecubital vein. Simultaneously, a 10-min dynamic PET list-mode acquisition was started immediately upon tracer injection. Stress imaging was performed approximately one hour later using identical acquisition parameters to the rest study, except that regadenoson was administered 10–20 s before the 13N-ammonia injection. Regadenoson (400 µg) was administered in a single intravenous bolus (< 10 s) via an antecubital cannula, followed by 5 mL normal saline flush. Dynamic PET images were reconstructed using three-dimensional ordered-subset expectation-maximization (3D-OSEM) with 6 iterations and 21 subsets, a 5-mm Gaussian post-reconstruction filter, and a dynamic frame definition of 12 × 10 s, 2 × 30 s, 4 × 60 s, and 1 × 180 s.

CMR protocol

All CMR acquisitions included cine imaging, LGE, and native and post-contrast T1 mapping. The scan protocol was performed according to the standardized protocols of the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (SCMR) [15]. Initial axial, coronal, and sagittal 2D localizer images were acquired for cardiac localization. Briefly, ECG-gated 2D balanced steady-state free-precession (bSSFP) cine imaging was performed during multiple breath holds in vertical long-axis (2-chamber), horizontal long-axis (4-chamber), and contiguous short-axis views (parameters: echo time, 1.4 ms; 15 segments; segment duration, 45 ms; repetition time, 2.8 ms without view sharing; slice thickness, 8 mm; field of view, 256 × 256 mm; and pixel size, 1.4 × 1.4 mm).

The T1 mapping mode was selected as cardiac native and cardiac enhanced for pre and post contrast T1 mapping scan, respectively. Native T1 measurements were performed using a Shortened Modified Look-Locker Inversion (ShMOLLI) sequence at left ventricular (LV) basal, mid, and apical levels with a 5(1)1(1)1 sampling scheme and motion correction [16]. Gadopentetate dimeglumine (Beijing Beilu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) (0.2 mmol/kg) was administered intravenously, followed by a 15 mL saline flush. Post-contrast Modified Look-Locker Inversion (MOLLI) scans were performed 10–15 min after gadolinium bolus injection using a 4(1)3(1)2 sampling scheme. Scanning parameters of T1 mapping before contrast agent injection were as follows: bSSFP single-shot readout with a 35° excitation flip angle, rate-2 parallel imaging, matrix size 192 × 144, pixel size 1.3 mm × 1.3 mm, slice thickness 8 mm, minimum inversion time (TI) of 180 ms with heart rate-dependent TI increments (1 RR interval per step), TE/TR 1.1 ms/2.8 ms. T1 imaging parameters after contrast agent were as follows: bSSFP single-shot readout with a 35° excitation flip angle, rate-2 parallel imaging, matrix size 192 × 144, pixel size 1.3 mm × 1.3 mm, slice thickness 8 mm, minimum TI of 100 ms with heart rate-dependent TI increments, TE/TR 1.1 ms/2.5 ms.

LGE images were acquired using a 2D phase-sensitive inversion-recovery (PSIR) TurboFLASH gradient-echo sequence during breath-hold, 10–15 min after gadolinium administration. Sequence parameters were as follows: TR/TE/flip angle, 8.0 ms/2.0 ms/25°; voxel size, 1.4 → 1.4 × 8.0 mm.

Optimal ECG gating and breath-holding were verified throughout scanning, and raw images and error maps were inspected in real time for artifacts, with immediate repeat scanning performed for suboptimal acquisitions [17].

Post-processing and imaging analysis

PET image interpretation

All PET images were independently reviewed by two nuclear medicine physicians with ≥10 years of experience in cardiac PET imaging using dedicated software (Syngo.via, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Relative perfusion images were interpreted semi-quantitatively using the standard 5-point visual scoring system and American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model. Global summed stress score (SSS), summed rest score (SRS), and summed difference score (SDS, calculated as SSS minus SRS) were derived to quantify perfusion abnormalities.

Myocardial blood flow (MBF) in mL/min/g was quantified from dynamic images acquired at rest and peak hyperaemia. Resting MBF was not normalized to rate-pressure product (RPP), a widely used surrogate of myocardial oxygen demand. While resting heart rate and blood pressure were documented at baseline enrollment, synchronized, time-aligned hemodynamic measurements were not acquired continuously during the rest and stress PET perfusion acquisitions. Given the absence of RPP values strictly matched to the dynamic flow sampling windows for both rest and hyperemic states across the full cohort, unadjusted MBF values are reported to avoid systematic bias introduced by mismatched hemodynamic sampling. Global myocardial flow reserve (MFR) was calculated as the ratio of global stress MBF to global rest MBF for the entire left ventricle. For CMD classification, regional MFR was calculated separately for each of the three major coronary supply territories, and CMD was defined as an MFR value < 2.0 in at least one territory. This non-invasive per-territory definition serves as a surrogate marker and is not equivalent to invasive CMD assessment based on thermodilution-derived CFR and IMR. Global left ventricular MFR, averaged across the entire left ventricular myocardium, was used for correlation analyses and ordered group stratification [18].

CMR image analysis

CMR data analysis was independently performed by two experienced cardiovascular radiologists who were blinded to all clinical and PET findings. CMR images were processed using the commercial available post-processing software CVI42 (version 5.11.1, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc.). LV endocardium, epicardium, and blood pool were manually delineated to define the myocardium in accordance with the standardized post-processing guidelines of the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (SCMR) [19]. Manual epi- and endocardial contours were traced using a 20% border-cutting algorithm. Six myocardial segments per short-axis slice were automatically delineated using the right ventricular insertion points as landmarks. Slices containing the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) were excluded. The papillary muscles, moderator bands, and epicardial fat were carefully excluded from all contours.

T1 values and ECV were measured in the 16-segment AHA model (excluding the apical cap) and global LV, by contouring the endocardium and epicardium on native and post-contrast T1 mapping images with hematocrit correction. Global T1 and ECV values were used for further analysis. Inter-observer averages for T1 and ECV were used as the final measurements. Hematocrit was measured in venous blood samples obtained within 3 days before the CMR examination for ECV calculation using the following standard formula: ECV = (1 – hematocrit) × [Δ(1/T1) myocardial/Δ(1/T1) blood pool] [20]. ECV values were expressed as percentages for all analyses. End-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were automatically derived from the cine images and corrected for body surface area (BSA).

LGE images were visually assessed for the presence of infarct or fibrosis according to the AHA 17-segment LV model [21]. Myocardial LGE was defined as focal hyperenhancement relative to remote normal myocardium, confirmed in at least two orthogonal views (both short-axis and at least one long-axis view). The same two blinded radiologists independently evaluated the presence and anatomical location of LGE, with consensus reached for any discrepant cases [19].

Intra-observer variability was assessed by re-analyzing 20 randomly selected participants at least 1 month apart by the same observer. Inter-observer variability was determined using the initial independent readings from both radiologists. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to quantify both inter- and intra-observer agreement for all quantitative and qualitative CMR parameters.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test, as appropriate. Categorical data, expressed as counts and percentages, were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous data, expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data, were compared using Student t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, respectively. For overall intergroup comparisons across three ordered strata, one-way analysis of variance was applied to normally distributed variables and the Kruskal-Wallis H test to skewed variables, to generate overall P values. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to explore the potential correlations if the variables had normal distribution and Spearman rank correlation analysis was used if the variables had skewed distributed. Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with elevated ECV. Variables for inclusion in the multivariable model were selected based on clinical plausibility and statistical significance in univariable analysis; a threshold of P < 0.10 was used to avoid premature exclusion of potential confounders. Potential multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF > 5.0 indicating significant collinearity. Participants were stratified into ECV tertiles to test for differences between groups. Linear trends across ordered strata were evaluated using variable-specific statistical tests. The Cochran–Armitage test was applied to assess trends in proportions for categorical variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, linear regression with the ordered group term treated as a continuous predictor was used to test for linear trend. For skewed continuous variables, natural log transformation was first applied to approximate normality, and trend testing was then performed using linear regression on the transformed values. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was made, as these trend analyses were exploratory in nature.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and Graphpad Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 101 consecutive patients underwent integrated PET-MRI at our institution. Among them, 9 patients were excluded for the following reasons: inadequate image quality (n = 6), claustrophobia (n = 2), and gadolinium-based contrast allergy (n = 1). This left 92 patients with complete PET-MRI examinations. Subsequently, an additional 6 patients were excluded from the final analysis due to insufficient myocardial tracer uptake (n = 2) and the presence of significant LGE (n = 4). A final cohort of 86 LGE-negative INOCA patients was analyzed in this study.

The median age of the entire cohort was 56 years (interquartile range 48 to 61 years); 50% of participants were female. These patients were divided into two groups based on per-territory MFR criteria: the CMD group (n = 39) and the non-CMD group (n = 47). The patient selection process is detailed in Fig. 1. Baseline demographics, cardiac risk factors, and concurrent medications of both groups are summarized in Table 1. The prevalence of diabetes was significantly higher in the CMD group than in the non-CMD group (P = 0.022). In addition, resting heart rate (HR) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) were significantly higher in the CMD group than in the non-CMD group (P = 0.017 for HR, P = 0.036 for SBP). No significant differences were observed in any other baseline clinical variables between the two groups (all P > 0.05). PET-MRI measures

Patients with and without CMD were well matched for LV function and mass (Table 2). For the whole study cohort, global MFR was negatively correlated with ECV (r = − 0.741, P < 0.001), while resting MBF was positively correlated with ECV (r = 0.539, P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between stress MBF and ECV (P = 0.187) (Fig. 4). Categorical comparison showed that patients with CMD had significantly higher resting MBF and lower MFR than non-CMD patients (1.15 [0.93, 1.42] mL/min/g vs. 0.77 [0.62, 0.93] mL/min/g, P < 0.001; 1.81 [1.44, 1.97] vs. 2.84 [2.53, 3.31], P < 0.001, respectively). Furthermore, patients with CMD had significantly longer native T1 (1225 ± 25 ms vs. 1212 ± 21 ms, P = 0.012) and higher ECV values (31.22 [29.93, 33.25] % vs. 26.66 [25.85, 27.55] %, P < 0.001) than non-CMD patients (Fig. 5).

Association between CMR-based parameters and CMD

As shown in Table 3, resting HR, SBP, diabetes, LV cardiac output (LVCO), and global MFR were all significantly associated with ECV in univariable linear regression analysis (all P < 0.10). Multivariable linear regression analysis further revealed that only global MFR (β [95% CI]: −1.905 [− 2.489, − 1.321], P < 0.001) was independently associated with ECV in LGE-negative INOCA patients.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that elevated ECV, reflecting expanded extracellular compartments and diffuse interstitial tissue changes, was the most prominent tissue signature in LGE-negative INOCA patients with CMD. A modest increase in native T1 was also observed, providing supportive evidence of diffuse myocardial tissue alteration, even in the absence of detectable focal fibrosis on LGE. In other words, in the absence of dense focal scarring, LGE imaging lacks sufficient sensitivity to detect subtle diffuse expansion of the extracellular matrix resulting from inflammation, early interstitial fibrosis, myocardial edema, or infiltrative processes. In this context, native T1 mapping and ECV measurement may offer particular diagnostic value. These observations suggest an association between CMD-related ischemia and progressive myocardial fibrosis in this patient population. Given the cross-sectional observational design of the study, however, a causal relationship cannot be definitively established, nor can the direction of effect be inferred.

In univariable and multivariable linear regression models with ECV as the dependent variable, global MFR emerged as the strongest independent correlate of elevated ECV in this LGE-negative INOCA cohort. Despite the well-established role of CMR in the diagnosis and management of INOCA, studies on CMR-related parameters in patients with INOCA remain relatively scarce. Myocardial fibrosis, a common pathological substrate for numerous cardiac diseases and a strong predictor of adverse clinical outcomes, is not routinely assessed in clinical practice because the reference standard (endomyocardial biopsy) is invasive [22]. CMR-derived ECV has been validated as a reliable noninvasive surrogate marker of myocardial fibrosis across a wide range of cardiac conditions and is consistently associated with adverse clinical outcomes [23]. However, the clinical significance and prognostic value of ECV in INOCA patients with different underlying etiologies remain poorly characterized. In contrast, Mygind et al. reported no significant association between measures of CMD and myocardial fibrosis, suggesting that CMD-induced myocardial ischemia does not promote the development of myocardial fibrosis in women with angina and no obstructive coronary artery disease [24]. This discrepancy between studies may be explained by several key factors: First, CMD is not defined uniformly across investigations. Our study uses a PET-derived MFR cut-off as a non-invasive surrogate, whereas other work may use invasive functional measures such as thermodilution-derived CFR and IMR. Second, there are differences in patient populations, disease phenotypes, and ethnic backgrounds across studies, particularly in the selection of control groups. Second, the relatively small sample sizes of both studies may have introduced statistical bias. Further large-scale, multicenter studies with standardized CMR protocols are therefore needed to resolve these conflicting findings.

Due to its high spatial resolution and soft tissue contrast, CMR is widely considered the reference standard for the assessment of cardiac function and quantitative myocardial tissue characterization [25]. ECV derived from pre- and post-contrast T1 mapping is a well-validated, reproducible measure of interstitial fibrosis that correlates closely with histological collagen volume fraction. Native T1, by contrast, is more susceptible to confounding from tissue edema, hydration status, and measurement variability, and provides adjunctive rather than definitive evidence of interstitial change. Given the lack of histological validation in our own cohort, we interpret ECV as the primary indicator of diffuse interstitial remodeling, with native T1 serving as a supportive secondary marker [26–28]. However, the discrepancy between native-T1 and post-T1 values in various studies may be due in part to differences in scanner vendors, imaging protocols, acquisition parameters, and contrast agent administration protocols, as well as patient characteristics such as ethnicity, sex, and age [29]. Our findings provide supportive evidence that ECV may serve as a useful adjunctive tissue marker for detecting interstitial tissue abnormalities in CMD patients in the absence of detectable LGE.

The coronary microcirculation plays a critical role in matching myocardial oxygen supply with metabolic demand [30]. In CMD, this homeostatic regulation is disrupted by inflammatory and structural changes, including endothelial edema, capillary rarefaction, and vascular smooth muscle proliferation, which may ultimately lead to myocardial ischemia and fibrosis [31]. The present study identified a significant positive correlation between resting MBF and ECV. It is also important to interpret elevated ECV broadly. This metric quantifies the total volume of the extracellular space. Coronary microvascular rarefaction, endothelial swelling and changes in vascular compartment volume related to CMD itself can all contribute to a higher ECV. The observed increase may therefore reflect a combination of interstitial matrix remodeling and microvascular structural changes, rather than interstitial fibrosis alone. Of note, the reduced MFR observed in this cohort may be driven in part by elevated resting myocardial blood flow, rather than by impaired hyperemic flow alone. These findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as resting MBF was not adjusted for RPP. The higher resting heart rate and systolic blood pressure observed in the CMD group may have increased myocardial oxygen demand at baseline, contributing in part to the elevated resting flow values. Accordingly, some of the between-group difference in resting MBF and the resulting MFR separation may reflect variation in cardiac workload, rather than microvascular dysfunction per se. This potential hemodynamic confound also applies to the observed positive correlation between resting MBF and ECV. Previous studies have proposed that reduced MBF may exacerbate myocardial fibrosis, which in turn further impairs microvascular perfusion, creating a vicious cycle [32, 33]. However, it is important to note that reduced MBF and myocardial fibrosis often coexist in clinical practice, and it may be necessary to conduct longitudinal studies to determine whether there is a causal relationship between these two processes.

Previous studies have reported that B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels are associated with CMD [34], as assessed by flow-mediated dilation (FMD) [35] and acetylcholine-induced forearm vasodilatation [36]. Uemura et al. demonstrated that endothelial-dependent microvascular dysfunction was associated with LGE using coronary artery acetylcholine provocation test in HF patients without obstructive coronary artery disease [37]. They proposed that these pathophysiological mechanisms contribute to the progression of HF. Their study also found that plasma BNP levels above 180 pg/dL were associated with a coronary flow reserve (CFR) < 2.5, which is indicative of an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events [38]. BNP is released from the myocardium in response to ventricular wall expansion caused by increased volume and pressure overload associated with wall stress in HF patients. Elevated plasma BNP levels were significantly correlated with reduced CFR, particularly in patients with LGE. These findings suggest that impaired hemodynamics and increased wall tension occur in regions of myocardial fibrosis, creating a vicious cycle. This vicious cycle is a key pathophysiological mechanism underlying the progression of HF syndrome. Targeted medical therapies and lifestyle interventions aimed at interrupting this vicious cycle may therefore improve clinical outcomes in HF patients. Although we observed no significant difference in serum NT-proBNP between groups, this finding likely reflects that systemic biomarkers lack sufficient sensitivity to detect the localized myocardial stress associated with early-stage CMD.

Elevated ECV, which reflects increased interstitial collagen deposition, leads to increased myocardial stiffness and impaired diastolic dysfunction. Previous studies have linked myocardial fibrosis and impaired relaxation to the development of HFpEF [39]. Our results are consistent with previous studies, the reference standard for noninvasive ECV assessment, where elevated ECV and CMD have been reported in HFpEF [40, 41]. Although our INOCA cohort did not include patients with clinically overt HF, the observed association between CMD and elevated ECV suggests that these conditions share common pathophysiological mechanisms involving microvascular dysfunction and adverse myocardial remodeling.

While prior studies have identified significant correlations between CMD and myocardial fibrosis, a definitive causal relationship has not yet been established [39, 41]. More recently, Sayama et al. reported that patients with structural CMD had numerically higher ECV values than those with functional CMD, though not statistically significant [42]. This observation highlights the complex and heterogeneous pathophysiological mechanisms underlying different CMD phenotypes.

While elevated ECV is most commonly attributed to interstitial fibrosis, it may also reflect other pathological processes such as myocardial edema, inflammation, or amyloid deposition [43]. Importantly, we excluded patients with a history of prior myocardial infarction or suspected cardiomyopathy in our study design. Although the presence of subclinical amyloidosis cannot be completely ruled out, the consistent associations observed in our study strongly suggest that interstitial fibrosis is the most likely contributor to elevated ECV in this patient population. Nevertheless, ECV values should always be interpreted in the context of the individual patient’s clinical presentation and other diagnostic findings.

Beyond diagnostic characterization, these findings carry practical implications for the clinical care of INOCA patients. Native T1 and ECV may add prognostic value for risk stratification in this heterogeneous population. Patients with elevated ECV despite absent LGE could face higher likelihood of adverse remodeling and incident heart failure over time, and might reasonably be considered for more intensive risk factor modification, including stricter glycemic and blood pressure control. Quantitative T1 and ECV measures could also serve as tissue-level endpoints for monitoring treatment response, particularly in investigational settings. Serial ECV assessment may help track changes in interstitial matrix volume in response to cardioprotective therapies such as renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, and could support individualized treatment adjustments as supporting evidence accumulates. In addition, detection of early interstitial fibrosis may help distinguish structural from purely functional forms of microvascular dysfunction. Patients with evidence of extracellular matrix expansion might be prioritized for anti-remodeling strategies, whereas those with functional impairment alone may be managed primarily with vasodilator and antianginal therapy. Finally, identification of subclinical fibrosis at the INOCA stage provides a rationale for earlier intervention, which may slow progression toward clinically overt HFpEF.

Integrated PET-MRI tissue characterization holds promise for improving longitudinal risk stratification and treatment monitoring in INOCA. Because both myocardial flow reserve and extracellular volume can be quantified in a single examination, changes in microvascular function and interstitial tissue structure can be tracked in parallel over serial follow-up. For patients on cardioprotective therapy or intensive risk factor modification, serial ECV and MFR measurements may detect tissue-level treatment responses earlier than standard clinical endpoints. These applications remain investigational at present, however, and prospective studies are needed to establish prognostic thresholds for integrated PET-MRI markers and to clarify their role in guiding individualized care. If validated in larger longitudinal cohorts, this one-stop imaging approach could help identify high-risk patients sooner, support timely treatment adjustments, and ultimately improve long-term outcomes in this patient population.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which carries a risk of overfitting in multivariable regression models. We have sought to mitigate this risk by limiting the number of covariates entered into the model, excluding variables with inherent algebraic interdependence, and applying a variance inflation factor threshold of 5.0 to rule out significant collinearity. Nonetheless, the statistical robustness of the multivariable findings remains constrained by the cohort size, and larger multicenter prospective studies are required for external validation. The sample size remains reasonable for a dedicated cardiac imaging study, and the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors was high in our cohort, supporting the inclusion of a representative at-risk population. Second, histological analysis via endomyocardial biopsy, the reference standard for myocardial fibrosis diagnosis, was not performed in our study. Consequently, our study, like most studies in this area, lacks direct histological confirmation. Third, long-term clinical outcome data were not available for this cohort, so we cannot draw definitive conclusions about the prognostic value of elevated ECV or its role in guiding clinical decision-making. All statements regarding clinical management and risk stratification are preliminary and hypothesis-generating only. Fourth, we did not perform subgroup analyses according to sex. Many studies have demonstrated a significant sex difference in MBF [44, 45]. Fifth, 17-segment level segmental MFR analysis was not reported, as the primary objective of this study was to investigate global left ventricular myocardial microvascular dysfunction. Sixth, this cross-sectional study design allows only associative conclusions. Causal links between reduced myocardial flow reserve and expanded extracellular volume cannot be confirmed from the current data, and prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and causal direction of these relationships. Seventh, myocardial blood flow values were not normalized to RPP. Only baseline vital signs collected at study enrollment were available; continuous hemodynamic monitoring synchronized with dynamic PET acquisitions was not performed, precluding reliable time-aligned RPP correction. The higher resting hemodynamic load in the CMD group may partially account for the between-group differences in resting MBF and derived MFR, as well as the positive resting MBF–ECV association, rather than these findings reflecting microvascular dysfunction alone. Future investigations with concurrent hemodynamic monitoring are warranted to validate these results using RPP-adjusted flow parameters. Finally, all PET-MRI examinations were performed exclusively at 3.0 Tesla field strength. Further confirmation of the results on a 1.5 Tesla platform is therefore required.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that diffuse extracellular compartment expansion and interstitial tissue changes in patients with CMD can be noninvasively detected by CMR, with ECV serving as the primary and robust tissue marker even in the absence of detectable LGE. Native T1 provides supportive but more modest evidence of myocardial tissue alteration. Furthermore, global MFR was independently associated with elevated ECV after multivariable adjustment in this cohort, consistent with a close link between microvascular function and interstitial tissue remodeling. Additionally, resting MBF and global MFR exhibited significant linear correlations with ECV. Further prospective multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these findings and define the clinical and prognostic significance of T1 mapping and ECV in LGE-negative CMD patients.
