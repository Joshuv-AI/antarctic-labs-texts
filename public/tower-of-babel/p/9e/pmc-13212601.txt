A retrospective study of defect closure and hernia belt compression for seroma prevention in laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair
Authors: Miaofeng Wang, Xiufeng Chu, Huajie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair (LIHR) is a widely adopted technique favored for its minimally invasive approach and positive patient outcomes. However, postoperative seroma formation remains a significant issue. This study investigates the efficacy of defect closure combined with hernia belt compression during LIHR to reduce the incidence of seroma formation. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 375 patients who underwent LIHR at our institution from January 2021 to January 2024. Among them, 121 patients received defect closure combined with hernia belt compression (Group A), 135 patients received defect closure (Group B), 117 patients received neither defect closure nor hernia belt compression (Group C). Collected data included patient demographics(sex, BMI, hernia orifice size, hernia site, and comorbidities such as diabetes) and perioperative variables (operative time, hospital stay, the time to resume normal activity, and seroma formation incidence). The operation times of the three groups were 64.54 ± 3.23 min, 65.23 ± 4.06 min and 58.44 ± 6.90 min, respectively. Closure of the hernia defect significantly prolonged the operation time. (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in intraoperative complications, hospital stay, the time to resume normal activity (P > 0.05). During follow-up period, the number of postoperative seroma cases in the groups was 3, 11 and 19, respectively. The incidence of seroma in Group A was significantly lower than that of Group B and Group C (P < 0.05). Our findings indicate that combing defect closure with hernia belt compression effectively reduces seroma formation following LIHR. These results provide valuable guidance for optimizing surgical practice and postoperative management in LIHR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47651-y.

Introduction

Laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair (LIHR) has revolutionized the management of inguinal hernias, offering a minimally invasive approach that reduces postoperative pain and accelerates recovery1,2. Numerous studies have demonstrated the superiority of LIHR in improving patient outcomes compared to traditional open surgical techniques3,4. With the development of surgical instruments and technology, it becomes increasingly important to understand the complications associated with LIHR .

Postoperative seroma formation remains a significant issue, potentially affecting recovery5–7. Seromas, characterized by abnormal serous fluid accumulation at the surgical dead space, arise from factors such as tissue trauma, inadequate closure of defects, and insufficient postoperative management8. The ongoing challenge in the field lies in identifying effective strategies to mitigate seroma formation and improve surgical outcomes.

This study addresses a gap in the current literature by evaluating the efficacy of combing defect closure with hernia belt compression during LIHR. While previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of defect closure9, the potential synergistic effect of adding postoperative compression remains underexplored. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 375 patients, categorizing them into three groups: those receiving defect closure and compression, those who received only defect closure, and those who received either intervention. The significance of this research lies in its potential to guide clinical practice in hernia repair. By providing evidence on the effectiveness of defect closure and hernia belt compression, we aim to optimize patient outcomes, reduce complication rates, and lower healthcare costs. Through a detailed analysis of patient outcomes, this study aspires to contribute valuable knowledge to the surgical community, ultimately enhancing postoperative care strategies for inguinal hernia repair.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted at Shaoxing Central Hospital, reviewing the medical records of 375 patients who underwent laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair between January 2021 and January 2024. The Shaoxing Central Hospital’s Institutional Review Committee approved this study, and waived off requirement for informed patient consent due to it being a retrospective study, and the document approval number is 2024-27(2024.10.27). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria: adults aged 18 years and older, diagnosed with unilateral inguinal hernia, and who underwent laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. Exclusion criteria included a history of previous hernia repairs, comorbidities affecting healing (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, severe renal or hepatic dysfunction), and those who underwent concurrent abdominal surgeries or bilateral inguinal hernia repair.

Group allocation

Patients were allocated into three groups based on their management strategy:

Group A: Patients who underwent defect closure combined with hernia belt compression.

Group B: Patients who underwent defect closure alone.

Group C: Patients who did not undergo defect closure and hernia belt compression.

Fig. 1 illustrates the use of hernia belts, with Fig. 2 showing a mannequin wearing the belt.

Surgical technique

Laparoscopic inguinal hernia repairs were performed by a team of experienced surgeons using a standardized technique. Figure 3 shows the hernia defect, and Fig. 4 shows the defect closure of a left inguinal hernia. A video of the defect closure procedure is available in the supplementary materials.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of seroma formation, defined as a palpable subcutaneous fluid collection at the surgical site, and it can be categorized into five types. Type 0: no clinical seroma ; Type I: clinical seroma lasting<1 month; Type II: clinical seroma lasting>1 month; Type III: (symptomatic seromas that may need medical treatment), minor seroma-related complications and Type IV: seroma that need to be treated, mayor seroma-related complications10. All patients diagnosed with seromas were examined by clinical physicians and confirmed by ultrasound. Secondary outcome measures included operation time, postoperative hospital stay, and postoperative complications.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records, including demographic information, surgical details, and postoperative outcomes.

Type of hernia mesh and fixation

A medium-weight, large-pore polypropylene mesh was used in TEP or TAPP. The mesh was fixated using glue.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 23.0), with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize patient demographics and outcomes. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables, while continuous variables were analyzed with t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate.

Results

The mean ages of patients in Groups A, B, and C were 67.40 ± 6.12, 66.59 ± 6.34, and 66.62 ± 5.10 years, respectively. No statistically significant differences were found in age, BMI, Hernia orifice size, hospital stay, and time to resumption of normal activity between groups (p > 0.05), as shown in Tables 1 and 2.

However, operation time was significantly longer in Groups A and B compared to Group C (p < 0.05). On the first postoperative day, pain scores were marginally higher in Groups A (1.39 ± 0.52) and B (1.36 ± 0.60) compared to Group C (1.20 ± 0.42), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). On the seventh day after the operation, there was no significant difference in pain levels among the groups.

Seroma formation was significantly lower in Group A (3 cases) than in Groups B (11 cases) and C (19 cases), as shown in Table 2. Among the 19 patients with seromas in Group C, 4 cases (21.1%) required aspiration due to symptoms such as discomfort from swelling, pain, and anxiety. In contrast, among the 11 cases of seroma in Group B, only 1 case (9.1%) needed aspiration because of mild discomfort; all 3 cases of seroma in Group A didn’t need aspiration. All aspiration procedures were done in the outpatient department, and there were no readmissions needed because of seromas.

The follow-up was conducted via either outpatient clinic visits or telephone calls for all patients. During the 6-month follow-up period, no patients developed seroma, ultrasonography examination was performed for 38 patients. And the time to return to normal activity was similar in patients with or without seromas.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that defect closure combined with hernia belt compression significantly reduces the incidence of seroma formation in patients undergoing laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair (LIHR). mitigatingAnalyzed data from 375 patients revealed that Group A ( received both interventions) experienced only 3 cases of seroma. In comparison, Group B (defect closure alone) and Group C (no intervention) had 11 and 19 cases, respectively. These differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05), highlighting the effectiveness of this combined approach in postoperative seroma formation.

Seroma is a common issue following hernia repair11,12, resulting from fluid accumulation in the subcutaneous tissue, and may be mistaken for an early hernia recurrence. The pathophysiology of seroma formation is complex, involving factors such as tissue trauma, surgical technique, and the body’s inflammatory response. In prior studies, the overall seroma formation rates ranged from 12% to 15.3%13–15, with results similar to those in Group C (without defect closure). By closing the defect, we effectively reduce the dead space created during surgery, a known contributor to seroma development. Additionally, the application of a hernia belt exerts external compression, which not only stabilizes the surgical site, but also promotes lymphatic drainage. This dual mechanism may explain the lower seroma rates observed in Group A.

In this study, operation time was significantly longer in Groups A and B compared to Group C (p < 0.05). The increased operation time for defect closure is mainly due to the duration required for suturing. Our findings regarding operation time are consistent with those reported in the literature16. Past researches have shown that effective closure techniques significantly reduce the risk of seromas16,17. The rate of seroma formation was higher in the non-closure group compared to the defect closure group in our study. This result is consistent with findings reported in several studies14–16. However, our study introduces a novel aspect by combining defect closure with postoperative compression, which has been less explored. Contrasts comparing (Group A) versus (Group B) were performed, we found the seroma rates in Group A was lower than that of the Group B ( P < 0.05). This result proves the potential effectiveness of hernia belt compression in the prevention of seroma formation.

In early postoperative period, mild pain was more common in patients with defect closure. On the first postoperative day, pain scores were marginally higher in Groups A and B compared to Group C, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). Pain may stem from deeply placed suture or nerve entrapment, as seen in cases like pubic osteitis. On the seventh day after the operation, there was no significant difference in pain levels among the groups. What more important, there was no significant difference in time to resume normal activity among the three groups .

To improve the risk-benefit balance, more attention has been drawn to nerve-sparing technique. Our nerve-sparing technique focused on several key principles to mitigate this risk. During defect closure, we aimed to place sutures superficially, avoiding deep bites into the underlying muscle or suspicious neurovascular structures. We were particularly cautious to identify and avoid the genitofemoral nerve and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve in the operative field. Sutures placed near the pubic tubercle or Cooper’s ligament were designed to engage only the ligamentous structure without taking deep periosteal bites. We provided a surgery video containing detailed procedure of defect closure in the Supplementary Materials.

Even though most seromas are self-limiting, our data demonstrate that they generate considerable patient anxiety, prompting additional outpatient consultations and examinations. More importantly, some patients with seromas require aspiration, which is an invasive procedure carrying a potential risk of infection. Therefore, the clinical implications of this study are substantial. Implementing a standardized protocol that includes defect closure and the use of hernia belts could enable surgeons to improve patient outcomes. Such an approach could not only reduce the incidence of seromas but also improve overall recovery times and patient satisfaction. Clinicians should consider routinely incorporating hernia belts into postoperative care, especially for patients with BMI (> 24.5 kg/m2 ) and larger hernial defects8.

However, several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. The retrospective design, conducted without a priori power calculation, lacked detailed EHS subclassification and a standardized follow-up protocol with mandatory imaging may introduce selection and treatment allocation biases. Additionally, our study was conducted at a single institution, and the cohort did not include patients managed with hernia belt compression alone, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Furthermore, the limited follow-up duration may not capture all long-term outcomes related to seroma formation and the effectiveness of the interventions. Moreover, another point deserves comment, retrospective allocation, incomplete control of mesh/fixation variables, and limited granularity in EHS subclassification may confound the observed association between closure + compression and seroma reduction. While we provide insights into early postoperative complications, further studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the long-term impact of defect closure and hernia belt compression on patient outcomes. Finally, other relevant factors such as quality of life, and overall patient satisfaction were not extensively assessed in this study.

Although seromas were less frequent after defect closure with compression, most were clinically mild, did not prolong hospitalization or delay return to activity, and resolved without intervention in our cohort. Given the absence of differences in day-7 pain and functional recovery, the decision to close the defect should be individualized, prioritizing larger medial (EHS M) defects and/or defects > 3 cm where dead space is greatest.

Looking ahead, we propose several avenues for future research. First, prospective randomized controlled trials are essential to validate the efficacy of combining defect closure with hernia belt compression across diverse patient populations. Studies should also investigate the optimal timing and duration of postoperative compression. Determining the ideal duration of hernia belt use could maximize therapeutic benefits while minimizing patient discomfort. Additionally, evaluating the cost-effectiveness of this combined approach relative to traditional methods would offer valuable insights for healthcare systems. And the focus should not only be on complications reduction but also on overall quality of life19, especially chronic pain after laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair20.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study underscores the efficacy of combining defect closure with hernia belt compression to prevent seroma formation following laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. It offers actionable insights that could redefine postoperative management practices for LIHR, advancing surgical care quality on a global scale. Future research should focus on further refining these strategies to ensure optimal outcomes for patients undergoing hernia repair.
