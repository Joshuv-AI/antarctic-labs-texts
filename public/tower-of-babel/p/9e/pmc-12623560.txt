Introduction

Cognitive impairment (CI) is a condition characterized by difficulties in thinking, learning, memory, judgment, and decision-making. Common signs include memory loss and trouble concentrating, completing tasks, understanding instructions, and solving problems. Behavioral changes, mood shifts, loss of motivation, and disorientation are also frequently observed [1, 2].

CI spans a spectrum from mild CI (MCI) to severe dementia, where the decline in abilities significantly interferes with daily life. While MCI increases the risk of developing dementia, it does not guarantee progression. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent cause of CI and dementia [3–6], and estimates indicate that ~ 30–50% of individuals with MCI will progress to dementia due to AD within 5–10 years [7–10]. Recent advancements in understanding the underlying amyloid pathology of AD have led to the development of amyloid-targeting therapies (ATTs), which aim to modify disease progression and represent a significant step forward in addressing the disease beyond symptomatic management [11, 12]. The ATTs donanemab and lecanemab are now approved for use in patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD in several countries, including the United Arab Emirates (UAE) [13–15].

Dementia prevalence is increasing globally, affecting an estimated 1.3 million people in Arab countries in 2021, with an associated cost of USD 10–14 billion, highlighting the need for early detection and specialized services [16]. A 2019 systematic literature review highlighted the lack of detailed data regarding dementia burden and prevalence in Arab countries, although general trends for the region reflected those seen in other countries [17]. For example, prevalence increased with age, from approximately 1.1–2.3% in people aged ≥ 50 years to 13.5–18.5% in those aged ≥ 80 years, and dementia risk was affected by education, socioeconomic factors, and comorbidities [17]. Nevertheless, the authors highlighted the need for further epidemiological research in Middle Eastern populations to help plan healthcare services according to the diversity and cultural factors associated with the region [17].

Although international guidelines are available for the diagnosis and management of AD, including the use of biomarkers in research and clinical settings [18–21], the UAE differs from Western countries both culturally and in terms of management resources; this limits the ability for local implementation of these guidelines. The UAE is also one of the countries where ATTs have been approved for clinical use. Here we propose the first CI management framework from the UAE region, using a comprehensive seven-step approach for diagnosing and managing CI, with a focus on AD, targeting primary care physicians, neurologists, and other healthcare providers involved in the care of patients with CI. We detail the clinical features, diagnostic criteria, and differential diagnoses of CI and emphasize the importance of patient-centric care and education. Recommendations are also provided for evaluating treatment responses, addressing treatment failures, managing complications and comorbidities, and planning long-term follow-up to ensure optimal care.

Methods

Assembly of an Expert Task Force

A task force of leading experts in the field of clinical neurology was convened, as recommended by the Emirates Neurology Society (EMINS) Scientific Board, to develop evidence-based position statements/recommendations to guide the diagnosis and management of AD, including the use of ATTs, in the UAE clinical setting. The task force comprised eight neurologists (EMINS members), along with two non-voting collaborators who provided subject-matter expertise in dementia and medical insights throughout the development of the manuscript. The non-voting collaborators did not participate in the final voting steps, as those decisions were intentionally reserved for practicing clinicians to ensure the clinical relevance and applicability of the recommendations.

The task force members developed a set of seven expert statements/steps after considering all the challenges regarding the management and diagnosis of CI in the UAE community. The expert statements were jointly drafted by the members following multiple rounds of online discussion. The statements were further reviewed and finalized in a panel meeting on February 20, 2025.

Delphi Survey and Analysis of Results

A modified Delphi survey method was chosen to obtain a consensus, ensuring that the drafted expert statements reflected diverse perspectives and experiences. Through a nomination and voting process, 17 experienced UAE-based neurologists selected by the EMINS Scientific Board were invited to participate in the survey. These 17 neurologists and the eight task force members jointly constituted the Delphi expert panel. The survey included the seven expert statements drafted by the task force, to be rated using a nine-point Likert scale (from 1 = ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to 9 = ‘‘strongly agree’’). ‘‘No opinion’’ was not provided as an option. Discordance was predefined as > 25% of the Delphi panelists rating an expert statement as ≤ 3 on the Likert scale. Consensus was predefined as a median rating of ≥ 7 without discordance. Expert statements achieving consensus were adopted.

Literature Review and the Development of Consensus Statements

Drs. Tauofik Alsaadi, Abubaker Almadni, Suhail AlRukn, and Derk Krieger performed a preliminary literature search in November 2024 to identify pertinent treatment guidelines, recommendations, approved product information, randomized controlled trials, and observational studies regarding the diagnosis and management of CI due to AD. These search results enabled the formulation of a more comprehensive literature search strategy for further PubMed searches, along with targeted searches. Recommendations were jointly drafted by members of the task force, who together represented the expert voting panel, and the scope was expanded to include recommendations for the use of ATTs. Additional modifications were made to international guidelines to align them with local clinical practice, with consideration of the aforementioned nuances of the patient population and healthcare infrastructure within the UAE. The expert task force also reviewed relevant published findings from various randomized controlled trials and observational studies during the development of the recommendations.

Following the development of all recommendations, each statement was critically evaluated and modified during a meeting conducted on February 20, 2025.

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and expert discussions and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. The study was approved and granted exempt status by the American Center for Psychiatry and Neurology Institutional Review Board (reference: ACPN_IRB_0072). All participants were aware of the study objectives and the intention to publish the findings. Consent to participate was indicated by taking part in discussions and providing votes. The independence of participants was verified based on professional credentials and consideration of potential conflicts of interest. Participants’ data were protected in accordance with applicable data protection requirements.

UAE guidelines need to consider cultural differences between the UAE and other countries. For example, multigenerational households are more common in the UAE than Europe, and families may be more likely to identify early signs of CI and provide support. Societal perceptions of CI and AD/dementia (including perceived differences between symptoms of these and normal aging) may also differ between countries/populations.

Early suspicion and assessment of CI are critical for improving outcomes in individuals at risk of or experiencing conditions such as AD. Detecting cognitive decline in its earliest stages allows for timely interventions that can slow progression, improve quality of life, and support patients and care partners in adapting to future challenges. Emerging therapies, particularly those targeting amyloid pathology in AD, are most effective when initiated early, further underscoring the importance of vigilance in identifying early signs of CI [23].

CI often manifests subtly, making it essential for healthcare providers to remain vigilant in identifying early signs. Symptoms may initially appear mild and sporadic, but they typically progress over time, impacting various domains of cognitive function [24–26]. Clinical symptoms that should prompt further evaluation include memory loss, executive dysfunction, language and/or attention problems, changes in spatial and visual perception and/or behavior, and daily living impairment (see Supplementary Material for further details).

These symptoms may initially be dismissed as normal aging but should not be ignored if persistent or progressive. Early identification allows for appropriate intervention with the potential to slow the rate of disease progression [25, 27].

Understanding risk factors for CI enables healthcare providers to identify individuals at higher risk, even before symptoms emerge. While some factors, such as age and family history, are non-modifiable, others can be addressed through targeted intervention [28–30].

A detailed medical history often provides critical insights into potential causes of CI. Certain features in the patient’s history or symptom presentation should raise immediate concern and prompt further investigation to exclude other conditions; for example, sudden onset of cognitive symptoms, rapid cognitive progression over 1–2 years, and progressive decline over months to years [31] (see Supplementary Material for further details).

Differential Diagnosis

Accurately distinguishing CI due to AD from other conditions is crucial for effective treatment. While AD is the most common cause of dementia, other neurodegenerative diseases, vascular conditions, and reversible causes must be considered. Features and mechanisms associated with other dementia etiologies are summarized in Table 2 and in the Supplementary Material. A systematic diagnostic approach incorporating clinical history, cognitive testing, imaging, biomarker analysis, and laboratory evaluation is needed to ensure accurate differentiation between different etiologies and to exclude reversible causes of CI, including vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, and normal-pressure hydrocephalus [32].

Confirming CI and differentiating it from other conditions that can mimic AD is a critical step in providing accurate diagnosis and effective management. A comprehensive evaluation should integrate clinical evaluation, neuropsychological assessment, and imaging and laboratory tests, ensuring a multidimensional approach to diagnosis. It is important that tools administered to patients are available in their first/chosen language to ensure that the findings accurately reflect cognitive rather than linguistic abilities. Tools should also reflect cultural and geographical features that the patient is familiar with; for example, tools asking patients to identify animals should feature only those commonly recognized in their region. Arabic versions of clinical assessment tools are scarce but should only be used for patients from the UAE and residents from other Arab/Gulf Cooperation Council countries, whereas functional impact and structured tools should be applied for non-Arabic residents.

Clinical Evaluation and Patient History

This initial step sets the foundation for identifying the cause of cognitive decline and determining the appropriate diagnostic and management approach.

(i) Gathering Symptoms and History

The evaluation begins with an in-depth exploration of the patient’s symptoms and their progression over time. Key aspects to investigate include memory loss, behavioral and psychological symptoms, and functional decline [25, 26, 32, 33] (see Supplementary Material for further details).

(ii) Care Partner Input

Care partner or family observations are invaluable, as they often detect symptoms that the patient may be unaware of, or unable to articulate, and help obtain a clearer picture of the patient’s functional status. These reports may provide insight into personality or behavioral changes, such as irritability, withdrawal, or paranoia; declining ability to perform routine tasks like cooking or managing appointments; or episodes of disorientation or confusion in familiar environments.

Available subjective measures of care partner burden relating to patient abilities include the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) [34], the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) Caregiver Distress Scale [35], and the Alzheimer’s Disease Caregiver Reported-Health Index (ADCR-HI) [36]. The ZBI and ADCR-HI measure several aspects of care partner burden, whereas the NPI Caregiver Distress Scale measures distress experienced by care partners relating to patient symptom domains assessed by the patient NPI instrument (discussed later in the patient Neuropsychological Testing subsection) [35, 37]. Care partner supervision time can also be used as an objective measure of care partner burden [38].

(iii) Risk Factors and Comorbidities

Understanding the patient’s risk profile helps to contextualize symptoms and guide differential diagnosis. Important considerations include age, family and medical history, and genetic risk factors (see Supplementary Material) [25, 28, 39–43]. Although the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene is the most established genetic risk factor for late-onset AD [25, 40], with APOE ε4 increasing the risk of AD and APOE ε2 being protective, APOE testing is not recommended at Step 2, which focuses on the initial clinical assessment. While APOE ε4 is a known genetic risk factor, it is also not required for the diagnosis of AD.

(iv) Detailed Symptom Analysis

A structured assessment of cognitive domains (memory, language, executive function, and attention and visuospatial skills) helps to identify patterns indicative of specific conditions (see Supplementary Material) [25, 26, 44, 45]. A detailed history and clinical evaluation also guide the selection of further tests such as neuropsychological assessments and biomarker studies. Early recognition of symptoms ensures timely intervention, which is especially important when initiating ATTs for AD.

Neuropsychological Testing

Neuropsychological testing is a critical component in confirming CI and differentiating AD from other dementias. By evaluating specific cognitive domains and functional capabilities, clinicians can establish a comprehensive profile of the patient’s cognitive deficits and track disease progression. Further details on the tools used to evaluate different domains and capabilities can be found in the Supplementary Material.

(i) Global Cognitive Screening

Global screening tools provide a quick overview of cognitive function and can indicate whether further detailed evaluation is needed. Available tools include the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [46, 47], Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [48, 49], Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS) [50, 51], and the Mini-Cog [52, 53].

(ii) Domain-Specific Evaluation

Detailed testing of cognitive domains, such as memory, execution function, language, visuospatial skills, attention, and working memory, is used to distinguish AD from other conditions (see Supplementary Material for further details) [25, 45, 54–63].

(iii) Functional Impact and Structured Tools

Standardized tools assessing cognitive and functional impairments (e.g., the Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] Scale, the NPI, and the Functional Activities Questionnaire) are valuable for tracking disease progression [37, 64, 65].

(iv) Digital Tools

Digital tools such as XpressO by MoCA [66] and the Cognetivity Neurosciences Integrated Cognitive Assessment [67] are becoming available as alternative methods for carrying out neurological testing, but are not yet routinely used in clinical practice in the UAE. Digital tools employing diverse methodologies—from computerized cognitive tests and eye-tracking metrics to retinal imaging and speech analysis—are opening new avenues for detecting early CI and AD. However, despite these promising innovations, rigorous clinical validation is still required to establish their accuracy, reliability, and applicability across varied clinical populations and settings [68, 69].

Initial Imaging and Laboratory Tests

Imaging and laboratory tests are indispensable in the diagnostic workup for CI. They serve two critical purposes: ruling out secondary causes and identifying hallmark features of neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD. These tests complement clinical evaluation and neuropsychological assessments by providing objective evidence of pathology and aiding in the differentiation of various dementias.

Structural Imaging

Structural imaging, such as MRI with dedicated sequences, is essential for detecting anatomical changes associated with CI. Digital tools to aid in diagnosis are in development and may be integrated into clinical practice in the future. High-quality MRI is a valuable tool for assessing brain structure in CI and can identify features associated with different etiologies, such as atrophy patterns, white matter lesions, hydrocephalus, and microhemorrhages [70–74] (further details are provided in the Supplementary Material).

Basic Laboratory Tests

Laboratory tests are crucial for excluding reversible causes of CI and identifying AD-specific biomarkers. Routine blood work helps identify treatable conditions that can mimic dementia, such as vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, infections (e.g., human immunodeficiency virus, Lyme disease, syphilis), or chronic liver or kidney disease (which can contribute to metabolic encephalopathy) [25, 75, 76].

Functional Imaging

Functional imaging provides insights into brain metabolism and perfusion, aiding in the differentiation of various types of dementia [33, 72, 77, 78]. Fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) reveals hypometabolism in specific regions of the brain, such as parietotemporal hypometabolism, characteristic of AD, or frontal and anterior temporal hypometabolism, often seen in frontotemporal dementia. Single-photon emission computed tomography evaluates regional blood flow. Reduced perfusion in the parietotemporal cortex suggests AD, while perfusion deficits in the basal ganglia and midbrain are more typical of dementia with Lewy bodies.

Utility in Differential Diagnosis

Imaging and laboratory tests not only confirm the presence of neurodegenerative pathology but also help differentiate AD from other forms of dementia [72, 79] (Table 2).

Step 3: Laboratory Confirmation of AD Diagnosis: Use of Biomarkers

We strongly recommend utilizing amyloid PET in the UAE to confirm the diagnosis and monitor AD progression, for all cases of MCI or mild dementia when diagnosis is in question.

Alternatively, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis is recommended for all suspected AD cases.

We recommend the use of fluid biomarker testing as per the 2024 Alzheimer’s Association revised criteria [18].

The amyloid-beta deposition, pathologic tau, and neurodegeneration classification is a biological framework proposed by the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association to categorize AD based on core pathophysiological processes, using biomarkers rather than clinical symptoms alone [55]. Therefore, biomarkers are essential in confirming the diagnosis of AD, enhancing accuracy, and differentiating it from other neurodegenerative conditions, whereas neuropsychological tests are subjective [80]. By identifying core pathophysiological features of AD—amyloid-beta plaques, tau neurofibrillary tangles, and neurodegeneration—biomarkers provide objective evidence of disease processes even in preclinical or MCI stages. The primary biomarker categories include fluid biomarkers and PET imaging.

Compared with individual biomarkers alone, these hybrid ratios have emerged as more robust indicators of AD pathology as determined by amyloid PET, which is considered the reference standard for in vivo detection of amyloid plaques. Notably, hybrid CSF biomarkers—such as the Aβ42/40 ratio combined with P-tau—have shown high concordance with amyloid PET results, supporting their use as reliable surrogates [85]. CSF analysis is still valuable but may become less important in many settings, thanks to highly accurate blood-based biomarkers like those explained in the following section.

Blood-based (Plasma) Biomarkers

Blood-based biomarkers represent a breakthrough in AD diagnostics, offering a less invasive, cost-effective alternative to CSF analysis. In May 2025, the United States Food and Drug Administration approved the Lumipulse® G pTau 217/β-Amyloid 1–42 Plasma Ratio as the first in vitro diagnostic device for the early detection of amyloid plaques in people aged ≥ 55 years with signs and symptoms of AD [86]. Advances in assay sensitivity and multiplex approaches have enabled the detection of key AD markers in blood with growing accuracy [87, 88]:

Blood-based biomarkers, particularly P-tau217, are transforming AD diagnostics by enabling early and scalable detection. They are especially valuable in clinical care and trials, where they can be used for screening and monitoring response to therapies.

PET Imaging

PET imaging offers in vivo visualization of AD pathology, complementing fluid biomarkers to confirm diagnosis [72, 77, 78, 94, 95]:

Amyloid PET: Uses radiotracers (e.g., florbetapir, florbetaben, flutemetamol) to detect amyloid-beta plaques. A positive scan confirms amyloid deposition, which is highly specific for AD. Amyloid PET has recently become available in UAE.

Tau PET: Tau PET imaging identifies tau neurofibrillary tangles, a feature strongly associated with cognitive decline and disease progression. While PET imaging remains a highly specific confirmatory test and a follow-up tool for treatment response, its accessibility and cost limit routine clinical use. It is particularly valuable for ambiguous cases or when corroborating results from blood biomarkers. In addition, the development of the Centiloid scale, whereby quantitative amyloid imaging measures are standardized through scaling to a 0–100 scale, permits direct comparisons of PET imaging findings across laboratories regardless of the analysis methods or tracers used [96].

The integration of CSF biomarkers, blood-based biomarkers, and PET imaging has the potential to revolutionize AD diagnosis in the future (Table 3; Table S1) [18]. CSF remains valuable, particularly with hybrid ratios enhancing specificity, while blood P-tau217 is a promising, noninvasive blood test with growing clinical utility. PET imaging provides in vivo evidence of amyloid and tau pathology, further solidifying AD diagnosis. A multimodal approach combining these tools ensures precise diagnosis, enabling timely interventions and optimizing patient outcomes.

Step 4: Patient and Care Partner Education

AD remains stigmatized in the UAE. We recommend enhancing public awareness, expanding education initiatives, and strengthening patient advocacy groups to improve patient and care partner support.

Patient and care partner education is the cornerstone of AD management. Education addresses misconceptions about AD, fosters realistic expectations, assists in shared decision-making around care, and equips care partners with the tools to provide compassionate and informed care. We believe there is a need for a representing body advocating AD diagnosis and treatment in the UAE, informed by expert healthcare providers, patients, and care partners. If such guidelines are accepted by authorities such as the Dubai Health Authority and Department of Health (Abu Dhabi), this may also help improve early referrals to specialized services to consider the access to the right medications, tailored to the right staging of the disease.

Importance of Education and Support

Education about AD and its progression is crucial for both patients and care partners to help with understanding the disease, promoting early intervention, reducing care partner burden, and enhancing quality of life [25, 29, 30, 97] (see Supplementary Material for further details). Structured education and emotional support ensure that patients and families feel more confident and less isolated in managing the challenges of AD and more equipped to participate in the shared decision-making process around care.

Strategies for Effective Communication

Effective communication is central to educating patients and care partners about AD while managing expectations and addressing emotional and practical needs [25, 98, 99]. Healthcare providers should use simple, clear language and engage in active listening, with information tailored to the disease stage (see Supplementary Material). Written materials and follow-up calls can help to reinforce key messages. A patient-centered approach should be used, recognizing individual differences in learning styles, literacy levels, and cultural backgrounds.

Resources for Patients and Families

Access to reliable resources empowers patients and care partners with ongoing education and practical support. Recommended resources include educational materials, support services, mobile applications, telehealth services, community resources, and legal and financial planning services [100–103] (further details regarding available resources can be found in the Supplementary Material).

Step 5: Cognitive Rehabilitation

We recommend actively addressing modifiable risk factors for CI and AD, particularly in the early stages, to improve overall brain health and help slow disease progression.

The management of AD requires a comprehensive approach that combines non-pharmacological strategies with pharmacological treatments tailored to the stage of the disease. Non-pharmacological interventions, such as cognitive stimulation, lifestyle modifications, and care partner support, are essential components across all stages, improving quality of life, slowing functional decline, and supporting patients and care partners without the use of medications. Evidence increasingly supports their role as first-line approaches, particularly in early stages of AD and as adjuncts to pharmacological treatments in severe disease [29]. Typical approaches are summarized in this section, with further details and examples of the available evidence provided in the Supplementary Material.

(i) Cognitive Stimulation and Rehabilitation

Cognitive-based interventions (such as cognitive stimulation therapy, cognitive rehabilitation, mindfulness, and mental exercises) aim to preserve cognitive function and promote mental engagement [104–106].

(ii) Physical Activity and Exercise

Physical exercise (aerobic exercise, resistance training, or combined programs) is a cornerstone of non-pharmacological care, offering potential cognitive and general health benefits [29, 107–112].

(iii) Social Engagement and Environmental Modifications

Social interaction plus music and art therapy may help with cognitive symptoms and mental health, while environmental modifications may help reduce confusion and improve safety [113–118].

(iv) Sleep Hygiene and Stress Management

The roles of sleep hygiene and stress management are increasingly recognized as pivotal modifiable factors in both the prevention and progression of AD [25, 119–121]. They intersect directly with brain clearance mechanisms, neuroinflammation, and cognitive reserve. Improving sleep quality and treatment of obstructive sleep apnea may help improve AD-associated symptoms [25, 119, 120, 122], whereas stress reduction techniques such as mindfulness, guided relaxation, and breathing exercises can alleviate care partner burden and reduce agitation or anxiety in patients [121].

(v) Care Partner Education and Support

Care partners play a crucial role in the management of AD. Education and training programs equip care partners with strategies to manage behavioral and psychological symptoms (e.g., agitation and wandering), support daily activities while promoting independence, and help with the emotional and physical challenges associated with care partnering [25]. Support groups, respite care, and counseling further mitigate care partner stress, fostering better patient outcomes.

Non-pharmacological strategies provide a holistic and patient-centered approach to managing AD. By incorporating cognitive stimulation, physical activity, social engagement, environmental modifications, and care partner support, these interventions address the multifaceted needs of patients and families. When implemented early and consistently, non-pharmacological strategies can improve cognitive function, delay disease progression, and enhance quality of life.

Step 6: Current Treatment Options

ATTs represent a significant advancement in AD management, particularly for patients in the early stages of the disease.

For ATT-eligible patients, physicians must evaluate treatment risks and benefits, offering treatment at the earliest time point. Informed consent from the patient and care partner education on expectations and potential side effects are essential.

In moderate-to-severe AD, we recommend a more patient-centered approach through symptom management focusing on optimizing quality of life. Emphasis should be placed on compassionate care, dignity, and supporting families in late-stage AD management.

Pharmacological treatments for AD should be tailored to the stage of the disease. For patients with MCI or early-stage AD, ATTs, have shown promise in delaying disease progression. Donanemab and lecanemab are now approved in the UAE for use in patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD [13, 14]. Both lecanemab and donanemab represent a paradigm shift in AD treatment by offering disease modification rather than symptomatic relief alone (see Supplementary Material for further details). However, they require biomarker confirmation (CSF/PET) and careful patient selection and monitoring due to potential adverse events. Appropriate use recommendations have been developed by the AD and Related Disorders Therapeutic Workgroup to guide the implementation of donanemab [123] and lecanemab [124] in real-world practice.

Symptomatic treatments, such as cholinesterase inhibitors and N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists, remain the cornerstone of therapy for cognitive and functional symptoms in early to moderate stages. Now that ATTs are available, these can also be prescribed for eligible patients. As the disease advances, treatment priorities shift to addressing behavioral and psychological symptoms, optimizing comfort, and supporting care partners. A holistic, stage-specific treatment strategy ensures that patients receive personalized care that addresses their evolving needs while maintaining dignity and improving overall outcomes.

Pharmacological Management of MCI and Mild Dementia due to AD

Early intervention in MCI and mild dementia is critical to slowing disease progression and preserving cognitive and functional abilities. While not all individuals with MCI progress to AD dementia, a recent meta-analysis found that those with biomarker-confirmed amyloid-beta pathology are at the greatest risk of conversion to AD dementia, whereas tau status alone was not predictive of AD dementia [125]. ATTs, such as donanemab and lecanemab, are now approved to target amyloid pathology early, reinforcing the importance of timely diagnosis and initiation of treatment.

Early treatment in MCI and mild dementia offers multiple benefits:

Delaying progression: ATTs have demonstrated efficacy in slowing cognitive decline and reducing amyloid burden in early disease stages [13, 14].

Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) are MRI-detectable changes associated with anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies. ARIA is classified into two types [128, 129]:

ARIA-E (edema/effusion)—fluid accumulation in the brain, possibly causing headaches, dizziness, or confusion

ARIA-E (edema/effusion)—fluid accumulation in the brain, possibly causing headaches, dizziness, or confusion

ARIA-H (hemosiderosis/microhemorrhages)—small areas of bleeding or iron deposits, usually asymptomatic

ARIA-H (hemosiderosis/microhemorrhages)—small areas of bleeding or iron deposits, usually asymptomatic

ARIA are likely caused by amyloid clearance in blood vessels with underlying amyloid deposition. Most cases are asymptomatic and resolve spontaneously, with treatment paused if needed [128]. APOE ε4 homozygotes have a higher risk for both types of ARIA and may require closer monitoring. MRI screening, with the recommended protocol, before and during treatment is crucial to detect ARIA early and minimize risks [130]. Temporary dose interruptions or permanent treatment discontinuations might be needed in the event of moderate-to-severe ARIA, and steroid treatment in symptomatic cases to reduce inflammation and edema.

For patients who may not qualify for ATTs or prefer alternative treatments, symptomatic management remains crucial. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) can be used to enhance cholinergic transmission, improving cognition and daily functioning [131]. The NMDA receptor antagonist memantine reduces excitotoxicity and may be considered in mild AD dementia, although it is more commonly used in moderate-to-severe stages of the disease [132].

While current ATTs primarily target amyloid-beta, future strategies may expand to other mechanisms driving AD progression [43, 133, 134]. The treatment landscape for MCI and mild AD dementia is rapidly evolving, with early intervention increasingly recognized as critical in altering disease progression. Lecanemab and donanemab mark a significant step forward, while future therapies might offer further therapeutic advancements. A personalized, multimodal approach—combining disease-modifying, symptomatic, and lifestyle interventions—remains essential to optimizing outcomes for patients in the early stages of AD. Since currently available ATTs are indicated in patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD only, treatment should be re-evaluated regularly, and discontinuation considered when the disease progresses beyond the stages with expected benefit.

Pharmacological Management of Moderate and Severe AD Dementia

As AD progresses to moderate and severe stages, patients experience increasing cognitive, functional, and behavioral impairments. The primary goals of treatment in these stages include symptomatic management, preservation of quality of life, behavioral symptom control, and care partner support. Pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions remain essential for maintaining comfort and dignity.

While cholinesterase inhibitors are most effective in the early stages of AD, they may provide continued benefits in maintaining cognitive and functional capacity in moderate AD [1310]. In some cases, withdrawal of cholinesterase inhibitors in late-stage AD has been associated with accelerated cognitive and functional deterioration, supporting their continued use if tolerated [135]. Memantine, approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD, reduces excitotoxicity and has shown efficacy in improving daily functioning, agitation, and overall disease progression [132]. Clinical trials suggest that combining memantine with cholinesterase inhibitors provides additional benefits in maintaining cognitive and functional abilities longer than monotherapy [136].

As AD advances, neuropsychiatric symptoms such as agitation, aggression, delusions, and depression become prominent. Management strategies including non-pharmacological approaches [137]—such as structured routines and care partner training, and environmental modifications—can help reduce behavioral disturbances. Pharmacological options include antidepressants and antipsychotics such as risperidone or olanzapine, although adverse event risk needs to be considered and monitored when considering antipsychotic use [25, 138, 139].

As AD progresses to its severe stage, patients may lose the ability to communicate, eat independently, or recognize loved ones. Managing moderate and severe AD requires a comprehensive, patient-centered approach that integrates pharmacological treatments, behavioral management, care partner education, and end-of-life care. The focus shifts from preserving cognitive function to ensuring comfort, dignity, and optimal quality of life for both patients and their care partners.

Step 7: Evaluation of Treatment Response and Adjusting Management

Given the chronic and progressive nature of AD, we recommend regular assessments every 3–6 months. At each follow-up, treatment response, disease progression, and therapeutic adjustments should be carefully evaluated.

We believe that neurologists should be the main responsible healthcare professionals, especially for those who receive ATTs, to monitor the disease and manage side effects.

We strongly recommend establishing a multidisciplinary memory service, with input from neurologists, psychologists/psychiatrists, and occupational therapists; and to manage comorbidities such as sleep disorders, mood, behavioral issues, etc. Care plans should include:

Our recommendation is that neurologists should lead treatment with ATTs. Neurologists have experience with biomarkers and imaging, are skilled in ARIA risk management, and are familiar with comorbid conditions. Patients should receive follow-up MRIs and adverse events monitoring.

It is important to carefully manage the expectations of patients and care partners, including the concept of “treatment failure.” Patients and families need to understand that patients on ATTs will still experience CI/dementia decline, but at a slower rate. Donanemab has a defined treatment period, and the UAE prescribing information states that “treatment should be maintained until amyloid plaques are cleared” or continued for “up to 18 months if monitoring of amyloid plaque clearance with a validated method is not possible” [13]. This contrasts with lecanemab, which does not have a specified treatment stopping point [14]. For ATTs without a fixed treatment period, there remains limited evidence to guide discontinuation, and clearer criteria for treatment failure or nonresponse are needed. Further consideration of which assessments/markers should be used to monitor treatment response/failure is also needed as the treatment landscape continues to evolve, with the addition of new biomarkers, diagnostic tools, and treatments expected by late 2025.

Both treatment response and safety require ongoing monitoring, with assessments of functional outcomes, quality of life, and care partner burden. Use of tools to assess meaningful outcomes should be tailored to the local setting; for example, in the UAE, care partner burden is particularly relevant. In addition to their potential for adverse events, ATTs are expensive, so patients need to continue to demonstrate meaningful benefit (i.e., not progressing to moderate or severe stages of AD) to continue these treatments. Treatment discontinuation should be considered for patients experiencing adverse events or risks that outweigh benefits and upon progression to later stages of dementia where ATT benefits have not been demonstrated.

In the first 3–6 months after diagnosis, monitoring should include cognitive assessments, such as the MMSE, MoCA, CDR, or Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale. Patients require baseline MRI screening for ARIA before receiving ATTs, followed by regular follow-up MRIs [13, 14]. Now that amyloid PET is available in the UAE, it can be considered to monitor and guide treatment response at 6 and 12 months following initiation of ATTs. Digital tools, including smartphone applications and wearable devices, are increasingly utilized in AD care to monitor mobility and daily activities [140]. These technologies can help patients to retain independence and reassure care partners by providing alerts in the case of falls or changes in location [140]. Future developments may help to provide clinicians with real-time data; for example, artificial intelligence-driven digital texts and video assessments may assist in monitoring cognitive function or sleep, possibly enhancing early detection and enabling personalized care strategies.

If cognitive and functional stage remains stable, therapy should be continued, with periodic reassessments recommended every 6 months. If treatment side effects (e.g., ARIA) occur, options include dose adjustment or temporary or permanent treatment discontinuation [13, 14]. If the disease progresses to mild dementia, additional symptomatic treatments should be considered.

Once a patient progresses from MCI or mild dementia to moderate dementia, the focus broadens from maintaining independence to managing neuropsychiatric symptoms and other troubling symptoms. Cholinesterase inhibitors can be initiated or continued through the moderate stage, and memantine may be introduced to help with behavioral symptoms and cognition [131, 132]. It is important to monitor for safety concerns, including gastrointestinal disturbances (cholinesterase inhibitors) and dizziness or confusion (memantine) [141, 142].

As AD progresses to its severe stages, the focus shifts from preserving cognition to managing behavioral symptoms and ensuring comfort. Patients experience profound cognitive decline, loss of independence, and an increased need for care partner support. Behavioral and psychological symptoms, such as agitation, psychosis, depression, and sleep disturbances, often require individualized approaches. Non-pharmacological interventions remain the first-line treatment, but when ineffective, medications may be considered. Low-dose atypical antipsychotics (risperidone, olanzapine) can help with severe agitation and hallucinations but require careful monitoring due to adverse event risks [25, 138, 139]. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (sertraline, citalopram) may alleviate depressive symptoms, while melatonin or trazodone have been used to try to improve sleep, but further research is needed to support the use of these medications in people with AD [143, 144].

As AD advances, certain treatments may no longer be beneficial, or appropriate [145, 146]. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine can be tapered or discontinued when they no longer provide meaningful functional support. Minimizing unnecessary medications helps reduce sedation, fall risk, and drug interactions. Regular reviews ensure that only essential treatments remain, prioritizing symptom relief over disease modification.

In the final stages, care focuses on comfort, dignity, and symptom relief. Pain management is essential, as patients may be unable to express discomfort, requiring care partners to monitor nonverbal pain indicators like grimacing and restlessness. Feeding difficulties are common, and hand-feeding is preferred over tube feeding, which has not been shown to improve survival or quality of life [147]. Early discussions on advanced care planning ensure that end-of-life preferences, including palliative care and hospice, align with patient and family wishes.

Discussion

This guidance builds on local expertise and international recommendations with input from experts in the diagnosis and management of CI and AD in the UAE. It considers clinical factors common to other countries but focuses on enhancing the quality of care in the UAE setting, considering cultural and healthcare resource factors specific to the UAE.

A holistic, evidence-based approach is paramount in managing CI due to AD. The advent of ATTs offers promising avenues to alter disease trajectories, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies. Our seven-step framework begins at the start of the patient journey when the initial symptoms of CI become apparent to patients and their families and clinicians. Concerns raised by patients and care partners should be considered seriously by healthcare professionals; early symptoms may be subtle and/or sporadic, and we highlight symptoms that require further evaluation, along with appropriate tools and technology. For patients later diagnosed with CI and/or AD dementia, education and support remain key for patients and care partners to ensure appropriate treatment, reduce burden, and maintain quality of life.

Patients with suspected CI should initially receive a clinical neuropsychiatric assessment to confirm CI, which should be appropriate to the patient’s background and language and cognitive abilities. Patients with confirmed CI should then undergo imaging and laboratory testing to rule out reversible causes of CI and aid in a differential diagnosis for those with, or who are at risk of progressing to, dementia [72]. We strongly recommend the use of PET in the UAE to confirm diagnosis and monitor disease progression. Fluid biomarkers can be used to confirm diagnosis, and their potential use for monitoring is being investigated. Early diagnosis is particularly important for patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD, who require biomarker confirmation of their diagnosis to enable access to ATTs [13, 14, 18]. Patients receiving ATTs also require ongoing monitoring for adverse events and assessment of when treatment can be discontinued.

Pharmacological treatment should be supported by lifestyle changes, such as the inclusion of cognitive stimulation, physical activity, and social engagement. As the disease progresses, treatment priorities change to focus on symptom control and optimizing comfort and quality of life. The needs of care partners also need ongoing consideration to ensure they receive the support required.

Although neurologists should be the main responsible healthcare professionals, especially for patients who receive ATTs, we strongly recommend a multidisciplinary approach, with further input from other professionals such as psychologists/psychiatrists and occupational therapists, to provide support across the full spectrum of care requirements.

Although this manuscript provides previously lacking guidance on the diagnosis and management of CI and AD in the UAE, considering local cultural and healthcare resource factors, it is limited by the lack of published UAE-specific data. Our guidance is therefore mostly reliant on a review of data from Western sources, although this is accompanied by expert input from UAE neurologists experienced in working with patients with AD and their families. By adhering to this framework, clinicians can enhance the quality of care for individuals with CI and/or AD, leveraging emerging therapies to improve patient outcomes. Clinicians should remain familiar with the range of ongoing developments, from imaging and biomarker analyses to supportive technologies, and use clinical judgement to ensure treatments and care are best tailored to the requirements of individual patients.

Conclusions

This seven-step framework provides guidance from the initial diagnosis of CI due to AD to ongoing management in the UAE, which requires consideration of international guidelines in the context of regional and local cultural sensitivities and healthcare resources. A holistic approach is recommended, combining the most appropriate pharmacological treatment with lifestyle interventions, education, and support for patients and care partners. Patients require ongoing monitoring to ensure the approach is tailored to the disease stage and provides optimal quality of life and reduced burden for patients and care partners.
