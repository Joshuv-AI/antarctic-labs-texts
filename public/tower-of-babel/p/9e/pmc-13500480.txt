Structural basis of PLPP3-mediated lipid phosphate dephosphorylation and its role in melanoma
Authors: Yingjie Wu, Di Xiao, Xingfan Li, Keyu Wang, Hongxu Zhang, Yuanyuan Zhao, Di Wu, Ruxi Qi, Mi Zhou, Han Han, Tao Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid phosphates serve as signaling molecules involved in diverse cellular processes such as cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, inflammation, immunity and cancer progression. Phospholipid phosphatases (PLPPs) modulate these signals by catalyzing the dephosphorylation of lipid phosphates. Here, we report the cryo-EM structure of PLPP3, revealing a tetrameric assembly. PLPP3 contains six transmembrane helices (TMs) and an extracellular domain that contains two extracellular loops. TMs 1–4 create a hydrophobic cleft that holds the tails of a phospholipid while the extracellular domain forms a positively charged pocket to accommodate the polar head group. Two conserved catalytic histidine residues in this pocket coordinate a putative zinc ion previously identified as a PLPP3 inhibitor. Structural mapping of somatic mutations with functional analysis reveals that PLPP3 acts as a tumor suppressor in melanoma. Together, our findings provide critical insights into the structure, substrate engagement, inhibitory mechanism, and cancer-related function of PLPP3.

Introduction

Phosphatidic acid (PA), lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P), and ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P) are key lipid phosphates that act as bioactive signaling molecules regulating diverse biological functions. PA is best known as a central intermediate in lipid metabolism that supports phospholipid synthesis and membrane biogenesis, while specific PA pools have also been implicated in signaling pathways regulating proliferation, survival, and membrane trafficking1–4. LPA and S1P influence cell motility, angiogenesis, vascular permeability, and immunity, primarily via G protein–coupled receptors5–11. Aberrant activation of LPA and S1P signaling pathways has been frequently observed across a variety of cancers, implicating their roles in tumor progression and metastasis12,13. C1P, derived from ceramide phosphorylation, functions as an intracellular messenger in pathways controlling proliferation, inflammation, and differentiation14–16.

The primary route for the degradation of these lipids involves dephosphorylation mediated by the type 2 phosphohydrolases (PAP2), which are Mg²⁺-independent and predominantly function as integral transmembrane proteins17. In mammals, PAP2 activity is carried out by six distinct enzymes belonging to the phospholipid phosphatase (PLPP) family, which possess highly conserved catalytic domains and can hydrolyze a variety of lipid phosphates while differing in substrate preferences18. PLPP1, PLPP2, and PLPP3—formerly known as LPP1, LPP2, and LPP3, or PAP2a, PAP2c, and PAP2b, respectively—target PA, LPA, S1P, C1P, and diacylglycerol pyrophosphate (DGPP). PLPP4 and PLPP5 prefer DGPP but can also act on PA and LPA, whereas PLPP6 favors presqualene diphosphate over PA and DGPP19–21. PLPP1–3 are insensitive to N-ethylmaleimide but inhibited by zinc ions and propranolol, whereas PLPP4–5 are sensitive to N-ethylmaleimide19,20,22.

Among PLPP family, PLPP3 is the predominant isoform expressed in lung, salivary gland, oral mucosa, duodenum, smooth muscle, and skin23. PLPP3 activity influences the biology and pathology of these tissues, as its deficiency in either endothelial or smooth muscle cells leads to increased inflammation and vascular permeability24,25. Genome-wide association analyses have identified PLPP3 as a genetic locus for coronary artery disease susceptibility, with heritable single-nucleotide polymorphisms within the gene linked to elevated disease risk26–29. Global knockout of PLPP3 gene in mice results in embryonic lethality, attributed to impaired development of the extraembryonic vasculature30. Targeted deletion of PLPP3 in the heart causes impaired myocardial function and ultimately leads to heart failure in mice31. Importantly, PLPP3 promotes endothelial cell–cell adhesion by interacting with integrins at the plasma membrane via its arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD) recognition motif32. PLPP3 is frequently downregulated in various cancers18, leading to elevated PA, LPA and S1P signaling, which promotes tumor growth and metastasis. In ovarian cancer cells, PLPP3 overexpression enhances extracellular LPA hydrolysis, thereby reducing colony formation and promoting apoptosis33. Collectively, accumulating studies have highlighted the involvement of PLPP3 in modulating key physiological pathways and its dysregulation in multiple pathological states.

PLPP3 is predicted to contain six transmembrane helices, with both its N- and C-termini oriented toward the cytoplasm when embedded in the plasma membrane; its three catalytic domains are exposed extracellularly and harbor the conserved phosphatase motif “(KX₆RP-XSGH-SRX₅HX₃D)” characteristic of the PAP2 enzyme family22,34. Although the structures of integral membrane PAP2 members such as bacterial PgpBs and human G6PC1 have been recently resolved35–39, evolutionary divergence among PAP2 family members presents challenges in elucidating the catalytic mechanism of phospholipid phosphatases and the precise binding mode of lipid substrates based on these existing structural models. In this study, we determine the structure of tetrameric PLPP3, with PA and a putative zinc ion observed at each catalytic site, providing the structural insight into substrate recognition and zinc-mediated inhibition. By integrating structural mapping of cancer-associated mutations with functional assays, we demonstrate that PLPP3 functions as a negative regulator of melanoma progression.

Results

Biochemical characterization and structure determination of PLPP3

We overexpressed Flag-tagged human PLPP3 in HEK293F cells and purified the recombinant proteins using an anti-Flag M2 resin. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) of PLPP3 revealed good behavior when purified in detergent solution, while SDS–PAGE analysis showed smeared bands (Supplementary Fig. 1), consistent with the presence of Asn170-linked glycosylation22. To further validate the activity of recombinant PLPP3, we incubated the protein with 1 mM PA at 37 °C, then measured inorganic phosphate released by Malachite Green Phosphate Assay. The result demonstrates that recombinant PLPP3 exhibits proper enzymatic activity in vitro (Fig. 1a). We determined the structure of PLPP3 by cryo-EM at 2.9-Å resolution (Fig. 1c–e, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3 and Table 1). Four PLPP3 protomers form a homotetramer. Therefore, C4 symmetry was assumed during data processing. In the cryo-EM map, the transmembrane helix core reached a local resolution of approximately 2.0–2.5 Å, allowing unambiguous distinction of most side chains and enabling clear identification of the associated endogenous lipids, which were well-resolved within the density.

Overall structure of PLPP3

The cryo-EM structure of the PLPP3 tetramer is consistent with earlier biochemical studies proposing its homo-oligomeric assembly40,41. Each protomer interacts with two neighboring protomers, and the interface between the two PLPP3 protomers buries a total solvent-accessible surface area of approximately 1280 Å2. The architecture of a protomer contains six TM helices and an extracellular domain, which is in line with the AlphaFold2-predicted structure of PLPP3 (Supplementary Fig. 2d). TMs 3-4 form the helical bundle core that is enclosed by TMs 1-2 and 5-6. The extracellular loop 1 (ECL1) and ECL2, which form the extracellular domain, are connected by a disulfide bond between Cys68 and Cys171 (Fig. 1f). Additionally, the conformation of ECL2 is further stabilized by an internal disulfide bond between Cys162 and Cys181 (Fig. 1f). The Cys68–Cys171 disulfide bond is conserved in PLPP1–3, whereas the Cys162–Cys181 bond within ECL2 is conserved across PLPP1–5 (Supplementary Fig. 4). The C68A/C171A mutant retained tetrameric assembly and dephosphorylation activity (Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, the C162A/C181A mutant, as well as the quadruple mutant (C68A/C171A/C162A/C181A), exhibited a higher-order oligomeric or aggregated state and showed a loss of catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting a critical role of the ECL2 disulfide bond in maintaining structural integrity and enzymatic function.

The homotetrameric assembly of PLPP3 is maintained through both membrane-spanning α-helices and loops at the extracellular region. The hydrophobic interactions among the TM1 and TM3 with the TM5 from the adjacent protomer form the membrane-oligomeric interface (Fig. 1g). Specifically, residues Leu39 in TM1, Phe135/Ile139/Phe143 in TM3, and Leu226/Leu229/Phe232/Met236/Phe239 in TM5 all contribute to the membrane-oligomeric interface. ECL1s and ECL2s interact with ECL1s from the neighboring protomer and these interactions are probably stabilized by hydrogen bonds between residues Tyr62 and Asn69 in ECL1 with residues Lys78, Glu71 in ECL1 of the adjacent protomer and hydrophobic contacts among residues Tyr 67 in ECL1, Tyr 175 in ECL2 and Tyr 75 in ECL1 of the adjacent protomer. Moreover, four endogenous phospholipids are identified at the membrane-oligomeric interfaces, potentially contributing to the stability of the tetramer (Fig. 1e, g). Mutations at the tetramer interface reduced PLPP3 activity, supporting a functional contribution of tetrameric assembly (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Substrate recognition of PLPP3

Each protomer of PLPP3 is composed of a hydrophobic cleft formed by TMs1-4. The opening of this cleft on the extracellular leaflet connects to the extracellular domain, forming a highly positively charged pocket that likely serves as the binding site for the polar head group of lipid substrates. A phospholipid density was observed in each cleft (Fig. 2a, b). Owing to both the morphology of the density and the chemical environment of the binding site, together with the detection of PA in the purified PLPP3 by mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 7a), each lipid was tentatively assigned as PA. Many residues, including Leu44/Phe51 in TM1, Ile93 in TM2, Leu134/Ala138 in TM3 and Phe202 in TM4 generate a hydrophobic environment to host the fatty acid chains of PA (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, our cryo-EM structure reveals that the positively charged pocket involves a hydrophilic interaction network, which facilitates lipid engagement. The phosphate group of this PA molecule has direct hydrophilic interactions with residues Lys148 in TM3 and His199 in TM4. The side chain of Arg155 in ECL2 interacts with a water molecule, which in turn forms a hydrogen bond with the phosphate group of PA. This interaction network is likely critical for recruiting the phosphate-containing substrate into the catalytic pocket (Fig. 2c). Remarkably, the imidazole ring of this strictly conserved histidine residue His199 is close to the phosphate ester bond of PA at a distance of 3.2 Å, which is in agreement with the proposed catalytic model in which His199 catalyzes the cleavage of the terminal phosphate group from the substrate. Substitution of Lys148, Arg155 and His199 with alanine reduced the catalytic activity, demonstrating that the hydrophilic interactions between the enzyme and phosphate group is critical for the substrate recognition (Fig. 2d). Mutations Phe51Ala and Phe202Ala did not affect Pi release, suggesting that the transmembrane region of the cleft primarily provides a hydrophobic environment for engaging lipid substrates, rather than contributing to specific substrate recognition (Fig. 2d). Together, the mutagenesis results indicate that PLPP3 preferentially recognizes the phosphate headgroup of lipid substrates over their fatty acid moieties. To examine whether PLPP3 can act on a broader range of phosphate-containing substrates, we tested both lipid phosphates (LPA and S1P) and soluble phosphate esters (G6P and ATP). Remarkably, PLPP3 hydrolyzed all of these substrates independently of Mg²⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 7b–d). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further support this result (Supplementary Fig. 7e), showing that both S1P and ATP bind to the same substrate pocket as PA. Interestingly, the ATP analog AMP-PCP failed to inhibit PLPP3-mediated PA hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Structurally, the phosphoester oxygen of PA, equivalent to the β–γ bridging oxygen of ATP, forms a key hydrogen bond with His199 (Fig. 2c). This interaction is abolished in AMP-PCP due to replacement by a methylene group, which likely prevents productive engagement of the catalytic site and highlights the importance of phosphate ester recognition. Catalytic core in PLPP3

Our cryo-EM structure of PLPP3 reveals the conserved catalytic site of PAP2 superfamily, which consists of the three signature phosphatase motifs and closely resembles the previously reported structures of PAP2 members PgpB and G6PC1 (Supplementary Fig. 8). The C1 motif (KX₆RP) and C2 motif (FSGH) are positioned at opposite ends of ECL2, while the C3 motif (SRX5HX3D) encompasses the region extending from the C-terminal end of TM5 to the N-terminal end of TM6 (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, the C2 motif of PLPP3 differs from the canonical signature motif (PSGH), with the proline residue replaced by phenylalanine. In the established two-step catalytic mechanism of PAP2 enzymes, catalytically essential residues include a histidine–aspartate (His251 and Asp255 in PLPP3) charge-relay pair located at the C-terminal end of the C3 motif. This pair initiates catalysis by mediating nucleophilic attack and forming a transient phosphate–enzyme intermediate. In the subsequent step, the histidine residue (His199 in PLPP3) within the C2 motif plays a key role in stabilizing the transition state and facilitating the cleavage of the substrate’s terminal phosphate group. In our structure of PLPP3, the distance between His251 and Asp255 in the C3 motif is 3.9 Å, which may form a weak charge-relay pair; instead, the hydroxymethyl group of Ser194 and the backbone carbonyl oxygen atoms of Ser194 and Lys250 engage in electrostatic interactions with His251 at distances of 3.6, 3.1, and 3.0 Å, respectively (Fig. 3e). These findings suggest that the conserved histidine in C3 motif is likely activated through cooperative interactions with surrounding residues, enabling the formation of a phosphate–enzyme intermediate during catalysis.

An ion-like density is observed at the catalytic center between the transmembrane domain and the extracellular domain. When PLPP3 was prepared in the presence of EDTA, this density was absent from the cryo-EM map (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating that it corresponds to a chelatable metal ion. A previous study22, together with our functional assays, shows that PLPP3 is inhibited by zinc (Fig. 1b). These observations therefore suggest that the density most likely represents a Zn²⁺ ion at the catalytic site, although an unambiguous assignment cannot currently be made. The putative Zn²⁺ ion is coordinated by His199 and His251 in the catalytic core and lies adjacent to a water molecule that facilitates PA recruitment to the positively charged pocket (Figs. 2c and 3e). Mutating both histidine residues to alanine (2HA) in the transmembrane domain markedly reduces zinc-binding affinity in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 10a). These histidines adopt similar conformations across structures, and zinc also inhibits the dephosphorylation activity of PgpB and G6PC1 (Supplementary Fig. 10b), suggesting a conserved mechanism of zinc-mediated inhibition among the PAP2 family. In the G6P-bound G6PC1 structure, the positively charged Arg170 in the C3 motif forms polar interactions with the phosphate group of G6P (Fig. 3f). In contrast, the equivalent residue in PLPP3, Arg245 in the C3 motif, rotates its side chain away from the phosphate group, likely due to the presence of the putative Zn²⁺ ion (Fig. 3g). Interestingly, Arg245 in the prediction AlphaFold model adopts a conformation resembling that in the G6P-bound G6PC1 structure (Fig. 3f), suggesting an alternate state in which Arg245 may engage the phosphate group within the catalytic center. Our mutagenesis data show that Arg245 is required for the phosphatase activity (Fig. 3d), highlighting its role in lipid substrate engagement. Since His251 coordinates the putative Zn²⁺ ion, it cannot be polarized to form the phosphate–histidine intermediate. Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that the current structure represents the inactive state.

To further validate this inactive state and explore the active conformation, we performed MD simulations of the PLPP3–PA complex in the presence and absence of zinc. The global protein backbone remained highly stable across all simulated conditions, confirming the structural integrity of the tetrameric assembly (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). In the presence of zinc, the overall root mean square deviation (RMSD) of zinc, water, the PA headgroup, and the sidechains of five critical residues (Lys148, Arg155, His199, Arg245 and His251) remained consistently around 2 Å, confirming that the complex adopts a stable conformation (Supplementary Fig. 10e). Upon removal of zinc, the coordinating water molecule was displaced (Supplementary Fig. 10f), and PA moved deeper into the catalytic core, establishing direct interactions with Lys148, Arg155, His199, Arg245 and His251 (Supplementary Fig. 10g). Notably, Arg245 reoriented to engage the phosphate group, adopting a conformation similar to that observed in the AlphaFold model and the G6PC1 structure (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 10g). To quantitatively assess these structural transitions, we analyzed the sidechain root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) and catalytic geometry metrics. The five critical residues maintained highly restricted fluctuations (<1.0 Å) in both states (Supplementary Fig. 10h), indicating that zinc removal does not alter the global flexibility of the active site. Instead, distance distribution analyses revealed a systematic rearrangement of the catalytic geometry: in the absence of zinc, the phosphate group moved significantly closer to the nucleophilic nitrogens of His251 and His199, and the sidechains of Lys148 and Arg245 (Supplementary Fig. 10i). Together, these findings support a model in which zinc stabilizes an inactive conformation, whereas its absence permits rearrangement of key residues to facilitate substrate binding and catalysis.

Somatic mutations in the catalytic core of PLPP3 contribute to cancer development

To investigate the functional role of PLPP3 in human cancers, we analyzed its somatic alterations using TCGA datasets, comprising a total of 10,953 cancer patients across 32 cancer studies. Among these, melanoma exhibited the highest mutation frequency, with a rate of 4.3% (Fig. 4a). However, whether these mutations contribute functionally to cancer development remains unclear. Our structure of PLPP3 provides a molecular framework for annotating somatic missense mutations linked to cancers (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, most of the mutations are located in the extracellular domain, with four mutations (R155H, S194F, R245C, and D255G) occurring at the catalytic center. All four mutations impaired phosphatase activity of PLPP3 (Fig. 4c). Among them, three mutations (S194F, R245C, and D255G) were identified in five patients diagnosed with melanoma, whereas the R155H mutation was associated with cervical cancer (Supplementary Table 2). Since the R245C mutation was identified in the largest number of patients (Fig. 4b), we utilized the A375 melanoma cell line to stably overexpress either the wild-type PLPP3, the cancer-associated R245C variant, or a catalytically inactive double mutant (H199A/H251A) to investigate the role of PLPP3 in melanoma (Fig. 4d). Colony formation assays revealed that both the R245C variant and the loss-of-function double mutant exhibited growth kinetics comparable to control cells, indicating that these mutations do not impair proliferation of melanoma cell. In contrast, cells expressing wild-type PLPP3 showed a significant reduction in proliferation, suggesting a suppressive effect on melanoma cell growth (Fig. 4e, f). To evaluate the tumor-suppressive role of PLPP3 in vivo, we performed xenograft assays by subcutaneously injecting the indicated stable A375 cells to M-NSG mice. We found that expression of the wild-type PLPP3 significantly inhibited xenograft tumor growth, whereas the cancer-associated R245C variant and the catalytically inactive mutant (H199A/H251A) did not (Fig. 4g, h). These results suggest that the tumor-suppressive function of PLPP3 in melanoma depends on its catalytic activity. Discussion

In this study, we report the cryo-EM structure of PLPP3 from the PLPP family. The structure reveals a homotetrameric assembly (Fig. 1c–e), providing a framework for understanding its molecular organization. This oligomeric state, stabilized by protein–protein interactions and membrane-associated phospholipids at the subunit interfaces (Fig. 1g), contributes to full enzymatic activation (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Sequence alignment indicates that most residues involved in interface interactions are conserved or semi-conserved in PLPP1–3, but not in PLPP4–6 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistently, prior biochemical studies have suggested that PLPP1–3 form homo-oligomeric assemblies40,41. The structures of PLPP1 and PLPP2 reported during the review of our manuscript also adopt tetrameric assemblies42–44, further supporting the conservation of this oligomeric state within the PLPP1–3 subfamily. In addition to homooligomer formation, PLPP1–3 have also been reported to form functional heterooligomers42. The physiological relevance of these assemblies and their potential contribution to functional diversification within the PLPP1-3 subfamily remain to be explored. In the PLPP1 structure, multiple PA molecules were observed at the interface and proposed to contribute to assembly stability. In contrast, our PLPP3 structure reveals phosphatidylcholine (PC) at the corresponding sites (Fig. 1e), suggesting variability in the interface lipid environment within the PLPP1–3 subfamily, with PA in PLPP3 more likely associated with catalytic-site substrate binding rather than structural stabilization. Beyond their shared oligomeric organization, PLPP1–3 exhibit a highly conserved catalytic architecture, including the arrangement of the active site and the membrane-associated substrate access pathway, consistent with a common catalytic mechanism for lipid dephosphorylation42–44.

The structural elucidation of PLPP3 in an intermediate state, with the substrate PA and a putative zinc ion observed at the active site, opens avenues for understanding the substrate recognition mechanism in lipid phosphate dephosphorylation and its regulation by zinc. PLPP3 employs a hydrophobic cleft to accommodate the lipid tails of its substrates, while the phosphate head group is specifically recognized by a positively charged opening at the cleft entrance. This substrate recognition mode is reminiscent of the bacterial lipid phosphatase PgpB, which also features an open hydrophobic cleft for various lipid substrates accommodation (Supplementary Fig. 8c). In contrast, the catalytic core of another PAP2 family member, G6PC1, is buried within the protein, restricting access primarily to soluble substrates (Supplementary Fig. 8d). This phosphate-directed recognition underlines PLPP3’s broad substrate specificity and its ability to act on structurally diverse phospholipids with varied fatty acid compositions and soluble phosphate substrates (Supplementary Fig. 7b). However, given its plasma membrane localization, PLPP3 is unlikely to encounter these soluble phosphate substrates under physiological conditions in vivo.

Zinc acts as an inhibitor of several phosphatases, such as PLPP1-3, Protein Phosphatase 2A, Protein Tyrosine Phosphatases and Receptor Tyrosine Phosphatase β22,45–48. Although a previous study on tyrosine phosphatase 1B has proposed that zinc inhibits the phosphatase activity through directly binding to the catalytic center49, no structural evidence has been reported to date. In our cryo-EM structure, a putative Zn²⁺ ion is observed near the catalytic residues His199 and His251, as well as a water molecule involved in phosphate binding. Together with our biochemical and computational analyses, these observations support a model in which zinc may interfere with catalysis by occupying the catalytic center and restricting formation of the histidine–phosphate intermediate. Our structure, therefore, provides a structural framework for understanding zinc-mediated inhibition in phosphatases and may offer broader insight into how zinc interacts with the catalytic center to suppress enzymatic activity.

Despite the rapid accumulation of cancer genomic data from projects such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), numerous somatic mutations have been identified in human cancers. However, the functional significance of these mutations, particularly in the context of their three-dimensional spatial structures, remains largely unexplored. By integrating cancer genomic and structural analysis, we uncover a tumor-suppressive role of PLPP3 in melanoma. Somatic mutations such as R245C, S194F, D255G, and R155H significantly impair phosphate hydrolysis, indicating a loss of enzymatic function. Notably, most patients harboring these mutations were diagnosed with melanoma, suggesting potential clinical relevance. The tumor-suppressive effect of PLPP3 was further validated through melanoma cell proliferation assays in vitro and a xenograft assay in vivo (Fig. 4d–h). These findings suggest that loss-of-function mutations disrupt PLPP3 enzymatic activity, thereby eliminating its inhibitory effect on tumor growth and contributing to melanoma progression. Although PLPP1–3 share conserved catalytic motifs, increasing evidence indicates that they are not functionally equivalent in cancer. In many tumor types, PLPP1 and PLPP3 are reduced, whereas PLPP2 is elevated, consistent with isoform-specific roles in lysophospholipid signaling, cell-cycle regulation, and tumor–microenvironment interactions. In particular, PLPP2 has been linked to increased c-Myc expression and promotion of G1/S progression50, whereas the function of PLPP3 appears to be more context dependent across cancer types51–53. Collectively, these observations support a context-dependent role for PLPP3 across different cancer types.

In conclusion, this study provides mechanistic insights into substrate recognition and zinc inhibition in PLPP3. Moreover, the functional analyses implicate PLPP3 as a tumor suppressor in melanoma, with loss-of-function mutations facilitating tumor progression. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the PAP2 superfamily but also aid in the development of potential therapeutics for cancers associated with PLPP3 dysfunction.

Methods

Ethics

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) of the TaiKang Center for Life and Medical Sciences, Wuhan University (protocol no. MA-009). Male NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgem1Smoc (M-NSG) mice (4 weeks old; Cat. NO. NM-NSG-001) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the animal core facility of the TaiKang Center for Life and Medical Sciences. Mice were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at 24 °C and 60% humidity, with free access to food and water.

Protein expression and purification

The cDNAs of human PLPP3 (UniProt ID: O14495) and G6PC1 (UniProt ID: P35575) were individually cloned into the pHL-sec vector, each with a C-terminal Flag-tag. The E. coli pgpB (UniProt ID: P01924) gene was cloned into the pET-28a vector with a C-terminal 6 × His-tag. Point mutations were introduced into the coding region of PLPP3 by two-step PCR using primers synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Supplementary Data 1). The coding region of each plasmid was sequenced to ensure the integrity of the construct. PLPP3 and its mutants as well as G6PC1 were expressed in HEK-293F cells using the transient transfection system. HEK-293F cells were cultured in 293 F Hi-exp medium (OPM) at 37 °C in 8% CO₂ conditions. Cell transient transfection was performed when the cell density reached ~3 × 106 cells/ml. PEI (1.28 mg, Polyscience) was mixed with 480 µg plasmids in 36 ml fresh medium. After incubation for 20 min, the mixture was added to 400 ml of cells. After growing at 37 °C for 8 h, 20 ml of supplementary feeding medium (OPM) and 1.62 g glucose were added per 400 ml of cells, and the cells were cultured for an additional 36 h before harvesting. The E. coli pgpB plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain. Cells were grown at 37 °C in Lysogeny Broth medium supplemented with kanamycin until the cell density reached an OD600 of 1.0. The temperature was then reduced to 25 °C before induction with 1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). After growth at 25 °C for 18 h, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g.

Protein purification was performed as previously described with a few modifications54. For PLPP3 purification, cells were disrupted by sonication in buffer A (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 5 μg/ml leupeptin. After low-speed centrifugation (3800 × g, rotor: M-S4-400, RWD, 4 °C, 5 min), the resulting supernatant was incubated in buffer A with 1% (w/v) n-Dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) (ExceedBio) for 1 h at 4 °C. The lysate was then centrifuged at high-speed (40,000 × g, rotor: R30AT-0808, Eppendorf, 4 °C, 25 min) to remove insoluble components, and the supernatant was loaded onto the anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma-Aldrich). After washing with two column volumes, the protein was eluted in buffer A supplemented with 100 μg/ml 3×Flag peptide (Shanghai Apeptide Co., Ltd), 0.02% (w/v) DDM and concentrated by a 30-kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge filter (Membrane Solutions LLC, UTPES150030N). The concentrated protein was purified by a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 size-exclusion chromatography column (GE Healthcare) in buffer A with 0.005% (w/v) glyco-diosgenin (GDN). For PLPP3 purified in the presence of EDTA, the purification procedure was identical except that 2 mM EDTA was added throughout the process. The purification procedure for G6PC1 is the same as that for PLPP3, except that the gel filtration buffer was changed to buffer B (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% (w/v) LMNG and 0.0005% (w/v) CHS). For PgpB purification, cells were resuspended in ice-cold buffer C (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 5 μg/ml leupeptin, and homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 90 min. The membrane fraction was resuspended in buffer C with 1% (w/v) n-Dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) for 1 h at 4 °C. After another ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 30 min, the supernatant was collected and loaded on 2 mL of Ni2+ affinity resin (Smart-Sciences). After incubation for 1 h, the resin was washed with 50 mL of Buffer C supplemented with 20 mM imidazole and 0.03% DDM. The protein sample was eluted with 10 mL Buffer C containing 500 mM imidazole, and 0.03% DDM, and it was concentrated to 1 mL. The concentrated protein sample was then loaded onto a Superdex-200 column (10/30; GE Healthcare) preequilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, and 0.03% DDM. Finally, the peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, and directly flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Inorganic phosphate (Pi) release assay

The activities of PLPP3 and its variants, as well as PgpB and G6PC1, were determined by a colorimetric assay that measures inorganic phosphate released by the Malachite Green Phosphate Detection Kit (Beyotime). 16:0 PA (Sangon Biotech, A430078) in methanol was dried in vacuum over night to remove the solvent. The lipid layer was resuspended in buffer A with 0.005% (w/v) GDN to reach a final concentration of 1 mM and sonicated in a water bath at room temperature for 30 min. Then, 500 nM protein was added to 60 μL of 1 mM PA, and the reaction was allowed to proceed at 37 °C for 45 min. Subsequently, 240 μL of a methanol-chloroform mixture (2:1, v/v) was added to terminate the reaction. After vortexing, 96 μL of chloroform and 96 μL of 1 N HCl were added, followed by centrifugation at 1200 × g for 5 min to extract the released inorganic phosphate (Pi). To quantify phosphate release, 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new transparent flat-bottom 96-well plate and mixed with 70 μL of pre-prepared color development reagent (Malachite Green Reagent A:Malachite Green Reagent B, 2:1, v/v). After incubation at room temperature for 30 min, color development was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 630 nm using a BioTek plate reader. When the substrate is LPA, S1P and G6P, the measurement of the Pi release amount is the same as that for PA, while the final concentration of G6P is 5 mM. When the substrate is ATP, the Pi release amount was measured by quantitating inorganic phosphate using the Malachite Green Phosphate Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich). Purified protein at a final concentration of 500 nM was incubated in 20 μL reaction buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% (w/v) GDN and 2 mM ATP. After 60 min incubation at 37 °C, 80 μL reagent was mixed with 20 μL reaction product in 96-well plates and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Absorbance at 620 nm was measured using a BioTek plate reader. For time-course analysis of ATP and G6P hydrolysis by PLPP3, ATP and G6P were used at final concentrations of 2 mM and 5 mM, respectively. The Pi release amount was measured according to the corresponding method described above. To assess whether AMP-PCP inhibits PLPP3-mediated PA hydrolysis, the protein was incubated with 1 mM AMP-PCP on ice for 10 min prior to the addition of 1 mM PA. For G6PC1, the G6P was resuspended in buffer B to reach a final concentration of 1 mM. For PGPB, the PA was resuspended in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, and 0.03% DDM to reach a final concentration of 1 mM and sonicated in a water bath at room temperature for 30 min. The phosphate concentration was determined using a phosphate standard curve. All the measurements were repeated at least three times independently for all the constructs.

Zinc inhibition assay

For the determination of the IC50 of zinc for PLPP3, ZnCl₂ was preincubated with PLPP3 at the indicated concentrations for 10 min on ice prior to the addition of PA. To assess zinc inhibition of PgpB and G6PC1, the proteins were similarly preincubated with 1 mM ZnCl₂ for 10 min before substrate addition. Pi release was measured as described above.

Chemical crosslinking assay

Purified PLPP3 and its mutants disrupting the disulfide bond were incubated with the crosslinker glutaraldehyde at final concentrations of 2 mM for 2 min at room temperature. The reactions were quenched by the addition of 1 M Tris, pH 8.0. Samples were then mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer and subjected to SDS–PAGE. Gels were subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

Cryo-EM sample preparation, data collection and processing

A volume of 3 μl of the protein sample at ~10 mg ml−1 was added to Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh Cu holey carbon grids (Quantifoil), blotted using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) and frozen in liquid ethane. The grids were imaged in a 300 kV Titan Krios (FEI). Raw movie stacks were collected with a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan) at 0.824 Å per pixel and a nominal defocus range of −1.2 to −1.8 μm. Each stack of 40 frames was exposed for 2.16 s, with an exposure time of 0.054 s per frame. The total dose for each stack was ~ 50 electrons per Å2.

Dark subtracted images were first normalized by gain reference (0.824 Å per pixel). Drift correction, CTF, Blob or template picking and particle extraction were performed and estimated using CryoSPARC55. Subsequent two-dimensional classification, multi-class ab initio modeling, heterogenous three-dimensional (3D) refinement, nonuniform refinement were performed in v4.4.0. All the masks for the refinement were automatically generated by CryoSPARC during the nonuniform refinement and local refinement.

Model building

The initial models were built based on the AlphaFold2 predicted structure of PLPP3 (AF-O14495-F1-model_v6) and then manually adjusted and refined using COOT56. Due to the limited resolution, residues 1–28, 283–311 were not built. The models were refined in real space using PHENIX57. For cross-validation, MolProbity58 was used to validate the final model. Local resolutions were estimated using CryoSPARC. Structure figures were generated using COOT56, PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org) and ChimeraX59.

Untargeted lipidomics analysis

Lipids from recombinant proteins were extracted using a modified methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE)/methanol approach60. Briefly, 40 μl of each sample was mixed with 300 μl methanol and 1 ml MTBE, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. Phase-separation was achieved by adding 250 μl H2O and centrifuging at 400 × g for 10 min. The upper (organic) phase was transferred to a new glass vial, and the lower (aqueous) phase was further subjected to a second extraction step with 400 μl of solvent mixture (MTBE/methanol/H2O:10/3/2.5). The combined organic phases were dried with a nitrogen flow. The dried lipids were reconstituted in 60% acetonitrile (ACN) for subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis. For LC-MS/MS analysis, the lipid separation was performed on a 10 cm C8 column using a Vanquish Neo LC System. A binary mobile phase system was used with a linear gradient (18 min, flow rate 400 nL/min), where mobile phase B was increased from 20 to 99%. Mobile phase A was ACN/water (60:40, v/v) supplemented with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid; mobile phase B was isopropanol/ACN (90:10, v/v) containing 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. The eluted lipids were introduced into an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) via a nanospray source. Typical MS settings were a spray voltage of 1.8 kV, capillary temperature of 275 °C and RF lens set to 40%. Mass spectrometry was operated in small-molecule mode with data-dependent acquisition (DDA) under negative ionization mode. Full-scan MS spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap (m/z 300–2000) at a resolution of 120,000. HCD fragmentation was performed with a stepped collision energy of 25% and 30% and AGC target of 200%. The mass spectra were processed with MS-DIAL (v5.5) with default parameters. The lipid identification and quantification were manually checked.

Cell lines

HEK293T (ATCC: CRL-3216) and the human melanoma cell line A375 (ATCC: CRL-1619) were purchased from ATCC. Both HEK293T and A375 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ (v/v).

Constructs and viruses

Plasmid encoding wild-type PLPP3 was generated via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from cDNA of the HEK293T cell line and subcloned into the pDONR201 vector using Gateway Technology (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as entry plasmid. PCR-based mutagenesis was used to generate the PLPP3 site mutations on the pDONOR201-PLPP3 construct. For the generation of stable cell lines expressing PLPP3 and its mutants, the entry plasmids were recombined into a Gateway-compatible lentiviral destination vector to express SFB-tagged fusion proteins. Lentiviral supernatants were produced by transient transfection of HEK293T cells with the helper plasmids pSPAX2 and pMD2.G by using polyethyleneimine (PEI) (23966-2, Polysciences) and harvested 48 h later. Supernatants were passed through a 0.45-µm filter and used to infect cells in the presence of 8 µg/mL hexadimethrine bromide (Polybrene) (Sigma-Aldrich). After viral infection, the stable cell lines were evaluated by western blotting using an anti-Flag M2 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804, 1:5000) for PLPP3 detection, with an anti-Tubulin antibody (Proteintech, 66031-1-Ig, 1:10,000) serving as the internal loading control. Peroxidase AffiniPure® Rabbit anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, 315-035-003, 1:5000) was used as the secondary antibody.

Zinc binding assay

Zinc or Calcium beads were generated using ProfinityTM IMAC resins (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, IMAC resins were washed with ultra-pure H2O for 5 times and incubated with 0.2 M ZnCl2 or CaCl2 solutions at 4 °C for 2 h. The metal-charged beads were washed 5 times with ultra-pure H2O, twice with acidic buffer (0.05 M sodium acetate, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 4.0), and stored in binding buffer (0.05 M sodium phosphate, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 8.0) at 4 °C. The binding assay was performed as previously described61, equal amount of PLPP3 (full length or TM domain) or PLPP3-H199A/H251A (full length or TM domain) proteins were add to NTN buffer (100 mM NaCl; 20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0; 0.5% Nonidet P-40) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors and incubated with the Zinc beads or the control beads (Calcium beads) at 4 °C overnight and then subject to western blotting assay. Mouse monoclonal anti-Flag M2 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804) and Peroxidase AffiniPure® Rabbit anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, 315-035-003) were used as the primary and secondary antibodies, respectively, to detect the indicated Flag-tagged fusion proteins.

System preparation for molecular dynamics simulations

Four distinct systems representing different substrate states within the catalytic pocket were constructed: (1) PLPP3 co-complexed with DLPA and Zn²⁺ (denoted as with Zn.PA); (2) PLPP3 with DLPA but lacking Zn²⁺ (without Zn.PA); (3) PLPP3 with ATP and lacking Zn²⁺ (without Zn.ATP); and (4) PLPP3 with sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and lacking Zn²⁺ (without Zn.S1P). All simulation systems were constructed using the full tetrameric PLPP3 assembly. The initial coordinates for the Zn.PA system was directly derived from our experimentally determined cryo-EM structure. The remaining systems were generated by removing Zn²⁺ ion and aligning the phosphate headgroups of the respective ligands (ATP or S1P) with that of the experimentally resolved DLPA in UCSF ChimeraX, ensuring optimal spatial matching within the catalytic pocket.

Force field parameters

Given that the Zn²⁺ ion in the with Zn.PA system exhibits a non-covalent, coordination-type interaction within the catalytic site rather than a structurally constrained, bonded geometry (e.g., as seen in zinc-finger motifs); a non-bonded model was employed. Specifically, the Lennard-Jones 12-6-4 (LJ 12-6-4) potential developed by Li and Mertz62 was utilized to accurately capture the charge-induced dipole interactions characteristic of the Zn²⁺ coordination sphere. For the ATP molecule, force field parameters were adopted from the study by Carlson et al.63.

The AMBER LIPID21 force field provides parameters for the sphingosine acyl chain of S1P (residue SA) but lacks the corresponding phosphate headgroup. To simulate the intact S1P molecule, we developed a customized phosphate headgroup residue (designated as PM) through quantum mechanics (QM) calculation and restrained electrostatic potential (RESP2) charge fitting64. To avoid unphysical conformational distortions in QM geometry optimization—where the negatively charged headgroup artificially bends toward the amine group due to a lack of solvent screening during in vacuo optimization—we extracted a representative S1P conformation from a high-resolution X-ray protein-ligand complex crystal structure (PDB ID: 2YG2, resolution 1.7 Å). This strategy bypasses the need for computationally exhaustive conformational sampling and iterative force field parametrization. The phosphate headgroup was isolated and capped with a methyl group (PM-cap), strictly following the modular residue-building strategy used in the LIPID21 force field. To ensure seamless assembly of the PM residue with the SA residue to form a complete S1P molecule with a net charge of −1, the net charge of the methyl cap was constrained to match the corresponding truncation atoms in the SA residue.

Subsequently, the PM-cap structure was subjected to hydrogen-position optimization in both the gas phase and implicit solvent phase at the B97-3c level of theory, followed by single-point energy calculations at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP level of theory using the ORCA 6.1 quantum chemistry package [https://www.faccts.de/orca/]. A two-stage RESP2 charge fitting was performed on the resulting electrostatic potentials using the Multiwfn program65, scaling the gas-phase and solvent-phase contributions at a 1:1 ratio. The derived RESP2 charges were then combined with the bonded parameters generated by the Antechamber and parmchk2 utilities in AMBER 24 (https://ambermd.org). All the resulting atom types and missing torsion terms were further assigned with LIPID21 parameters.

Simulation details

The prepared protein-ligand complexes were submitted to the CHARMM-GUI membrane builder66 to embed the systems in a POPC bilayer, solvated with an OPC water box, yielding initial system dimensions of approximately 96 × 96 × 102 Å³. The protonation states of ionizable amino acid residues were determined at pH 7.5 using Propka367. Based on the predicted pKa values, Glu55, His63, His157, His199, His249, His251, and His252 were modeled as deprotonated across all four systems. Additionally, Lys250 was deprotonated specifically in the presence of Zn.PA system. The systems were then neutralized with chloride ions, and additional NaCl was added to achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M using the LEaP module in AMBER 24.

All MD simulations were performed using the AMBER 24 package (https://ambermd.org). The protein, lipids, and glycosylation residues were described using the ff19SB, LIPID21, and GLYCAM 06j force fields, respectively. A cutoff distance of 10 Å was applied for van der Waals interactions, and long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method68. Standard energy minimization, gradual heating, and stepwise equilibration protocols were executed prior to the production runs. Production simulations were carried out in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 310 K and 1 atm, utilizing semi-isotropic Berendsen pressure coupling. The hydrogen mass repartitioning (HMR) scheme69 was applied to permit a stable integration time step of 4 fs. For each system, three independent production runs (1 μs per run) were initiated with randomized initial velocities. Trajectory analyses were conducted using the CPPTRAJ module implemented in AMBER 24 and custom in-house scripts. All structural figures and plots were generated using ChimeraX and gnuplot, respectively. The last 600 ns of the simulation trajectories were used for statistical analysis. All curves were smoothed with the Bezier algorithm implemented in gnuplot.

Bioinformatic analysis of PLPP3 somatic mutations

The bioinformation analysis was described previously by Han et al.70 with a few modifications. Human PLPP3 gene alteration data were obtained from the cBioportal database (http://www.cbioportal.org), where 10,953 cancer patients across 32 cancer studies (https://bit.ly/474yB8N) were selected for the bioinformatic analysis. All the PLPP3 gene alteration data and their associated patient samples were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Colony formation assays

For colony formation assays, 1 × 103 of the indicated A375 cells were seeded in triplicate into 6-well plates and fed intermittently with DMEM. After 10 days, colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. After staining, cells were washed three times with water and the dye was solubilized with acetic acid. The absorbance of the crystal violet solution was measured at OD 595 nm and normalized to wild-type A375 cells to indicate the relative cell proliferation.

Xenograft tumor assays

For the xenograft assay, the indicated A375 cells (2 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into male M-NSG mice. Tumors were collected 30 days after injection, and tumor weights were measured after the mice were euthanized. Tumor burden was monitored regularly throughout the xenograft experiments in accordance with the animal protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the TaiKang Center for Life and Medical Sciences, Wuhan University (protocol no. MA-009). The maximal tumor size/burden permitted under this protocol was 1.5 cm in diameter, and this limit was not exceeded in any animal used in this study.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized, and the investigators were not blinded to allocation during the experiments and outcome assessment. Each experiment was conducted at least three times independently with similar results.
