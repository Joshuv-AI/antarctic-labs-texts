Comorbidity Disease Pattern in Japanese Elderly Population: A Network-Based Analysis
Authors: Shuko Nojiri, Wataru Urasaki, Masao Iwagami, Masashi Nagao, Takuya Uematsu, Muneaki Ishijima, Hiroyuki Daida, Yuji Nishizaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo clarify the disease pattern for elderly individuals in the general population in Japan with multimorbidity networks based on an analysis of common chronic conditions.Patients and MethodsThis study is a population study of disease network analysis in elderly in Japan. We obtained medical records for 4,797,907 elderly patients in Japan (from January 1, 2020 to December 31, 2020) from administrative claim databases. The administrative claim records were transformed for temporal disease occurrence pattern analysis and used to find frequently occurring disease sequences to predict the onset of disease progression.ResultsIn those aged 75 years and older, circulatory and respiratory diseases gained predominance, while the burden of mental disorders remained substantial across all age groups. In men aged below 75 years, disseminated intravascular coagulation co-occurred frequently with acute myocardial infarction and respiratory failure. In women aged below 75 years, the pairing of vestibular dysfunction and hearing loss exhibited the highest association. For women aged 75 years and older, renal disease pairings, psychiatric comorbidities, and fall-related injuries were dominant. Network analyses reported increasing multimorbidity complexity with age. Respiratory, circulatory, and digestive disease clusters were central across age groups, with psychiatric and fracture-related conditions becoming more prominent in older adults, particularly women. Disease heatmaps further highlighted systemic comorbidity hubs involving pneumonia, iron deficiency anemia, kidney disease, and cardiovascular conditions.ConclusionIn this study, we identified major comorbidity patterns and reported that atherosclerosis, angina pectoris, gastritis, and duodenitis were the most important comorbid diseases in the elderly population in Japan. The identification of distinctive comorbidity profiles from routine clinical data provides insights that may be leveraged to improve diagnosis and identify treatment targets for patients.

Japan has the most rapidly aging society in the world,1 and comorbidities are a natural component of the aging process, playing a central role within a specific disease context. Many studies on comorbidities, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), obesity, mental disorders, immune-related diseases, and cancer, have been conducted.2,3 Comprehending multimorbidity patterns in elderly populations has crucial implications for effective interventions that may reduce the subsequent disease and economic burden of multimorbidity.4

Recent studies have emphasized the strength of disease relationships and their quantitative assessment on the basis of certain distances or measurement methods between diseases.5 Siggaard et al6 developed a disease trajectory network tool for 7.2 million Danish patients. However, given the contextual descriptions, empirical measurements, and model frameworks, it is still unclear what qualitative assessments are appropriate.7 Furthermore, most existing studies have focused on a small group of chronic diseases, with limited exploration of multimorbidity on a wider spectrum. Comorbidities, such as chronic coronary and peripheral artery diseases (>50%), arterial hypertension (∼50%), diabetes (20%-30%), COPD (20%-30%), and anemia (20%-30%), are common in elderly patients with chronic heart failure; all of these comorbidities affect heart failure severity, morbidity, hospitalization, and mortality,8,9 but their associations are still unknown.

Analytic techniques such as a dynamic network influence approach,10 which can potentially be used for the analysis of critical transition phase changes in disease patterns and other disease conditions that cause remodulation, allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of the relationships between and across various disease (symptom) clusters. Recently, large-volume health record data have been used to clarify the associations between disease patterns and Mendelian loci.11 The identification of disease correlations and patterns of comorbidities has emerged as a promising way to provide scientific supportive data to contribute to the development of effective public policy, one of which is constructed from the determinants of population health.12 Our study purpose was to clarify observed disease patterns across the spectrum of pathologies in the elderly population in Japan. Next, to clarify the sequential transition of comorbidity status, we selected heart failure patients who are known to suffer from a wide range of comorbidities.13

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This cross-sectional study of the general elderly population was designed to identify and characterize disease patterns in elderly individuals using an administrative database (Desc; https://desc-hc.co.jp/en) in Japan (Supplemental Appendix, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org).14 Administrative claims are coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10; Supplemental Table 1, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org), whose methodology uses a taxonomy of 5-digit codes. The first three digits represent the general diagnosis, and the remaining 2 digits are used as modifiers to indicate factors such as location or severity. We used only the first three digits (general diagnosis). This allowed us to prevent similar diagnoses from being represented as multiple edges within the network, which would affect the overall interpretability of the results.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We employed consistent inclusion criteria for the datasets as follows: (1) baseline records for each inpatient and outpatient visit, (2) health records with ICD-10 codes (Supplemental Table 1), and (3) records for individuals aged 60 years and above. The exclusion criteria were as follows: health records with only ICD-10 codes from Chapters 15-22 (except Chapter 19), as these correspond to congenital diseases, poisoning, pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium, among others.

Study Population

Administrative data were obtained from the Desc database for any patient aged above 60 years who had at least one claim data from April 1, 2014 to October 31, 2022. This administrative database includes individuals with employer-based plans such as the Kenko Hoken Kumiai (employee health insurance associations), the municipal-based Kokumin Kenko Hoken (National Health Insurance), and the Koki Koreisha Iryo Koiki Rengo (Late-Stage Elderly Medical Care System) (Kenko Hoken Kumiai). Employee health insurance associations are health insurance societies established by large companies or federations of smaller companies, covering employees and their dependents. National Health Insurance is administered by municipal governments; this scheme is intended for self-employed individuals, retirees, part-time workers, and others not enrolled in employment-based insurance. The Late-Stage Elderly Medical Care System covers individuals aged 75 years and older, as well as those aged 65-74 years with certified disabilities. It is operated by wide-area unions established by prefectural governments. These collectively ensure universal access to healthcare across all demographic segments, regardless of employment status or age, including primary care and specialized care. Importantly, the outpatient records in this database include visits to both primary care physicians and specialist physicians. In Japan’s health care system, patients have direct access to both types of providers, and the management of chronic conditions often involves a combination of primary care and specialty care services. Thus, the dataset reflects a comprehensive view of health care utilization across different levels of medical care.

In our database, the health insurance societies and the residence-based National Health Insurance system for individuals under 75 years of age and the Medical Care System for the advanced elderly for individuals above 75 years of age were included.15 To obtain more reliable estimates, rarely diagnosed diseases (prevalence <3%) were also excluded. The prevalence of disease was calculated as the number of disease claims divided by the total number of patients in 2020. We used the observed-to-expected ratio (OER)7 to measure the strength of the association between a pair of diagnoses within one year (see Supplemental Appendix).

A relational database was developed on a server to store the variables at the individual level, with a table for patient characteristics and another one for multiple diagnosis records. We used free R software and statnet, sna, and igraph libraries to construct the database and networks and obtain network parameters and node attributes. Network models use graphs to describe the statistical relationships between phenomena and have been used in many different scientific disciplines, from biology and physics to psychology and the social sciences.16 Networks are composed of 2 elements: a set of nodes (units) and a set of edges (connections). Node size is determined by the prevalence, whereas edge thickness varies in proportion to the OER value. Nodes were color-coded on the basis of the ICD-10 chapters. To identify the subsets of potential comorbidities that were more closely related, community detection was conducted via the fast unfolding cluster algorithm, which models the highest modularity score among the different clustering layers.17 The threshold used for the association measures was OER ≥1.2, which we applied instead of the α criterion because a large sample size would have resulted in P values below the significance threshold even for associations with a very small magnitude of effect. We also counted the number of associations, which is equivalent to comorbidity status in the condition of an OER of 1.2 and above along with a prevalence of more than 15%, stratified by age and sex.

Ethical Issues

We obtained approval from the Juntendo University Ethics Committee to conduct the study (No. E21-0264). Because of the retrospective observational and population-based design, the data used for the analysis were completely anonymized with no variables that might allow the identification of individual patients, and the need to obtain informed consent from the participants was waived.

Community-Based Network Analysis

In general, common disease communities included respiratory, digestive, circulatory, endocrine, and eye-related disorders (Figure 4). In early-stage elderly males (<75 years), strong OERs were observed for pairs such as disseminated intravascular coagulation (D65) with heart failure (I50), myocardial infarction (I21), type 2 diabetes, and respiratory failure (J96). In late-stage elderly men (≥75 years), a distinct community emerged involving fractures, cerebrovascular diseases, and mental disorders. Elderly women showed a smaller endocrine-related community. In early-stage elderly women, dorsopathies and non-pathological osteoporosis (M17, M47, M54, M75, and M81) were more prevalent, while in late-stage women (≥75 years), frequent co-occurrences were observed for fractures, cerebrovascular diseases, pneumonia, and psychiatric disorders.

The heatmap (Supplemental Figure 2, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org) illustrates the frequency of disease pairings across ICD-10 chapters. Among men with less than 75 years of age, dense comorbidity clusters were observed within J00–J99 (respiratory) and between I00–I99 (circulatory) and K00–K93 (digestive). Notable patterns included horizontal bands for J18 (pneumonia) and D50 (iron deficiency anemia), reflecting broad multimorbidity. In men with 75 years of age and above, these respiratory, circulatory, and digestive clusters intensified. Similar patterns were seen in women with less than 75 years of age, especially for respiratory and circulatory chapters, with frequent pairings involving E86 (volume depletion), J18, I21, and D50. In women aged 75 years and above, co-occurrence patterns involving J00–J99 and I00–I99 remained prominent, with additional links between F20 (schizophrenia), J01 (acute sinusitis), and multiple systemic disorders.

Discussion

This large-scale, population-based analysis revealed distinct patterns of multimorbidity in elderly Japanese patients, stratified by age and sex. The most prevalent disease clusters involved the respiratory, circulatory, and digestive systems, consistent with the known burden of chronic conditions in aging populations. Notably, comorbidities such as pneumonia, anemia, myocardial infarction, and chronic kidney disease were highly interconnected across age groups and sexes, underscoring their central role in multimorbidity networks. Among females, psychiatric and neurologic comorbidities—especially involving schizophrenia, dementia, and depressive disorders—were prominent, highlighting sex-specific vulnerabilities. In addition, otolaryngologic conditions and sensory impairments showed tight associations, particularly in women, suggesting diagnostic or physiological linkage. Network and community analyses further illuminated how disease clusters evolve with aging. Although early elderly individuals exhibited simpler disease associations, the networks in patients aged 75 years and above became more complex, with multimorbidity spanning multiple systems. For example, mental–vascular–fracture triads emerged prominently in the oldest groups, especially among females, reinforcing the need for integrated, interdisciplinary geriatric care. Heatmap visualizations confirmed that a limited number of diseases (eg, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, and anemia) commonly co-occur with a wide range of other conditions, serving as potential targets for clinical intervention or care coordination. These findings support the development of sex-sensitive and age-sensitive multimorbidity management strategies, particularly focusing on prevention of respiratory infections, falls, and cardiovascular deterioration in elderly populations.

Our study revealed a sex difference in patients aged less than 75 years, and a cluster of patients with osteoarthritis appeared in the male group. From 1990 to 2020, the global prevalence of osteoarthritis was greater in females than in males, with a 2020 global age-standardized prevalence of 8058·9 per 100,000 for females and 5780·1 per 100,000 for men.18 The causes of sex differences may be multifactorial and include anatomic differences, previous trauma, and genetic and hormonal issues.19,20 For instance, the association between excess risk for osteoarthritis with obesity is stronger in women than in men.21,22 The anatomic differences between males and females that may play a role include narrower femurs, thinner patellae, larger quadriceps angles, and differences in tibial condylar size.23

With respect to comorbidities related to cancer, we may need to consider bidirectional associations. The risk of death from comorbid conditions can increase for patients subjected to cancer treatment. Some studies have shown associations between specific comorbid conditions, such as hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, COPD, and breast cancer.24, 25, 26, 27

We identified a network cluster for sleep apnea and mental disorders, such as depression and schizophrenia. Stubbs et al28 reported similar results, in which 25.7% of people with serious mental illness had obstructive sleep apnea, and higher frequencies were found among patients with major depressive disorder than among patients with bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. Obstructive sleep apnea results in neurocognitive and mood deterioration among people with mental health problems through intermittent hypoxia and disrupted sleep.

The strengths of this study were as follows. First, our data contain outpatient care in addition to inpatient information, which means that they are almost comparable to those of the general elderly population in Japan. Second, relatively few studies regarding clusters in comorbidity maps have been conducted due to the paucity and fragmentation of data. Furthermore, the problem with multiple dimensions and factors for human diseases is how the corresponding measures scale with dimensionality, suggesting the need for statistical models to control for high dimensionality. Third, the Japanese population is composed of females, whose average lifespan is 87.1 years, whereas the average lifespan of males is 81.1 years. The treatment or diagnosis of a disease might interact negatively or positively with the treatment or natural course of a coexisting disease29; for example, treatment of exacerbations of COPD, including oral corticosteroids for diabetes mellitus and β-blocker prescriptions for chronic heart disease, might have adverse effects on patients who also have asthma or COPD.

Limitations

Human disease data have further structural features with comorbidities that can be close due to interdependencies, reducing the possibility of exploiting variation when computing distance measures.7 However, several limitations still exist in this study. First, our data were not inclusive of all Japanese patient data but were limited to selected health insurance unions, national health insurance, and elderly health insurance. Second, our data did not include genetic or proteomic information. Further analysis, including genetic factors and other biomarkers, is needed to elucidate disease progression in elderly individuals. Third, our study design was cross-sectional, and causality is very difficult to detect in these types of studies. van Weel and Schellevis30 suggested that the clinical aspects of these cross-sectional relationships can be considered clusters. In future research, longitudinal studies that use methods to evaluate changes in symptom clusters over time are needed. Furthermore, considering the longitudinal nature of the administrative database, advanced network-based cohort analyses could be conducted in future studies. Specifically, trajectory analysis using disease networks can help identify sequential patterns of comorbidity development and progression over time, which could be possible to observe changes in disease connectivity and clustering across different time points or stages of disease evolution. Recent methodological advances, such as dynamic network analysis, sequence mining, and latent class trajectory modeling, provide powerful tools to reveal the temporal architecture of multimorbidity, which could deepen our understanding of the natural history of complex disease progression in the elderly population. Fourth, one important limitation of this study is the potential for disease misclassification inherent in administrative claims data. Some conditions might have been assigned temporary codes for claim issues, leading to disease code misclassification. In particular, diagnoses within claims databases may sometimes reflect provisional assessments or be recorded primarily for insurance billing purposes rather than being strictly based on clinical validation. Although we attempted to address this concern by excluding records with suspected diagnoses, it remains possible that a degree of misclassification bias persisted. This potential bias could have influenced the accuracy of disease prevalence estimates and the observed patterns of comorbidities and thus should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

This study offers a detailed characterization of multimorbidity patterns among elderly individuals in Japan, using a nationwide administrative claims database that includes both primary care and specialist care encounters. Our findings report that multimorbidity in this population is not random but follows recognizable patterns, with cardiovascular diseases, respiratory disorders, osteoporosis, and mental health issues (such as sleep disorders) forming distinct clusters. Furthermore, identifying high-risk comorbidity clusters allows health care systems to design integrated care pathways rather than managing diseases in isolation, which is essential for an aging society.
