Factors influencing multidisciplinary clinical decision-making in the critical care unit: a systematic review and mixed-methods meta-synthesis
Authors: Kenki Matsumoto, Brigitta Fazzini, Hannah Malcolm, Jack Eldridge, Zudin Puthucheary, Magda Osman, Timothy J. Stephens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe intensive care unit (ICU) is a dynamic environment that necessitates daily clinical decisions regarding organ support treatments. The decision-making process varies significantly between clinicians (i.e. doctors, nurses, and allied healthcare practitioners), even where internationally accepted treatment guidance exists. The factors and the processes influencing clinical decision-making are poorly understood. This systematic review aims to generate a decision-making model by evaluating current evidence on the decision-making process and the factors that affect decisions on organ support treatments in the ICU.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search on three databases (PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL) including all papers exploring factors that influenced organ support decisions (PROSPERO: CRD42021283290). A mixed-methods meta-synthesis was performed to enable the generation of distinct themes and subthemes used to generate the decision-making model.ResultsAfter screening 8967 records, 33 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. The mixed-method interpretation of the data found that decision-making can be linear and primarily dictated by patient factors (i.e. patient’s clinical parameters). However, the analysis identified 11 factors that can influence and strain clinician’s decision-making. Four themes: 1) human, 2) team, 3) system, and 4) patient emerged as the potential modifiable factors to optimise the decision-making process.ConclusionsDecision-making surrounding organ support treatment is complex and dynamic. However, there are four distinctive potentially modifiable themes that influence the multidisciplinary decision-making process. Further studies should focus on understanding interventions to improve decision-making and if different decision-making processes directly affect patients’ outcomes.Systematic review protocolPROSPERO (CRD42021283290).

Multidisciplinary decision-making is a complex cognitive process, complicated by varying degrees of situational and clinical uncertainty.1 Historically, there were multiple competing theories of decision-making processes including hypothetical deductive, intuitive, and multiple processing models.2 However, the current prevailing model is a dual thinking process, whereby clinicians (i.e. doctors, nurses, and allied healthcare practitioners) have two systems of decision-making.2,3 System 1 thinking is typically described as low-effort, rapid, and based on past experiences or heuristics. Conversely, system 2 is a slower, more cognitively taxing process based on deliberation and reasoning. Despite this framework, multidisciplinary decision-making can be highly variable from clinician to clinician even in the highly critical setting of the intensive care unit (ICU). Critical care clinicians make >100 decisions every day and often these are under significant pressure and time constrained. Additionally, the multidisciplinary nature of critical care entails a wide range of decision-makers differing in seniority and profession.8 This high degree of heterogeneity brings richness of opinion but may also negatively affect patient outcomes. Complex decisions around organ support treatments are varied and their outcomes can drastically differ.8 A recent narrative review on decision-making around invasive mechanical ventilation and weaning demonstrated that nurse-led decisions are protocol driven compared with heterogeneous doctor-led decisions which are guided by both subjective and objective information, and ‘gestalt’.4 Another narrative review using decision-making theories to understand variation in critical care decision-making concluded that there was a need to increase the self-awareness of decision-making processes among critical care clinicians.2 We suggest that there may be a relatively unexplored problem of persistent, unwarranted variation in clinical decisions within and across ICUs even when standardised protocols (e.g. for ventilation and sepsis-related care) are in place. This variation is not solely benign; it can lead to inconsistencies in care, difficulties in comparing outcomes across studies and units, and potentially impaired patient outcomes.9,10 This highlights the need to understand the decision-making process and the factors which influence clinicians’ decisions at the individual and team level.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-synthesis is to address this knowledge gap by: 1) systematically summarising the literature on clinical decision-making for organ support in the critical care setting and 2) generating a conceptual model through a mixed-methods meta-synthesis.

The systematic review and meta-synthesis have been briefly described in a recently published manuscript focussing on decision-making during the COVID-19 pandemic.11 However, this paper provides a more detailed account of the analysis and findings.

Methods

We reviewed literature from the period January 2002 to December 2021 in PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, following the Preferred Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines12 and prospectively registered the review on PROSPERO (CRD42021283290). We followed the SPIDER framework13 to frame our clinical question (Supplementary Table S1). We used deliberately broad search terms to capture all relevant studies. A full list of search terms can be found in the Supplementary file and the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study are outlined in Supplementary Table S2. The initial search was followed by a forwards and backwards citation search.14

Study team member, experience, and positionality

TS is a critical care nurse and clinical lecturer, with specific skills in qualitative research. KM, JE, and HM are junior doctors, who were undertaking a critical care research rotation at the time of this study. BF is an advanced critical care practitioner and mixed-methods researcher. ZP is a professor of critical care medicine. MO is a behavioural psychologist and professor of decision science. TS and MO provided qualitative methodology training and support to the study team.

Mixed-method approach

An initial screening of the literature showed that studies used a mixture of methodologies such as quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research. We followed the JBI framework for mixed-methods systematic reviews using the convergent integrated synthesis approach.15 The extracted data from the quantitative studies and the quantitative component of the mixed-method studies were transformed into qualitative data and subsequently a meta-synthesis conducted.16 This method technically called ‘qualitisation’ was chosen to allow aggregation and integration of data and the development of new concepts.17 This decision was based on our review questions, which were designed to be addressed by both quantitative and qualitative research designs, aligning with JBI’s recommendation to use the convergent integrated approach in such cases.15

Specifically, we followed the steps mentioned below.1)Extracted quantitative and qualitative data separately, maintaining fidelity to the original data as per JBI recommendations.2)Transformed quantitative findings into qualitative categories (qualitisation) after data extraction, grouping them with qualitative findings to identify overarching themes and patterns.3)Synthesised all data into integrated thematic domains, ensuring that both data types contributed equally to the final meta-synthesis.

Quality assessment

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used for quality assessment.18 This tool was chosen as it is specifically designed for the appraisal of studies with diverse methodological designs, including qualitative, quantitative (randomised, non-randomised, and descriptive), and mixed-methods studies. Given that our review included a variety of study types, the MMAT provided a unified and consistent framework for appraising methodological quality across all included studies. This tool includes two screening questions followed by one of three distinct 5-point checklists for qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies. The quantitative and qualitative elements of mixed-method studies were further assessed using the quantitative and qualitative checklists, respectively. As proposed, a cut-off score for quality was not set and the MMAT was used as a general guide of study quality. Two reviewers independently scored for inclusion/exclusion (KM/HM) with a third reviewer arbitrating in case disagreements occurred (TS). Studies were excluded if they failed the first two screening questions of the MMAT, if there were sufficient concerns around the collection, synthesis or interpretation of the data, or both. The responses to the screening questions of the MMAT for each study are outlined in Supplementary Tables S3–S5. We acknowledge that variability in the quality of included studies may remain, despite removing lower-quality papers which would not significantly contribute to the overall synthesis.19

Data extraction, qualitisation, and thematic synthesis

Data extraction involved inputting all relevant findings from a paper into a common table on an Excel Sheet. This was conducted by KM, HM, and TS, and at least two authors were involved in the data extraction process for each paper. For quantitative papers and the quantitative elements from the mixed-methods studies, the findings were initially presented in their numerical format then transformed into textual descriptions, following the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology of qualitising quantitative data described above.15 This was deemed preferable to quantifying qualitative data as it is less error prone. The distinction between first-order constructs (participants’ descriptions often in the form of quotes) and second-order constructs (original authors’ interpretations of the participants’ descriptions) often overlapped and therefore were synthesised together.20

We acknowledged that quality variability has potential to affect thematic analysis. Therefore, we analysed first the five higher-quality papers based on the number of participants, methodology used, and the MMAT score,18 using an inductive approach. Thematic analysis involved grouping relevant findings from the same papers, different papers, or both into codes (and categories) with the goal of grouping these further into themes. The remaining 27 papers were subsequently analysed in a more deductive manner, sensitised by these initial categories and themes, whilst remaining open to any new emerging themes. This process was conducted in-person by KM, HM, and TS, with initial training provided by the latter to the coding team. Three data meetings were held with the whole study team to present, discuss, sense check, and finally agree upon the emergent findings.

To examine how these different factors interact and develop a higher-order interpretation (i.e. a ‘line of argument’), the themes and subthemes identified were continuously compared against each other alongside the original data.17,21 Given the complexity and heterogeneity of the included studies and the exploratory nature of our research questions, we determined that this interpretive meta-synthesis approach was better suited to capture nuanced insights and generate a comprehensive conceptual framework.

Human factors

Clinician experience

Clinicians reported that the dynamic ICU environment required them to process a high volume of moving data for clinical decision-making. However, there are significant differences in the assimilation and processing of this information across experience levels.

For instance, all clinicians use ‘cues’; however, those more experienced appear to identify cues that are higher in quantity and variety.40,45 Less experienced clinicians report that they awaited the occurrence of more cues before they have the confidence to enact their plans.28 This can result in experienced clinicians making proactive decisions compared with the reactive decisions made by less experienced clinicians.40 It was noted that clinicians often process information using heuristics or conscious pattern recognition.22,24,29,30,41,43,46,49,50 Experienced clinicians were reported to favour this type of information processing.22,24,29,30,49 Conversely, less experienced clinicians used this only in standardised tasks, such as nurse-led protocolised extubation.46 Heuristics and pattern recognition appear to introduce cognitive biases and partly explain why experience does not necessarily equate to better decision-making.49,50

Professional and personal risk

Critical care clinicians frequently make complex and clinically important decisions that carry a high level of responsibility and professional risks.53 Some clinicians seek to reduce the anxiety associated with this by altering their decision-making. One method reported involves spreading the risk by discussing decisions with clinicians who are higher up the decision-making hierarchy, whether this is junior members of staff discussing with seniors33,46 or nurses and other allied health professionals discussing with doctors.33,46,49

A different practice described is reducing the number of decisions by refusing accountability for any decisions outside of the clinician’s perceived remit.33,41 For example, decision-making around weaning is often driven by doctors, and there were reports of critical care nurses being reluctant to take accountability in weaning even when the decision-making fell under their domain.33

Another type of behaviour reported is defensive medicine. This may include clinicians ordering unnecessary investigations with the hope to detect issues before they arose51 or avoid future possible problems or litigation.24

Negative team cultures, in particular a blaming culture22,25,27or one with a rigid hierarchy,27,36 were highlighted in studies as exacerbating factors. Additionally, lack of support may also increase the level of risk that clinicians perceive.25,27,33,46 This could be from seniors,25,27 other professions (e.g. nurses and doctors)25 or the professional body.33 Conversely, good leadership and a problem-solving team dynamic can help alleviate anxiety and optimise decision-making.46

Uncertainty

Studies demonstrated that uncertainty can slow down clinicians’ thought process, but this can also lead to delay, ‘freeze’ decision-making, or both.23,24,30,51 Prognostication of critically ill patients, for example, can be especially challenging.23 Degrees of uncertainty mean that clinicians will often find it difficult to prognosticate the outcome of a patient and in turn whether to escalate or de-escalate treatment. Uncertainty may occur because of the complexity of the problems/pathology/illnesses of the patient25,51 or the complexity of the decision,23 for example sedation management in patients with substance withdrawal syndromes or head injuries. Uncertainty may also occur as a result of the clinician not previously facing a similar problem, especially if inexperienced.30 Because of this uncertainty, a clinician may delay making a decision until they gain more information.23

Characteristics of the decision-maker

Studies revealed a wide variation in decision-making amongst clinicians. Part of this appears to be attributable to underlying characteristics such as age, gender, and religious beliefs which are beyond clinical experience alone. It was noted that the personality of the clinician, in particular their confidence, assertiveness, and risk appetite, affected their willingness to make decisions.27,30,38 Clinicians who were younger, had religious beliefs, or both were also found to be more likely to commence organ support.34 Further, one study investigating the number of decisions made by intensivists during the ward round, defined in the study as a verbalised action plan, reported that female intensivists made more decisions than their male counterparts.8 It was also described in studies that a clinician could gain decision-making autonomy through formal education.28,38 The role of the clinician appeared to be important but not their area of specialty.8,52 Finally, it was noted that clinicians interpret the same information differently and their view on a treatment can shape their decision-making around its utilisation.22,24,26,39

System factors

Protocols and guidelines

There was conflicting evidence on protocol and guideline utility regarding clinical decision-making between studies. Authors advocating for protocol, guideline usage, or both in their studies were from contexts that did not have pre-existing local guidelines. These authors argued that implementing local guidelines could reduce variability30,52 and uncertainty in decision-making, and give a legal basis for decisions.33 Conversely, authors from centres with existing protocols argued that they were not helpful as they were often rigid, and were neither specific nor tailored to each individual patient and situation, especially when these were increasingly complex and evidence is less clear (e.g. prolonged weaning).24,25,26,28,31,33,36,49 Instead, previous experiences36, 37, 38,40,44,49 or other clinicians’ experiences were preferred.35,37 Some authors had a more nuanced view suggesting that protocols and guidelines are useful in certain situations. The weaning process, for example, could be protocolised as objective markers dominate in the decision-making process compared with the decision to start weaning (i.e. switching from assisted to spontaneous ventilation), which is harder to quantify.49

Protocols were also noted to be best used in conjunction with experience and therefore some authors advised caution on inexperienced clinicians using these.38,44,48 On the one hand, it may help these clinicians work with confidence and within safe markers but on the other hand, it can result in decision-making without understanding the fundamentals leading to inflexibility in one’s decision-making.36,38,46,48 Ultimately, it was noted that implementation of protocols requires the support of the whole medical workforce, especially as clinicians prefer to obtain knowledge from colleagues over guidelines.42,45

Time and workload

Because of the busy nature of ICUs, studies showed that some decisions are made for pragmatic reasons rather than clinical ones. Time constraints and increased workload, for example, delays weaning or extubation decisions and extends sedation duration.23,30,33,46 Staff levels have similar effects; weaning was often only commenced when there were enough staff to complete observations and a lower nurse-to-patient ratio increases risk of unplanned extubation.39,47 Conversely, higher nurse-to-patient ratios were associated with increased perceived autonomy and influence.26 The team composition was also of importance as a lack of experienced staff meant that these individuals spent more time supervising inexperienced staff members, thereby spending less time on their own decisions.51 Finally, having more patients appeared to increase the number of action plans that were verbalised per patient during the ward rounds.8

Hospital structure

The design, layout, and internal organisational structure of the ICU were highlighted as logistical strains on decision-making. For instance, it was noted that implementation of conservative measures for sleep promotion (e.g. through reducing noise and artificial lighting) was difficult if the ICU was too small and lacked daylight.22,25 Bed shortages were noted to have an anticipatory effect in decisions potentially leading to early weaning decisions for stable ventilated patients.36 In units where clinicians were more fiscally aware, budgetary constraints and financial incentives also influenced decision-making.23,24,51 Ventilation, for example, was continued longer in units where this increased funding.23 Finally, corporate governance can have an impact on decision-making, with one study describing the unintended influence of increased physical restraint and sedation use in units where there was pressure to reduce unplanned extubation.22

Team factors

Support

Studies showed that the level of support received by clinicians can impact their decision-making, especially for those who are inexperienced. Studies found that without senior support, inexperienced clinicians find initiating management (e.g. reducing ventilator support) more difficult and make poorer decisions concerning complex patients.25,30,45 Conversely, senior support seemed to help clinicians detect issues earlier and respond more appropriately.33,45 Formal education was also described to empower clinical decision-making (e.g. weaning) through providing not only the theoretical and practical skills,25,33,48 but also the credibility for the clinician to action their management plans.38

Team dynamics

Studies revealed that multidisciplinary decisions are highly interlinked and one decision can directly impact the next one.51 For instance, if patients were not extubated in a timely manner, this could increase the risk of unplanned extubation and lead to physical restraint use.22 Ambiguous communication was also described to impact decision-making. Studies demonstrated that statements such as ‘wean off’ or ‘wean as able’ from more senior decision-makers can lead to different interpretations and more variable decision-making downstream.25,33

Team culture and hierarchy

Intraprofessional and interprofessional hierarchy were also identified as important influences on decision-making. Those perceived to be at the bottom of the hierarchy generally had less autonomy to make decisions.27,36,46 This hierarchy was often determined by the clinician’s label (profession or grade) rather than the individual’s decision-making acumen.33,39

Decision-making across the multidisciplinary team was more fluid in teams that harbour a collaborative atmosphere.30,33,49 Doctors, for example, may trust nurses to identify the patients who require weaning and instigate this. This arrangement, however, appeared dependent on the doctor’s trust on the nurse’s ability. Therefore, this delegation of decision-making authority was often reserved for experienced clinicians.48 Further, the ultimate decision-maker is still seen as those at the top of the hierarchy.33,48 For instance, senior nurses reported that they did not feel comfortable in stopping a weaning decision from a senior doctor.33 This implicit hierarchy on decision-making appeared to exist even in more collaborative teams.

Beyond the multidisciplinary relationships, the unit-based practice and cultural norms can also impact clinical decision-making.39,46 Around 50% of nurses reported that other nurses’ knowledge and attitudes influenced their own sedation management.39 Additionally, clinicians (even experienced ones) may adopt unit-based practice despite disagreeing with it.46

Patient factors

The patient’s clinical condition and the associated factors emerged as the predominant confounding factor affecting decision-making.8,22,23,25,27,29,30,31,32,34,38,47,49 The patient’s status alone could justify a clinician’s decision.36,41,48,51 For example, a clinician may decide to work outside their perceived remit if there was clinical need.41 Experienced clinicians may also decide to take more control in complex decisions such as weaning.36,48

As well as the clinical condition, the underlying patient co-morbidities appeared to heavily impact decision-making.25,38,49 Patients with mental health diagnoses are often kept sedated for longer25 or patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are anticipated to need more complex weaning.38 Beyond the clinical characteristics of a patient, there are also explicit and implicit pressures from family.8,23,24 Some families, for example, may not respond well to mismatched expectations, such as the use of physical restraints to prevent unplanned extubation.22

Discussion

This mixed-method systematic review found that there are four key confounding factors affecting clinicians’ decision-making: 1) human, 2) system, 3) team, and 4) patient factors. Patient factors which included the patient’s clinical condition was the predominant theme across all papers. This resonates with a classic, linear process of clinical decision-making (Fig. 3). This involves the clinician interpreting the patient’s presenting condition and then deciding upon their management plan. This decision-making process may involve heuristics, but also potentially more deliberate decisions. If the weaning decision was as simple as decreasing ventilator support according to the patient’s clinical parameters, the external influences on a clinical scenario may be limited. However, we found key influences on the clinical scenario which can place strain on the decision-making process. This may be because the content of the decision is more complex (e.g. initiating weaning from mechanical ventilation in a frail patient or difficult-to-sedate patients such as those with haemodynamic instability), multilayered (e.g. requiring consideration of patient’s family or co-morbidity), the decision-maker is less experienced or there are other system influences (e.g. unit workload or hospital bed pressures). In these situations, there are external influences that may affect the ‘linear’ decision-making process either as challenges or facilitators as outlined in Figure 4. These are broadly split into human, system, and team factors.

Human factor: the decision-making black box

In regard to human factors, our study has outlined the importance of experience in obtaining relevant, high-quality clinical parameters and in information processing. However, there was a lack of data around how experienced decision-makers navigated difficult decision-making; in this review there were fewer studies of decision-making in doctors (vs nurses) and the overwhelming majority of the existing literature in doctors was quantitative research rather than qualitative. This has meant that the decision-making process in critical care doctors is still somewhat of a black box. Further studies utilising naturalistic observation are required to characterise the decision-making process and improve decision-making in difficult scenarios.

Our study also found that clinician’s characteristics have an influence on decision-making. However, most studies focussed on associating the characteristic with the decision made rather than the outcome of the decision.8 An experienced or confident decision-maker can suffer from cognitive biases leading to poor decision-making, and so it is important for future studies to pair decisions with outcomes to understand which characteristics truly lead to improved decision-making.

System factor: setting up the ICU for success

A recurring theme in this review was that the set-up of the hospital and ICU—in terms of its rota, workload, and layout—can make easy decisions more difficult but conversely, difficult decisions more manageable.46,51

There were disparate opinions in the literature on protocol implementation in aiding decision-making. Real-life data, however, highlight its utility. Data of ventilatory practices during COVID-19, for example, suggested that deviation from evidence-based acute respiratory distress syndrome management strategies led to worse outcomes.6 These discordant findings may be the result of the varying decisions made in the ICU, some amenable to being protocolised and others less so. Decisions with objective parameters, such as the act of weaning, could be led by protocols but the decision to commence a wean may need a more complex clinical decision support system (CDSS).4 The heterogeneity in the literature may also simply reflect the differing opinions of clinicians and a recent review highlighted the facilitators and barriers to the implementation of CDSS at the clinician, intervention, and organisation level.4 Any future study looking to implement CDSS or protocols should not only consider the overall outcomes of its implementation, but also survey clinician’s opinions before its implementations to determine whether there was a mismatch between clinician’s expectations to its outcomes.

Team factor: creating a culture of support

Decisions within the ICU rely on interprofessional teams with varying expertise.54 We found that collaboration and support in the unit increase each individual clinician’s autonomy in decision-making. This appears self-explanatory but this ethos is not implemented across all units. One barrier may be that delegation of decision-making requires a level of trust in an individual’s knowledge or skills with little or no shared history.54 This leads to a rigid hierarchy (both intraprofessional and interprofessional) and the sharing of decision-making powers only occurs in those most experienced.48 In situ or multidisciplinary simulations may be a solution for understanding each member’s capabilities, and limitations, and help improve team-based decision-making whilst protecting each clinician’s decision-making autonomy.55

There is interconnectivity between human, system, and team factors. Clinician experience affected its use of protocols/guidelines (human-system), the workload of an experienced clinician affected how well they could support a more junior team member (system-team), and a more supportive team allows clinicians to adjust the level of risk they are willing to take (team-human). In COVID-19, the most commonly co-expressed influences to decision-making were uncertain pathophysiology, limited resources, and physical barriers, whilst strong teamwork was seen as a commonly identified mitigating factor.11 Further naturalistic observational studies are required to understand how these factors interact, especially in contexts where decision-making may be strained.

Practical recommendation for advancing multidisciplinary clinical decision-making

Our synthesis identifies key factors such as communication quality, team structure, leadership dynamics, cognitive biases, and organisational culture that influence decision-making in critical care. These findings can inform several practical initiatives at both the unit and health system levels.

Training and education initiatives

ICU physicians and multidisciplinary team members could benefit from targeted training in evidence-based decision-making tools that directly address the influences on decision-making identified in our study (e.g. structured communication, to optimise team-working or cognitive bias mitigation, to prevent error at times of high workload/decision strain). This training could be achieved also through case-based scenario simulation so clinicians could have a practical experience of their decisions in a safe environment. Educational curricula for ICU staff, including physicians, nurses, and allied health professionals, should be designed or updated to incorporate these domains. Simulation-based training, case studies, and interprofessional workshops can be used to reinforce best practices in collaborative decision-making.

Quality and safety processes

Morbidity and mortality conferences and other quality assurance processes can be standardised to systematically review cases through the lens of the identified modifiable factors. For example, structured debriefs could explicitly address communication breakdowns, leadership challenges, or cognitive errors that contributed to adverse events. Regular audits of decision-making processes, with feedback mapped to the domains highlighted in our analysis, can help units identify and address specific areas for improvement.

Organisational and system-level interventions

Hospital and ICU policies can be crafted to support multidisciplinary collaboration, such as implementing standardised handoff protocols, fostering a culture of psychological safety, and ensuring equitable participation in decision-making. Health systems can use these findings to justify investments in team training, decision-support tools, and staffing models that optimise multidisciplinary input.

In summary, the data from our meta-analysis provide a robust foundation for designing targeted interventions aimed at improving multidisciplinary decision-making in the ICU. By integrating these domains into training, quality improvement, and organisational policies, units and health systems can enhance both the process and outcomes of critical care decision-making.

Strengths and limitations

This mixed-method review is the first work aiming to model the complex multidisciplinary decision-making process in the ICU. We evaluated quantitative and qualitative research with a systematic approach and integrated data using established methods for data aggregating. Use of the MMAT ensured that data synthesis included only the highest quality papers. Our findings, however, are limited to the available data and are prone to intrinsic bias for qualitative and observational studies. We also accept that the exclusion of non-English studies may be a source of potential bias in our results and may limit the global applicability of these findings. Finally, there were fewer studies available in the literature evaluating decision-making in doctors. None of the identified subthemes were profession-specific (Supplementary Fig. S1) and there were sufficient findings from the doctor-specific/interprofessional studies to analyse the studies together. However, we acknowledge that there will be some differences in the influences on decision-making between doctors and nurses, especially with the support structure or the types of decisions made. This again highlights the importance of further research on decision-making in doctors.

Conclusions

Complex clinical decision-making is affected by four key factors: the human, the system, and the team, and intrinsic patient factors. This study has modelled a complex clinical decision-making generating the hypothesis of what the modifiable influences are. This allows us to understand what the interventions may be and how to implement those to leverage better decision-making. Further studies should focus on understanding how adjusting these modifiable influences can optimise the decision-making process and directly improve patients’ outcomes.
