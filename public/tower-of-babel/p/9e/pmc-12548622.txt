Associations of NLR and SIRI with all-cause mortality among adults with nasal Staphylococcus aureus colonisation: a cross-sectional study of NHANES, 2001–2004
Authors: Ju Zou, Yuanyuan Xiao, Biyue Tian, Siyao Chen, Anhua Wu, Chunhui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to explore the associations of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) with all-cause mortality among adults with nasal colonisation of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus).DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThe National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2001–2004).ParticipantsA total of 10 600 adults aged 18 years or older were included in this study, of whom 2805 were colonised with nasal S. aureus.Primary and secondary outcome measuresAll-cause mortality was considered as primary outcomes. Survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards models were applied to evaluate the associations of NLR and SIRI with all-cause mortality after adjustment for confounders. Predictive performance was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and survival differences were illustrated by Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models further examined potential non-linear associations.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 197 months, 595 deaths occurred. The fully adjusted Cox model showed that elevated NLR (1.87 (1.32, 2.65), p<0.001) and SIRI (1.88 (1.43, 2.48), p<0.001) were significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality risk. These associations were consistent across subgroup analyses by sex, age, education, race, family poverty income ratio, smoking, drinking, diabetes, cancer and long-term care. K-M curves showed significantly lower survival rates in high NLR and SIRI groups (p<0.0001). RCS analysis revealed non-linear relationships between NLR and SIRI, with all-cause mortality (p>0.05).ConclusionsElevated NLR and SIRI levels are positively associated with higher all-cause mortality risk among adult individuals with nasal S. aureus colonisation.Keywords: BACTERIOLOGY, Clinical Decision-Making, INFECTIOUS DISEASES, Public health, Infection control, Mortality

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a prevalent opportunistic pathogenic bacterium that colonises multiple skin and mucosal surfaces, where the primary reservoir is the vestibulum nasi. As much as 20% of the human population persistently carries S. aureus in the nasal cavity, while approximately 60% do so intermittently; this prevalence is notably higher among individuals with underlying health conditions.1 Elegant researches have consistently reported around 80% of infectious strains genetically correspond to colonisation isolates in the same patient.2 Consequently, nasal colonisation of S. aureus is acknowledged as a major risk factor that increases the probability of developing patent staphylococcal infections by two- to tenfold.2 This poses considerable medical challenges, contributing to increased mortality rates and healthcare costs for these patients.

S. aureus impedes the human immune response by inhibiting complement activation, blocking and damaging phagocytic cells and altering host B and T cell responses, thereby leading to nasal colonisation.3 However, the host immune environment undergoes changes during the process of colonised S. aureus strains becoming invasive and causing host damage. Overactivation of immune cells and subsequent inflammation have been considered the primary culprits of secondary infection and its deadly complications.4 While the initial colonisation of S. aureus is harmless, it is a risk factor for resulting in severe invasive and non-invasive infections.5 Hence, an innovative, affordable, rapid and convenient inflammatory marker is urgently required to predict mortality risk in people with nasal S. aureus colonisation.

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)6 and systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), which comprises the absolute number of neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes,7 serve as crucial haematological parameters reflecting innate and adaptive immunity in inflammatory diseases. Thus, these two indexes garnered attention as potential biomarkers for the progression and prognosis of numerous disorders, including cancer,8 inflammation9 and metabolic syndrome.10 11 The crosstalk between nasal colonisation of S. aureus and leukocytes modulates pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory attributes of diverse immune cells.12 The link between NLR or SIRI levels and mortality risk in nasal S. aureus carriers is unclear, necessitating more well-designed cohort studies.

In order to address existing research gaps, this study represents the first prospective evaluation of the associations between NLR and SIRI levels with all-cause mortality among U.S. adults who carry S. aureus nasally, using a large, nationally representative sample.

Methods

Study design and populations

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a biennial cross-sectional survey designed to evaluate the nutritional and health status of non-institutionalised civilians in America. It employs a nationally representative, complex multi-stage sampling methodology. Details of the NHANES database are available on the website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). The study protocol received approval from the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Centre for Health Statistics, obviating the need for further ethical clearances. Till now, NHANES data have been used in many studies, proving its validity and quality.13 14 Data used in this study were obtained from two consecutive 2 year survey cycles (2001–2004), and for nasal swab screening, data for S. aureus colonisation were measured in these year cycles only.15

In our study, inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) participants enrolled in the NHANES 2001–2004 cycles; (ii) age ≥18 years; (iii) availability of nasal swab culture data for S. aureus colonisation and (iv) complete follow-up information. Exclusion criteria were: (i) age <18 years; (ii) absence of nasal S. aureus colonisation data; (iii) missing follow-up information and (iv) pregnant women. Participants with malignancies, inflammatory diseases, autoimmune diseases or acute infections were not excluded, in order to preserve the representativeness and generalisability of the study population. Thus, our study initially comprised 19 108 respondents. After excluding individuals with missing or incomplete data concerning nares screening (n = 482) and follow-up information (n = 8026), the final analysis comprised 10 600 adult participants aged between 18 and 85 years. Out of the total, 7795 were not colonised with S. aureus in their noses, while 2805 were. Among 2805 individuals with nasal colonisation of S. aureus, 2210 survived and 595 died (figure 1).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not directly involved in the design, recruitment or conduct of this study, as it was a secondary data analysis of de-identified data from the NHANES database. Nevertheless, we recognise the value of patient and public involvement in research and intend to disseminate our findings through cohort website, public sessions and a press release to ensure accessibility to the broader community.

Assessment of NLR and SIRI

NLR and SIRI were computed using the following formulas: NLR is defined as the ratio of neutrophil count to lymphocyte count, while SIRI is calculated as the product of neutrophil count and monocyte count divided by lymphocyte count. Neutrophil, lymphocyte and monocyte counts (expressed in units of×109 /L) were measured using automated haematology analysis equipment.

Assessment of nasal colonisation of S. aureus

Nasal swabs for the detection of S. aureus colonisation were obtained from eligible participants during the 2001–2004 survey cycles. Mannitol salt agar was used to evaluate the presence of S. aureus in nasal swab specimens. Subsequently, S. aureus isolates were screened for methicillin resistance ulising the disk diffusion method as outlined by the National Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. More details on the S. aureus screening test are available on the NHANES website.

Assessment of all-cause mortality

The all-cause mortality information was sourced from the National Death Index (NDI) records (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm), which was linked with the NHANES database. Each participant was monitored from enrolment until death or 31 December 2019 (the last update date of the NDI database). As of the most recent follow-up date, 595 deaths were reported among 2805 Americans colonised with S. aureus in their nasal cavities (figure 1).

Assessment of covariates

Several covariates were investigated as potential characteristics indicative of all-cause mortality associated with nasal carriage of S. aureus. Sex,16 age,16 education, family income, race, smoking status,17 18 drinking status, body mass index (BMI),19 diabetes,20 cancer21 and long-term care22 were adopted from demographic, physical examination and health questionnaires of the NHANES survey and were modelled categorically, respectively. Laboratory analyses included albumin (g/dL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), alanine transaminase (ALT, U/L), total cholesterol (TC, mg/dL), triglyceride (TG, mg/dL), haemoglobin (Hb, g/L), platelet (PLT, 109 cells/L), serum potassium ion (K+, mmol/L) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mg/dL); all of them were analysed and modelled continuously.

Age was coded dichotomously (less than 65 and greater than or equal to 65). Education was categorised as follows: less than high school, high school grad/ general educational development (GED) or equivalent, college or associate of arts (AA) degree, college graduate or above. As a marker of socioeconomic status, the family poverty income ratio (PIR) is the ratio of family income to poverty threshold and was categorised into <1, 1–3 and ≥3. The race was categorised as follows: non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American and other races. Those who self-reported as “other Hispanic” were classified as “other races” in our analysis. The smoking status of respondents was evaluated by inquiring whether they had consumed a minimum of 100 cigarettes over the course of their lifetime. Alcohol consumption was assessed using a questionnaire and categorised into four groups: non-drinker, 1–5 drinks/month, 5–10 drinks/month or 10+drinks/month. BMI was categorised into two distinct groups: those with a BMI of 25 kg/m² or less and those with a BMI greater than 25 kg/m².

Participants with diabetes were identified based on the presence of any of the following criteria: (a) haemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) concentration of 6.5% or higher; or a fasting plasma glucose level of 1.26 g/L or higher23; (b) an affirmative response to any of the following questions: “Has a doctor told you that you have diabetes?”, “Are you now taking insulin?” or “Are you now taking diabetic pills to lower your blood sugar?”. Participants with a history of cancer were identified based on an affirmative response to any of the following questions: (a) “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had cancer or a malignancy of any kind?”; (b) among male participants who reported an enlarged prostate, “Was the enlargement due to cancer?”. Long-term care was defined as any self-reported stay as a patient in a long-term care or rehabilitation facility during the past 12 months; residential settings without healthcare services were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Given the intricate probabilistic clustering framework employed by NHANES, all estimates were calculated using sample weights, clustering and stratification. We examined missingness for all laboratory variables and displayed variable-level rates with a Pareto plot (online supplemental figure S1A) and used ‘mice’ with predictive mean matching to create five imputed datasets and combined results using Rubin’s rules.24 Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were shown as mean and SD if normally distributed, or median and IQR if not. Categorical comparisons used the χ2 or Fisher exact test, and continuous comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U or Student’s t-test.

The optimal thresholds for NLR and SIRI were established by maximally selected rank statistics using the ‘maxstat’ R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=maxstat) online supplemental Figure S1B and S1C). The cut-off values were determined to be 3.31 for NLR and 1.22 for SIRI. Survey-weighted Cox regression analysis was used to compute HRs and 95% CI for the associations of NLR and SIRI levels with risks of all-cause mortality among patients with nasal colonisation of S. aureus. The study encompassed three models: Model 1 contained no variable adjustments; Model 2 accounted for sex, age, education, family PIR and race and Model 3 further adjusted for smoking, drinking, BMI, diabetes, cancer, long-term care, albumin, AST, ALT, TC, TG, Hb, PLT, K+ and BUN based on Model 2.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings to different missing data handling strategies, we conducted sensitivity analyses based on two additional approaches. First, we identified a subgroup of patients with complete data across all relevant variables in the original dataset (ie, no missing values) and re-performed variable selection and model construction on this complete-case cohort. This allowed us to assess whether the model results were substantially influenced by imputed data. Second, we also applied K-nearest neighbour (KNN) imputation as an alternative non-parametric method to handle missing data. For both the complete-case and KNN-imputed cohorts, survey-weighted Cox regression analysis was performed to compute HRs and 95%CIs for the associations of NLR and SIRI levels with the risk of all-cause mortality among patients with nasal colonisation of S. aureus. These estimates were then compared with those obtained from the primary MICE-based analysis to evaluate the consistency and stability of the results.

Given the observational design and the potential for reverse causality, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants who died within the first year and, separately, within the first 2 years of follow-up. This approach aimed to minimise bias from pre-existing severe illness, in which alterations in baseline haematological indices might reflect consequences of impending mortality rather than causal predictors. After removing these early events, survey-weighted Cox regression models were refitted to reassess the associations between baseline haematological markers and all-cause mortality among individuals with nasal colonisation of S. aureus.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used for predictive value analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CIs at 3 year, 5 year and 10 year were computed to quantify the predictive power of NLR and SIRI for all-cause mortality. Survival outcomes were depicted using Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves, with between-group comparisons via log-rank tests. We further performed stratification and interaction analyses by sex, age, education, race, family PIR, smoking, drinking, diabetes, cancer and long-term care.

Finally, we fitted a survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards model using restricted cubic splines (RCS; (four knots)) to assess potential non-linear associations between NLR or SIRI and all-cause mortality in patients with nasal colonisation of S. aureus. Statistical evaluations in this investigation were executed using R software (http://www.r-project.org). All statistical tests were two-sided, with a P-value of less than 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

Among the 2805 participants with nasal S. aureus colonisation, 595 died during follow-up, corresponding to an all-cause mortality rate of 21.2%, while 2210 (78.8%) survived. The baseline clinical characteristics of nasal S. aureus carriers who survived and those who died are compared in online supplemental table S1.

Individuals with nasal S. aureus colonisation who died tended to be over 65 years old (65% vs 8.5%, p<0.001), non-Hispanic white (67.9% vs 51.1%, p<0.001), with education less than high school (39.3% vs 33.6%, p<0.001), with lower income (family PIR less than 3, 70.1% vs 58.8%, p<0.001), smoking (55.6% vs 45.5%, p<0.001) and no drinking (41.8% vs 36.6%). Comorbidities were more prevalent in the death group, including diabetes (29.9% vs 8.69%; p<0.001), cancer (20.7% vs 4.12%; p<0.001) and having stayed in a long-term care in the past year (3.87% vs 0.23%; p<0.001).

Compared with those who survived, individuals who died seem to be with a significantly higher level of HbA1c (5.60 (5.30 to 6.10) vs 5.30 (5.10 to 5.50), p<0.001), BUN (16.0 (12.0 to 21.0) vs 12.0 (9.00 to 15.0), p<0.001), TC (201 (174 to 230) vs 194 (167 to 224), p<0.001), TG (131 (89.0 to 204) vs 111 (73.0 to 166), p<0.001), serum potassium (4.10 (3.80 to 4.40) vs 4.00 (3.80 to 4.20), p<0.001), monocyte count (0.60 (0.50 to 0.70) vs 0.50 (0.40 to 0.60), p<0.001), neutrophil count (4.20 (3.40 to 5.30) vs 4.00 (3.10 to 5.20), p<0.005), NLR (2.26 (1.65 to 3.18) vs 2.00 (1.48 to 2.64), p<0.001) and SIRI (1.31 (0.89 to 1.92) vs 1.03 (0.70 to 1.49), p<0.001), and lower levels of albumin (4.10 (3.90 to 4.30) vs 4.30 (4.10 to 4.50), p<0.001), ALT (19.0 (15.0 to 24.0) vs 22.0 (16.0 to 30.0), p<0.001), lymphocyte counts (1.80 (1.40 to 2.40) vs 2.00 (1.60 to 2.50), p<0.001), Hb (14.1 (12.9 to 15.1) vs 14.6 (13.4 to 15.6), p<0.001) and PLT (247 (208 to 293) vs 266 (225 to 312), p<0.001).

Among the follow-up event, individuals with nasal S. aureus colonisation in the high NLR and SIRI groups were remarkably linked to a higher proportion of death (NLR: 36.1% vs 18.8%; p<0.001; SIRI: 29.2% vs 15.6%; p<0.001) and shorter mean survival time (NLR: 192 (119 to 210) vs 200 (185 to 214), p<0.001; SIRI: 197 (164 to 212) vs 201 (187 to 215), p<0.001) compared with low NLR and SIRI groups (figure 2).

Relationships between NLR and SIRI with all-cause mortality

Survey-weighted Cox regression analysis was employed to explore the association of NLR and SIRI (as continuous and categorical variables, respectively) with risk of mortality among participants with nasal S. aureus colonisation, adjusting for various covariates (online supplemental table S2). In all analysed models, a positive correlation was observed between elevated NLR and SIRI levels with high all-cause mortality of nasal S. aureus colonisation.

In the crude model (Model 1), individuals with higher NLR (HR=3.04, p<0.001) and SIRI (HR=2.24, p<0.001) exhibited a heightened risk of all-cause death (both P for trend<0.001). After adjusting for sex, age, education, family PIR and race (Model 2), elevated NLR (HR=2.13, p<0.001) and SIRI (HR=2.14, p<0.001) remained an independent factor for predicting the risk of all-cause death among nasal S. aureus carriage (both P for trend<0.001). Model 3 further adjusted for additional covariates of smoking, drinking, BMI, diabetes, cancer, longer-term care, albumin, AST, ALT, TC, TG, Hb, PLT, K+ and BUN. This significantly positive association still remained stable in Model 3 (NLR: HR=1.87; SIRI: HR=1.88, both P for trend<0.001). Moreover, a significant positive association was observed between each 1-unit increase in NLR or SIRI and the risk of mortality among participants with nasal S. aureus colonisation across all models (all HR>1, all p<0.001).

To assess the robustness of our findings, we took a sensitivity analysis. First, we evaluated the impact of different missing data handling strategies by repeating the analyses in a complete-case cohort and in a KNN-imputed cohort. The results of these analyses yielded HRs and P values that were highly consistent with those from the primary MICE-based analysis (online supplemental table S3 and S4). Second, to minimise potential reverse causality, we excluded participants who died within the first year (online supplemental table S5) and within the first 2 years of follow-up (online supplemental table S6). In both scenarios, the associations of NLR and SIRI with all-cause mortality remained directionally consistent and of similar magnitude compared with the original survey-weighted Cox regression models. Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed associations are robust, not materially affected by the missing data approach, and unlikely to be fully explained by reverse causality.

The predictive ability of NLR and SIRI for all-cause mortality

Time-dependent ROC analysis displayed the AUC values for NLR in predicting all-cause mortality in individuals with nasal S. aureus colonisation, which were 0.66 (95%CI 0.60 to 0.72) at 3 years, 0.65 (95%CI 0.60 to 0.69) at 5 years and 0.63 (95%CI 0.59 to 0.66) at 10 years (figure 3A). Similarly, the AUC for SIRI was 0.68 (95%CI 0.63 to 0.73) at 3 years, 0.67 (95%CI 0.63 to 0.71) at 5 years, and 0.64 (95%CI 0.61 to 0.67) at 10 years for all-cause mortality (figure 3B).

Throughout a follow-up until 31 December 2019, a total of 595 (21.2%) all-cause deaths among 2805 individuals with nasal colonisation of S. aureus occurred. KM survival curves suggested that elevated NLR and SIRI levels were related to increased all-cause mortality (both log-rank p<0.0001, figure 3C and D) in the following life.

Subgroup analysis of NLR and SIRI in relation to all-cause mortality

We further conducted subgroup analyses to investigate whether the above positive association between NLR and SIRI levels with mortality of nasal S. aureus colonisation differed according to participant characteristics. No interactions were observed between NLR levels and age (P for interaction = 0.062), smoking status (P for interaction = 0.437), drinking status (P for interaction = 0.986), diabetes (P for interaction = 0.121), cancer (P for interaction = 0.31) and long-term care (P for interaction = 0.787), as shown in figure 4A. For NLR, modest effect modification was observed by sex, education, race and income (P for interaction=0.011, 0.001, 0.044 and 0.049, respectively), with stronger associations in men and in college-educated (HR=4.22) and non-Hispanic white (HR=2.48) participants.

Subgroup analysis of the association between NLR (A) and SIRI (B) levels with all-cause death among nasal S. aureus carriers. Each stratum was unadjusted for other covariates. NLR, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio; SIRI, systemic inflammatory response index.

Among adults with nasal S. aureus colonisation, the association between SIRI and all-cause mortality was consistent across strata of sex, age, family PIR, smoking, drinking, diabetes, cancer and long-term care status (all P for interaction >0.05). In contrast, significant heterogeneity was observed by education and race (P for interaction=0.012 and 0.016, respectively; figure 4B). Overall, elevated NLR and SIRI robustly predicted mortality among adults colonised with nasal S. aureus.

Non-linear associations of NLR and SIRI with all-cause mortality

We employed RCS to flexibly model and visualise the potential non-linear correlations between NLR and SIRI levels with all-cause mortality. After adjusting for all covariates in the master analytical Model 3 above, a J-shaped relationship was observed between NLR level and all-cause mortality (P-overall < 0.0001, P-nonlinear = 0.0161), with an inflection point of 1.77 (figure 5A). Similarly, RCS also illustrated a J-shaped association between SIRI level and all-cause mortality (P-overall < 0.0001, P-nonlinear > 0.0143), with an inflection point of 0.84 (figure 5B). Precisely, with rising NLR and SIRI levels, the risk of all-cause mortality first declined and then ascended among the overall population with S. aureus presence in cavum nasi.

Discussion

The results of our study indicate that individuals carrying S. aureus in the nasal cavity exhibiting elevated NLR and SIRI are at a heightened risk of all-cause mortality compared with those with lower levels of these markers. These associations remained robust across multiple subgroup analyses and were supported by restricted cubic spline models, which suggested potential non-linear relationships. This finding underscores the potential for timely interventions to mitigate hospitalisation expenses and adverse outcomes in populations with nasal S. aureus colonisation, particularly among high-risk populations.

Initially, we conducted a comparative analysis of the clinical characteristics between the survival and deceased cohorts among individuals with nasal S. aureus colonisation. We found that the proportion of elderly population in the death group was significantly higher than that in the survival group. Elderly individuals, due to the presence of chronic comorbidities such as diabetes, cancer and cardiovascular disease, together with immunosenescence,25 26 frailty and malnutrition, often experience impaired immune defenses. As a result, nasal colonisation with S. aureus in this population is more likely to progress to invasive infections, thereby increasing the risk of adverse outcomes, including mortality.16 Cigarette smoke has been shown to promote S. aureus colonisation by delaying immune response and bacterial clearance17 and enhances S. aureus biofilm formation through oxidative stress.18 In addition, a history of long-term care residence was also more frequent in the death group. Prior studies have shown that S. aureus, including methicillin-resistant strains, is highly prevalent in long-term care settings, and colonisation in this population is associated with subsequent invasive infections and poor outcomes.27 In conclusion, we concluded that greater attention should be paid to S. aureus nasal carriage in elderly individuals, as well as in populations of smokers, those with diabetes, cancer or a history of long-term care residence, in order to reduce the risk of severe secondary infection-related deaths and all-cause mortality.

Our preliminary analysis indicated significant differences in the NLR and SIRI between individuals who succumbed and those who survived among those colonised with S. aureus in their nasal cavity. In order to further exploration, we analyse the relationships between NLR or SIRI and the risk of all-cause death. Our study pioneeringly showed that the NLR and SIRI were positively associated with the long-term all-cause mortality of S. aureus nasal colonisation, as evidenced by both crude and adjusted models. Considering that individual characteristics and clinical parameters may affect this correlation, a subgroup analysis was conducted to adjust for these potential confounders, confirming the robustness of our findings. Therefore, it is recommended that clinicians should monitor NLR and SIRI levels in nasal carriers of S. aureus and incorporate the identified positive risk correlation into their therapeutic decision-making processes.

Although, the AUC values derived from our time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrate that NLR and SIRI exhibit limited predictive capabilities for all-cause mortality, nonetheless, NLR and SIRI remain valuable indicators due to their simplicity and widespread accessibility. Our research also showed that SIRI demonstrated a superior efficacy to NLR, as evidenced by the time-dependent ROC analysis. This suggests that SIRI may hold greater potential for clinical application. Similarly, SIRI demonstrated superior predictive value over NLR in forecasting the prognosis of carcinoma28 and autoimmune disease.29 Hence, SIRI—comprising monocyte, neutrophil and lymphocyte counts— seems to be a more comprehensive marker to reflect systemic inflammation than NLR.7 As a part of the innate immune system, neutrophils30 and monocytes31 play a primary role in the clearance of extracellular pathogens through a variety of mechanisms, including phagocytosis, release of cytokines and chemokines and expression of pattern recognition receptors. In addition to their role in the innate immunity, the neutrophils32 and monocytes33 also function as antigen-presenting cells in adaptive immunity to present antigens and activate T cells. Conversely, lymphocytes, such as regulatory T cells, typically function to suppress inflammatory responses and maintain immune homeostasis within the adaptive immune system.34 In conclusion, increased NLR and SIRI indicate heightened pro-inflammatory innate responses, alongside possible deficiencies in anti-inflammatory lymphocyte functions. This imbalance may contribute to the inflammatory and immunopathogenic processes that underlie the severe consequences of S. aureus nasal colonisation.

Our study provides further evidence that NLR and SIRI levels are nonlinearly correlated with the mortality risk of nasal S. aureus carriage using RCS, independent of other confounding variables. Specifically, NLR and SIRI were independently associated with all-cause mortality risk in a U-shaped manner, whereby the risk of mortality initially decreases and subsequently increases with rising NLR and SIRI levels. This suggests that both extremely low and extremely high NLR or SIRI levels are associated with high mortality risk. A low NLR or SIRI generally signifies a reduced neutrophil count or the presence of neutropenia, indicative of a compromised immune system.35 Elevated NLR or SIRI values typically reflect a diminished proportion of lymphocytes, which also suggests impaired immune function or potential immunosuppression.36 Accordingly, clinicians should be vigilant about the mortality risk associated with extremely low or extremely high levels of NLR and SIRI in individuals colonised with S. aureus in the nasal passages, thus developing personalised treatment plans in clinical practice. However, this study is only a preliminary exploration of the relationship between inflammatory markers and the risk of death in individuals who carry S. aureus in their nasal passages, and future investigations should delve into the mechanisms underlying this correlation.

This analysis provides novel insights into the relationship between elevated key haematological parameters, including NLR and SIRI, and heightened mortality risk of nasal S. aureus carriage within the community. A significant strength of our study is that the results are relatively robust and generalisable due to the assessment of the general population from large, prospective and long-term follow-up cohorts. Additionally, incorporating sampling weight increases the applicability and universality of our conclusions to the U.S. populace. In summary, our results provide a basis for predicting mortality in nasal S. aureus carriers, thereby enabling timely decolonisation therapies.

Our research, however, is subject to several limitations. First, it can only demonstrate association rather than causation because this research is an observational study using a cross-sectional design. Second, this research is inevitably subject to various types of biases. One issue is the recall bias, as the answers to the questionnaire are derived from the subjective recall of the interviewee. Another issue is the recalling bias introduced by participants during the questionnaire survey, as well as the selection bias due to the exclusion of patients with incomplete data. Third, the measurements of NLR and SIRI levels were conducted solely at the interview moment, and this study did not account for the effect of their potential trajectories on the risk of all-cause mortality across the follow-up period.

Fourth, not all potential covariates that could influence NLR and SIRI were included in the analysis—for example, active infections, autoimmune diseases and corticosteroid use, which may significantly alter leucocyte counts. These factors could not be incorporated due to limitations of the NHANES dataset. Fifth, the dataset itself was relatively old (2001–2004), and more recent NHANES cycles did not include S. aureus colonisation testing. Thus, our findings may not fully reflect the current epidemiological situation. Finally, the discriminative performance of NLR and SIRI was modest (time-dependent AUCs~0.60–0.70 at 3, 5 and 10 years), corresponding to limited discrimination; therefore, these markers should not be used as stand-alone prediction tools. Rather, they may serve as complementary features within multivariable risk assessment.

Despite these limitations, our study provides novel insights into the relationship between inflammatory markers and mortality risk among S. aureus-colonised adults. Future real-world studies with more comprehensive clinical information are warranted to validate and extend our findings. In conclusion, additional prospective research is essential to confirm the observed associations and clarify underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

Our study creatively indicates a positive correlation and a nonlinear pattern between systemic immune-inflammatory biomarkers—NLR as well as SIRI—and the risk of mortality in nasal S. aureus carriers, which is promising for prognosis prediction and antimicrobial agent stewardship in such populations.
