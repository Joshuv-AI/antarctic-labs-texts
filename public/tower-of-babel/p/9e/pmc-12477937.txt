Predictors of immediate survival in Emergency Department cardiac arrest in Ethiopia: Insights from a resource-limited setting
Authors: Zigale Tilaye Ayalew, Alemayehu Beharu Tekle, Molla Asnake Kebede, Melaku T Berhanu, Ashenafi Jemal, Etsegent akloge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) in Emergency Departments (EDs) remains a leading cause of mortality, particularly in low-resource countries. This study aims to identify the clinical characteristics and predictors of immediate survival among ED cardiac arrest patients in Ethiopian hospitals.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted among 215 adult patients who experienced IHCA in the EDs of Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College and Addis Ababa Burn, Emergency, and Trauma Hospital between January 2022 and December 2023. Logistic regression was used to identify predictors of immediate survival, defined as returns of spontaneous circulation sustained for at least one hour.ResultsAmong 215 patients, the mean age was 50.7 years, and 58.6 % were male. The overall immediate survival rate was 46.5 % (n = 100). Independent predictors of survival included male sex (AOR 2.20; 95 % CI 1.06–2.89), respiratory failure as the cause of arrest (AOR 15.04; 95 % CI 2.34–95.13), continuous cardiac monitoring (AOR 4.94; 95 % CI 1.59–15.39), and initial shockable rhythms—ventricular fibrillation (AOR 37.93; 95 % CI 2.20–655.58) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (AOR 25.64; 95 % CI 5.08–129.47). Admission for sepsis was associated with lower survival (AOR 0.05; 95 % CI 0.01–0.68).ConclusionNearly half of patients with ED cardiac arrest in Ethiopia achieved immediate survival. Continuous monitoring, rapid recognition of shockable rhythms, and prompt management of reversible causes such as respiratory failure improve outcomes, while sepsis remains a predictor of poor survival. Strengthening ED monitoring and resuscitation capacity could improve cardiac arrest outcomes in similar African settings.Keywords: In-hospital cardiac arrest; Cardiopulmonary resuscitation; Immediate survival; ROSC, Emergency department; Ethiopia

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) remains a critical emergency requiring rapid recognition and coordinated resuscitative efforts. Defined as the abrupt cessation of cardiac mechanical activity in hospitalised patients who initially had a pulse, IHCA carries significant morbidity and mortality worldwide [1,2]. Global estimates suggest that IHCA occurs in approximately 1–5 per 1000 hospital admissions, though outcomes vary considerably depending on patient demographics, institutional protocols, and resource availability [1,2].

Despite notable advances in resuscitation science, including the development of standardized protocols such as from Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS), survival rates remain unsatisfactory in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3,4]. Challenges in resource-limited settings—such as inadequate monitoring, delayed recognition, limited staff training, and lack of equipment—further compromise outcomes [5,6]. While registries and large studies in high-income countries have greatly advanced understanding of IHCA predictors and outcomes [7,8], comparable evidence from African emergency departments remains scarce [[4], [5], [6]].

Immediate survival, defined as return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) lasting at least one hour, is a key indicator of successful resuscitation. It serves as an early outcome measure that precedes longer-term results such as survival to hospital discharge and neurological recovery [1,3,8,9].Reported predictors of immediate survival include demographic factors (age, sex), comorbidities, initial arrest rhythm, etiology, time of event, and whether the arrest was witnessed and monitored [[7], [8], [9]].

Emergency departments (EDs) are particularly critical in the chain of survival, as they manage undifferentiated, critically ill patients, including trauma cases and acute medical emergencies [5,6]. Cardiac arrests in EDs may differ in prognosis and characteristics compared to those occurring in hospital wards or ICUs [1,3]. However, in LMICs, EDs remain under-researched despite heavy patient loads and resource constraints [[4], [5], [6]].

In Ethiopia, there is very limited evidence on predictors of immediate survival after IHCA, especially for patients who sustain arrest in the ED. Identifying these predictors can guide quality improvement, inform clinical training, and support resource allocation tailored to local needs.

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics and predictors of immediate survival after IHCA in the EDs of two tertiary hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. By exploring factors associated with successful ROSC, we hope to offer context-specific insights to improve resuscitation outcomes in resource-limited emergency settings.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study at Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC) and Addis Ababa Burn, Emergency, and Trauma (AaBET) Hospital in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. SPHMMC is a 700-bed tertiary center, while AaBET specializes in trauma and emergency care, together serving over 600 daily emergency visits [10,11].

Study population

The study included adults (≥18 years) who developed IHCA in the EDs between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023. Eligible patients presented with a documented pulse at triage, received cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and had complete medical records. Exclusions were age >80 years, advanced malignancy, chronic kidney disease, cardiac arrest outside the ED, or incomplete records.

Sample size

During the study period, 240 patients with IHCA were identified. After exclusions (n = 25), 215 patients remained, all of whom were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Data were abstracted from medical records using a structured tool, covering demographics, comorbidities, causes and rhythms of arrest, interventions, and outcomes. Trained nurses and interns collected data under daily supervision by the investigators. A pretest on 5 % of charts ensured clarity.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Epi Info and analyzed in SPSS v26. Descriptive statistics summarized patient characteristics. Variables with p ≤ 0.2 in bivariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) and p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Ethical approval

Approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of SPHMMC (Reference: pm 23/126). Patient consent was waived due to the retrospective design. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing data.

Causes of cardiac arrest and initial rhythms

Respiratory failure was the leading cause of cardiac arrest (25.1 %), followed by hypotension (21.9 %) and fatal arrhythmia (19.5 %). Other causes included metabolic disturbances (16.7 %), myocardial infarction (11.2 %), sepsis (8.4 %), and trauma (5.6 %). Non-shockable rhythms predominated, with asystole in 39.1 % and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) in 33.0 %. Shockable rhythms were less common: ventricular fibrillation (VF) in 9.3 % and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT) in 4.7 % (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

Advanced airway management was performed in 48.4 % of patients, continuous cardiac monitoring in 60.5 %, and defibrillation in 27.9 %. Most arrests occurred during non-office hours (51.6 %). CPR duration exceeded 29 min in 25.1 % of cases. Medications administered during resuscitation included IV fluids, bicarbonate, magnesium sulfate, amiodarone, and glucose (Table 2).

Immediate survival (ROSC sustained ≥1 hour) was achieved in 100 patients, giving a survival rate of 46.5 %. Survivors were more likely to be male, to have had continuous monitoring, shorter CPR duration, and shockable rhythms, while non-survivors more often presented with sepsis or hypotension.

Predictors of immediate survival

On multivariate analysis, independent predictors of survival included male sex (AOR 2.20; 95 % CI 1.06–2.89), respiratory failure as the primary cause of arrest (AOR 15.04; 95 % CI 2.34–95.13), continuous cardiac monitoring (AOR 4.94; 95 % CI 1.59–15.39), and initial shockable rhythms—VF (AOR 37.93; 95 % CI 2.20–655.58) and pulseless VT (AOR 25.64; 95 % CI 5.08–129.47). Sepsis was strongly associated with lower survival (AOR 0.05; 95 % CI 0.01–0.68) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found an immediate survival rate of 46.5 % among patients with IHCA in two Ethiopian EDs, higher than rates reported in some African and high-income settings [12,13]. Independent predictors of survival included male sex, respiratory failure as cause of arrest, continuous cardiac monitoring, and initial shockable rhythms, while sepsis predicted poor outcomes.

The strong association of shockable rhythms with survival reinforces the importance of rapid rhythm recognition and defibrillation, which are often limited in African EDs [[7], [8], [9]]. Similarly, continuous monitoring was associated with nearly fivefold higher survival, consistent with prior studies showing that monitored or witnessed arrests allow faster intervention [14,15]. In resource-limited hospitals, prioritizing monitoring for high-risk patients may improve outcomes.

Interestingly, respiratory failure as the primary cause of arrest predicted better survival, likely because it is often reversible if promptly managed with airway support or ventilation. Conversely, sepsis was strongly associated with mortality, reflecting its systemic effects and consistent with evidence linking critical illness to poor post-arrest outcomes [3,4,9].

The association of male sex with better outcomes has been observed in other studies [4,9], possibly reflecting differences in etiology, rhythm patterns, or early recognition. However, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Most arrests occurred during non-office hours, raising concerns about limited staffing and delayed responses outside working hours, a challenge common in LMIC hospitals [1,16].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides the first Ethiopian data on ED IHCA outcomes using multivariate analysis. Limitations include the retrospective design, lack of detailed CPR quality metrics, and restriction to ED arrests, limiting generalizability to ward or ICU settings.

Conclusion and recommendation

Immediate survival after ED cardiac arrest in Ethiopia was nearly 50 %, with outcomes strongly influenced by monitoring, shockable rhythms, and reversibility of cause. Strengthening continuous monitoring, defibrillation capacity, and airway management, while prioritizing sepsis recognition and treatment, could improve outcomes. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and assess survival to discharge and neurological outcomes.
