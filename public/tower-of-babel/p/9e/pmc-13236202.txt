Pancreatic tail solid pseudopapillary neoplasm mimicking mucinous cystic neoplasm: case report and literature review

Introduction

Solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs) of the pancreas are rare tumors, accounting for 1–2% of all pancreatic neoplasms, with a strong female predominance[1].

The World Health Organization classifies it as a low-grade epithelial malignancy with a favorable prognosis following surgical resection[2].

They represent a distinct clinicopathological entity, typically appearing as large, well-circumscribed, encapsulated masses with mixed solid and cystic components, often predominantly solid due to hemorrhage and degeneration, and may demonstrate internal septations, calcifications, and heterogeneous enhancement on imaging[3].HIGHLIGHTSRare pancreatic tumor (SPN) mimicking mucinous cystic neoplasm on imaging.Definitive diagnosis is achieved only after distal pancreatectomy and histopathology.Surgical resection provides excellent outcomes, despite diagnostic challenges.

Despite these features, radiological differentiation from other cystic pancreatic lesions, such as mucinous cystic neoplasms, pseudocysts, parasitic cysts, or congenital cysts, can be challenging, as imaging findings are not always definitive.

We report a case of a 30-year-old female presenting with left upper abdominal pain, in whom imaging revealed a multicystic lesion in the pancreatic tail suggestive of a mucinous cystic neoplasm; however, a definitive diagnosis following distal pancreatectomy and splenectomy confirmed an SPN, highlighting the diagnostic challenge and radiological overlap between these entities.

This case is reported in accordance with the SCARE guidelines to ensure comprehensive and transparent documentation of the patient’s presentation, management, and outcome[4].

Case presentation

A 30-year-old female with no known comorbidities presented with a 2-month history of left upper abdominal pain that was gradual in onset, dull in nature, and radiated to the back. The pain was aggravated by body movements and food intake, with no identifiable relieving factors. She also reported early satiety but denied vomiting, changes in bowel habits, gastrointestinal bleeding, fever, weight loss, jaundice, or prior trauma. There was no family history of malignancy, and her blood glucose levels were normal.

On examination, she was alert, afebrile, and hemodynamically stable, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0. There was no pallor, jaundice, lymphadenopathy, or peripheral edema. Abdominal examination revealed a normal contour without scars or distension, no palpable masses or organomegaly, and normal bowel sounds. Mild tenderness was noted in the left upper quadrant. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable, with a white blood cell count of 5.77 × 109/L and a hemoglobin level of 11.3 g/dL.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen demonstrated a well-defined multicystic lesion arising from the pancreatic tail, measuring approximately 7 cm, with thick, enhancing septations and areas of calcification. There was no evidence of invasion into adjacent structures, and the lesion was radiologically suggestive of a mucinous cystic neoplasm (Fig. 1). The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy with distal pancreatectomy and splenectomy under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a well-circumscribed cystic lesion was identified in the distal pancreas near the splenic hilum, with no gross local invasion. The splenic vessels were ligated, and the spleen was mobilized. Distal pancreatectomy with en bloc splenectomy was performed, hemostasis was secured, and a drain was placed prior to closure.

Gross examination of the specimen revealed a pancreatic mass measuring 7 × 6 × 5 cm, with cystic cavities containing thick material, along with solid friable areas and calcifications. The spleen was grossly unremarkable (Fig. 2). Histopathological analysis showed a well-circumscribed neoplasm with pseudopapillary and solid architecture, composed of uniform discohesive cells with round to oval nuclei and rare mitoses. Fibrovascular cores and rosette-like structures were present, along with areas of cystic degeneration, hemorrhage, calcification, and cholesterol clefts. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient received analgesia, antibiotics, and routine postoperative care, and was discharged in stable condition. At the 1-month follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, with good recovery, normal oral intake, and no evidence of postoperative complications.

Discussion

First described by Dr. Virginia Kneeland Frantz in 1959, SPNs are rare pancreatic tumors with low metastasis potential and high survival rates[5].

They have historically been referred to by various names, including solid and cystic pancreatic tumors, Frantz tumors, solid and papillary epithelial neoplasms, Hamoudi tumors, and papillary-cystic tumors[2].

Literature reports indicate that SPNs are more frequently located in the pancreatic body and tail (62.4%) than in the head and neck (37.6%), with rare extra-pancreatic occurrence (1.1%) and a mean tumor size of approximately 6.1 cm[6].

Our case involved a pancreatic tail lesion measuring about 7 cm, aligning with the typical location, and slightly exceeding the average reported size.

SPNs predominantly affect young females in their second or third decade of life, with a marked female-to-male ratio of approximately 11:1; a small proportion of cases occur in the pediatric population[7,8].

Our patient, a young adult female, is consistent with the typical demographic and epidemiological pattern of SPNs as reported in the literature.

The marked female predominance of SPNs is thought to reflect their origin from pluripotent genital ridge-related cells incorporated into the pancreas during embryogenesis, supported by their demographic distribution and expression of sex hormone receptors[9].

Molecularly, most SPNs harbor CTNNB1 mutations, leading to β-catenin accumulation, with rare associations to familial adenomatous polyposis via dysregulation of the adenomatous polyposis coli/Wnt–β-catenin pathway[10].

Retrospective national study by Hanada et al, evaluating the clinicopathological features of 288 patients with SPNs, showed that asymptomatic presentation (54.2%) was the most common, followed by nonspecific abdominal pain (28.4%), with nausea/vomiting (1.7%) and weight loss (0.7%) being rare, and no cases presenting with a palpable mass[11].

In contrast, our patient was symptomatic, presenting with left upper abdominal pain, which aligns with the second most common presentation reported in the literature.

European experts’ consensus identifies major cystic pancreatic lesions as intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms, mucinous cystic neoplasms, serous cystic neoplasms, microcystic adenomas, pancreatoblastomas, calcified hemorrhagic pseudocysts, and SPNs[12].

According to the European evidence-based guidelines on pancreatic cystic neoplasms, the diagnostic accuracy of imaging remains variable, ranging from 40% to 95% for MRI/MRCP and 40% to 81% for CT, with limitations in reliably distinguishing specific subtypes, small lesions, and ductal communication[13].

CT plays a complementary role, particularly in detecting calcifications, assessing suspected malignancy and vascular involvement, and evaluating postoperative recurrence, with multimodality imaging recommended in selected cases[13].

In our case, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a well-defined multicystic pancreatic tail lesion with enhancing septations and calcifications, suggestive of a mucinous cystic neoplasm, further illustrating the radiological overlap and diagnostic challenge in distinguishing SPNs from other cystic pancreatic lesions preoperatively.

Due to these radiological limitations, endoscopic ultrasound with fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) is recommended to improve diagnostic accuracy, as imaging alone demonstrates variable sensitivity and specificity; EUS morphology, cytology, and cyst fluid analysis enhance overall diagnostic performance, particularly in differentiating subtypes of pancreatic cystic neoplasms[14].

Systematic review by Gennaro Mazzarella et al demonstrates that, despite their distinct behavior from pancreatic cancer, SPNs may carry malignant potential; therefore, surgical resection is recommended irrespective of radiological findings[6].

In our case, EUS-FNA was not performed as the patient was planned for upfront surgical intervention. Given the radiological uncertainty and the recognized malignant potential of such lesions, proceeding directly to resection allowed for definitive tissue diagnosis and appropriate management, thereby avoiding delays associated with additional diagnostic procedures.

Clinical and radiological predictors of malignancy include (1) jaundice, (2) enhancing mural nodules ≥5 mm or solid components, (3) positive cytology, (4) main pancreatic duct dilatation ≥10 mm, (5) duct dilatation 5–9.9 mm, (6) cyst growth ≥5 mm/year, (7) elevated CA 19-9, (8) symptoms, (9) enhancing mural nodules <5 mm, and (10) cyst size ≥40 mm; patients with these features should be considered for surgical resection if fit[13].

Our patient presented with symptoms and a large cystic lesion measuring approximately 7 cm (>40 mm), both recognized risk factors for malignancy, despite the absence of other high-risk features such as jaundice, solid components, or ductal dilatation; thus, surgical resection was justified and enabled definitive diagnosis and appropriate management.

Systematic review of 1384 patients undergoing pancreatic surgery for SPNs: all patients underwent operative management, most commonly distal pancreatectomy (52.6%) and pancreaticoduodenectomy (27.0%), with additional procedures including enucleation (9.4%), central pancreatectomy (6.5%), and vascular reconstruction (1.0%). Most surgeries were performed via an open approach (81.3%), with laparoscopic (18.2%) and robotic-assisted techniques (0.5%) also utilized, and outcomes were favorable[6].

Our patient underwent an open distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy, which aligns with the most commonly performed surgical approach reported in the literature.

A retrospective 17-year analysis by Tjaden et al showed malignant behavior in 13% of cases, with recurrence in some patients, 100% overall survival, and 5- and 10-year disease-free survival rates of 89.0% and 81.6%, respectively; postoperative outcomes were favorable (ECOG 0–1), though exocrine insufficiency and diabetes were reported, with parenchyma-preserving surgery identified as a risk factor for recurrence[15].

In contrast, our patient had no malignant features on histopathology, an uneventful postoperative course, and will be closely followed up in view of the reported recurrence risk and potential long-term complications.

Conclusion

SPNs are rare pancreatic tumors with significant radiological overlap, making preoperative diagnosis challenging. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone for definitive diagnosis and treatment, offering an excellent prognosis and low recurrence rates. Careful postoperative follow-up is essential to monitor for potential recurrence and long-term complications.
