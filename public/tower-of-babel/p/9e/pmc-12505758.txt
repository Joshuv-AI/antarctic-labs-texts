Caring behavior: perspectives from nurses and parents of hospitalized children
Authors: Mehri Ansariniaki, Seifollah Alaei, Mojtaba Khoshdast Kakhki, Majid Mirmohammadkhani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe active presence of parents during the care and treatment of children in the hospital serves a crucial role in care provision. Considering the perspective on caring behavior from both nurses and clients is essential for planning and enhancing the quality of nursing care. This study assessed nurses' and parents' perceptions of nursing care.MethodA cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted between December 2022 and June 2023 at a tertiary pediatric referral hospital in Iran, involving 115 nurses and 115 parents. Demographic data and responses to Wolff’s Caring Behavior Questionnaire were collected during morning and evening shifts, with questionnaires retrieved at the end of each shift. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.ResultNurses' perception yielded an average caring behavior's score of 234.37±15.25, whereas parents' perception averaged 213.01±43.7. A statistically significant difference was observed between nurses' and parents' average scores of caring behavior perception (p<0.001). The highest average scores for caring behavior perception dimensions were associated with attentiveness to others' experiences and professional knowledge and skills among nurses and parents. A statistically significant difference (p<0.001) existed between nurses and parents across all domains. Also, age, insurance coverage of families, and working stability of nurses exhibited significant associations with their perception of caring behavior.ConclusionAlthough nurses and parents reported above-average caring behavior scores were above average, nurses exhibited significantly higher levels. Misalignment in parents’ perceptions may stem from specific familial challenges influencing their interpretation of nursing care. Hospital and nursing leadership should consider strategies to strengthen caring behaviors among nurses. Enhancing role clarity may align service recipients’ expectations with professional responsibilities. Furthermore, improving nurses’ job stability is essential to support consistent and effective caring behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06153-y.Keywords: Caring behavior perception, Nurses, Parents, Hospitalized children

Background

Care is the cornerstone of the nursing profession, representing a fundamental human need. Nurses, constituting the largest caregiving cohort across various levels of prevention, hold a pivotal role in realizing the objectives of the healthcare system [1]. Furthermore, if nurses implement caring optimally, patient satisfaction will finally improve the quality of nursing services [2]. In contrast, professional misconduct undermines patient safety and quality care [3]. Caring behavior is the center of nursing practice; therefore the better the nurse’s caring behavior in providing nursing services to patients, the better the patient satisfaction with nursing services [4]. Caring is considered the essence of nursing practice, but it has been understood differently in various countries [5].

Caring behavior is expressed through direct nurse-patient interactions at the bedside, where nurses typically serve as the most accessible and engaged care team members. By demonstrating appropriate caring behaviors, nurses can alleviate patient anxiety, improve comfort and satisfaction, and support faster recovery. Satisfaction with healthcare services is a multidimensional outcome shaped by how delivered care aligns with patient and family expectations, reflecting their perception of care received [6, 7].

Leininger stated that care encompasses comfort, coping, empathy, love, sensitivity, touch, and trust. Similarly, Watson defines caring as encompassing attentive listening, patient comfort, accountability, transparency, appropriate eye contact, sensitivity, respect, availability, patient-centeredness, and provision of information. Other theorists, such as Swanson, emphasize the importance of knowing the patient, taking supportive action, maintaining presence, and fostering trust in caregiving. These definitions frame care as an abstract concept that is construct challenging to quantify in clinical practice, often requiring operationalization through measurable concepts such as perceived caring behavior [8]. Even though all nursing interventions are verified through nurse caring behaviors, poor perception of caring behaviors reduces the well-being of the patients [9].

Caring behavior is generally delineated by sensitivity, empathy, attentive listening, honesty, and non-judgmental acceptance [10]. These behaviors, activities, and attributes exhibited by professional nurses signify an understanding of patient care and demonstrate the nurse’s investment and attentiveness toward the patient [1]. Quality care empowers patients to confront their illnesses and regain equilibrium by addressing their physical, emotional, social, and cultural needs, enhancing their overall quality of life. This form of care necessitates unity and a humanistic perspective, focusing on care’s technical and psychosocial facets [6].

Criticism often surrounds studies on care that separately address nurses’ and patients’ perspectives [7]. From the patient’s standpoint, an exemplary nurse offers emotional and psychological support alongside meeting physical needs [8]. Nonetheless, research in developing nations has frequently accentuated physical care over psychosocial aspects, yielding disparate outcomes within Iranian studies. While some emphasize physical care [11], others discern no substantial disparity between physical and psychosocial realms [12]. Such disparities in care behavior perceptions between nurses and patients can engender dissatisfaction with nursing care [13]. Consequently, exploring care behavior from both perspectives becomes imperative, considering care’s significance and diverse interpretations across societies [10]. The patients are often accompanied by their family members for information and support, and family members of hospitalized patients may experience difficulty without appropriate support. According to one study, some families of hospitalized patients reported poor perception of cognitive, emotional, and overall support from nurses [14].

Pediatric departments witness a substantial proportion of emergency and non-emergency admissions, rendering them among the hospital’s most stressful units [10]. Hospitalization pediatric patients usually have to receive various treatments, such as invasive procedures that result in anxiety either to children or parents [15]. The hospitalization process of children often needs invasive procedures, for instance, injection, infusion, or other invasive procedures causing fright to the children [16]. The better the nurses’ caring behavior, the lower the level of parental anxiety, and the higher the patient satisfaction in the pediatric inpatient ward [2]. Children and adolescents grappling with heightened mental and emotional challenges constitute a particularly vulnerable demographic, placing considerable strain on nursing staff. Given children’s limited ability to articulate their needs, parents assume a pivotal role in caregiving. Given the distinctive characteristics of pediatric patients and their families’ sensitivity, pediatric nursing emerges as a crucial domain in healthcare provision [17]. Parental anxiety could be decreased by nurses’ caring behavior [15].

Identifying care behaviors exhibited by pediatric nurses and the factors shaping them becomes essential [18]. In pediatric inpatient settings, parental perceptions often mirror children’s views, as parents serve as legal guardians and advocates for their hospitalized offspring. The active presence of parents during the care and treatment of children in the hospital serves as a crucial avenue for articulating their assessment of care provision, thereby enriching family-centered care and care quality [19, 20]. Meanwhile, some parts of pediatric care are family-centered and use the parent-family approach, a win-win solution for health service providers [15].

Perceptions of care behavior vary across societies and may not invariably align with team members’ views [21]. Patient and family compatibility and behavior can influence care quality [13], with divergent perspectives between clients and nurses potentially precipitating dissatisfaction [22]. Dissatisfaction of health service recipients and failure to meet their expectations will have a negative impact on the quality of services. Despite studies on nurses’ caring behavior and its determinants in pediatric inpatient settings, it is essential to address this gap. Given that parents participate in the childcare process and are considered the voice of children in evaluating the services received. Therefore, the current study investigated the Caring Behavior Perspectives of Nurses and Parents of Hospitalized Children.

Methodology

A cross-sectional descriptive study assessed the perception of nursing care behaviors among nurses and parents of children admitted to a tertiary pediatric referral hospital in Iran.

Participants and procedure

This study was conducted from December 2022 to June 2023. The research sample comprised 115 nurses and 115 parents. The sample size was determined based on a pilot study on 34 eligible parents and nurse members using G*power statistical software. Considering that the correlation coefficient between caring behavior given to parents and nurses in the pilot study was 0.36, and also considering a confidence level of 95% and a test power of 90%, the sample size was 82 people, by considering a maximum sample attrition rate of 40% (Table 1).

Stratified random sampling was employed to select half-eligible nurses from each ward based on the work schedule. Similarly, an equal number of parents were chosen via convenience sampling, corresponding to the selected nurses in each ward or work unit of the hospital.

Inclusion criteria for nurses encompassed a minimum of three months of experience in children’s care wards and willingness to participate. For parents, inclusion criteria entailed willingness to partake, provision of care for the child for at least one complete shift, and absence of addictions or mental disorders.

Data collection

Research instruments comprised a demographic questionnaire for nurses and parents of hospitalized children. Age, gender, department of employment, work experience, work experience in the children’s department, employment status, education level, marital status of nurses. Age, gender, occupation, and parents’ education are considered demographic characteristics. Alongside Wolff’s Caring Behavior, there is a perception questionnaire (1998), which is comprised of 42 questions. Each question implemented a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from “never” [1] to “always” [6], with a total score range of 42 to 252, where higher scores indicated more appropriate caring behavior perception. The questionnaire encompassed five subscales: respect for others, ensuring human presence, communication and positive attitude, professional knowledge and skills, and attention to others’ experiences [23]. Rafii confirmed the questionnaire’s validity in Iran [24], with reliability established through Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81 in our study.

After obtaining written consent from the eligible nurse participants and explaining the study methods and objectives, they were provided with demographic information and Wolff’s caring behavior questionnaires to be completed in a self-reported manner (in the absence of the researcher) when they had free time in the ward. The researcher visited the wards during the morning and evening shifts. If the nurse only provided service during the night shift, the questionnaire was left at the ward nursing station after a phone call and prior coordination to be completed during the night shift and then placed at the nursing station so that the researcher could collect them when the researcher returned to the relevant ward. In case of unavailability after two visits or if the nurses were unwilling to complete the questionnaire, another nurse was substituted.

Also, after obtaining written consent from the participating parents, they were provided with a caring behavior questionnaire completed by one of the patient’s parents in a self-reported manner (in the absence of the researcher) and delivered to the researcher an hour later. Questionnaires were provided to parents in the second half or last third of the child’s hospitalization.

Data analysis

Collected data were analyzed using SPSS 25 software and appropriate statistical tests. Mann-Whitney tests are used for comparing care behavior perception between nurses and parents, the Logistic Regression Model for reporting odd ratio (OR) of caring behavior’s perception (using the median of the observation values, which was equal to 236, it was classified into two groups), and also Pearson tests are used for determining the relationship between parents’ demographic variables and their perception of caring behavior’s perception.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Semnan University of Medical Sciences (IR.SEMUMS.REC.1401.165 code). Nurses and parents provided informed consent before participation. In this regard, it was explained to the intended samples that their personal information would be remain confidential, participation in the study would be voluntary, and the collected data would be used solely for research purposes.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the perception of care behaviors between nurses and parents of hospitalized children. The findings demonstrated a high score in care behavior perception among nurses, consistent with a study on pediatric nurses in Iran [25] and one study in Eastern Ethiopia [9]. Zare et al. found that intensive care unit nurses self-evaluated their caring behavior’s perception highly [1], surpassing the scores of general hospital nurses [26].

Similarly, the study revealed a relatively high care behavior perception score among parents, albeit significantly lower than that of nurses. Meanwhile, Aupia et al.‘s study in Indonesia indicated that although nurses’ care behavior scores were lower than those in the current study, they remained average, yet nurses, students, and patients shared a common understanding of care [27]. In contrast, a study in Khalkhal City, Iran, revealed that patients rated nurses’ caring behavior higher than nurses themselves, possibly due to the swift provision of emergency care during the patients’ short stays in the emergency department [28]. The findings closely align with Safa et al.‘s study regarding patients’ perception of care behavior in a public hospital in Kashan [29]. Notably, most of the referenced studies have compared nurses’ perceptions of their caring behavior with those of patients rather than with parents and families, potentially influencing the present study’s consistency or inconsistency with similar research.

Irawan Rifai’s study in Indonesia on parents shows that most respondents assess nurses’ caring behavior as adequate, and a significant relationship exists between anxiety and satisfaction with nurses’ caring behavior [2]. Conversely, a study in Nigeria found that only some infants’ mothers perceived care positively [19].

Concerning caring behavior dimensions, nurses and parents rated attention to experiences and professional knowledge and skills as the highest. This alignment suggests confidence in nurses’ professional competencies and collaborative engagement in the care process, such as intravenous line establishment. In a study by Salmani et al., mothers expressed interest in nurses’ vein-taking skills and the number of attempts needed for successful insertion [12]. The finding of one study shows that higher nurses’ caring behavior may lead to lower parents’ anxiety in invasive treatment situations [15]. Additionally, nurses demonstrated a growing awareness of their role, while parents expressed a desire for increased involvement in their infants’ care [30].

Moreover, nurses perceived respect for others as the least scored dimension, while parents rated communication and positive attitude lowest, echoing Jafari et al.‘s findings [28]. This disparity may stem from nurses’ workload and communication skill deficiencies. Hospital nursing shortages may compel nurses to prioritize tasks, potentially altering task performance methods and manners [30]. Positive communication between mothers and nurses may foster a sense of security, shorten hospital stays, reduce costs, and bolster employee motivation to deliver superior services [12].

Regarding the relationship between nurses’ demographic variables and their caring behavior’s perception, no significant relationship was found between general work experience and caring experience with caring behavior’s perception. However, age and job status emerged as predictors of nurses’ caring behavior’s perception. Job security is considered a predictor of patient availability [31]. In line with this finding, one study in Indonesia shows that nurses with civil servant status are 3.6 times more likely to behave in caring than nurses with contract employment status [4]. One study in Iran showed that the nurse’s sense of responsibility towards patients, personal achievement in professional activity, the tendency to deliver high-quality care, and personal problems are important factors in caring behaviors [5]. The convergence of nurses’ and parents’ average ages in this study suggests age may impact caring behavior perception, with older nurses potentially exhibiting lower scores.

A qualitative study conducted in Iran identified multiple contributors to professional misconduct among nurses, including human factors (e.g., professional and personal characteristics of nurses and patients, patient clinical conditions), procedural factors (e.g., procedural context and ability to substantiate misconduct), and organizational factors (e.g., recruitment practices, resource availability, misconduct management, bureaucratic processes, and ward-specific conditions) [3]. Nursing managers should address the antecedents of anticipated care by improving nurses’ working environments, revising routine care protocols and inter-professional collaboration, and implementing structured training programs focused on clinical reasoning, decision-making, and time management [32].

In a study from Eastern Ethiopia, 63.4% of nurses demonstrated a favorable perception of caring behaviors. Educational attainment, workload, job satisfaction, and shared decision-making were significantly associated with positive perceptions of caring behavior [9].

Aupia et al.‘s study demonstrated a positive relationship between patients’ average age and the respect subscale [27], paralleling the current study’s findings regarding parents’ age, employment status, and insurance status. Although economic status did not significantly influence caring behavior perception, notable differences were observed. Safa et al. showed no significant relationship between caring behavior’s perception and parents’ education level [29]. while educational status and number of children significantly influence mothers’ perception of pediatric nursing care [19]. According to the finding of Kurniawati et al. in Surabaya City, Indonesia, patients’ perceptions of nurse caring behavior were related to theirs self-acceptance and education level but were not related to their income [33]. However, the current study suggests that parents’ employment status, insurance coverage, and, to some extent, education level impact their caring behavior perception. Financial burdens and misconceptions regarding nurses’ roles may diminish parents’ care perception. Therefore, interventions should target nurses’ workload and socio-economic support for parents and clarify nurses’ professional roles.

One strength of this study is the inclusion of perspectives from both nurses and families across all pediatric care units. However, the single-center design and self-reported data limits the generalizability of the findings. Additional limitations include time constraints among nurses, short patient lengths of stay, and the need for simultaneous presence of both nurses and parents, which may have affected response accuracy. Furthermore, this study did not allow for a comparative analysis of caring behaviors across emergency, general, and intensive care pediatric units. Also males were greatly underrepresented in the sample can take account as another limitation of current study.

Conclusion

Although nurses and parents perceived caring behaviors toward hospitalized children as above average, nurses rated their performance more highly than parents. Several variables including nurse age and job stability, parent age, employment status, and insurance coverage were identified as influencing perceptions of caring behavior. Therefore, targeted efforts are needed to enhance job stability among nurses, expand supportive services for families and patients, and improve public understanding of nurses’ professional roles. These strategies may collectively enhance care quality in pediatric hospital settings. In addition to promoting longer-term employment contracts and offering financial and managerial support for nurses, hospitals should ensure that job descriptions for all staff in pediatric units are accessible to patients and families.

The findings of this study may inform managerial interventions designed to strengthen collaboration and engagement between caregivers, families, and healthcare providers. Moreover, results could guide the development of educational programs to improve communication skills and resilience among nurses and nursing students. Given the study’s limitations, future research should include assessments of parental perceptions following the child’s discharge. Such investigations would offer a more comprehensive understanding of caring behaviors. Additionally, multicenter studies across specialized pediatric hospitals could facilitate comparative analyses of perceived caring behaviors across emergency, general inpatient, and intensive care units.
