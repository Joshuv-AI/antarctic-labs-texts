‘I dream of the airplane, but I cannot board it’: Exploring end-of-life aspirations with people who have lived experiences of homelessness
Authors: Merryn Gott, Stella Black, Janine Wiles, Lisa Williams, Tess Moeke-Maxwell, Jackie Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Little is known about how people experiencing homelessness discuss their end-of-life aspirations or the extent to which these align with current models of Advance Care Planning.Objectives:To explore how people with lived experience of homelessness discuss their end-of-life aspirations and identify implications for Advance Care Planning.Design:A qualitative study was undertaken underpinned by a social constructionist framework.Methods:In-depth interviews were conducted with 16 participants with lived experiences of homelessness living in a major city in Aotearoa New Zealand.Results:Participants had difficulty in formulating aspirations and these were magnified when considering end-of life due to the challenges of predicting future resources, including shelter, food, and sources of social support and connection. End-of-life aspirations discussed involved basic needs being met, for example, through warm and dry shelter, access to a safe, non-chaotic space, and having access to food and showers. A good relationship with a trusted supporter was identified as an important enabler of end-of-life aspirations being realised. However, participants were unable to identify who would support them at end-of-life. Experiences of healthcare shared indicated that people were not provided with appropriate information, and the relational characteristcs were not present, to enable them to make decisions about their care and treatment.Conclusion:Mainstream approaches to Advance Care Planning do not meet the needs of people with lived experience of Homelessness. New professional approaches are needed that are responsive to experiential knowledge and context, connect different care networks, and recognise the injustices linked to structural disadvantage. Approaches must be developed in partnership with people with lived experience of homelessness. Supporting aspirations for living is a necessary first step to consider aspirations when dying for people experiencing structural disadvantage and discrimination.

Background

People with lived experience of homelessness die with a high burden of unmet palliative care need. 1 A lifetime of trauma and stigmatisation relating to structural oppression results in higher rates of serious mental health problems and substance use when compared with the housed population.2,3 Negative experiences with institutions, including healthcare environments, are common and trust in health professionals is limited.4,5 It is therefore unsurprising that people experiencing homelessness are low users of healthcare relative to need, including use of generalist and specialist palliative care.6,7

Addressing inequities in palliative care access has been identified as an urgent priority. 8 An important first step is making visible the resources and privileges that currently determine palliative care access and experience. 9 In this context, a key task for researchers is expanding an evidence base which currently disproportionately reflects the end-of-life aspirations and experiences, of white, middle class, male cis-gender, hetereosexual people, living in Global North countries. 10 A critical review of evidence regarding the intersection between poverty and palliative care 11 observed a striking absence of research reporting the views and experiences of people with lived experience of poverty, including homelessness. Similarly, a review of adult models of palliative and end-of-life care concluded that the voices of those experiencing homelessness were rarely included. 1 This is a worrying oversight given that lived experience has been identified as foundational for new equity-focused approaches to palliative care, including in Aotearoa New Zealand, the setting for the current study. 12

The need to centre lived experience and participatory knowledge generation as a means of progressing the equity agenda in palliative care also aligns with Miranda Fricker’s arguments regarding ‘epistemic justice’. 13 She highlights that: ‘An understanding of what it is like to live with poverty . . . and what the psychological pressures, emotional traumas, and deliberative constraints really are on the ground, cannot be imagined from the outside’. 14 This has important implications given that people with these lived experiences are rarely included in creating the shared social meanings and concepts that determine how societies operate, which she terms ‘hermeneutic injustice’. 13 This insight is highly relevant to palliative care for people experiencing homelessness. It suggests that these communities may neither hold shared societal assumptions about death or dying, nor have a shared language to talk about end-of-life issues with people who have not lived through similar circumstances.

This is partly borne out by current evidence regarding the end-of-life aspirations of people experiencing homelessness, which, whilst limited, 15 points to both similarities and differences with the housed population. A US study of people with experience of homelessness concluded that ‘The vast majority did not want to die alone and wanted to die surrounded by loved ones in a state of reconciliation, with a sense that they had been important to others’. 5 In line with other research, 16 additional priorities included not being a burden, dying at home, and receiving adequate pain control. Whilst these themes resonate with the wider literature, the context of homelessness was also invoked in discussions of access to adequate shelter and, for people who used drugs, reliable access to these at end-of-life. 5 Canadian research also highlights that day-to-day worries about survival may leave little room for other concerns, 17 including future planning. More research is needed to better understand the context within which end-of-life aspirations are considered and, if relevant, formulated for people with lived experience of homelessness.

A further consideration for research in this space is the growing realisation that palliative care initiatives must grow out of their local geographical and socio-cultural context. 18 Indigenous perspectives on end-of-life aspirations remain an evidence gap, particularly within the context of homelessness. Within Aotearoa New Zealand, engaging with a te ao Māori (Māori worldview) understanding is imperative to meet the obligations of Te Tiriti o Waitangi. This is particularly important given the significant over-representation of Māori in homeless communities due to the ongoing trauma of colonisation. 19

Aim

To explore how people with lived experience of homelessness discuss their end-of-life aspirations and identify implications for current approaches to Advance Care Planning (ACP).

Methodology

Critical social theory, 20 Kaupapa Māori 21 approaches, and feminist perspectives22,23 informed our social constructionist framework. 24 We view knowledge and experience as culturally and historically situated and consequently we attended to researcher positionalities 25 and explored the role of intersectional social identities as a determinant of participant experience. 26 These approaches prompted us to strive to make social structures of oppression visible, including colonialism, racism, and classism, as well as orient our findings towards ethical solutions.

Methods

A qualitative design using individual, semi-structured interviews was adopted to gain in-depth insight into participants’ lived experiences. The setting for this study was a large, urban area in Aotearoa New Zealand. We worked in partnership with a provider of services for homeless and vulnerably housed people. A convenience sample of participants with lived experience, past or present, of homelessness were invited to participate in the study by staff with whom they had trusted relationships. For the purposes of our study, we adopted the official definition of homelessness used in Aoateroa New Zealand, namely ‘a living situation where people, with no other options to acquire safe and secure housing, are: without shelter – for example, sleeping rough or living in a car, in temporary accommodation, or living in uninhabitable housing’. 27 Participants were eligible for inclusion if they had a lived experience of homelessness; participants were excluded if they did not have the capacity to consent at the time of the interview. All participants approached by staff agreed to participate.

Te Ārai Palliative Care and End-of-life Research Group work within a partnership framework set by Te Ārai Kāhui Kaumātua (Māori Elder Advisory Group) whereby Māori and non-Māori researchers co-lead projects. J.R. (Pākeha researcher) and S.B. (Ngāi Tūhoe, Whakatōhea, Te Whanau-a-Apanui, and Ngāti Whakaue) led this project. S.B. led the way in which the study was conducted for Māori participants, including conducting the interviews for all participants, including Māori participants, and leading the analysis of all Māori data with support from Te Tōputanga o te Mauri (Māori Advisory Group). Te Ārai Kāhui Kaumātua advised on the study design. A glossary of Māori kupu (words) is provided in Table 1.

Interviews were conducted between the 24th of August and the 28th of September 2023. Most were conducted in a private room within the building where a range of services for the homeless community are provided. A trusted Key Worker facilitated whanaungatanga (introductions) before leaving. Four interviews were conducted in the participant’s current home in the presence of their Key Worker.

Each interview took between 30 and 90 min. A koha (gift) of a $100NZD supermarket voucher was always offered at the start of the interview and participants were assured that it was not dependent upon completing the interview. Written informed consent was obtained prior to commencing the interview by S.B. S.B. took fieldnotes immediately following the interview which were included in the analysis. Interviews were professionally transcribed by a transcriber who had signed a confidentiality agreement. All transcripts were checked by S.B. and edited if required.

Analysis

Data were treated as taonga or treasured knowledge and we foregrounded the expertise in lived experience. Te Ārai Kāhui Kaumātua (Māori Elder Advisory Group) supported us to adopt a strengths-based approach for the study, which helped to highlight the ways in which participants expressed rangatiritanga, or self-determination. The approach to analysis was led by the nature of the data collected. In particular, we were attentive to ‘silences’ within the data around certain topics. 13 We also sought to accommodate participant narratives, which were non-linear, with participants returning to certain ideas at different points in the interview.

The research team met bi-monthly for 3 months, discussing the approach to analysis and reviewing several transcripts together. We are a group of women researchers with extensive experience in conducting qualitative research within a palliative care context and with a focus on structurally disadvantaged communities. We came from a range of academic backgrounds (spanning Palliative Care, Kaupapa Māori Research, Addiction, Mental Health and Criminal Justice Research, Health Geography, Gerontology, Nursing, and Design and Creative Methods), which enabled expertise to be shared, different disciplinary perspectives aligned and divergent views discussed and reconciled. We openly discussed our assumptions and reactions to the data as a means of engaging reflexively with the analysis. Interpretation of Māori data was led by S.B.

M.G., J.W., and S.B. then met weekly for another 3 months to review the remainder of the transcripts. This provided S.B. with an opportunity to share insights from having conducted the interviews and her expertise in Kaupapa Māori Research, Addiction, and Mental Health Research and understanding of Te ao Māori. These discussions supported M.G. and J.W. to develop a coding framework, which they applied to all transcripts. NVivo 11 software (QSR International) was used as an organising tool to help manage the large amount of data collected; however, during this final analysis stage, and during writing, M.G. returned repeatedly to the full transcripts. Hence, whilst coding was thematic, attention was always paid to the wider context within which an extract was situated.

Participants

Sixteen participants were interviewed; 11 men and 5 women. Two participants were friends and requested to be interviewed together, both had a longer association with several homelessness services and one was employed as a peer worker; therefore, 15 interviews were undertaken. Five participants identified as New Zealand European, seven as Māori, and four as Pacifika (two Samoan and two Cook Islands). All interviews were conducted in English; interviews in other languages were not requested. Ages ranged from 20 to 70 years. Current living situations varied, but all participants had lived experience, past or present, of homelessness and/or insecure housing (Table 2). Experiences of trauma included being in prison, relationship breakdowns, unemployment, drug use, grief and loss, gang affiliations, violence, a mental health condition, cultural disconnection, sexual abuse, and having been deported from Australia back to Aotearoa. We did not question participants directly about their health, but most offered this information, typically towards the end of the interview (Table 3).

Thirteen of the 16 participants disclosed a current serious mental and/or physical health condition. Mental health conditions disclosed included severe depression and/or anxiety, suicidality, and schizophrenia. Physical health conditions disclosed included cardiac disease, cancer, respiratory disease, neurological conditions, and diabetes, with many participants reporting more than one condition. Findings

There was great diversity among participants and our analyses identified few clear intersectional differences by socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, or family situation. Additional important contributors to experiences and views appeared to be childhood circumstances, the length of time people had experienced homelessness, their employment history, whether they had children or not, and their relationships with them. We highlight divergent views and experiences where relevant. The role of te ao Māori in shaping Māori whānau (extended biological and non-biological family) experiences is also explored. Each participant has been given a pseudonym devised by S.B., who chose names that would reflect their gender, cultural identity, and age. Care was also taken to maintain the privacy of each individual and we sought advice from staff at the organisation we partnered with about the suitability of the names chosen, particularly checking that they could not be confused with current or previous whānau clients.

There was complexity and ambivalence in the information participants shared. Their narratives pointed to long histories of trauma and struggle, even if the nature of this struggle could not be fully articulated. How and what information was shared was pertinent to understanding participant experiences and views. While ‘silences’ in the data were often related to trauma, we also noted difficulties in discussing end-of-life issues and aspirations. These went beyond societal discomfort with the topic and encompassed difficulties in thinking ahead and holding aspirations in general, as well as the ability to predict future resources, including sources of social support and connection. They may also reflect the lack of a shared language and common experience to discuss death and dying, 13 or even the willingness and courage to be honest and openly vulnerable about the uncertainty of life.

End-of-life aspirations

To understand the context for discussion of end-of-life aspirations, we must first acknowledge the way in which participants talked about aspirations more broadly. Of note, four participants could not engage with this discussion at all, as exemplified below:

Okay, cool. Let’s say that you’re well, that you’re mentally well, you’re physically well, everything is going good.

Yes. . . .

Okay, so it’s all positive, there’s nothing stopping you from having the life that you want, the life that you deserve. Tell me what that looks like Raniera, where are you?

Man. I wouldn’t . . . (silence).

In the context of their struggles with health and other aspects of life, several participants stated that just being alive was an achievement. As Moana shared: ‘I’ve been f**** waking up wishing I was dead’.

Other participants were able to share aspirations and goals; these focused predominantly around the desire for recovery and improved wellbeing, being independent, securing employment, and connecting with family and whānau. Many were very focused on achieving their goals, as Temauri shared: ‘I’ve got a lot of things I’ve gotta do, got my visions and I am not giving up’. For several participants, a challenge in discussing end-of-life concerns related to their sense of being someone who has the potential to recover from addictions and homelessness.

However, participants were also ambivalent as to how achievable their goals were. Some noted that addictions hampered their ability both to have dreams, and to realise them. As Vaipuna shared: You know, we all have dreams, you know, yeah. It’s just getting from A to B is the problem because, you know, we’re kind of alcoholics. The first thing we get up, the first thing we need to have a drink ‘cause then if we don’t, we’re coming down.

You know, we all have dreams, you know, yeah. It’s just getting from A to B is the problem because, you know, we’re kind of alcoholics. The first thing we get up, the first thing we need to have a drink ‘cause then if we don’t, we’re coming down.

When Stella tried to ask this participant questions about end-of-life they were not able to share any further information, reflecting the difficulties many participants had in engaging with this topic. Ngahere, when asked what support she would want if she got very sick, started by saying: ‘I don’t know. That’s a tricky question to ask. I would not know, yeah’. She eventually summed this inability to both articulate and realise such big goals very aptly: ‘I want an airplane eh? And sometimes you can’t get the airplane?’

End-of-life aspirations shared rarely related to medical procedures or even physical health. Rather, shelter and basic resources were prioritised. Participants expressed that at the end-of-life, a key thing was a place to rest, to be protected, in the context of the hardness of being on the street. As Elijah shared: ‘I think I’d rather be here [supported apartment], I’m safe here. If I die here . . . at least, at least, yeah, I’ll be happy’. For some participants, this discussion was emotional, as they currently didn’t have access to this sort of space, or lacked predictable access to resources such as social support. For example, Albert became tearful reflecting on previous accommodation: ‘I used to have a 4x4 box I used to live in. I was happy there, just a stove, fridge, simplicity’. For this participant, home was a place that enables choices.

Necessities like money, food, a shower, and a place to do washing were also prioritised. Temauri highlighted that meeting the shelter and basic resource needs of street whānau now through more social housing and night shelters would ensure they were met when they did face the end of their lives. As he said: ‘why do we have to wait until we are dying?’

A minority of participants mentioned palliative care unprompted; a priority in this context was being assured that accessing these services would not be financially prohibitive. As Stanley stated: ‘I would want to have access to any palliative care available as I needed it, not just immediately, but knowing that I can access that without any prohibitive costs, financial costs’.

The healthcare provided by the social care organisation partnering in the study was discussed by most as an important resource, particularly in terms of ease of access, practically and financially. Some extrapolated this to what they would want at end-of-life, including social work and mental health support. Others expressed concerns about their ability to navigate health and social services. While some participants did see rest homes and hospital or hospice services as a potential option for end-of-life, some women interviewed expressed concern about the appropriateness, acceptability, and accessibility of formal care spaces. Others shared negative experiences, including of hospice, for example when friends were discharged ‘cause they don’t die’ or pain management was inadequate. Nuku shared a traumatic story of their friend who was addicted to morphine being denied morphine when dying in hospice: I’ve seen a lot of my friends have passed away in palliative care and it’s been a very ugly scene for the family to cope with and that. And I don’t want that . . . It was like f**** tragic . . . I would like to go to sleep on opiates. That’s peaceful, you know, you’re not hurting anyone, you know. It’s just gentle. There’s no chaos or violence or anything involved. Not upsetting, you know.

I’ve seen a lot of my friends have passed away in palliative care and it’s been a very ugly scene for the family to cope with and that. And I don’t want that . . . It was like f**** tragic . . . I would like to go to sleep on opiates. That’s peaceful, you know, you’re not hurting anyone, you know. It’s just gentle. There’s no chaos or violence or anything involved. Not upsetting, you know.

This idea of a peaceful end that was unchaotic and pain-free was raised by several participants. However, although many examples of death and dying were shared, no participant could recall a positive example of good dying for street whānau.

Having access to trained health professionals who could provide appropriate and well-secured pain medication, and had experience of supporting people with drug addictions was another important aspiration. An interesting discussion was held between Sina and Whaitiri, who were friends and one of whom worked as a peer support worker. They said they ‘would be too scared’ to have responsibility of supporting people to receive appropriate pain medication in the context of drug addiction. They did, however, feel that they could be helpful working in partnership with a nursing team to support street whānau.

Over half of the participants spoke explicitly about sorting out affairs and legacy planning in relation to their death. Even if I write a story, you know, and put down my writing. . . . Or something, yeah. You know, my beliefs, you know. . . . Maybe I could do that and I wouldn’t have to worry about what people think and that, ‘cause I won’t be here anyway. You know, I could just let it out, you know.

Maybe I could do that and I wouldn’t have to worry about what people think and that, ‘cause I won’t be here anyway. You know, I could just let it out, you know.

Others were focused on ‘tidying up’ affairs, managing paperwork and sorting out what should happen to any belongings they had. Waimarama talked about how this was an immediate concern for her:

So what does the future look like?

Me? F*** I’ve got to get my affairs into place. I’m sick sis. It’s a shock. I haven’t been able to come out of the closet since I’ve got an understanding of what’s wrong with me. I’ve just been hiding away bro. I gave up work and everything.

It’s so unfair, so unfair.

It’s just too much.

Yeah.

I’ve got to leave something for my kids, that’s. . . .

Some raised concerns about who might want their belongings after they died, underlining their social isolation. For example, Albert expressed great sadness that he couldn’t think of anyone who would want his belongings and that they might just be thrown away. As he shared: ‘They will. Even, even my collection of fun chimes and things, I’m just going to, eventually they’ll be deleted. But I don’t, well, what could I do about it? . . . Who do I give them to?’.

For other participants, questions about end-of-life wishes led to wider existential concerns, including for Edward, who didn’t see any point in knowing about a terminal condition over which he had no control, but did want to try and find a sense of purpose before he died:

But God don’t want me just yet, eh? I hang my cross up there. No, the other one’s gone, they broke it. It’s a lifetime of bad luck. Yeah. No, God don’t want me yet, but he’s not telling me why I’m here and it pisses me off, you know?

Oh well, while you’re here, you may as well look after yourself.

Sure, as best as I can, you know? I just try not to hurt other people, that’s all I try and do, mmm.

The role of family and whānau at end-of-life

Whilst connection with family and whānau was important to participants, relationships were experienced within the context of lifelong struggles to build and maintain this connection. Most had experienced a childhood characterised by instability and, for many, violence and abuse. This was an issue about which there were many ‘silences’ in the data. Respecting participant wishes not to discuss their family was important to the development of trust with Stella, as evident below:

So what about your family? Do you maybe want to talk about your family?

No.

No, kei te pai, kei te pai (that’s all good).

For many, family and whānau extended beyond kinship ties to encompass family and whānau of choice. Some found connections within prison and with gang whānau. For example, one participant talked about childhood experiences of racism, which led him to feel safer in prison, where he connected with Māori friends and, through that, connected to te aoMāori (the Māori world) more broadly. Similarly, gangs were discussed as providing familial relationships that were missing within biological families and whānau, especially during traumatic periods of people’s lives:

So, you lost your partner and you lost your child?

Yeah, so it turned me quite inward, I turned quite bitter. I ended up in a gang, yeah, in the end. And they were like my family; they were really supportive of me. Yeah. I’ve drawn away from that quite considerably over the last couple of years.

However, as in this example, participants also discussed needing to sever these ties to avoid criminal activity and addiction, and to maintain relationships with children. The two friends that had a lot to do with supporting others within the homeless community reflected on the role of ‘street family’, particularly at end-of-life:

But, yeah, you do have a family, your street family. They become your family.

Okay. And so they would be the ones some of our whānau living on the streets would want around them, supporting them in that time [at end-of-life]?

Around them, yeah. Those are the ones they want around them at the time. They don’t want their family. And yet when they pass, when our Streeties pass, we ask their family to come get them, so where were you when they were out here? All of a sudden, you wanna take them home, why? They hadn’t been to youse for 20, 30 years and all of a sudden, you wanna, what, take them back to there, where they’ve never been most of their life? They’ve been out here with us, we’re their family. So I’ve taken a few, a couple of them . . . back to their homes.

Some participants reflected on how the loss of family and whānau relationships, either through bereavement or estrangement, was the catalyst for them becoming homeless, often at a young age. Although participants tried hard to focus on positive aspects of their current family and whānau relationships, for most, these remained difficult as Niko shared: Yeah that’s the sort of things why I don’t get on with my father. Because . . . after staying at his house, [my important belongings] all just went missing. And I know he does things like that . . . once you have an argument with him, he’s vindictive. Like he turns quite septic, yeah it’s the nasty side of his personality.

Yeah that’s the sort of things why I don’t get on with my father. Because . . . after staying at his house, [my important belongings] all just went missing. And I know he does things like that . . . once you have an argument with him, he’s vindictive. Like he turns quite septic, yeah it’s the nasty side of his personality.

Meaningful efforts to reconnect were reported, particularly with children from whom participants had become estranged. Being able to have a parent/child relationship and providing financial and other support to children was a strong motivator to tackle addiction problems, improve physical and mental health, and try and find employment. Success supported wellbeing:

I’m starting to rise up, I’m starting to get things together, like getting my family, my sons together, my daughter, getting them together.

Wow, I’m so happy for you, that’s so beautiful.

However, some found it hard to articulate how these relationships could be repaired; others stated that some relationships had been lost altogether. This could lead to feelings of loneliness and alienation:

It just builds up my, ‘cause I don’t have, you know, my kids are old and my older ones just took off [overseas]. I don’t have anyone here, I don’t, you know, sit down and talk to somebody. I just come here, but sometimes I’m not here, I just go, you know? ‘Cause it’s just, I don’t know, the anxiety, that feeling. Don’t wanna go out, don’t wanna open up my door to anyone who knocks at the door. I just don’t have any family around me.

Family and whānau also experienced their own struggles that limited their ability to support participants, and trauma was often inter-generational in nature. Specifically at the end of life, some participants did focus on family, for example, Nuku went on to share that they ‘just wanna have kids around me, so, you know, all my nieces, nephews, they make me laugh. Watching them running around playing, getting told off by their cousins. Yeah’.

However, difficulties with these relationships meant it would be difficult to realise aspirations around family involvement. For example, Niko shared that he would ‘definitely’ want his brother present when he was dying, but went on to explain that ‘He’s going through a few issues at the moment . . . we had a big argument last week. In the end he kicked me out of his house . . . so that was unfortunate, but I’d like [him] to be around me’. Similarly, Tamanui, musing on what the end-of-life might be like, shared: Will the ones that are alive love me enough to come see me off? . . . yeah, I don’t think no one even gives a f***. So, to be honest, yeah, they know who my name is, they know who I am. But I don’t even think I’m gonna go home.

Will the ones that are alive love me enough to come see me off? . . . yeah, I don’t think no one even gives a f***. So, to be honest, yeah, they know who my name is, they know who I am. But I don’t even think I’m gonna go home.

Most Māori participants discussed positive feelings of connection to te ao Māori. These were in the form of childhood memories and connections to whenua (land), iwi (tribes), and whānau (extended family), either existing or aspirational. Reviving, or establishing, these connections was an important but not straightforward aspiration. The damage of colonisation was evident. Similar narratives were shared by Pacific participants who discussed positive cultural connections but also highlighted difficulties. For example, one participant talked about their negative experiences of the church. Others discussed the fact that it was unlikely they would ever have the resources to return to their homeland:

. . . I’m from an island [redacted] . . ., yeah.

Wow, fabulous. Have you been back? No?

Can’t afford. It’s so dear, I can’t, I’ve gotta worry about staying alive here in New Zealand. But I’d love to go back . . . I’m a rangatira (leader) of my iwi (tribe).

Are you?

Yes, and I’m lost, been homeless for something like 15 years.

The role of relationships in supporting wellbeing

Whilst many participants could articulate what they needed to support their wellbeing, they also relied upon their relationships with trusted supporters to support their access to resources, including food, shelter, financial and legal aid, and health care. This is exemplified by Elijah’s discussion of a time when his addiction and mental and physical health problems meant he was unable to find help by himself: . . . my brain was so f***** up, I didn’t know where to go, who to turn to. But the doors just opened, just opened and these guys opened the doors for me, mmm. And I took, I took everything I could ‘cause I knew I needed it, mmm, mmm.

The need to have a trusting relationship with a supportive person who could help enact their choices at end-of-life was also discussed, with Moana stating that they would want: ‘a person to make sure I’ve got a place, make sure I can survive, like yeah do things. Make sure I eat well. Like yeah so if I had all those things I can do, I wouldn’t be in this mess . . .’.

Participants discussed how this type of support should be offered in a way that supported their mana, or authority. A professional supporter being helpful was described as one who had a non-judgemental approach, was accepting of the person and their circumstances, and made them feel seen and heard. A paramount aspiration was that their personal information was treated carefully, and confidentiality maintained. The two participants who knew a lot about the homeless community with one employed as a peer support worker, shared their lived experience of how difficult it was for street whānau to engage with services when they all required them to share their typically traumatic stories during every new interaction. The need for continuity of care from trusted supporters was evident from diagnosis to death:

The main problem . . . [is] they’ll tell a story to one person, next minute they get passed on and they’ve gotta start all again. That’s a no-no, because they don’t wanna go through that. They’ve been going through that all their lives; they just want somebody to be there with them through the walk, right through the walk to whenever, to the end. And they’re just getting passed around and that’s what happening to a whole lot of people, not just our people [Māori whānau], to everybody. They get passed around.

Pass the buck.

Most participants were clear that they did not want to solely be the recipients of support but also aspired to contribute. Several discussed helping peers in various ways with examples including providing resources and/or advice, and leading fundraising activities for the housing support organisation. Tamanui discussed connecting with young people to prevent them from choosing the same pathway as he had, a form of giving back. His description of this process is rooted in the Māori cultural values of kaitaikitanga (acting as a guardian or protector) through a process of manaakitanga (caring).

I tell whānau, don’t come out here on the streets. It’s not beautiful, it really isn’t. It was beautiful for nothing . . . And this is why I speak about peer support because the next person can help the next person. And it’s so simple. Our whānau used to help, neighbours helping neighbours, families helping families. Just go and help that next neighbour over there, you know?

Within these discussions, there was consistent reflection on the importance of lived experience of homelessness as an enabler of providing support from a place of shared connection and understanding. It was also apparent in the way that participants described actions to support their wellbeing that they do exercise agency, but this was based on relational conditions being present, such as trust, continuity, consistency, and support.

When these conditions were not present and participants experienced interactions that were not respectful and mana-enhancing (engagement which enhances rather than diminishes people’s experiences), agency was not supported. Furthermore, such interactions could be damaging in reducing their trust in ‘the system’ as a whole. Many negative encounters with healthcare were described, ranging from a receptionist being rude and judgemental, to someone with a history of being a victim of violence receiving a gastroscopy without evidence of a trauma-informed approach being adopted. Edward shared his story of refusing follow-up care for Barrett’s oesophagus, which he understood as a pre-cancerous condition:

They wanted me to go back and get one of those gastro things again, I’ve told them to f*** off.

What’s involved in that?

Shoving a tube down your f**ing gob. While you’re conscious.

Oh, okay.

And then they get pissed off ‘cause I fight them, well, you’re trying to choke me man, what the f** am I gonna do? So then they had to sedate me more so they had to rebook it. And no, f** that shit. . . . I’m not gonna go back for another one.

You’re not? Why?

Well, I didn’t like it the first time. I don’t think I’m gonna like it the second. ‘Cause then you need to get someone to pick you up, you’re not allowed to leave by yourself, you’re not allowed to do this or that. And no, that’s just f**ing shit, f** off. No thanks, you know?

Encounters of this nature stopped street whānau seeking healthcare in the future, including when they were living with a chronic, life-limiting condition.

Rongoā (traditional Māori medicines) and alternative therapies were discussed as providing the positive and holistic therapeutic relationship that the Western health system did not. Some Māori participants discussed their healthcare encounters within the context of colonisation, identifying distrust with Western medicine and health professionals as a key barrier to accessing healthcare.

Do you know sis, when he diagnosed me with that emphysema they should have told me what it was. All they did was give me four letters. It’s called ODC something. They only gave me four individual letters. They never actually explained to me. No doctor and no nurses. And no one explained to me how bad my lungs are. They’re like dripping they reckon. Just recently when I went back in. So, I was so weak and apparently my lungs are just like dripping because of the smoking. That’s all I’ve got bub, you know?

So how did you find out? Did you have to go and research?

Well, I waited 11 hours up at the Hospital to be seen. But, they gave me this pill and it just blew me away anyway, because I don’t take white man’s medicine. I’m too used to the old school Māori way.

Discussions of this nature indicated that people were not being given information in a way they understood, nor were the relational conditions present, that supported them to make decisions about their care and treatment, even in the context of chronic life-limiting conditions.

Discussion

This paper explores the end-of-life aspirations of people with lived experience of homelessness. All participants had difficulty articulating their aspirations for end-of-life; a minority could not express aspirations at all. An extreme difficulty in articulating one’s aspirations among people experiencing homelessness who tend to have a poor prognosis even if they have no known health condition can be seen in other research. This largely relates to educational and career aspirations amongst younger people experiencing homelessness and points to a pervasive issue of low aspirations serving as a barrier to achieving wishes and goals. 28 The resulting need to build aspirations among homeless youth as a precursor to supporting entry into education and employment has been recognised. 29 Our findings indicate that a similar approach may be needed for people experiencing homelessness when thinking about planning for end-of-life. Fundamentally, supporting aspirations for living seems a necessary first step in providing opportunities for consideration of aspirations when dying. This might begin by recognising that a difficulty in expressing aspirations is not an inherent personal lack so much as circumstances that make it impossible to achieve.

There may also not be a shared conceptual basis from which to start this conversation. Understandings of ‘good dying’ that permeate palliative care are rooted in the experiences, values, resources, privileges, and preferences of those who are structurally advantaged. 30 People experiencing homelessness therefore experience both ‘hermeneutic injustice’, 13 in that their experiences, values, challenges, and preferences are not represented here, as well as ‘epistemic injustice’, which refers to their views and experiences not being given credibility when they do speak because of the prejudice they experience as a group. Therefore, creating spaces where people can share their own views and experiences in their own language, or through other medium such as art or music, is important to developing a conceptual basis for new approaches to supporting end-of-life care within homeless communities.

No participants in this study were able to share positive experiences of dying for people experiencing homelessness to which they could aspire, a finding consistent with previous research. 5 However, although most participants could not articulate aspirations for end-of-life, many described their current goals, which primarily related to recovery, being well, and (re)connecting with family and whānau. Whilst there was ambivalence as to how achievable these goals were, some reported that they had made progress through their relationships with trusted supporters, such as those working directly with the homeless community, or through their own contributions and support given to others. They were clear that to be of help, supporters had to hold certain values and characteristics. Of note, these characteristics align with Virginia Held’s framing of feminist care ethics, which values those caring activities that uphold ‘sensitivity, trust and mutual concern’ 31 (p. 543). When these relational conditions were present, participants had the potential to make choices and undertake actions to achieve them. As such, our findings point to the value of a relational ethics approach which moves beyond a focus on autonomy as individual towards understanding that we are all interdependent. This interdependence was recognised by our participants not just in the way they received and gave care and support, but also in their acknowledgement of the reciprocal nature of these actions. In line with Joan Tronto’s feminist ethics of care approach, 22 many recognised care as a collective responsibility which is foundational to a successful society, as well as individual wellbeing. These findings lead us to concur with Smith and Stajduhar 32 that relational ethics provides a useful lens to progress the equity agenda in palliative care and develop ethically informed ways of caring for, with, and about highly marginalised people.

The end-of-life aspirations that were discussed in this study related predominantly to meeting basic needs such as warm and dry shelter, food, and access to showers. The goal of being in a safe, non-chaotic space was also raised. All these concerns reflect the situations in which people were living and align with previous research.5,16 They also highlight that the right to be able to meet these basic needs is an invisible privilege or resource upon which the housed population can generally draw. Achieving these basic necessities do not need to be part of end-of-life care considerations for most people. Similarly, many securely-housed people have access to social capital, including the relational conditions necessary, to support their end-of-life care 33 and, in particular, family and whānau who provide the majority of hands-on palliative care. However, the stories from our participants highlight that these often taken-for-granted resources are typically absent for people with lived experience of homelessness. Inter-generational trauma, which for Māori and Pacific populations relates to the ongoing violence of colonisation, means that family and whānau may not have the resources to draw upon to provide support. For some, family and whānau breakdown and bereavement were the catalyst for homelessness. For others, the lived experience of homelessness and, for many, addictions, had led to disrupted family and whānau relationships. Whilst familial relationships within prison, gang and homeless communities were discussed, these groups were typically not themselves adequately resourced to provide significant care and support, as also indicated in previous research. 34 A key finding was that few participants could identify with any certainty who would be able to care for them if they became seriously unwell.

Another form of invisible privilege enjoyed by most of the housed population is the expectation of support from, and a trusting relationship with, health professionals throughout the illness trajectory and at the end-of-life. However, as with previous research, 35 our participants reported few positive interactions with health professionals. Instead, many participants described being re-traumatised when engaging with healthcare professionals, resulting in disengagement with healthcare services. This becomes a difficult issue for people with long term life-limiting conditions where regular contact with a healthcare professional is essential in maintaining a good quality of life for as long as possible. Very few participants were able to describe any experiences where health information was shared by service providers in a way that supported them to feel involved in treatment decision-making. Healthcare settings such as hospitals and hospices were not viewed as safe spaces and numerous negative experiences in both settings were recounted. For example, managing pain is a key goal of palliative care and, as our participants raised, it is important that prescribers have the skills and experience to manage pain within the context of substance use. 36 We therefore support the increasing call for palliative care and addiction specialists to work together, sharing knowledge and skills to ensure that people with histories of trauma who use drugs receive adequate pain management at the end-of-life. 37 Overall, the need for continuity of care from trusted supporters who have the skills to uphold people’s autonomy and maintain their confidentiality throughout the illness trajectory to end-of-life was clear.

Working within a critical theory paradigm supported an ambition to move beyond describing the aspirations and experiences of people with lived experiences of homelessness, to identifying ethically sound actions that could support positive change. 38 To this end, we identified tensions between the aspirations and experiences of people with lived experience of homelessness and ACP which, despite showing mixed effectiveness in improving end-of-life outcomes,39,40 remains a mainstay of palliative and end-of-life care policy internationally.41,42 A recent study has also highlighted that an extended version of ACP may provide a relational, healing space for Māori experiencing homelessness. 43 However, and in line with our previous research with housing key workers, 4 there are fundamental problems regarding the conceptual fit between the assumptions that underpin ACP within mainstream health setttings and the lived experience of people with a lifetime experience of stigma and discrimination (Table 4).

This lack of alignment between assumptions inherent in ACP and the lived experience of homelessness is unsurprising given, as Hickman et al. recognise, ‘the unique needs of historically marginalized populations are not well represented in prior definitions and outcomes identification’. 44 A particular consideration is that ACP is aligned with Western conceptualisations of the ‘good death’, which frame individual autonomy (and assume secure access to the most basic of material and social resources) as central to wellbeing at end-of-life, as evident in policy directives such as this, from NHS England. 45

Planning for future care is an empowering act that allows people to feel confident their wishes will be considered if they are ever unable to fully participate in decision-making. By having good quality conversations about future treatment, people will have a greater sense of control over their ability to live and die well. (p. 3)

Planning for future care is an empowering act that allows people to feel confident their wishes will be considered if they are ever unable to fully participate in decision-making. By having good quality conversations about future treatment, people will have a greater sense of control over their ability to live and die well. (p. 3)

This framing aligns with the argument that lower rates of ACP conversations within structurally disadvantaged communities are a key contributor to poorer end-of-life outcomes. 46 However, this argument does not recognise the pervasive nature of the structural barriers to healthcare access and utilisation identified by our research and the wider literature.17,36 Nor does it address the ethical implications of asking people to make choices as individuals about their preferences for end-of-life care when the relational conditions for these to be achieved are not present, and the formal care systems and their social networks are inadequate to fulfil these preferences. 47 Moreover, none of our participants talked about healthcare as empowering and there is little to suggest that this would change within the context of ACP. This individualistic autonomy focus also fails to recognise the need our participants expressed to have good relationships with trusted supporters to facilitate access to and negotiation of needed resources, including healthcare. A relational autonomy approach 48 fits better with their views and experiences, and is also more in line with the collective nature of end-of-life decision-making within Indigenous cultures 49 and favoured by many older people. 50 All these factors support the argument that ACP initiatives, and the ways in which they are developed, need to be adapted to the unique needs of structurally disadvantaged communities. 51 Fundamentally, more research is also needed to further explore the end-of-life aspirations and experiences of people experiencing homelessness, including the congruence between these and their actual end-of-life experience.

Strengths and limitations

Whilst this study builds on, and adds unique new perspectives about the end-of-life aspirations of people experiencing homelessness, limitations must be acknowledged. In particular, the resources of the study only supported one interview with participants; additional participant engagement would have further illuminated the issues explored.

Conclusion

E moemoeā ana au mō te waka rererangi, engāri kāore au e taea te eke/I dream of the airplane, but I cannot board it.

E moemoeā ana au mō te waka rererangi, engāri kāore au e taea te eke/I dream of the airplane, but I cannot board it.

This whakataukī (proverb), created by S.B. from one of our participant’s words, speaks to the dreams and aspirations whānau participants may have but are unable to access or realise. It also highlights how their priority must be on survival above all else. Within the kupu (word) moemoeā are the concepts of moe – to sleep, to rest or to lie down, and moeā, which refers to the dream state experienced during sleep. It is understandable that people experiencing homelessness cannot articulate their dreams and aspirations when they experience fundamental challenges such as sleep deprivation due to lack of warm, dry, and safe shelter, as well as addictions and other mental and physical health problems. Our findings are clear: supporting these aspirations for living is a necessary first step to consider aspirations when dying for people experiencing structural disadvantage and discrimination.

Overall, our findings indicate that people with lived experience of homelessness experience difficulties in discussing end-of-life issues and aspirations. These go beyond societal discomfort with death and dying and encompass difficulties in holding aspirations in general, as well as the ability to predict future resources, including sources of social support and connection. They also reflect an openness about the inherent uncertainty that characterises the lived experience of homelessness. This is reflected in the end-of-life aspirations that were discussed in this study, which related predominantly to meeting basic needs such as warm and dry shelter, being in a safe, non-chaotic space, and access to food and showers. There were several other reasons identified as to why mainstream approaches to ACP are not suitable for communities experiencing homelessness, indicating a need for new approaches to support agency and mana motuhake (right or condition of self-determination) at the end-of-life. We have highlighted the value of a relational ethics of care approach to frame such approaches, which recognise the interdependency of care relationships and the importance of social context. Such approaches must be developed in partnership with people with lived experience of homelessness and serve to connect homeless communities with social care staff who work in this area, key palliative care providers, and other trusted supporters.
