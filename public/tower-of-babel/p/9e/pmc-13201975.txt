Craniosynostosis is the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures. It can result in various craniofacial deformities. Craniosynostosis can also lead to a progressive increase in intracranial pressure (ICP).1 This condition was traditionally treated via open cranial vault reconstruction. However, endoscope-assisted strip craniectomy with postoperative helmet therapy has emerged as an effective approach. It has short operative times, minimal blood loss, low transfusion rates, and short hospital stays. Cosmetic outcomes have been reported to be similar to or superior to those of open techniques.2,3 However, this minimally invasive approach remains controversial for use in multisuture synostosis, older infants, and suture refusion. We present our experience managing a child with bicoronal, bilambdoid, and sagittal craniosynostosis treated endoscopically with postoperative cranial orthosis, who later developed refusion of the lambdoid suture on one side and was managed by endoscopic re-release.

Illustrative Case

We present the case of a premature male born at 30 2/7 weeks’ gestation, twin pregnancy, weighing 1690 g. His Apgar scores were 7 and 8 at 5 and 10 minutes, respectively. His head circumference was 29.5 cm (Fig. 1). He was referred to our craniofacial team for concerns of abnormal head shape. He appeared scaphocephalic, suggesting sagittal synostosis. A craniosynostosis 65-gene panel was negative, and brain MRI showed only a cavum vergae. After 2 months in the neonatal ICU for respiratory support, he was discharged home on room air and tolerating oral feeds. He followed up in the craniofacial clinic at 3 months of age (1 month corrected). His head shape was now turricephalic, concerning for multisuture synostosis. CT revealed fusion of the bicoronal, bilambdoid, and sagittal sutures (Fig. 2) with multiple bony defects, suggestive of increased ICP (Fig. 3A and B). Endoscopic and open approaches are used at our institution, with endoscopic release favored for younger patients based on our experience and positive results. His family decided to pursue endoscopic release with planned postoperative helmet therapy, which was performed at 2 months of corrected age.

Surgical Technique and Postoperative Course

A single bolus of tranexamic acid (TXA; 50 mg/kg) is given prior to incision for all endoscopic cases. In open approaches with longer surgical time and greater blood loss, a TXA infusion is given. A modified sphinx position was used to allow access to all involved sutures. Navigation was used to mark the squamosal and occipitomastoid sutures with the distorted anatomy to complete the coronal and lambdoid suturectomy releases. Two 4-cm transverse incisions were marked across the midline, the first posterior to the anterior fontanelle and the second at the lambda. Cefazolin (30 mg/kg) was given. A precordial Doppler device is used for all cases to monitor for air emboli. We do not routinely place an arterial or central line, and none were used in this case. A wide subgaleal dissection is completed from the anterior fontanelle to the lambda and laterally to the squamous sutures and asterion along the planned craniectomy trajectories. A 7-mm pediatric craniotome was used to create a burr hole off midline at the lateral edges of each midline incision (four burr holes in total). The right frontal burr hole was used to make a small durotomy with the tip of the No. 11 blade for placement of a subdural ICP monitor (Pressio, Sophysa). The opening ICP was 22 mm Hg (end-tidal CO2 36 mm Hg). A wide sagittal craniectomy (8 × 7 cm) was performed as previously described by Jimenez and Barone,4 but without the barrel stave osteotomies. This allowed wide access to continue endoscopic dissection with the 30° rigid endoscope along the epidural space anteriorly to reach the squamosal sutures on both sides. The strip craniectomies for coronal release (width approximately 5 mm) were performed with a 5-mm Kerrison rongeur (40° up-bite) under direct vision and then completed with a straight Ferris-Smith Spurling rongeur under endoscopic visualization. The endoscope was then used for epidural release from above toward the asterion. A strip craniectomy for lambdoid release (width approximately 5 mm) was completed on both sides with a straight Ferris-Smith Spurling rongeur. External manipulation of the bone showed the frontal, parietal, and occipital bones moved independently postrelease. Hemostasis was achieved at each step by cauterization of the bone edges with an ear, nose, and throat monopolar suction coagulator at 60 W. We then measured the closing ICP through the same durotomy, which was 7 mm Hg (end-tidal CO2 34 mm Hg). Closure was performed with absorbable subgaleal sutures and Steri-Strips. There were no intraoperative complications, and the total surgical time was 171 minutes.

The patient was given packed red blood cells (100 mL) by the anesthesia team in anticipation of prolonged surgical time and blood loss, and this resulted in an elevated postoperative hemoglobin of 16 g/dL (preoperative 11.4 g/dL). This led us to change our practice; we no longer initiate transfusion in multisuture endoscopic cases unless intra- or postoperative clinical or laboratory parameters necessitate transfusion. The decision was made to keep him intubated, given the duration of surgery in the sphinx position and concern for airway swelling, and he was transferred to the pediatric ICU for postoperative management. Extubation was attempted the following day. However, ongoing desaturation requiring stimulation necessitated reintubation. He was found to be rhinovirus positive with copious secretions and was extubated successfully on postoperative day 10. He had a prolonged hospital stay as oxygen was being weaned, had difficulty clearing his secretions, and was discharged home on room air with a nasogastric (NG) tube on postoperative day 41. Our protocol for helmeting is to begin on day 5 postoperatively once surgical swelling has resolved. In this case, it was delayed due to the ongoing hospital admission. The helmet was fitted and delivered 1 month from surgery during his hospital stay to begin postoperative cranial orthosis therapy. The NG tube was successfully removed within a month of discharge.

Clinical Follow-Up and Refusion

The patient was followed closely after discharge. The family was compliant with helmet therapy and attended regular follow-up appointments with our craniofacial team and orthotist. When the patient was due for a second helmet at 6 months of age (uncorrected), unexpected fitting difficulties resulted in a 3-week delay in delivery. Two months later, a posterior asymmetry in which the left side had corrected more than the right was evident on clinical examination and on laser scan anthropometric measurements (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the neosuture along the right lambdoid was no longer palpable. A repeat CT study revealed partial re-refusion of the right lambdoid suture (Fig. 3C). All other sutures remained patent. After a thorough discussion with the family regarding treatment options, he underwent endoscopic re-release of the right lambdoid suture, with the hope that it would lead to continued growth in that vector and improvement in asymmetry, obviating the need for a posterior vault reconstruction. Endoscopic Re-Release

In the second surgery, the positioning and preoperative setup were similar to the first. We used navigation in this case, given his multiple bony defects, to help guide a strip craniectomy trajectory that would release the occipital from the parietal bone without leaving any restrictive bony bridges that may lead to failure of the procedure. Only the posterior midline incision was opened, and subgaleal dissection was performed along the right fused lambdoid to reach the bony defect above the asterion. A preexisting cranial defect was identified, and opening pressure was measured per our protocol using an ICP monitor at 8 mm Hg (end-tidal CO2 38 mm Hg). The area of fusion was identified, and epidural dissection was performed through the preexisting cranial defects using the 30° endoscope. Rongeurs and Mayo scissors were used to recreate the neosuture. Mobility of the cranial bones was confirmed, and hemostasis was achieved. We did not encounter any significant scarring during the dissection from the first procedure. The closing ICP was measured at 3 mm Hg (end-tidal CO2 36 mm Hg). Surgical time was 137 minutes; no blood transfusion was required.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative course. He was discharged home the following day and continued helmet therapy on postoperative day 4. At the time of this writing, it has been 1 year since his re-release. His head circumference continues to increase on his curve (Fig. 1), and his posterior and anterior asymmetry continues to improve (Fig. 4). Cognitively and developmentally, he is progressing appropriately and achieving milestones similarly to his unaffected twin. Clinical photographs (Fig. 5) and laser scan data (Fig. 4) demonstrate marked changes following his re-release at 7 months of age.

Discussion

Endoscopic release for craniosynostosis has been shown to be a safe and effective treatment.5 When compared to open techniques, it has lower blood transfusion rates and complications, less operative time, and shorter hospital stays with comparable results.6–8 However, the literature reporting its use in multisuture craniosynostosis remains limited,9–11 and hesitation in its application is evident in one of these studies, which states that an endoscopic approach does not constitute a first-choice technique, despite reported good outcomes.9 It is perhaps not unexpected that if surgeons do not routinely use endoscopic approaches for single-suture craniosynostoses that present at a young age, many are unlikely to consider endoscopic release an option for multisuture craniosynostosis. A 2021 publication on craniosynostosis guidelines11 cautions against the use of endoscopic release for bicoronal craniosynostosis, citing a single study reporting high reoperative rates.12 This unfortunately does not take into account other reports of good outcomes with endoscopic release and potential confounding factors such as surgical technique and helmeting duration.10

Surgical Approach and Reoperation Rates

Endoscopic release for craniosynostosis is a more minimally invasive approach to managing this disease, but certain tenets should be followed. The primary objective is to achieve release from a patent suture to a patent suture to allow appropriate growth along that vector. Any remaining bony islands will continue to restrict growth. The size of the craniectomy is not standardized and varies among surgeons. At our institution, we perform a wide vertex craniectomy for sagittal synostosis that is approximately 6–7 cm wide in very young infants and narrows to approximately 3–4 cm in older infants, as described by Jimenez and Barone.13 For metopic, coronal, and lambdoid synostosis, we perform a narrow approximately 5-mm-wide strip craniectomy with good results14 as described by Jimenez et al.15,16 This is performed posterior to the fused coronal and anterior to the fused lambdoid suture, which is often displaced and indented. We find that creating a new osteotomy is simpler and achieves good correction of the deformity. Whether a wider craniectomy in this case might not have led to refusion along the lambdoid suture is difficult to answer. Based on our experience with this technique and the positive outcomes, we have not changed our approach, and we believe additional factors were likely involved in this case.

Reoperation rates vary among reports using this technique in multisuture craniosynostosis. One study of 10 infants reported that 1 patient who did not achieve adequate orbital coverage at 12 months of age underwent reoperation.17 Another small series of 7 patients with multisuture craniosynostosis achieved optimal results for all children.9 They did not require any further surgeries during a mean follow-up of 20 months. Perhaps the highest reoperation rate is that reported by Rottgers et al. among 18 children with bicoronal craniosynostosis.12 Six later underwent fronto-orbital advancement (1 nonsyndromic and 5 syndromic) at a mean of 15 months from their endoscopic release.12 A case series by Jimenez and Barone including 21 children with multisuture craniosynostosis, with a mean follow-up of 61 months, did not require a secondary procedure.10 We believe the primary factor influencing these rates, in the absence of early refusion and observed ongoing improvement, is the “early failure” diagnosis. In our experience, we continue to see improvement several years after endoscopic treatment, and caution is warranted if the trend continues to normalize during follow-up. However, certain combinations of sutures, genetic predispositions, and syndromic associations, as well as technique and helmeting, likely play important roles in these variable rates of reoperation.

Risk of Suture Refusion After Surgical Release

In our current case, the clinical examination and laser head scans clearly showed worsening asymmetry, leading to appropriate imaging and diagnosis of the refusion. The laser scans of our patient were a useful method for following head growth vectors in all directions and for identifying the plagiocephaly that reoccurred after refusion (Fig. 4). CT of the head at that time helped show the bony gaps at the coronal and left lambdoid regions, as well as neosuture formation along the course of the sagittal suture. Neosuture formation may occur in 24%–40% of children after endoscopic release.18,19 The presence of a gap or neosuture several months after surgery may correlate with improved correction.20 Pathological versus normal bone formation along the craniectomy site early on can be difficult to separate. To make that distinction, we rely on clinical examination, imaging evidence of gap or neosuture formation, appropriate head growth, and laser scans to demonstrate improvement along the desired trajectory or identify stasis/regression.

The causes of refusion are likely multifactorial. Patients with multisuture craniosynostoses may be at higher risk of refusion than those with single-suture synostosis.21,22 Additionally, the prolonged supine positioning and delayed helmet therapy during his hospital stay may have contributed to bone formation along the lambdoid neosuture, leading to early refusion. Our protocol is to begin helmet therapy on postoperative day 5. Others report various helmeting timelines, including 2 days,23 within 1 week,24 and 2 weeks postoperatively.25 The type of suture involved may also play a role. One study found that bilambdoid involvement was independently associated with secondary craniosynostosis. However, their numbers were too small for a definitive correlation.26 Another study involving endoscopic release for unicoronal craniosynostosis in 17 children found good improvement in phenotype in those who continued to have a gap or neosuture evident at follow-up (range 7.5–41 months) and poor improvement in those who developed early bony refusion (2 children).20 In a cohort of 67 children with single-suture craniosynostosis treated with suturectomy and short postoperative orthosis (3–5 months), reoperation was needed in 10 children, 7 of whom had unicoronal involvement (total 12) for a 58% failure rate during helmet therapy.27 This rate is extremely high. The fusion always occurred along the superior temporal line, which the authors hypothesized may be due to the helmet promoting bone growth along this vector, and therefore they no longer use this technique for unicoronal synostosis. Unfortunately, we believe this last study shows the importance of proper helmet therapy, as these failure results have not been reported by others,20,28–30 and that close follow-up is required for early identification of refusion, as outcomes are likely to remain suboptimal or poor in these cases.

We considered endoscopic release for the refused lambdoid suture in this case based on our experience with this technique and the ongoing improvement in cranial asymmetry in older infants.14 While many have considered the only option to be an open approach at this age, we show here the ongoing improvement achieved with an endoscopic release for an early refusion.

Craniosynostosis and ICP

The other significant finding in this case was the ICP at the second surgery, which remained within normal parameters (8 mm Hg) 5 months after his original surgery, where the opening pressure was high at 22 mm Hg. Intraoperative ICP monitoring is standard for all craniosynostosis cases at our institution. We find this information informative to the surgeon, family, and ophthalmologist regarding the ICP state at surgery and for consideration during follow-up. The high opening and normal closing ICPs help confirm the adequacy of the release and guide closer follow-up postoperatively. The normal ICP found during the second surgery helped show that we were dealing with a deformity recurrence without a return to elevated ICP. Multisuture craniosynostosis carries a higher risk of increased ICP than single-suture involvement.1,31,32 Open and endoscopic techniques have been shown to successfully decrease ICP intraoperatively.31–33 What has been unclear is the long-term control of ICP after these approaches. This case report cannot provide that answer by the nature of its single outcome, but we have not identified clinical indicators of increased ICP in the majority of our children treated with endoscopic release, and the current finding is a point of data supporting that ICP likely remains within normal, at least early on during follow-up. Although our anesthesia protocol and positioning are standardized for each suture, other factors, such as end-tidal CO2, head position, dural tears, CSF leaks, and duration of monitoring, which can affect ICP readings, should be considered. In our case, end-tidal CO2 was relatively stable, and no dural tears or CSF leaks were encountered.

Observations

Our case report appears to be the first to demonstrate successful endoscopic re-release of a refused synostosed suture following primary endoscopic suturectomy in a child with multisuture craniosynostosis with ongoing normalization of ICP.

Lessons

Endoscopic release for multisuture craniosynostosis was effective in reducing cranial asymmetry and decreasing ICP in our patient. Close clinical follow-up with laser scans helped identify any halt or regression in directional cranial growth, which indicated refusion, and appropriate imaging provided further anatomical details. Endoscopic re-release corrected the secondary deformity that occurred in this case. Communication between the surgical and anesthesia teams is important for monitoring blood loss and intervening appropriately and promptly, especially in young infants.
