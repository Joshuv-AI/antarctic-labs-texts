Codeine-induced pancreatitis in a previously Cholecystectomized patient: double the trouble
Authors: Karam Karam, Houssein Chebbo, Sarah Saleh, Karim Al Halabi, Philippe Attieh, Sarah Jalloul, Pierre Hani, Elias Fiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe causality between codeine and acute pancreatitis has not yet been fully deciphered in medical literature. Codeine-induced pancreatitis is a rare entity and the underlying mechanism of action mediated by codeine has not been completely elucidated. One plausible theory is that codeine increases the pressure of the sphincter of Oddi (SOD), which results in spasms and subsequently acute pancreatitis ensues. Codeine-induced pancreatitis has a predilection for patients with a previous surgical history of cholecystectomy. We herein present a case of a 74-year-old male patient presenting for severe epigastric pain radiating to the back after codeine intake for tension headache. His past surgical history was pertinent for a previous cholecystectomy that was done 3 years ago after which he reported no biliary-related symptoms. Upon detailed anamnesis, the patient reported that his pain was directly preceded by the intake of an analgesic medication for his tension headache. The painkiller consisted of 500 mg of acetaminophen, 8 mg of codeine and 30 mg of caffeine. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis was performed and revealed a prominent pancreatic head with surrounding edema suggestive of acute pancreatitis. Codeine-induced pancreatitis is an uncommon entity. However, it has been documented in medical literature with a preponderance to patients who underwent previous cholecystectomy. Multiple pathophysiological elements come into play, such as decreased bile storage capacity and aberrant nervous wiring at the level of the SOD muscularity. These alterations result in increased SOD pressure and acute pancreatitis ensues. Nonetheless, this article should trigger future studies to further elucidate the causality between codeine and acute pancreatitis.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is medically defined as the inflammation of the pancreas. Three main elements characterize the diagnosis of AP: severe and worsening abdominal pain often radiating to the back, elevated pancreatic enzymes three times greater than the upper limit of normal and imaging findings peculiar of acute pancreatitis [1]. Gallstones and alcohol are the most common etiologies of acute pancreatitis [2]. Drug-induced pancreatitis follows gallstones and alcohol in commonality [3]. The prevalence of drug-induced pancreatitis is around 3.6% whereby azathioprine, hydrocholorothiazide and doxycycline are the likely culprits [4]. This issue merits special attention as codeine is deemed to have a causal relationship with acute pancreatitis, specifically in patients who previously underwent surgical removal of the gallbladder [5]. The physiological rationale as to why codeine might have different effects post-cholecystectomy is attributed to codeine-induced potentiation of muscular fibrosis and smooth muscle hyperplasisa at the level of the sphincter of Oddi. This case underscores the causal relationship between codeine intake and acute pancreatitis in a previously cholecystectomized patient.

Case presentation

We describe a case of a 74-year-old male patient presenting for severe unrelenting epigastric pain radiating to the back. His symptoms began 24 hours prior to presentation to the emergency department. Patient had hypertension controlled by lisinopril 5 mg once a day. The physical exam was pertinent for a remarkable epigastric tenderness. Patient was unable to sit still while performing the physical examination due to his lancinating pain. The patient was afebrile with a temperature of 36.7°C, a blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg, an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air and a body mass index of 23.7 Kg/m2. His past surgical history was pertinent for a previous cholecystectomy that was done 3 years ago after which he reported no biliary-related symptoms. Upon detailed anamnesis, the patient reported that his pain was directly preceded by the intake of an analgesic medication for his tension headache. The painkiller consisted of 500 mg of acetaminophen, 8 mg of codeine and 30 mg of caffeine. He was prescribed the painkiller at a dosage of two tablets every 8 hours as needed. He stated that he took the very first two tablets after which his symptoms surfaced. On biochemical work-up, his pancreatic enzymes were elevated: amylase [1507 U/l (27–131)], and lipase [2100 U/l (8–78)]. Complete blood count was normal including a white blood count of 7000 per microliter, a hemoglobin level of 14.5 g/dl and a hematocrit of 45%. Serum calcium was 8.8 mg/dl, serum glucose was 88 mg/dl and triglycerides level was 133 mg/dl. Aspartate aminotransferase level was 33 U/l, alanine transaminase level was 35 U/l, gamma-glutamyl transferase level was 39 U/l, alkaline phosphatase level was 77 IU/l, and a total bilirubin of 0.7 mg/dl with a direct bilirubin of 0.29 mg/dl. The remainder of blood tests and electrocardiogram were normal. The patient stated that his epigastric pain started around 1 hour following codeine intake. His pain subsequently worsened in severity with a 10/10 intensity and lancinating quality. Ultrasound of the abdomen was performed and revealed no stones in the common bile duct (CBD) and absence of intra and extra-hepatic biliary ductal dilatations. Patient was non-alcoholic and a non-smoker. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis was performed and revealed a prominent pancreatic head with surrounding edema suggestive of acute pancreatitis. No collections or findings of peripancreatic necrosis were noted. The main pancreatic duct was not dilated. The pancreatic body and tail were unremarkable (Fig. 1). The CT scan did not reveal any anatomical defects in the abdominal wall structures, tumors or images suggestive of periampullary neoplasms.

Supportive care was initiated whereby oral feeding was stopped and patient was kept nil per os (NPO). Symptomatic treatment was started including fluid resuscitation and pain control.

The patient was started on fluid replacement with intravenous normal saline 0.9% at a rate of 250 ml/hour.

His pain decreased gradually during his hospital stay and subsided completely 72 hours following supportive care. His serum amylase and lipase normalized within 48 hours of hospitalization. In the absence of ileus, nausea or vomiting, oral feeding was reinitiated gradually, and it was well-tolerated. The patient was eventually discharged home after a full recovery and complete pain resolution on day 3 post-hospital admission. His hospital stay was uneventful.

Discussion

A myriad of mechanisms has been proposed regarding codeine-related acute pancreatitis. Codeine increases the pressure of SOD resulting in sphincteric spasms and ultimately the manifestation of acute pancreatitis [1]. What is striking is the fact that codeine-induced pancreatitis is solely described in patients whose gallbladders had been removed. In light of this, our patient underwent cholecystectomy 3 years ago, which further consolidates the aforementioned statement. A plausible adopted theory posits that patients who underwent cholecystectomy have a decreased bile storage capacity [3]. Therefore, a larger volume of bile accumulates into the common bile duct. Thus, this process raises the intra-ductal pressure of the CBD [6]. Another explanation is that a previous cholecystectomy triggers an alteration of SOD motility likely because the nerves relaying the gallbladder and SOD go awry. Such erroneous nervous wiring engenders fibrosis and smooth muscle hyperplasia of SOD and results in sphincteric stenosis [5]. Hence, SOD pressure increases, and spasm ensues. In light of this, codeine-induced pancreatitis is almost never reported in uncholecystectomized patients owing to the intact reservoir capacity of the gallbladder.

The mechanism linking codeine to acute pancreatitis remains hypothesized rather than definitively established, with evidence largely derived from case reports and observational studies. Opioids such as codeine are known to increase sphincter of Oddi basal and phasic pressures, which in turn can impede pancreatic duct outflow and elevate ductal pressure, a recognized contributor to pancreatitis in animal models and suggested in human studies of SOD. Experimental work demonstrates that induced SO obstruction combined with stimulated pancreatic secretion can produce acute pancreatitis in animal models, supporting the concept that elevated outflow resistance may play a role in disease pathogenesis [7, 8]. In humans, multiple case reports have documented acute pancreatitis occurring within hours of codeine ingestion, including cases with positive rechallenge, implicating SO spasm as the likely trigger, particularly in patients with prior cholecystectomy who lack a gallbladder reservoir and may be predisposed to transient SO dysfunction [9, 10]. A large retrospective study also found that codeine use was associated with a higher risk of hospitalization for acute pancreatitis compared with non-use or NSAIDs in patients post-cholecystectomy [3]. While these observations support a pathophysiological link between opioid-induced SO pressure changes and pancreatitis, definitive mechanistic proof in humans is lacking, and alternative pathways cannot be excluded.

To date, only a limited number of case reports have documented codeine-induced acute pancreatitis, underscoring both its rarity and clinical relevance (Table 1). A recent comprehensive table of published cases identified at least 14 individual reports of acute pancreatitis temporally associated with codeine ingestion. Among these, a series of 4 cases in the same report occurred exclusively in patients with prior cholecystectomy, and subsequent individual case reports include additional instances in cholecystectomized patients, bringing the total with documented cholecystectomy to at least 9–10 cases. In contrast, only a very small number of reports (e.g. 1 clearly documented case) describe codeine-induced pancreatitis in patients without a history of cholecystectomy. This distribution suggests that while codeine-induced pancreatitis is rare overall, it appears to be reported predominantly in post-cholecystectomy patients, possibly reflecting an increased susceptibility in this subgroup [10].

Despite its rarity, codeine-induced pancreatitis carries important clinical implications given the widespread use of codeine-containing analgesics, as early recognition and avoidance particularly in previously cholecystectomized patients can prevent recurrent and potentially serious pancreatic injury.
