Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a cerebrovascular disorder of unknown etiology characterized by progressive steno-occlusive changes at the terminal of the internal carotid arteries (ICAs) and formation of fragile collateral vessels.1,2 The superficial temporal artery (STA)–middle cerebral artery (MCA) bypass with indirect bypass, pial synangiosis, is the generally performed procedure to improve hemodynamic insufficiency.3–6 Although this procedure is safe and effective,3,7 de novo aneurysm formation at or near the anastomotic site is an extremely rare complication.8–12

The etiology, prognosis, and optimal management of this rare complication are largely unknown. Only 5 cases8–12 of de novo aneurysm formation after revascularization in MMD patients have been reported; among these, 2 patients experienced rupture from the aneurysm, both resulting in a fatal intracerebral hemorrhage and severe neurological sequelae.11,12 These poor clinical outcomes resulting from a de novo aneurysm highlight the importance of establishing appropriate preventive treatment strategies.

In most of the reported cases,8,11 direct microsurgical procedures were performed. However, this traditional approach poses technical challenges: reopening the skin, muscle, skull, and dura mater carries the risk of injuring already developed pial synangiosis and disrupting established collaterals. On the other hand, an endovascular approach, using sophisticated modern endovascular devices and techniques, could theoretically be a better therapeutic option, as it can preserve existing collateral circulation. Despite this significant advantage, there have been no reports of de novo aneurysms after STA-MCA anastomosis being treated endovascularly.

In this report, we present the rare case of a de novo aneurysm arising at the STA-MCA anastomotic site in a patient with MMD. The patient was successfully treated with an endovascular approach for the first time. The procedure required an unusual access route via the bypass donor STA, which necessitated specific procedural considerations. This case demonstrates that this novel strategy can be a viable treatment option for similarly rare cases of clinically significant complications of MMD.

Illustrative Case

History

A 53-year-old man with a family history of MMD had undergone left-sided revascularization 14 years earlier, consisting of a STA-MCA anastomosis combined with encephalo-duro-myo-synangiosis. He was followed annually in the outpatient clinic with MRI and remained asymptomatic. Five years after surgery, MR angiography (Fig. 1A) detected a small de novo aneurysm measuring 2 mm in diameter, adjacent to the left STA-MCA anastomosis site. Given the small size of the aneurysm and uncertainty regarding the natural course of this rare condition, we continued conservative follow-up. However, during follow-up, the aneurysm gradually enlarged: by 10 years after surgery, it had increased in size (Fig. 1B); by 14 years, it had reached approximately 7 mm with daughter sac formation (Fig. 1C), although the patient remained asymptomatic. Vessel wall imaging with gadolinium enhancement revealed that the aneurysm was embedded in the brain surface with circumferential wall enhancement (Fig. 1D, right panel, arrow), suggesting a risk of further enlargement or rupture.13 Cerebral angiography demonstrated a de novo aneurysm arising at the left STA-MCA anastomosis (Fig. 2A, arrow). Three-dimensional rotational angiography (Fig. 2B) confirmed the aneurysm measured 6.5 × 4.9 mm with a neck width of 2.6 mm. The aneurysm was assessed as having a relatively narrow neck and was also considered to have arisen from hemodynamic stress where STA inflow directly impinged on the recipient vessel wall of the MCA cortical branch. The donor STA (Fig. 2A and B, arrowheads), which could potentially serve as an access route for the endovascular approach, was well developed. The lumen diameter of the STA (approximately 3.5 mm) was sufficient for navigating the microcatheter and distal access catheter for endovascular treatment.

Endovascular Treatment

Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin (100 mg) and prasugrel (3.75 mg) was initiated 2 weeks prior to the intervention. Under general anesthesia and with systemic heparinization and motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring, a 5-Fr guiding sheath (ASAHI FUBUKI Dilator KIT, Asahi Intecc Co., Ltd.) was navigated and positioned in the left external carotid artery via the right femoral approach. Continuous perfusion of heparinized saline containing nitroglycerin (5 mg/L) was maintained into the access route throughout the procedure, to prevent vasospasm or kinking, which could result in insufficient bypass flow and access failure. Despite the marked tortuosity of the STA, a 3.4-Fr distal access catheter (GuidePost, Tokai Medical Products) could be advanced distally into the STA to provide a stable support for microcatheter manipulation (Fig 2C, arrow). The microcatheter (Excelsior SL-10, Stryker Neurovascular) was then navigated into the aneurysm sac, and coil embolization was initiated using a 4.5-mm framing coil (Fig. 2D). Five detachable coils (29.0 cm in length) were deployed to achieve favorable intra-aneurysmal packing (Fig. 2E). Postprocedural angiography (Fig. 2F) demonstrated that the aneurysm had been completely obliterated, with flow preservation in both the STA and MCA.

Postoperative Course

The postoperative course was uneventful. The dual antiplatelet therapy was switched to aspirin monotherapy immediately after the procedure and continued for 1 month. The patient was discharged 7 days after the procedure. At the 6-month follow-up, cerebral angiography (Fig. 3A and B) demonstrated complete obliteration of the aneurysm. Moreover, both conventional time-of-flight MR angiography (Fig. 3C) and silent MR angiography (Fig. 3D) performed at 1 year postoperatively demonstrated that there is no evidence of radiographic recanalization.

Discussion

We report a rare case of a de novo aneurysm at the STA-MCA anastomosis after revascularization for MMD, successfully treated with endovascular coiling—the first such case described. Because the endovascular approach can completely preserve established pial synangiosis, it represents a safe and effective therapeutic option for this uncommon but clinically significant complication in MMD.

Observations

De novo aneurysm formation at the STA-MCA anastomosis after revascularization for MMD is exceptionally rare, with only 5 cases reported previously.8–12 Including the present case, clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among the reported cases, 2 patients presented with rupture, both resulting in a fatal intracerebral hemorrhage and severe neurological sequelae.11,12 In contrast, 3 asymptomatic cases, including ours, underwent preventive treatment, and all achieved favorable outcomes.8,10 This high morbidity in ruptured cases, contrasted with favorable results in preventively treated cases, highlights the importance of timely and appropriate preventive intervention. Interestingly, one conservatively managed case demonstrated spontaneous regression of the aneurysm,9 most likely representing a pseudoaneurysm secondary to surgical manipulation, which may explain its distinct clinical course.9 In clinical practice, differentiating a pseudoaneurysm from a true aneurysm, as seen in the presented case, can be challenging. However, careful evaluation of the clinical course and morphology, as well as advanced imaging techniques such as vessel wall imaging, may help to make this distinction. This is crucial for determining the optimal therapeutic strategy for this complex situation. The reported interval between revascularization and aneurysm treatment has ranged widely, from 6 months to 20 years, with aneurysm sizes varying from 4 to 26 mm. This heterogeneity suggests that such aneurysms may develop at any timing after revascularization and may require intervention. Based on our historical series of 665 MMD patients who underwent combined revascularization by a single surgeon (M.F.) since 2004, with annual MRI surveillance, de novo aneurysm formation (> 5 mm) at the anastomotic site was observed in 3 cases, corresponding to an estimated incidence of 0.5%. Although the natural history of such aneurysms remains uncertain, these findings highlight the importance of long-term follow-up in patients with MMD after surgery.

Regarding treatment, all previously reported cases were managed microsurgically, by clipping or aneurysm resection. In the setting of revascularized MMD, therapeutic planning should account for the preservation of established collateral circulation, particularly pial synangiosis. Aburakawa et al.8 reported a de novo aneurysm 11 years after revascularization, successfully treated with navigation-guided microsurgery to preserve pial synangiosis as much as possible, underscoring its importance. With recent advances in endovascular devices and techniques, the endovascular approach offers a valuable alternative in this complex situation, as it allows complete preservation of pial synangiosis. When anatomically feasible, this novel strategy could therefore be considered a promising first-line option for similar cases.

In our case, the aneurysm was a narrow-neck saccular lesion, which was suitable for simple coil embolization without adjunctive techniques. Moreover, gradual maturation of the bypass flow resulted in a well-developed donor STA (approximately 3.5-mm lumen diameter), providing a favorable access route for coiling. However, unlike conventional access routes such as the ICA or vertebral artery, the STA is highly tortuous and lacks osseous support, predisposing it to kinking and vasospasm. To mitigate these risks, intraoperative MEP monitoring was used to detect reduced bypass flow, and continuous perfusion with vasodilator-containing saline was helpful. Endovascular coiling is a valuable option for treating this rare aneurysm; however, careful patient selection and meticulous procedural planning are essential to ensure its safety and efficacy.

The precise pathogenesis underlying de novo aneurysm formation at the STA-MCA anastomotic sites in patients with MMD remains unclear. Although increased hemodynamic stress from bypass flow is reported as the key trigger of aneurysm formation,12 it seems that intrinsic vascular fragility in MMD patients also plays a key role.10,14 Histopathological studies15,16 have demonstrated degradation and loss of smooth muscle cells in the media, resulting in thinning not only at the terminal ICA, but also in the peripheral MCA. Together with waviness and duplication of the internal elastic lamina,15,16 this pathological change makes intracranial arteries more sensitive to the hemodynamic stress. Furthermore, disease progression in MMD, characterized by gradual shifts in intracranial perfusion toward the external carotid artery system,17,18 may also play a significant role. As disease progresses, bypass vessels are known to enlarge,19 thereby increasing hemodynamic stress and contributing to aneurysm formation and growth. However, similar anastomotic aneurysms have been reported10,20 following STA-MCA bypass in patients with atherosclerotic ICA or MCA occlusion. This implies that, in addition to factors specific in MMD, other mechanisms, such as surgical injury, focal inflammation or infection, and genetic predisposition, may also contribute to the formation of de novo aneurysms.

Lessons

De novo aneurysm formation at the STA-MCA anastomosis site after revascularization for MMD is an extremely rare but clinically significant complication. With advances in endovascular techniques, this approach has become a valuable therapeutic option, offering the advantage of preserving established pial synangiosis. Optimal patient selection and meticulous procedural planning based on morphological assessment of the aneurysm and related vasculature are essential to ensure the safety and efficacy of endovascular coiling.
