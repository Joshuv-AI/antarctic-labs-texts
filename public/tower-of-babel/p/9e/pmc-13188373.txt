Thoracic myosteatosis and adipose tissue in COPD: associations of muscle quality and CT biomarkers with GOLD classification
Authors: Murathan Koksal, Cem Kolsuz, Emine Arguder AYBU Tip Fakultesi, Bahadir Benli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterized by airflow limitation and systemic manifestations including changes in body composition. This study aimed to evaluate the association between thoracic muscle quality, adipose tissue distribution, and pulmonary function using computed tomography (CT) biomarkers in patients with COPD.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted on 130 patients diagnosed with COPD at a tertiary care facility. Non-contrast chest CT scans were analyzed to quantify thoracic skeletal muscle area, skeletal muscle density, subcutaneous adipose tissue, and intramuscular adipose tissue. Spirometric data including forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and FEV1/FVC ratio were collected. Statistical analyses included Pearson correlation coefficients to assess relationships between CT biomarkers and lung function, and one-way ANOVA to compare imaging parameters across different stages of disease severity based on the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) classification.ResultsHigher skeletal muscle area and density were positively correlated with FEV1 and FVC values (p < 0.01). In contrast, increased subcutaneous and intramuscular adipose tissue were significantly associated with lower FEV1 and FVC (p < 0.01). There were statistically significant differences in thoracic body composition measures across GOLD severity stages (p < 0.05), with advanced stages showing reduced muscle mass and greater fat accumulation.ConclusionThoracic CT-derived body composition markers are strongly associated with pulmonary function in individuals with COPD. Muscle quality and fat distribution serve as important indicators of disease severity and may enhance clinical evaluation and risk stratification beyond conventional indices such as body mass index. Incorporating CT-based biomarkers into routine assessment may facilitate more personalized disease monitoring and management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04259-2.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic inflammatory responses in the airways and lung parenchyma, most commonly caused by tobacco smoke and environmental pollutants [1]. It represents a major public health burden, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 390 million individuals in 2019, particularly affecting populations in South-East Asia and the Western Pacific [2].

Beyond pulmonary impairment, COPD is increasingly recognized as a systemic disease with important extrapulmonary manifestations, particularly alterations in body composition. These include loss of skeletal muscle mass, deterioration of muscle quality, and abnormal fat distribution, which contribute to reduced exercise tolerance, poorer quality of life, and increased mortality [3–6]. Importantly, these changes are not adequately captured by body mass index (BMI), as BMI cannot distinguish between lean mass and adipose tissue and therefore fails to reflect the complex body composition alterations seen in COPD [4].

Sarcopenia, defined as a generalized and progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, is a clinically relevant syndrome, commonly assessed using whole-body indices such as skeletal muscle index (SMI), calculated as skeletal muscle mass (kg) divided by height squared (m²) [7]. Reduced SMI and appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMMI) have been associated with worse pulmonary function, exercise intolerance, and adverse clinical outcomes in COPD, as demonstrated both in our cohort and in previous studies [8]. In contrast, computed tomography (CT)-derived muscle parameters including skeletal muscle area (SMA), skeletal muscle density (SMD), and intramuscular adipose tissue (IMAT) provide regional, imaging-based measures of muscle quantity and quality [9]. Muscle wasting and increased myosteatosis are associated with lower functional status, higher morbidity, and increased mortality in COPD [10], and reduced SMI has been shown to independently predict functional impairment and poorer quality of life [11].

Myosteatosis specifically refers to lipid infiltration within skeletal muscle fibers and is considered a marker of impaired muscle quality rather than generalized adipose tissue dysfunction [12]. Although myosteatosis and abnormal adipose tissue distribution may coexist in COPD, they are biologically distinct entities driven by overlapping but not identical inflammatory and metabolic pathways and should not be interpreted interchangeably [13].

CT-based assessment of thoracic muscles, including the paraspinal, pectoralis, and intercostal muscles, has gained increasing attention as a tool for evaluating COPD severity and outcomes. Imaging biomarkers such as muscle cross-sectional area (CSA), SMI, and muscle attenuation are commonly used; however, substantial heterogeneity exists across studies. Measurements are performed at different vertebral levels (e.g., T4, T12, or L3), alternatively derived from whole-body techniques such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) [4, 9, 14]. Variability in segmentation methods, density thresholds, and inclusion of IMAT further limits comparability, underscoring the need for standardized assessment protocols [10, 12].

Spirometry remains the cornerstone of COPD diagnosis and severity assessment, with post-bronchodilator measurements of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), and the FEV₁/FVC ratio serving as validated and reproducible clinical markers [15]. This study focuses exclusively on spirometric indices as spirometry is universally available, directly linked to GOLD classification, and most commonly used in routine clinical practice and outcome prediction, thereby enhancing clinical relevance and comparability [16].

Despite growing evidence linking CT-derived muscle quantity and quality to pulmonary function, data correlating thoracic CT-based body composition metrics with spirometric parameters across COPD severity stages remain limited. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between CT-derived thoracic muscle and adipose tissue biomarkers specifically SMA, SMD, and IMAT and spirometric measures of lung function (FEV₁%, FVC%, and FEV₁/FVC) in patients with COPD. We hypothesized that reduced muscle quantity and increased myosteatosis would be independently associated with greater airflow limitation, providing clinically meaningful imaging-based markers of disease severity beyond conventional anthropometric measures.

Methods

Data collection

This study was designed as a retrospective observational analysis conducted at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, a tertiary care center. Electronic medical records were retrospectively reviewed, and patients were consecutively selected from all individuals diagnosed with COPD between January 2019 and December 2023 who had available non-contrast chest CT examinations. All eligible patients meeting predefined inclusion criteria were included, and no selective sampling was performed, ensuring representation across GOLD stages 1–4 and GOLD groups A-D.

The cohort included both newly diagnosed patients and those under long-term follow-up. Chest CT scans and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were obtained during clinically stable periods not during acute exacerbations. PFTs were performed as close as possible to CT acquisition, (typically within ± 3 months) to ensure physiologic consistency. Although detailed medication data (e.g., systemic corticosteroid exposure) were inconsistently available due to the retrospective design, the use of stable-phase measurements likely minimized acute treatment effects on body composition.

Healthy controls were not included, as the primary objective was to evaluate within-COPD body composition changes relative to disease severity, rather than comparisons with healthy populations. Chest CT examinations were performed exclusively for clinical indications, including COPD assessment, evaluation of exacerbations, and exclusion of comorbid pulmonary conditions. Screening of asymptomatic CT scans were excluded, reducing selection bias.

Collected variables included demographic data (age, sex, height, weight, BMI), clinical data (smoking history, respiratory symptoms, mMRC dyspnea score), spirometric parameters (FVC, FEV₁, FEV₁/FVC, FEF25–75), and GOLD staging. Serum lipid profiles were unavailable and not analyzed. Patients were classified using GOLD 2022 or GOLD 2023 criteria, depending on the guideline in effect at evaluation; spirometric thresholds for GOLD stages remained unchanged, ensuring consistent disease severity classification.

Retrospective CT analysis yielded the following body composition parameters: skeletal muscle area (SMA), skeletal muscle index (SMI), skeletal muscle density (SMD), intramuscular adipose tissue (IMAT), subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), IMAT-to-SMA ratio (IMAT/SMA), SAT index (SATI), IMAT index (IMATI), skeletal muscle gauge (SMG), and intramuscular adipose tissue content (IMAC), which were analyzed in relation to pulmonary function.

Sample size

A total of 130 patients with confirmed COPD who underwent non-contrast chest CT were included. As an exploratory retrospective study, all eligible patients during the study period were analyzed, and no a priori power calculation was performed.

Imaging protocol and analysis

CT scans were acquired using a 128-slice multidetector CT scanner (Revolution EVO, GE Healthcare) and analyzed using Thoracic VCAR software (Advantage Workstation 4.7, GE Healthcare). Quantitative measurements were performed at the T12 vertebral level.

Hounsfield units (HU) represent a standardized scale for radiodensity on CT imaging. Tissue segmentation used validated HU thresholds [17–19]:

Skeletal muscle: −29 to + 150 HUIMAT: −190 to 0 HUSAT: −190 to − 30 HU

SAT: −190 to − 30 HU

SAT: −190 to − 30 HU

SMA, IMAT and SAT were calculated from a single axial slice at T12 expressed in cm² and normalized to height² to derive indices (e.g., SMI, SATI). Derived ratios and composite metrics (IMAT/SMA, IMATI, SMG, IMAC) were calculated accordingly.

Emphysema severity was assessed using a visual semi-quantitative scoring system, with each lung graded from 0 (none) to 3 (> 50%), yielding a global score ranging from 0 to 6. Bronchiectasis was evaluated visually using established radiologic criteria, and patients were classified as bronchiectasis-positive or -negative. Emphysema assessment was included to account for its confounding effects on spirometric outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical variables. Levene’s Test assessed variance homogeneity and one-way ANOVA evaluated differences across COPD severity categories (p < 0.05 considered significant).

Pearson correlation analysis examined relationships between CT-derived body composition parameters (i.e., SMA, SMI, SMD, IMAT/SMA) and pulmonary function measures (FEV1%, FVC%, FEV1/FVC). Subgroup analyses within each GOLD stage were conducted to assess severity-specific associations. Emphysema extent was considered when interpreting correlations, and post-hoc analyses were applied to significant findings to clarify associations between respiratory function and body composition.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study included 130 participants with COPD. The mean age of participants was 62 years (range: 34–80), and the mean BMI was 26.05 ± 4.83 kg/m² (range: 17.4–39.7). Most participants were male (89.2%), and 56.9% were current smokers. COPD severity was distributed as follows: GOLD 1, 11.5%; GOLD 2, 46.2%; GOLD 3, 27.7%; and GOLD 4, 14.6%. Emphysema was present in 87.7% of participants, with CT-based emphysema extent ranging from 1 to 24% (27.7%) to ≥ 76% (19.2%).

Smoking exposure, measured in pack-years to reflect cumulative tobacco use, averaged 44.37 ± 25.85 pack-years (range: 0–150). (See Table 1).

Association between CT-derived body composition and pulmonary function

Skeletal muscle area (SMA) was strongly positively associated with FEV₁% (r = 0.891, p < 0.01), indicating that patients with higher thoracic muscle mass tend to have better airflow. Similarly, skeletal muscle index (SMI) demonstrated a moderate negative correlation with FEV₁% (r = − 0.608, p < 0.01), suggesting that reduced muscle quantity may be linked with impaired lung function. Markers of muscle quality also showed significant relationships with pulmonary function. Skeletal muscle density (SMD) was modestly but significantly associated with spirometric measures (FEV₁: r = − 0.409, p < 0.01; FVC: r = − 0.334, p < 0.01), supporting its role as a biomarker of muscle quality in COPD. In contrast, higher intramuscular adipose tissue relative to muscle area (IMAT/SMA, myosteatosis index) was strongly negatively correlated with FEV₁/FVC (r = − 0.920, p < 0.01), highlighting the relevance of thoracic myosteatosis in airflow obstruction. Between-group differences in body composition and lung function

Statistically significant differences were observed across groups in key measures of body composition, lung function, and smoking exposure. Participants differed significantly in skeletal muscle area (SMA), with the highest values observed in Group X and the lowest in Group Y (F = 1133.036, p < 0.001). Similarly, skeletal muscle density (SMD) varied across groups (F = 51.191, p < 0.001), as did the skeletal muscle index (SMI) (F = 285.540, p < 0.001). Intramuscular adipose tissue relative to SMA (IMAT/SMA) also showed significant between-group differences (F = 4.128, p = 0.044).

Regarding pulmonary function, forced vital capacity (FVC%) and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1%) were significantly different between groups, with FVC% (F = 93.444, p < 0.001) and FEV1% (F = 361.121, p < 0.001) showing the expected pattern corresponding to body composition differences.

Additionally, smoking exposure differed significantly among groups (F = 32.257, p < 0.001), potentially contributing to the observed variations in lung function. These findings are summarized in Table 3.

Post-hoc comparisons across emphysema extent categories

Post-hoc Tukey HSD comparisons were performed to examine differences in body composition across emphysema extent categories. A statistically significant difference was observed in subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), with participants having ≥ 75% emphysema showing higher SAT compared to those without emphysema (mean difference = 34.72, p = 0.019) (Table 5). No statistically significant differences were found for other body composition parameters, including skeletal muscle area (SMA), skeletal muscle density (SMD), intramuscular adipose tissue (IMAT), skeletal muscle index (SMI), skeletal muscle gauge (SMG), intramuscular adipose tissue-to-muscle area ratio (IMAC), IMAT Index, SAT Index, or IMAT/SMA across emphysema categories. These findings suggest that extensive emphysema is associated with increased subcutaneous fat, whereas muscle-related parameters remain largely unaffected by emphysema severity.

Discussion

COPD is a systemic disease characterized by progressive airflow limitation and extrapulmonary manifestations, including alterations in body composition. The principal novel finding of this study is that CT-derived skeletal muscle quantity and quality, particularly intramuscular fat infiltration are independently associated with pulmonary function in COPD, even after adjustment for BMI and emphysema severity. These findings suggest that body composition changes may actively contribute to impaired lung function rather than representing a passive consequence of airflow limitation. Importantly, imaging-based muscle quality metrics appear to capture systemic disease features not fully reflected by spirometry alone, distinguishing our work from prior studies that primarily focused on total muscle mass or non-imaging assessments.

Specifically, skeletal muscle area (SMA) showed a strong positive association with FEV1%, while intramuscular fat infiltration (IMAT/SMA) was inversely related to FEV1/FVC. These findings indicate that both reduced muscle quantity and increased fat deposition may exacerbate respiratory impairment through diminished respiratory muscle strength and altered thoracic mechanics. Unlike earlier studies, our results uniquely emphasize intramuscular fat as a determinant of lung function impairment, providing a more granular understanding of muscle quality-respiratory interactions. Although the correlations were modest, they support interpreting CT-based body composition markers as complementary rather than surrogate indicators of spirometric function.

From a clinical perspective, CT-derived muscle quality metrics may serve as actionable biomarkers for identifying COPD at increased systemic risk, particularly those who may benefit most from interventions targeting muscle preservation or fat reduction. These metrics may enhance risk stratification beyond conventional anthropometric measures.

BMI stratification provides additional insight into COPD-related body composition changes, as patients with similar BMI values may exhibit markedly different muscle quality and fat infiltration profiles. Our findings reinforce the importance of body composition phenotyping beyond BMI alone, with CT-based muscle quality parameters remaining independently associated with lung function.

Notable between-group differences in SMA, SMI, SMD, and IMAT further highlight the relevance of these measures in characterizing disease burden. CT-derived body composition indices may therefore function as adjunctive imaging biomarkers of systemic involvement rather than direct determinants of airflow limitation.

Although statistically significant, the observed correlations were modest, which is expected given the heterogeneity of COPD. Emphysema, which was highly prevalent in our cohort (87.7%), likely attenuated the strength of associations between body composition and spirometry, underscoring the multifactorial nature of airflow limitation in COPD.

Adipose tissue measures, including IMAT and total fat indices, did not significantly vary across COPD stages. This may reflect limited statistical power, disease heterogeneity, and the influence of confounders such as age, physical activity, nutrition, and comorbidities. Larger, longitudinal studies using advanced or volumetric fat quantification techniques may better detect stage-dependent differences, particularly in subgroups with sarcopenic obesity.

Exacerbation history represents another clinically relevant factor influencing body composition. Although not directly analyzed, frequent exacerbations are known to promote systemic inflammation, inactivity, and muscle wasting. The observed associations between reduced muscle quality (lower SMD and higher IMAT/SMA) and impaired lung function suggest that patients with a higher exacerbation burden may experience more pronounced adverse body composition changes, consistent with prior longitudinal and clinical studies [14, 20]. Future analyses incorporating exacerbation frequency and severity are warranted.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating positive associations between muscle mass and lung function [8, 21, 22], while extending this evidence by identifying intramuscular fat infiltration as an independent contributor to functional impairment.

The influence of emphysema further contextualizes these results. Occhipinti et al. (2019) showed that spirometric indices closely reflect emphysema severity. In our cohort, the high prevalence of emphysema likely weakened correlations between body composition and spirometry, reinforcing the interpretation that muscle and fat indices primarily reflect systemic disease components [23].

Consistent with this interpretation, Wienker et al. (2024) demonstrated that greater CT-derived muscle volume was associated with improved lung function following lung volume reduction surgery [24]. Our cross-sectional findings independently confirm the relevance of muscle composition to respiratory performance in COPD. Zhu et al. (2020) further linked systemic inflammation to reduced FEV1, and our results complement this evidence by showing that adverse body composition represents a parallel systemic pathway contributing to disease severity [25].

In summary, CT-derived body composition metrics provide clinically meaningful information beyond spirometry. While not a replacement for pulmonary function testing, they offer valuable insight into systemic manifestations of COPD and may help identify patients at increased risk of sarcopenia, metabolic dysregulation, and poor outcomes.

Conclusion

This study stresses the considerable influence of body composition on pulmonary function in patients with COPD. CT-derived measures indicated that increased SMA and SMD are significantly linked to better lung function, whereas increased IMAT and subcutaneous fat (SAD) adversely affect respiratory performance. These results stress the value of muscle quality evaluation together with conventional spirometry in the assessment of the severity of COPD. The integration of body composition analysis into clinical practice has the potential to strengthen risk stratification and inform targeted interventions to maintain muscle mass and decrease fat infiltration to enhance COPD outcomes.
