Genomic analysis of high pathogenicity avian influenza viruses from Antarctica reveals multiple introductions from South America
Authors: Maria Ogrzewalska, Ralph Eric Thijl Vanstreels, Elisa Cavalcante Pereira, Emandi Campinas, Leonardo Correa Junior, Jennifer Oliveira Melo, Larissa Macedo, Luciana Reis Appolinario, Ighor Arantes, Martha Lima Brandao, Bruno Rocha Pribul, Claudio Marcos Rocha, Fabricio Souza Campos, Ueric José Borges de Souza, Bruna Paredes Galarza, Alanis Silva Melgarejo, Luciana Trilles, Lucas Machado Moreira, Wim Degrave, Maithê Magalhães, Daniel Moreira, Roberto do Val Vilela, Fernando Couto Motta, Marilda M. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spread of high pathogenic avian influenza virus (HPAIV) H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b into Antarctica poses a major threat to polar wildlife. We report the detection of H5N1 in carcasses of eight species during the 2023-2024 and 2024-2025 austral summers in the South Shetland Islands: Antarctic shag, Antarctic tern, kelp gull, pintado petrel, Antarctic petrel, skuas, Antarctic fur seal, and southern elephant seal. Whole-genome sequencing, mutational profiling, and phylogenetic reconstruction revealed that the viruses detected in these hosts descended from distinct introduction events. One group of strains including complete and partial viral genomes from a gull, skuas, fur seals, an Antarctic tern, and a southern elephant seal clustered with H5N1 strains previously detected in marine mammals in South America and formed a polyphyletic lineage consistent with at least two independent introductions into Antarctica. A second group of strains including complete and partial viral genomes from petrels, shags, and skuas clustered with H5N1 strains previously detected in seabirds and marine mammals in South Georgia and with a previously reported HPAIV detection from Torgersen Island, Antarctic Peninsula. These findings reveal extensive epidemiological connectivity between South America and Antarctica, with South Georgia serving as a “stepping stone” for virus spread in the region.

Introduction

Global spread of the high pathogenicity avian influenza virus (HPAIV) subtype H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b has caused unprecedented mortality among wild animals1. In late 2022, viruses of the B3.2 genotype were introduced to the Pacific coast of South America, disseminating rapidly and causing outbreaks in poultry and wildlife2–4. Phylogenetic reconstruction reveals that shortly after arrival to South America these viruses diverged into two sub-clades: an “avian” sub-clade that spread inland and was detected predominantly in poultry and terrestrial wild birds, and a “marine mammal” sub-clade that spread coastally and was detected predominantly in marine mammals and seabirds5,6. In October and November 2023, HPAIV H5N1 spread from South America onto the Subantarctic region, causing outbreaks in seabirds and marine mammals in South Georgia and Falkland (Malvinas) Islands7. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the viruses from South Georgia were monophyletic, descending from the South American “avian” sub-clade, whereas the strains from the Falkland (Malvinas) Islands were polyphyletic, descending from the South American “marine mammal” sub-clade7.

As the virus disseminated in wildlife in South America and South Georgia, concerns were raised that it could spread further onto the Antarctic Peninsula8,9. The first reports of wildlife mortality suspected to be due to HPAIV H5N1 in the Antarctic region were made in December 202310, and several confirmed detections have been made along the Antarctic Peninsula since then10–13. During the austral summers of 2023/2024 and 2024/2025, we collected swabs and tissue samples from deceased wildlife in the South Shetland Islands, Antarctic Peninsula, to perform molecular testing and genomic characterization of HPAIV H5N1.

Results

Phylogenetic reconstructions of the concatenated whole genomes indicated the viruses recovered in this study belong to two clearly distinct groups, “a” and “b” (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Phylogenetic group “a” comprised 10 complete genomes (recovered from 3 Antarctic fur seals, 1 kelp gull, and 3 skuas) that clustered with viruses previously detected predominantly in marine mammals in South America (i.e. the South American “marine mammal” sub-clade2). Sequence identity suggests that 6 partial genomes (recovered from 1 Antarctic tern, 2 skuas, 1 Antarctic petrel, 1 southern elephant seal) were also part of this group. Phylogenetic reconstruction of concatenated whole genomes suggested that this group could be further divided into four subgroups, each potentially descending from a separate introduction event to Antarctica (Fig. 1a). On the other hand, phylogenetic group “b” (Fig. 1b) comprised 8 complete genomes (recovered from 1 Antarctic shag, 1 pintado petrel, and 3 skuas) clustered with viruses previously detected in seabirds and marine mammals from South Georgia and with a previously reported HPAIV detection in a brown skua (Stercorarius antarcticus) from Torgersen Island in Antarctica, which in turn descended from viruses that circulated in poultry and wild birds in South America (i.e. the South American “avian” sub-clade2). Sequence identity suggests that one partial genome (recovered from Antarctic shag) was also part of this group. This group was consistently monophyletic across all phylogenetic reconstructions (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3), suggesting that these viruses and the Torgersen Island virus descended from a single introduction event to Antarctica.

A comparison of cycle threshold (Ct) values of the Real Time RT-PCR revealed that birds could be classified as either oral shedders, which had higher viral loads (i.e. lower Ct values) in the oropharyngeal swabs than in the cloacal swabs, or fecal shedders, which had higher viral loads in cloacal swabs than in oropharyngeal swabs. Birds infected with group “a” strains were more frequently oral shedders (2 skuas, 1 tern) than fecal shedders (1 skua), whereas birds infected with group “b” strains were more frequently fecal shedders (1 skua, 1 petrel) than oral shedders (1 skua) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Mutational analysis revealed amino acid substitutions that distinguished phylogenetic groups “a” and “b” across all proteins except M1 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1). Most group “a” strains recovered from both birds and mammals showed two key mammalian adaptation substitutions in PB2 (PB2-Q591K and PB2-D701N); another mammalian adaptation substitution in NP (NP-N319K) was detected in a group “a” strain recovered from the brain of an Antarctic fur seal (individual O2904). All group “b” viruses had a deletion of 17 amino acids in the neuraminidase stalk (positions 58-74). Lastly, two amino acid motifs were observed in the hemagglutinin cleavage site. The original PLREKRRKRGLF motif was found in most viruses from both phylogenetic groups, comprising 14 complete genomes and 5 partial genomes. Discussion

Although HPAIV-suspected mortality of brown skuas was reported at the South Orkney Islands on 22 December 202310, our detection of HPAIV in a kelp gull on 8 January 2024 represents the earliest confirmed case in Antarctica. More importantly, however, our phylogenetic reconstruction reveals that there were at least three separate introduction events of HPAIV from South America to Antarctica, with one of these introductions having used South Georgia as a “stepping stone” (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Conceptual representation of the hypothesized pathways of spread of high pathogenic avian influenza virus (HPAIV) H5N1 in the Subantarctic and Antarctic region, based on the findings of this study and prior studies that performed phylogeographic and phylogenetic reconstruction5,7,13,25,28.Arrows represent the potential routes of spatial dissemination of the HPAIV H5N1 South American “marine mammal” (blue) and “avian” (red) sub-clades (sensu Uhart and colleagues5).

Dissemination of “marine mammal” viruses from South America to Antarctica

The sequences of the viruses recovered from a kelp gull at Livingston Island in January 2024, an Antarctic petrel at King George Island in November 2024, an Antarctic fur seal at Robert Island in December 2024, two Antarctic fur seals and a southern elephant seal at King George Island in December 2024, and five skuas at King George Island in January–March 2025 were part of phylogenetic group “a”, This group descends from the South American “marine mammal” sub-clade, a cluster of viruses detected in association with large-scale outbreaks in marine mammals (sea lions, elephant seals, fur seals, porpoises, and otters) and, to a lesser extent, in seabirds (terns, fulmars, and petrels)5,6,14–16 and isolated cases in poultry14 and a human17. These “marine mammal” viruses emerged in early 2023 on the coast of southern Peru/northern Chile and in 2023–2024 they spread southward along the Chilean coast, then northward along the coasts of Argentina, Falkland (Malvinas) Islands, Uruguay, and Brazil (represented with blue arrows in Fig. 3)5,6,14–16. Remarkably, these strains have several mammalian adaptation mutations and appear to be capable of transmitting effectively among both mammals and birds5,6,18.

Our analyses suggest that group “a” strains recovered from Antarctic wildlife were polyphyletic; however, different analytical methods disagreed on the number of introduction events that might have originated them. All analyses agreed that the virus from the kelp gull sampled at Livingston Island in January 2024 (individual O2601) was clearly distinct from the remaining group “a” strains, representing an isolated introduction event. However, the analyses disagreed on whether the remaining group “a” strains resulted from multiple introduction events or not. Hence, until further data is available to corroborate additional introduction events, we conservatively consider that the group “a” strains detected in this study descended from only two separate introduction events. Future genomic data from South American, Antarctic and Subantarctic HPAIV H5N1 viruses may help clarifying the number of introduction events and whether they occurred directly from South America or indirectly via Subantarctic islands. In particular, it is worth noting that one of the strains recovered from Antarctic fur seal (individual O2913) at King George Island in December 2024 was closely related to a strain from a southern fulmar (Fulmarus glacialoides) sampled in the Falkland (Malvinas) Islands in October 2023, hinting at the possibility that the archipelago may have served as a “stepping stone”.

The routes of dissemination of group “a” viruses are unknown, but it seems likely natural movement of seabirds and/or pinnipeds played a key role. Among seabirds, skuas and kelp gulls stand out as potential vectors as they could have been exposed to the virus by predating, kleptoparasitizing (i.e., deliberately taking the food from another animal), or scavenging on infected animals19,20. Kelp gulls were frequently seen scavenging on pinniped carcasses during HPAIV H5N1 outbreaks in South America in late 20235,16, and kelp gull populations breeding in the Antarctic Peninsula often disperse to the north, reaching the southern tip of Argentina and Chile21. Similarly, brown skuas breeding at the South Shetland Islands are known to overwinter on the Patagonian shelf off northern Argentina, Uruguay, and southern Brazil22, where they could have been exposed through interactions with other seabirds or scavenging on marine mammal carcasses afloat. South Polar skuas (Stercorarius maccormicki) breeding on the Antarctic Peninsula will also forage on the Patagonian Shelf waters during their return migration from the northern hemisphere23,24. It is also important to acknowledge the potential role of other birds species as fulmars and giant petrels—both of which have been reported as infected or suspected to participate in HPAIV H5N1 spread7,25—were not sampled in this study, either because they are locally less abundant, experienced less mortality, or are less likely to be detected as carcasses. Among pinnipeds, the southern elephant seal stands out as a potential vector species since it was heavily impacted by HPAIV H5N1 outbreaks in Argentina in late 20235 and there is significant genetic connectivity between the elephant seal populations from Argentina and the South Shetland Islands26.

Virus dissemination through South Georgia as a “stepping stone”

The sequences of the viruses recovered from a pintado petrel at Livingston Island in December 2024 and two Antarctic shags and three skuas at King George Island in January–March 2025 were part of phylogenetic group “b”. This group comprised HPAIV H5N1 strains detected in South Georgia in October–December 20237 and a strain detected at Torgersen Island (~300 km southwest), Antarctic Peninsula, in December 202413, both of which descend from the South American “avian” sub-clade. In South America, this clade emerged in Peru and spread southward to Chile and eastward across the Andes to Uruguay, Argentina, and Brazil (represented with red arrows in Fig. 3), being detected predominantly in poultry and wild birds (mainly geese, swans, gulls, and egrets) with rare spill-over to mammals (coatis, a sea lion, and a dolphin)5,6,27. After arriving to South Georgia, however, these strains demonstrated their ability to spread and cause mortality in seabirds (skuas, gulls, terns, and shags) and pinnipeds (fur seals and elephant seals)7. In September–December 2024 these viruses have also spread eastward from South Georgia across the Subantarctic region, causing outbreaks in seabirds and/or pinnipeds at Gough, Crozet, and Kerguelen Islands25,28.

Our phylogenetic reconstruction suggests that the ancestors of the South Shetland Island and Torgersen Island strains had diverged during the austral autumn-winter of 2024 (late May to early June; see Supplementary Fig. 2). However, at present, there is insufficient genomic data of HPAIV H5N1 from South Georgia and the Antarctic Peninsula in 2024 to determine whether this divergence occurred before or after the virus arrived in Antarctica (i.e., if these strains descend from two separate introduction events from South Georgia to Antarctica or if the virus was introduced once to Antarctica and persisted locally through the winter). Conservatively, we consider that the group “b” strains detected in this study and in Torgersen Island descended from a single introduction event.

Although group “b” viruses had been previously implicated in outbreaks among pinnipeds in South Georgia7, thus far in the Antarctic Peninsula they were only detected in seabirds, namely skuas, Antarctic shags, and pintado petrel. The wintering grounds of brown skuas from both South Georgia and the Antarctic Peninsula overlap extensively on the Southwest Atlantic Ocean, especially in deep waters off the Patagonian Shelf22,29,30; it is therefore plausible that this species played a role in carrying HPAIV H5N1 from South Georgia to Antarctica. On the other hand, blue-eyed shags such as Antarctic shags and South Georgia shags (Leucocarbo georgianus) usually remain year-round near their colonies with extremely limited genetic connectivity among remote archipelagos31, and therefore are unlikely to have played a significant role in the long-distance dissemination of HPAIV H5N1. Although pintado petrels breed at both South Georgia and the Antarctic Peninsula32, little is known about their non-breeding distribution or the population connectivity between these sites.

Genomic features

As expected, the HPAIV H5N1 strains recovered from Antarctic wildlife inherited many mutations from their ancestral South American strains. The mammal adaptation substitutions PB2-Q5691K and PB2-D701N are suggested to be one of the reasons why the South American “marine mammal” sub-clade viruses were associated with mass mortalities of pinnipeds in Argentina and Chile5,6, and these substitutions were carried-over to the Antarctic group “a” viruses in this study. PB2-Q591K enhances the polymerase activity at lower temperatures of the mammalian upper respiratory tract and PB2-D701N improves nuclear import and polymerase activity in mammalian cells, and their paired presence is known to synergistically enhance transmission and virulence in mammals33–35. Interestingly, the virus recovered from the cloacal swab from a skua (O3592-CL) presented the PB2-D701N substitution whereas the oropharyngeal swab from the same bird (O3592-OR) did not, suggesting there may be negative selective pressure for this mutation in avian hosts.

Other substitutions were also consistently present in group “a” strains and may contribute to adaptation to mammals: (a) PA-M86I has been implicated in enhanced polymerase activity in mammals36, and (b) NS1-R21Q can interfere with RIG-I signaling, modulating the immune response in mammals37. Furthermore, another group “a” strain (O2904-BR, recovered from the brain of an Antarctic fur seal at King George Island) showed an amino acid substitution (NP-N319K) that is known to enhance binding to importin α1 in mammalian cells38; this strain also stood out in that it presented some amino acid substitutions (PA-M86V and NP-D101E) that were otherwise associated with group “b” strains, suggesting there may be positive selection for these mutations in Antarctic/Subantarctic hosts leading to convergent evolution.

Additionally, we found a variety of “signature” amino acid substitutions that distinguished group “a” and “b” strain from each other and from early B3.2 genotype strains (Fig. 2). These mutations should be considered when designing and validating diagnostic protocols for the surveillance of HPAIV H5N1 in Antarctic and Subantarctic wildlife, to ensure reliable detection of strains from both groups. Yet, the phenotypic and epidemiological implications of these various mutations are largely unknown at present. For instance, it is remarkable that all group “b” strains had a deletion of 17 amino acids in the neuraminidase stalk. HPAIV H5N1 strains that were genetically engineered to have a similarly shortened neuraminidase stalk showed enhanced adaptation and virulence in chickens, although the cellular/immune mechanisms for this could not be elucidated39. Similarly, we found that some group “a” strains showed a slight variation in the hemagglutinin cleavage site, leading to an amino acid motif (PLKEKRRKRGLF) that had been previously observed in Asian HPAIV H5N6 strains40, with unknown phenotypic implications. Further studies will therefore be necessary to determine the significance of these recurring amino acid substitutions and whether they enhance the ability of these viruses to infect and transmit among seabirds and marine mammals or if they contribute to viral resilience in polar environments.

Conservation and management implications

The detection of HPAIV H5N1 in Antarctica is acutely concerning from an environmental perspective. It is estimated that more than 500,000 seabirds and 50,000 marine mammals died from HPAIV H5N1 in South America8. If similar impacts occur in Antarctica, the consequences for coastal and marine Antarctic ecosystems could be disastrous. Of particular concern is our result that strains descending from the South American “marine mammal” sub-clade were detected with high viral load (as indicated by low Ct values) in the brain of Antarctic fur seals found deceased at the South Shetland Islands. South Shetland Antarctic fur seals are a genetically and ecologically unique population, and their abundance has declined by >95% since 200741,42. Considering that these strains have several mammalian-adaptive mutations and were previously implicated in the mass mortality of pinnipeds in Chile, Argentina, Uruguay, and Brazil5,6,14–16, their arrival poses an existential threat to South Shetland Antarctic fur seals42. Another concern is the large proportion of HPAIV H5N1 detections in skuas, which corroborates that skuas are a particularly vulnerable to the impacts of HPAIV H5N1 in the Antarctic and Subantarctic region11,12,25,28,43. This is an acutely troubling pattern, as it parallels the HPAIV H5N1 outbreaks in the United Kingdom in 2021/2022 where skua populations collapsed by 60–70%44,45.

Continued research to monitor the spread and the impacts of these viruses in Antarctica in the coming years will therefore be of paramount importance. While our phylogenetic results corroborate that HPAIV H5N1 dissemination in the Subantarctic and Antarctic region has probably been driven by natural wildlife movements, there is substantial (and increasing) human presence in the region associated with research and tourism activities. Therefore, the implementation of strict biosafety protocols and control measures will be important to prevent human-mediated spread of HPAIV in the region and to mitigate zoonotic risks. Considering that transmission can be bidirectional and that genetic reassortment between HPAIV and human influenza viruses is possible, it would also be important that persons approaching or working with Antarctic wildlife should be vaccinated against seasonal influenza and, if possible, against avian influenza subtype H5.

Our genomic data reveal that there were at least four separate instances where HPAIV H5N1 were introduced to Antarctica, with the earliest known detection of the virus in January 2024. This demonstrates that even this remote and pristine environment is not immune to human-associated disease threats, underscoring that a One Health approach is critical to protect Antarctic wildlife on a changing planet. The ongoing spread of HPAIV in avian and mammalian hosts within this region warrants enhanced biosecurity measures and international collaboration to mitigate the ecological and epidemiological risks. Furthermore, continued research on the pathogen ecology in Antarctic and Subantarctic wildlife will be essential to improve our understanding of how native and introduced pathogens may synergize with other ongoing threats, such as climate change, invasive species, tourism, and fisheries.

Methods

Sample collection

This study was conducted in compliance with all relevant ethical and environmental regulations. No live animals were handled or sampled, as all biological material was obtained from carcasses. Research activities were carried out under license LW-20/24 issued by the Animal Use Ethics Committee of the Vice-Presidency for Research and Biological Collections (VPPCB), Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (Fiocruz). Sampling activities in Antarctica were authorized by the Environmental Assessment Group of the Brazilian Antarctic Program (PROANTAR). Fieldwork was conducted within the framework of the FIOANTAR and VIDEANTAR projects, with logistical support provided by the Brazilian Navy. All procedures followed strict biosafety protocols to ensure sample integrity and researcher safety. Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed information on the animals sampled. Oropharyngeal, oral, nasal, and cloacal swabs and brain tissue samples were collected in tubes with 1 mL of DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research, Irvine, USA) and stored at –80 °C. Samples were then transported with a strict cold-chain to the Laboratory of Respiratory Viruses, Exanthematous, Enteroviruses, and Viral Emergencies at the Oswaldo Cruz Institute (IOC) for further analysis.

RNA extraction and real-time RT-PCR

RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 52906) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Detection and subtyping of influenza A virus were performed by real-time RT-PCR using two assays: (i) the Biomanguinhos INFA/INFB/SC2 Multiplex Kit (ANVISA Registration No. 8014217006095), and (ii) the CDC Influenza A/B Typing Panel (Flu A/B Typing Kit, Cat. No. FluIVD03-6), targeting the matrix (InfA) and hemagglutinin (H5a and H5b) genes46,47 (Supplementary Data 1). H5a was developed to detect specific groups of H5 viruses of Asian origin (A/goose/Guangdong/1996 lineage and derivatives, which include many HPAI strains). H5b is complementary, covering genetic variations of H5 that could escape detection by the H5a assay. A viral load index was calculated for each sample by subtracting the Cycle threshold (Ct) value from 40.

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly

Whole-genome sequencing of positive samples was performed using a Multisegment Reverse Transcription-PCR (M-RT-PCR) amplification protocol48,49. The sequencing library was constructed using the COVIDSeq kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Sequencing was conducted on the MiSeq platform (Illumina), and genome assembly was carried out using the Iterative Refinement Meta-Assembler (IRMA) pipeline50. Clade classification was performed using NextClade v3.10.1 (https://clades.nextstrain.org/).

Bayesian evolutionary analysis

To place the viruses recovered from Antarctic wildlife in a global context, we downloaded 822 globally-representative HA gene sequences from Gs/Gd clade 2.3.4.4b viruses during 2021–2024 using NextStrain (https://nextstrain.org/). Phylogenetic relationships were inferred for the HA gene using the Maximum Likelihood methods available in IQ-Tree v. 2.4.0 with a GTR model of nucleotide substitution with gamma-distributed rate variation among sites. Publicly-available sequences of influenza A viruses from South America and Antarctica were obtained through Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data - GISAID and GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/) (Supplementary Data 2). Because this initial analysis confirmed that all Antarctic/Subantarctic strains were descendent from South American strains, subsequent evolutionary analyses omitted viruses from other continents. The temporal structure of the datasets in each segment was evaluated using TempEst v1.5.351 through a root-to-tip linear regression. The significance of the correlation between collection date and genetic distance was assessed using a Pearson correlation test, with adjustments for multiple comparisons made via the Benjamini–Hochberg method for false discovery rate control. Time-scaled phylogenetic trees were estimated using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach implemented in BEAST v1.10.452, with BEAGLE v4.0.053 to improve computational efficiency. Bayesian trees were reconstructed under the nucleotide substitution model selected by jModelTest v2.1.1054, the non-parametric Bayesian Skyline coalescent demographic model55, and a relaxed molecular clock model with a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) rate reference prior56. The molecular clock, the time to the most recent common ancestor (TMRCA), and the spatial dynamics of each viral segment were reconstructed using a discrete phylogeographic model57 implemented in BEAST v1.10.4. A symmetric diffusion model was intentionally adopted as a conservative framework, allowing directional patterns of viral spread to emerge from the data rather than being imposed by prior assumptions, particularly given the limited and uneven geographic sampling in Antarctica. In addition to segment-based analyses, concatenated gene datasets were also constructed and evaluated under the same Bayesian phylogeographic framework to capture genome-wide evolutionary signals. Sequences were coded according to both their sampling locations and host. For each dataset, independent MCMC runs were performed and subsequently combined to confirm that all continuous parameters achieved an effective sample size (ESS) greater than 100–200, as verified using Tracer v1.758. The maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree was summarized with TreeAnnotator v1.1052. Trees were visualized using R v4.4.159 with the packages treeio v1.30.060 and ggtree v3.14.061.

Mutational analysis

The translated sequence of all eight gene segments was compared to evaluate the presence of amino acid substitutions using NextClade v3.10.1 (https://clades.nextstrain.org/). An early B3.2 genotype strain (A/turkey/South Dakota/22-008485-001/2022; GISAID accession code EPI_ISL_11971481)62 was used as a reference.
