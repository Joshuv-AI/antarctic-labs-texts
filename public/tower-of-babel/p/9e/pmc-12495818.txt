Barriers and facilitators to physical activity in rural communities: using the Behaviour Change Wheel to identify intervention functions
Authors: Chelsea Pelletier, Kirsten Ward, Cameron Aird, Mary Jung, Gloria Fox, Anne Pousette, Guy Faulkner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople living in rural communities face increased barriers and fewer opportunities for regular participation in physical activity compared to those living in urban centers. This project aimed to map barriers and facilitators of physical activity in rural communities to the Theoretical Domains Framework and Behaviour Change Wheel to identify intervention functions.MethodsWe conducted individual semi-structured interviews with participants from rural communities in two sparsely populated regions in British Columbia, Canada. Interview questions were framed using the Theoretical Domains Framework and focused on beliefs and lived experiences of physical activity. Deductive analysis involved identifying barriers and facilitators to physical activity and mapping them to the Theoretical Domains Framework and capability, opportunity, and motivation (COM-B) of the Behaviour Change Wheel. Barriers and facilitators were used to identify potential intervention functions and policy levers to change physical activity behaviour in rural communities.ResultsParticipants included 46 individuals aged 22 to 77 years (36 women) living in rural communities (population size < 100–13,000 people). Barriers and facilitators to physical activity were predominant in the domains of Social Influences, Environmental Context and Resources, and Beliefs About Consequences. Specific barriers and facilitators were identified related to the availability of friends or family with whom to be physically active; weather-related factors; programming and infrastructure for physical activity; knowledge of local opportunities; and beliefs about healthy aging and fear of injuries. Based on mapping barriers and facilitators to the Behaviour Change Wheel, physical activity intervention strategies for rural communities should focus on environmental restructuring, modelling, and enablement.ConclusionTo improve physical activity in rural communities, interventions at municipal and provincial levels should focus on addressing barriers, such as infrastructure and policy supportive of physical activity, and leveraging existing facilitators, such as access to outdoor physical activity opportunities.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24672-2.Keywords: Physical activity behaviour, Rural health, Exercise, Behaviour change wheel, Theoretical domains framework, Recreation, Small town

Background

As a health-promoting behaviour, physical activity plays a pivotal role in maintaining and improving health and wellbeing for individuals. Regular physical activity is associated with reduced rates of chronic disease, enhanced mental health, improved quality of life, and increased longevity [1, 2]. Despite a plethora of evidence on the benefits of physical activity for all populations, there are substantial inequities in global physical activity participation based on country, community, and individual level factors [3, 4]. These inequities are perpetuated by unequal access to opportunities to be active and gaps in understanding the interactive effects of factors influencing activity, impacting intervention design and implementation [5–7].

Approximately 20% of the Canadian population resides in rural communities [8]. There are numerous definitions of rurality used across fields of health promotion, health services, and policy [27]. Definitions vary across jurisdictions and are often based on characteristics including population size, density, and distance from large population centres (e.g., access to health services, opportunities and resources) [9, 10]. The British Columbia Ministry of Health identifies communities as remote (0–1,000 people), small rural (1,000–3,500 people), rural (3,500–20,000 people), small urban (20,000–75,000 people), urban (75,000–175,000 people), and large urban (175, 000 + people) based on population size and level of available care (e.g., access to inpatient and specialized acute services, primary and community care) [11].

Rural populations in Canada have a lower life expectancy, higher rates of chronic disease, and different patterns of physical activity behaviour compared to urban residents [12, 13]. Rural-urban differences in meeting physical activity guidelines are influenced by self-reported sex with 47.4% of rural males (43.5% urban males) and 36.7% of rural females (39.4% urban females) meeting physical activity guidelines [13]. Rural-urban differences in physical activity participation indicate urban residents participate in more active transportation to meet physical activity guidelines, while rural residents engage in more occupational or household physical activity [14]. Rural communities are distinctive in their geographical, socioeconomic, and cultural characteristics, contributing to a unique context influencing physical activity participation and health status [15]. Rural health promotion strategies must reflect a place-based approach to understanding physical activity behaviours. Specific challenges to regular physical activity participation for rural residents include limited access to recreational facilities, sparse physical activity facilitating infrastructure, long distances between opportunities, and scarcity of human resources [16, 17]. Rural communities face a lack of social support systems and limited access to health services which contribute to a lack of physical activity promotion [18]. Gender and other social categories (e.g., income, education) interact with physical activity differently in rural and urban communities resulting in distinct social (e.g., lack of social support) and environmental (e.g., access to low cost facilities) correlates of activity across the rural-urban continuum [19].

By exploring barriers and facilitators (or enablers) to physical activity, researchers, policymakers, and practitioners can collaboratively devise evidence-based strategies to improve physical activity levels and overall health outcomes in rural populations. The design and implementation of evidence-based health behaviour change interventions are best supported by using theoretical frameworks to guide their design and evaluation [20, 21]. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) integrates key psychological theories and behaviour change constructs to provide a structured approach for analysing and influencing behaviour across fields of health care services and behavioural medicine [22, 23]. The TDF provides a systematic framework for identifying and addressing determinants of behaviour in 14 domains incorporating 33 behaviour change models across 128 constructs considering cognitive, affective, social and environmental influences on behaviour [22, 23]. The TDF can be connected to the Behaviour Change Wheel constructs (capability, opportunity, and motivation; COM-B) to link the determinants of behaviour (e.g., what needs to change) to 19 intervention functions and policies anticipated to be effective for changing a particular behaviour [24]. The TDF and Behaviour Change Wheel have been used to map factors influencing behaviour and subsequently identify intervention functions related to physical activity and nutrition interventions [25–27].

Although data on rural physical activity patterns are scarce when compared to urban populations, previous studies have described barriers and facilitators to physical activity for rural adults in countries such as Canada [16], the United States [28], and Australia [29] using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. Commonly identified barriers include those related to the built (e.g., lack of walkability), natural (e.g., wildlife, weather), and social environment (e.g., social cohesion and connection), while studies comparing rural and urban environments have noted limited differences in individual level barriers (e.g., cost, time) [16]. Fewer studies focus on facilitators of activity, which limits opportunities for a strengths-based approach to health promotion. As the definition and conceptualization of rurality differs between jurisdictions within and between countries, local assessments of factors influencing behaviour are warranted. There is a large evidence gap related to how to effectively promote physical activity among rural communities [30]. Systematic mapping of factors associated with physical activity behaviour in rural areas will enable identification of theory-informed context-specific intervention strategies to help close gaps in physical activity participation.

Using the TDF and Behaviour Change Wheel, the aims of this project were to (1) identify factors influencing physical activity behaviour in rural communities, and (2) identify theoretically-informed intervention strategies for physical activity promotion in rural communities.

Methods

Participants

Eligible participants included adults over 18 years of age living in a remote, small rural, or rural community (population < 20,000 people) as defined by the British Columbia Ministry of Health [11] in the interior or northern regions of British Columbia, Canada. Participants were recruited via email and social media platforms through existing networks and working with community partners (e.g., local health authorities, community organizations). All participants provided written or verbal informed consent and the project received harmonized research ethics approval from the University of Northern British Columbia (REB #H20-02257). Participants were offered a $25CAD gift card to a vendor of their choosing (e.g., retail or coffee shop).

Data generation

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted using the Zoom videoconference platform by two research team members between June 2021 – March 2022, audio recorded, and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriptionist. Participants completed a basic demographic questionnaire during the interview or independently following the interview.

The interview guide was created based on the TDF, with consideration of intersectionality, and guidelines for intervention development to understand target behaviour (additional file 1) [23, 24, 31]. The interview guide was developed through iterative collaboration among all members of the research team, which included those working in health policy and health promotion (GF, AP). Additional probes were added to the interview guide after an initial batch of interviews were completed to ensure depth of responses in alignment with TDF domains.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed inductive and deductive approaches based on guidelines for TDF-based analysis following a framework approach [32, 33]. Two authors (KW, CA) familiarized themselves with the data by reading and re-reading all transcripts and field notes for general immersion in the data followed by development of a preliminary coding guide (additional file 2). The coding guide was developed to provide a definition, description, and examples for each code in alignment with the TDF domains. Five transcripts were independently coded by KW and CA using NVivo 12 software, and the guide was refined for clarity following discussion with the remaining co-authors. Each coder was then responsible for completing coding on half of the remaining transcripts. The two coders met frequently to discuss and align coding within the TDF domains. In cases where a coder was uncertain, this was resolved through discussion with the other coder or a co-author. A coding matrix (spreadsheet) was created where data from each interview was charted based on TDF domain. Barriers and facilitators (i.e., increasing or decreasing the likelihood of engaging in physical activity) aligning with each domain were extracted from the data and added to the spreadsheet along with quotes from relevant transcripts. In the second phase of analysis, themes were identified within each of the dominant or larger TDF domains as representing factors perceived to influence engagement in physical activity [32].

Throughout the analysis, we considered an intersectional approach to understanding the factors influencing physical activity behaviour. This was done by considering how social factors (e.g., age, gender, disability status) interact with rurality to impact physical activity across each TDF domain. Further analysis was completed using the Behaviour Change Wheel to determine theoretical intervention functions in response to the barriers and facilitators identified by participants [22, 24]. Based on guidance for using the Behaviour Change Wheel to develop interventions, and considering judgement based on the context, barriers, and facilitators identified, we examined the relevance of intervention functions identified in the Behaviour Change Wheel, along with relevant policy levers and example interventions [22, 24].

Results

Participants

A total of 46 individuals completed semi-structured interviews (average length: 41 min). Most participants identified as women (n = 36, 78%), between the ages of 30–39 years old (n = 18, 39%), and had university or college level education (n = 33, 72%). Participants were from communities with population sizes ranging from < 100 to 13,000 people and almost half of the sample were from communities with a population of 1000–4999 people (Table 1).

Barriers to physical activity

Barriers to physical activity identified by participants aligned with the opportunity and motivation of the COM-B model, most frequently citing the influence of environmental context and resources, social and professional role and identity, and reinforcement.

Environmental context & resources

The most prominent barriers related to environmental context and resources discussed by participants related to community resources and regulations. Participants mentioned a lack of appropriate infrastructure and staffing, and limited accessibility for different activities. Participants described changes in policies and regulations, which altered access to physical activity opportunities:

“I used to [have access for physical activity] until I ran into all this stuff with deactivating roads, now that’s causing a serious problem [with access for doing physical activity] and plus all the other restrictions.” (P44, man).

Similarly, participants described how the local road infrastructure, local industry, and policies impacted their perceptions of safety, as P32 mentioned:

“It does make me quite nervous. I wouldn’t bounce if anybody hit me now with a car … You know, the big log trucks, they go flying down and … my driveway is on a corner at the bottom of a hill, so people wouldn’t necessarily see me and if they’re not paying attention.” (P32, woman).

Other environmental barriers identified included the weather and wildlife. Participants described weather concerns related to outdoor temperatures that were too hot or too cold for activity, and snow and ice. As P39 described, seasonal weather patterns influenced their physical activity patterns throughout the year:

“Ya, and then just the climate too, can be a challenge, right. Getting up and going for a walk when it’s like it is today is not a challenge, it’s a joy. But getting up when it’s −20 and going for a walk, ya, it doesn’t happen.” (P39, woman).

Related to wildlife, participants described their fear of running into unfriendly animals as barriers to activity:

“I’ve run into lots of bears and rattle snakes and stuff like that, So that’s always a kind of a concern.” (P25, man).

Social & professional role & identity

Individual’s professional roles, including their daily job tasks, was the most discussed element within this domain. Participants described inability to be physically active for most of the day due to the sedentary nature of their jobs (i.e., working at a computer), and a lack of time left in their day after working long shifts or more than one job.

“And I find my biggest restriction of that is work cause I work two jobs and then sometimes I’m just tired, I really don’t have enough time to go do something” (P10, man).

Further, participants often noted that their professional roles were only a part of their busy lives, with parenting requiring the rest of their time. Participants explained that they had great difficulty or were unable to create time for themselves to be physically active because of the need to look after their children’s needs and activities. This sentiment was commonly mentioned by women participants.

“Again there’s fitness classes at the rec centre and there’s things I could sign up for but I am not choosing to sign myself up for things whether that’s the time they’re being posted feels like it conflicts with nap or after school play or dinner or bath and bedtime, like there’s always going to be a thing I don’t feel I can leave because I’m the mom” (P27, woman).

Reinforcement

Participants explained four main barriers within this domain, but the most discussed were the difficulties in initiating physical activity and having bad days that resulted in no physical activity. Often, participants noted that they were unlikely to partake in physical activity because they were not in a routine and getting started again was extremely difficult.

“And what I really found was every night before the class, I’d be like … I don’t want to go, I’m just tired, we just had supper, I feel blah I don’t want to go … So that’s a real disincentive for me” (P13, woman).

Others explained that when they had bad days, the last thing they wanted to do was any type of physical activity, preferring sedentary activities to destress and relax.

“Having a tough day at work whether it’s physically or mentally tough, kind of like I just want to go home, I’m done. … I’m just not feeling like doing anything else, I just want to go home and relax … The motivation is like not there for me, it’s really not” (P6, woman).

Facilitators for physical activity

Facilitators to physical activity discussed by participants aligned with the opportunity of the COM-B model including knowledge, social influences and environmental context and resources.

Knowledge

Facilitators identified by participants included understanding their own body, why they were partaking in certain activities (e.g., weightlifting for strength, hiking for stress management, etc.), and the benefits to be gained from being physically active. Some participants explained how important knowing themselves, their abilities and personal health helped focus their engagement in physical activity:

“I think you need to be very careful in terms of how you choose your activities. I know for myself I’m going through menopause, I’m losing muscle mass so for me I’m focusing a lot more on strength training now that I did when I was 20 years younger” (P44, woman).

Further, knowledge about their local environment and how to stay safe while being physically active was viewed as an important component of being active. This knowledge included understanding how to navigate the various seasons, having safety plans in place, and avoiding unwanted contact with wildlife.

“You know, I’m well aware that I need to let people know where I am and I have a Garmin GPS thing so if I’m out of cell service I can call for help and I don’t hike alone. Take bear spray. “(P29, woman).

Social influences

Participants identified many social influences contributing to their physical activity including support from friends, spouses, and family. Physical activity was often described as facilitating participants’ time with their family and encouraging family bonding.

“We want to do more hiking as a family, again as a way to see and explore and do things and right now the biggest thing that I’m loving with the boys is taking them to the swimming pool … so I am choosing activities that we’re doing as a family that are successful for their ages and stages” (P27, woman).

It was also made clear by participants that their communities played a large part in supporting them to be physically active. Communities were described as being physically active in general, and participants noted social opportunities encouraged them to increase their physical activity. Many local groups and organizations were named as supports for physical activity and ways by which to engage in socializing.

“There’s quite a lot of groups here, running groups and hiking groups and there’s a meet-up group and Facebook groups a lot that are a bunch of people looking for people to go with them or just do things so.” (P18, woman).

Environmental context & resources

Access to the outdoors, availability of various activities, sufficient facilities and infrastructure, and community programming were identified by participants as facilitators of physical activity. Participants discussed how having infrastructure available to accommodate activity during seasonal weather changes enabled them to be active year round.

“So pretty much all of this terrain that we use we can use all year long and there’s something different we can do depending on the season whether it’s there’s snow or it’s warm or whatever.” (P30, woman).

The culture of being physically active was also prominent in participants’ communities. When participants saw other people in the community doing activities, it increased their desire and likelihood of participating in physical activities. Municipalities encouraged activity by prioritizing and funding appropriate infrastructure and resources.

“[We have] local interest groups, whether it’s ski tour associations, hiking groups, we have a Trails Alliance here, we have an amazing amount of trail networks … both winter and summer for biking. We are an active community, we are like-minded people in this community and we will support that as best as we can.” (P47, man).

Discussion

The aim of this project was to systematically map barriers and facilitators to physical activity for adults living in rural communities. By mapping interview findings to the TDF, we have identified barriers to physical activity related to the opportunities to be active (e.g., influences from social norms and support) and motivation (e.g., highlighting benefits of being active, balancing work and family responsibilities). Facilitators were commonly associated with opportunities and capability (e.g., local knowledge and beliefs in capability).

Previous studies have identified barriers to physical activity in rural communities related more to opportunities and environmental factors than individual motivational or resource-related factors [16]. Lack of access to opportunities for activity is a consistent finding across all sociodemographic factors for rural communities, with 70% of rural participants reporting at least one environmental barrier to activity [16]. In the current study, specific environmental (e.g., opportunity) barriers related to costs associated with activity, rules and government regulations impacting access to recreation, and aspects of the natural and built environment including weather, wildlife, and lack of infrastructure. In alignment with our previous work, some environmental factors, such as wildlife and weather, were related to limited access to indoor recreation opportunities, suggesting physical activity engagement in rural communities may be more sensitive to seasonal weather patterns [34]. The increased reliance on outdoor recreation can be viewed as both a facilitator (e.g., close proximity) and barrier (e.g., not always accessible) to activity, dependent on the state of infrastructure in communities and individual characteristics such as skills, knowledge, and comfort accessing outdoor spaces.

According to the Behaviour Change Wheel, opportunity-level barriers can be best addressed with intervention functions related to environmental restructuring, regulations, and legislation [24]. For rural communities, examples of intervention functions and potential policy levers include providing better signage and trail markings, prioritisation and support for cycling routes and safe active transportation, and the development of walkable infrastructure and investing in appropriate facilities. Similarly, considering how climate change may impact rural communities and exposure to phenomenon such as wildfire smoke and animal interactions, appropriate infrastructure may facilitate feelings of safety and reduce harm associated with outdoor recreation.

Social influences were identified by participants as important factors impacting physical activity behaviour in rural communities. This was noted through opportunity aspects of the COM-B (social influences and environmental context) and related to aspects of motivation. The role of social connections or support as a facilitator of physical activity is well covered in the literature in urban and rural communities across the lifespan [35, 36]. There is some evidence to suggest lack of social support is a greater barrier to physical activity for rural compared to urban residents [19]. Related to social opportunity, theory-informed intervention functions may include education, such as media campaigns focusing on community members being active or using local infrastructure. Facilitating interactions between inactive and active people can help encourage inactive people to be more active [37] and may be one mechanism by which people find pleasure and enjoyment from physical activity. For community groups working in rural communities, organizing group activities to encourage people to be active while engaging socially could address education, training and enablement intervention functions. Thus, intervention strategies could focus on both adding intervention functions that increase social supports (e.g., opportunity) or promoting available social supports to increase behaviours (e.g., motivation).

The Behaviour Change Wheel does not provide a blueprint for intervention design but instead is used to identify theory-based functions or strategies that can be applied or integrated into intervention design to elicit behaviour change given the context [38]. Designing interventions based on the Behaviour Change Wheel requires judgement and an understanding of context, to be weighed against theoretically driven intervention functions and policies predicted to impact a particular behaviour [38]. Thus, while we have provided some intervention functions based on our analysis in rural communities throughout British Columbia, specific discussion and partnerships with local communities would be necessary to understand how to enact and deliver interventions in each rural community while considering APEASE criteria (affordability, practicability, effectiveness/cost-effectiveness, acceptability, side effects/safety, equity) [24].

Limitations

While the focus of this project was to understand factors influencing physical activity behaviour specifically, guidance for understanding behaviour typically includes understanding competing behaviours, such as sedentary behaviour. Future work should consider physical activity participation in the context of 24-hour movement behaviours and alignment of each behaviour with 24-hour movement guidelines.

Our sample represents mostly women with relatively high socioeconomic status. Due to this sample bias, our ability to conduct intersectional analysis was limited and would require a more intentional recruitment strategy to recruit individuals with different racial or socioeconomic backgrounds. Additionally, while we did have participants from various community sizes across rurality designations, almost half of our sample were from communities with a population between 1000 and 4999 people. While participants from Indigenous backgrounds were included in our sample, we have not conducted an intentional analysis on Indigenous perspectives on physical activity which would need to be a focus of specific additional work.

Conclusions

Addressing barriers and leveraging facilitators to physical activity can help improve population health and prevent noncommunicable disease. Factors influencing physical activity for rural communities include those related to social opportunity, policy & programming, and infrastructure. While there are numerous barriers reported, ample opportunity and policy levers could enable increased physical activity and improve health equity across the rural-urban continuum. Policy implications of this work include the identification of theory informed intervention strategies such as: environmental restructuring to facilitate incidental physical activity during daily life, education on safety (e.g., bear aware, lighting on sidewalks) and modelling local usage of existing trails or facilities (e.g., creating video vignettes of local people using infrastructure), ensuring cultural relevance and interest in activities and infrastructure (e.g., promoting use of local lakes for fishing or outdoor recreation) and enablement by enacting supportive policies (e.g., childcare). Collaboration with local communities to fully understand context is critical for the adaptation of evidence-based interventions to individual rural or small communities.
