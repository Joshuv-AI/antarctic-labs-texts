Region and Cell‐Selective Induction of Zbtb16/Plzf by Multiple Stressors in the Adult Murine Hypothalamus
Authors: Mina Roueinfar, Pardis Mohammadzadeh, Luke A. Schwerdtfeger, Robert J. Handa, Stuart A. Tobet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, a neuroendocrine system that regulates responses related to feeding, reproduction, and aggression, among other homeostatic functions. Stressors significantly impact gene expression along the HPA axis and in hypothalamic nuclei that drive it, including the paraventricular nucleus (PVN). To identify genetic regulators of stress responses in the PVN, adult mice underwent 2 h of multi‐modal stress before gene expression profiles were analyzed using bulk RNA sequencing. A transcription factor zinc finger and BTB domain containing 16 (Zbtb16), also known as PLZF, was identified as a stress responsive, glucocorticoid receptor (GR) target in the PVN. Zbtb16 mRNA expression was increased by two‐fold in male and female mice within 2 h of restraint stress or injection of a synthetic glucocorticoid, dexamethasone (DEX). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) confirmed Zbtb16 protein expression and localization in the PVN following 20 min of restraint stress and 4 h of recovery. Cellular analyses revealed that Zbtb16 was highly expressed in CRH neurons in the PVN, neurons routinely activated post‐stress as indicated by colocalization with c‐FOS. Adult mice were also exposed to an immune stress by injection of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) to assess Zbtb16 regulation. Expanded analyses indicated that the cell specificity of Zbtb16 expression was region‐specific, colocalizing with CRH neurons in the mid‐PVN but more in astrocytes surrounding the PVN. These findings identify Zbtb16 as a glucocorticoid‐ and cytokine‐inducible transcriptional regulator with region‐ and cell type‐specific roles in PVN stress circuitry.

Introduction

Environmental or psychological stressors can have negative effects on physiologic systems, including immune (Glaser and Kiecolt‐Glaser 2005), metabolic (Rabasa and Dickson 2016), and neuronal (Oyola and Handa 2017) functions. Long‐term stress responses increase the risk of several diseases, including mood and metabolic disorders and cardiovascular disease (Yaribeygi et al. 2017). The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis is a major regulator of stress responses, working to maintain physiological homeostasis (de Kloet et al. 2005; Sapolsky et al. 2000; Sheng et al. 2021). Stressors frequently influence HPA axis gene expression, including neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) that are involved in regulating hormonal, behavioral, and autonomic responses to environmental perturbations (Ferguson et al. 2008). Neurons in the PVN synthesize and secrete neuropeptides, such as corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH) and arginine vasopressin (AVP), in response to stress (Gibbs et al. 1984; Whitnall 1993). The secretion of these neuropeptides into the hypothalamic‐hypophyseal circulation regulates the systemic portion of the HPA response, leading to adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) release from the pituitary and subsequent glucocorticoid release from the adrenal gland (Flak et al. 2011; Ostrander et al. 2006). Glucocorticoids exert their effects by binding to glucocorticoid receptors (GRs), which belong to the nuclear steroid receptor superfamily (Timmermans et al. 2019; Vandevyver et al. 2014). Glucocorticoids and their receptors can induce the expression of target genes and regulate a wide range of biological processes (Garabedian et al. 2017; Koning et al. 2019) in response to stress.

The zinc finger and BTB domain containing 16 (Zbtb16) is a transcription factor that belongs to the Krüppel‐like zinc finger family and is highly conserved across species (Suliman et al. 2012). Zbtb16 plays a central role in many developmental and biological processes including cell proliferation and differentiation, and energy metabolism, among others (Cheng et al. 2021). Zbtb16 has been identified as a crucial regulator of immune system function, and loss of Zbtb16 leads to increased expression of inflammatory cytokines (Sadler, Rossello, et al. 2015; Sadler, Suliman, et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2024). Zbtb16 carries out these functions as a transcription factor or chromatin remodeler, activating or repressing gene expression in a context‐dependent manner.

In development, mice lacking functional Zbtb16 exhibit limb defects (Lin et al. 2019; Usui et al. 2021). The role of Zbtb16 in regulating HPA axis response to stress in the adult brain is not fully understood (Cheng et al. 2020). The current study investigated the mRNA and protein expression of Zbtb16 in the adult mouse PVN under different stress conditions including restraint stress, glucocorticoid administration (dexamethasone, DEX), and systemic inflammation induced by tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) injection, while accounting for sex as a relevant biological variable. Within the PVN, colocalization was examined of immunoreactive Zbtb16 with AVP, CRH, and oxytocin (Oxt) neurons in the PVN along with a relationship to astrocytes and microglia. The findings suggest that Zbtb16 is a stress‐ and cytokine‐responsive transcription factor that may regulate neuronal and glial responses to stress within specific PVN subpopulations.

Material and Methods

Animals

For multi‐modal stress experiment, male and female C57BL/6N mice aged 2–5 months were used, while for DEX and restraint experiments, C57BL/6J mice of the same age were utilized. These sub‐strains were used based on availability and established use of specific experimental paradigms and transgenic lines on defined genetic backgrounds. Importantly, all experimental comparisons were conducted within sub‐strain and condition, rather than directly between sub‐strains. All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and Colorado State University Lab Animal Resources under protocols CoA # 17‐7702A and CoA # 5483. Mice were housed on a 12‐h light/dark cycle with access to standard chow (Envigo TD.2919) and water ad libitum.

Transgenic Mouse Strains

Transgenic animals to identify CRH, AVP, and oxytocin (OT) cell phenotypes based on selective promoters driving TdTomato were previously generated in the laboratory as follows: Crh‐IRES‐Cre; Ai14 were generated by crossing B6(Cg)‐Crhtml(cre)Zjh/J (Crh‐IRES‐Cre; Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME; RRID:IMSR_JAX:012704) with B6.Cg Gt (ROSA)26Sortml4(CAG‐TdTomato)Hze/J (Ai14; Jackson Laboratories; RRID:IMSR_JAX:007914) strains (Heck et al. 2020). AVP‐IRES2‐Cre‐D:Ai14 were generated by crossing the B6.Cg‐Avp tm1.1(cre)Hze /J (AVP‐IRES2‐Cre‐D) (Jackson Laboratories; RRID:IMSR_JAX:023530) (Harris et al. 2014) with Ai14 animals. Oxytocin‐Ires Cre:Ai14 animals were also bred in‐house by crossing B6;129S‐Oxt tm1.1(cre)Dolsn /J (Oxytocin‐Ires Cre) (Jackson Laboratories; RRID:IMSR_JAX:024234) (Wu et al. 2012) with Ai14 mice.

Multi‐Modal Stress Exposure

Male and female mice were divided into control and stressed groups. Control mice were left in their home cages while the stress treatment group was exposed to multi‐modal stress (MMS) for 2 h before being returned to their home cage and euthanized 90 min later (n = 3 males, 4 females per group). Mice exposed to MMS were restrained inside a mouse DecapiCone (Braintree Scientific, Product No: MDC‐200), and the bag was tied at the base of the tail to prevent escape. The DecapiCone was taped and secured to a laboratory shaker (210 rpm) and was placed under a bright light (1000 lm) close to speakers playing hip‐hop music at 90 db (Lin et al. 2018; Maras et al. 2014). After stress recovery, animals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane before decapitation. Female animals were screened for estrous cycle stage using vaginal cytology. Cage mates were processed simultaneously to avoid unnecessary stress of temporarily single housing.

Bulk RNA‐Seq

After MMS exposure and brain removal, tissue was flash frozen in 2‐methyl‐butane (MX0760‐1; Sigma Aldrich) on dry ice and maintained frozen at −80°C until further processing. Flash‐frozen brains were sectioned on a cryostat (Leica CM3050 S, Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL) at 300 μm. The PVN was sampled using stainless‐steel micro‐punches (0.99‐mm‐diameter cannula) obtained from three consecutive coronal sections per animal, spanning approximately 900 μm along the rostral–caudal axis. In the mouse, the PVN extends approximately 600–700 μm along this axis; therefore, this approach was used to ensure complete and consistent sampling of the nucleus across animals. Punches were centered on the PVN using anatomical landmarks from the mouse brain atlas, and given the size of the punch relative to the PVN, it is expected that immediately adjacent hypothalamic tissue was included. This sampling strategy was applied uniformly across all animals and experimental conditions. RNA extraction from PVN punches was performed using RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Heck et al. 2020). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometry, and only samples with 260/280 ratios between 1.90 and 2.10 were used for downstream processing. RNA integrity was assessed prior to library preparation, and only samples with RNA integrity numbers (RIN) ranging from 7.1 to 7.3 were submitted for sequencing. For library preparation, 1 μg of total RNA per sample was used. Poly‐A mRNA enrichment was performed using NEBNext Poly (A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB #E7490; New England Biolabs, MA) followed by library preparation via NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep kit (NEB # E7760; New England Biolabs, MA) according to manufacturer directions. Libraries were sequenced at the Colorado State University NGS core using the Illumina NextSeq 500 sequencing system and a High Output 75 cycle kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). After sequencing, raw fastq reads were given to Genewiz to process for bioinformatic analysis: Adapter sequences were trimmed to and reads were aligned to the ENSEMBL mouse reference genome using STAR aligner v.2.5.2b. Unique gene counts were generated using “feature counts” from the Subread package v.1.5.2 with only uniquely mapped within exon regions included. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R, with Wald testing was used to generate p‐values and Log2 fold changes. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed when the adjusted p‐value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change > 1. Gene ontology analysis was conducted on significantly differentially expressed genes using GeneSCF, with the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) GO biological process list used for functional clustering and statistical assessment (GENEWIZ, South Plainfield, NJ).

Dexamethasone Injection

Male mice (n = 3–4/group) were subcutaneously injected with either 1.2 mg/kg DEX (Sigma‐Aldrich) dissolved in 25% Trappsol (w:v, Hydroxypropyl Beta Cyclodextrin CTD Inc., in 0.9% [v:v] saline) or 25% Trappsol vehicle for control animals (Sheng et al. 2023). For mRNA expression analysis, brains were harvested 2 h after DEX or vehicle injection. This time point was selected to capture early glucocorticoid receptor–mediated transcriptional responses, which are known to occur rapidly following DEX administration. PVNs were isolated on ice and processed for digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) to quantify Zbtb16 mRNA levels. For protein expression analysis via immunohistochemistry (IHC), mice were allowed to recover for 4 h after stress before being transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (w:v) in 0.1‐M phosphate buffer (PB), pH 7.4. This interval was chosen to allow sufficient time for downstream protein‐level changes and cellular localization events to develop while still reflecting acute glucocorticoid effects.

Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNF‐α) Treatment

Male and female mice (n = 3–4 per group) received intraperitoneal injections of 63 μg/kg (Biesmans et al. 2015) recombinant murine TNF‐α (PeproTech, Cat# 315‐01A). TNF‐α was reconstituted at a stock concentration of 1 mg/mL in sterile water and stored in aliquots at −20°C to minimize freeze–thaw cycles. On the day of injection, doses were calculated based on individual body weights recorded the day prior. Control animals received an equivalent volume of saline. For protein expression analysis via IHC, mice were allowed to recover for 4 h post‐injection before being transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (w:v) in 0.1‐M phosphate buffer (PB), pH 7.4. Female animals were sacrificed during estrus, as determined by vaginal cytology showing a predominance of cornified epithelial cells. All cage mates were processed simultaneously to avoid stress associated with temporary single housing.

Restraint

Male and female mice received either no restraint or were restrained for 20 min followed by 2 or 4 h of recovery in their home cage prior to sacrifice (n = 5–6/group). Restraint stress was performed using a Mouse DecapiCone (Braintree Scientific, MA) tied off at the end to prevent escape. The animals being restrained were simultaneously placed in DecapiCones and set together in their home cages for 20 min before being released from the restrain condition to their home cages and left undisturbed until sacrifice. Female animals were sacrificed during estrus as determined by vaginal smears showing a predominance of cornified epithelial cells. All cage mates were processed simultaneously to prevent unassociated stress of temporary single housing.

Digital Droplet PCR (ddPCR)

Reverse transcription digital droplet PCR (RT‐ddPCR) was employed to quantify the absolute transcript levels of Zbtb16. Mice were exposed to restrained stress as mentioned above, and after 2 h of recovery, PVNs were micro‐dissected on ice and preserved in 1× DNA/RNA shield solution before RNA purification using a Quick‐RNA Miniprep Plus kit (Zymo Research; Irvine, CA). The cDNA synthesis was carried with an iScript cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio‐Rad, Munich, Germany) using 40 ng of RNA from each sample. ddPCR was performed on a QX200 system (DG8 cartridge, QX200 droplet generator, PX1 PCR plate sealer, C1000 Touch thermal cycler, and QX200 droplet reader; Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA). The ddPCR supermix was prepared by combining 10‐ng cDNA, 10 μL of EvaGreen (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA), and 5 μM of primers and nuclease‐free water up to a total volume of 20 μL. Primer used specific to mouse Zbtb16 was (forward: CTG TGG GCA TGA AGG CTG AG and reverse: GGA ACC GTT TTC CGC AGA GT). Actin‐β was used as a reference gene for relative quantification using (forward: GAGGTATCCTGACCCTGAAGTA and reverse: CACACGCAGCTCATTGTAGA). All systems and reagents used for ddPCR were sourced from Bio‐Rad (Hercules, CA). Droplet generation, PCR amplification of template molecules in each droplet, reading, and quantification of absolute template expression in copies/μL were conducted using QuantaSoft software, as detailed previously (Mohammadzadeh et al. 2023). Female animals were sacrificed during the estrus phase, as determined by vaginal cytology demonstrating a predominance of cornified epithelial cells. All cage mates were processed concurrently to minimize stress associated with temporary single housing.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice used for IHC were transcardially perfused with phosphate buffered saline (0.05‐M PBS, pH 7.4), followed by fixation with freshly solubilized 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1‐M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Brains were removed, post‐fixed in 4% PFA in 0.1‐M phosphate buffer (PB), pH 7.4 overnight, and then placed into 1X cold PBS buffer. In brief, fixed brains were embedded in 4% agarose (Sigma Aldrich), and 50‐μm coronal sections were cut using a vibrating microtome (VT1000S; Leica microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Free‐floating serial sections were collected in 1× PBS at 4°C. Sections were then incubated in 0.1‐M glycine for 30 min at 4°C, followed by three 5‐min PBS washes. Sections then received 0.5% (v:v) sodium borohydride at 4°C for 15 min, followed by three 5‐min washes with PBS. For sections being processed for anti‐Zbtb16, tissue received antigen retrieval incubation in sodium citrate (0.05 M, pH 8) at room temperature for 1 h and then transferred to preheated (80°C) sodium citrate buffer for 30 min before returning to room temperature for 30 min (Dellovade et al. 2001; Frahm and Tobet 2015). Following antigen retrieval, sections were washed three times in PBS for 5 min and then blocked in a PBS solution with 5% (v:v) normal goat serum (NGS; Lampire Biological, Pipersville, PA), 1% (v:v) hydrogen peroxide, and 0.3% (v:v) Triton X‐100 (TX) for 30 min. Tissue was then incubated with primary antisera containing 0.5% Triton X‐100 and 1% (v:v) BSA and rabbit polyclonal anti‐Zbtb16 (3.55 μg/mL; ABclonal# A58863), rabbit polyclonal anti‐c‐Fos (1:2000; Abcam, ab190289) for two nights at 4°C. After incubation in primary antisera, sections were washed in RT PBS with 1% (v:v) NGS and 0.02% TX four times for 15 min. Next, sections labelled for Zbtb16 were incubated for 2 h at RT with PBS containing 1% NGS, 0.32% TX with either Cy3 conjugated goat anti‐rabbit (1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc.; #111‐166‐045) or Alexa Flour 488 conjugated goat anti‐rabbit (1:500; Invitrogen; #A‐11008). Sections labelled for C‐Fos were incubated in Biotin‐SP‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit (1:2500; Jackson Immune Research; # 711‐065‐152). Subsequently, sections were washed four times at 15 min intervals in PBS with 0.02% TX. Sections that received biotin‐conjugated secondary were placed in Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated streptavidin (1:500; Invitrogen; #S32354) constituted in PBS with 0.32% TX for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were washed in PBS, mounted on slides and cover slipped with aqueous mounting medium (Aqua‐Poly/Mount, Polysciences, Warrington, PA).

Antibody Specificity and Validation

To assess the specificity of the Zbtb16 antibody used for immunohistochemistry, multiple validation approaches were employed. First, control sections processed in parallel with omission of the primary antibody showed no detectable immunoreactivity, confirming the absence of nonspecific secondary antibody binding. In addition, Zbtb16 expression was independently evaluated using an in situ hybridization probe generated in our laboratory. Zbtb16 mRNA signal was detected in the same PVN subregions that exhibited Zbtb16 immunoreactivity, demonstrating concordance between transcript and protein localization. These data are presented in (Figure S1).

Sequential Immunofluorescence

Free‐floating coronal brain sections containing the PVN were processed for sequential immunofluorescence. Antigen retrieval for Zbtb16 was performed as described above. Sections were blocked in 5% normal goat serum with 0.3% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with rabbit monoclonal anti‐Zbtb16 (2.5 μg/mL; Abclonal #A21185) for 48 h at 4°C. Following washes, sections were incubated with Cy3‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit secondary (1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch #111‐166‐045) for 2 h at room temperature. Sections were then washed 3 × 15 min in PBS and immediately progressed to the second round of labeling. For neuronal co‐labeling, sections were incubated in mouse anti‐NeuN (1:1000; Invitrogen #MA5‐33103) overnight at 4°C, washed, and then incubated with Alexa flour‐488 goat anti‐mouse secondary (1:500; Invitrogen) for 2 h at room temperature. For astrocyte co‐labeling, sections were incubated in rabbit anti‐S100β (Novus Biologicals, recombinant rabbit monoclonal; SC57‐02) overnight at 4°C, washed, and then incubated with Alexa flour‐488 donkey anti‐rabbit secondary (1:500; Invitrogen) for 2 h at room temperature. Sections were washed in PBS, mounted on slides, and cover slipped with aqueous mounting medium (Aqua‐Poly/Mount, Polysciences, Warrington, PA).

Anatomical Definition of PVN Subregions

Along the rostral–caudal axis, the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) exhibits well‐defined anatomical and cellular differences. In the present study, PVN subregions were defined based on coronal level, cytoarchitecture, and proximity to the third ventricle, in accordance with standard mouse brain atlases (Allen Mouse Brain Atlas). Rostral PVN was defined as the anterior extent of the nucleus where the PVN first becomes visible, characterized by a smaller cross‐sectional area and a relatively lower density of CRH‐expressing neurons. The mid‐PVN was defined as the central portion of the nucleus, corresponding to the largest cross‐sectional area, close apposition to the third ventricle, and the highest density of CRH neurons. Caudal PVN was defined as the posterior extent of the nucleus, where the PVN tapers, CRH neuron density decreases, and fiber tracts and non‐neuronal elements become more prominent. These anatomical criteria were used to ensure consistent regional sampling across animals and experimental conditions.

Tissue Imaging and Analysis

Images were acquired using either a Zeiss LSM 880 or Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope, both equipped with Axiocam 503 mono cameras (Carl Zeiss Inc.) and 40×/0.95 Corr Plan‐Apochromat oil‐immersion objectives. Image analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ v1.54f). For quantification of Zbtb16 immunoreactivity, single‐plane images were selected based on PVN cellular density. A rectangular region of interest (ROI; 269 × 300 μm) was drawn within the PVN. Images were thresholded based on optical density, and measurements were made using the Fiji “Analyze Particles” function with the “Composite ROIs” option. The PVN ROI was then applied to thresholded Zbtb16 images to quantify the area of the PVN exhibiting Zbtb16 immunoreactivity.

For CRH analysis, images were thresholded based on optical density, and measurements were made using the same ROI defined above before using the “Analyze Particles” function with “Composite ROIs.” CRH ROIs were applied to the corresponding Zbtb16 images to determine the extent of Zbtb16 immunoreactivity within CRH‐positive regions. Colocalization analysis was performed in the mid‐PVN using two 50‐μm‐thick coronal sections per animal, spaced 150 μm apart, spanning bregma coordinates −0.59 to −0.95 mm (The Allen Mouse Brain Atlas). Quantification was conducted in three animals and yielded consistent results across animals, with approximately 72% of Zbtb16‐positive neurons colocalized with CRH neurons.

Sample Size Determination

Sample sizes were guided by an a priori power analysis suggesting that eight animals per group would be optimal. Final analyzed group sizes ended up being four to five animals due to exclusion of animals with compromised PVN tissue resulting from technical damage during brain extraction. Exclusions were determined prior to data analysis and were unrelated to experimental outcomes. The reduced sample sizes did not decrease statistical power as the effect sizes were large. The results were consistent across animals and experimental approaches.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism (v10, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA), and results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For the DEX experiment, an unpaired Student's t‐test without Welch's correction was used to compare Zbtb16 mRNA and protein levels. To assess the effects of sex (male vs. female) and treatment (restraint vs. vehicle) on Zbtb16 mRNA and protein expression, a two‐way ANOVA was conducted followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post hoc test. Similarly, the effects of sex and treatment (TNF‐α vs. saline) on Zbtb16 protein levels were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. For all statistical comparisons, excluding bulk RNA‐seq data, a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MMS Altered PVN Gene Expression

To evaluate the impact of stress on gene expression, PVNs were collected from control and stressed male and female mice for bulk RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) analysis. Gene expression profiles in the PVN were significantly altered by stress. In males, 16 genes were up‐regulated, and two genes were down‐regulated (Table S1). Notable up‐regulated genes included Hr, Zbtb16, and Plin4, while Efcab6 was down‐regulated. In female mice, a total of 38 genes were up‐regulated, and none were down‐regulated (Table S2). Volcano plots show differential regulation of genes with green points being down‐regulated and red points up‐regulated. Significance was determined when genes had both a log2 fold change of at least 0.75 and a −Log10 (adjusted p value) > 1.25. Notably, Zbtb16 was significantly altered only in male animals (Figure 1A,B).

Red dots indicate significantly upregulated genes, and green dots indicate significantly downregulated genes (n = 3 per group for males; n = 4 per group for females).

Zbtb16 Protein and RNA Expression in the PVN Were Glucocorticoid Dependent

Injection of the synthetic glucocorticoid agonist DEX resulted in greater Zbtb16 immunoreactivity within cells of the PVN after 4 h compared to saline treated animals (Figure 2A,B). The change in immunoreactivity was quantified by counting Zbtb16 immunoreactive cell numbers. There were more than twice as many immunoreactive cells per ROI in the PVNs of DEX injected mice compared to saline injected animals (p < 0.01; Figure 2C). DEX also increased Zbtb16 mRNA by ~4‐fold within 2 h of injection (p < 0.001; Figure 2D). The anatomical pattern of Zbtb16 immunoreactivity in the PVN was further supported by in situ hybridization data, which demonstrated Zbtb16 mRNA localization in the same PVN regions and cell populations (Figure S1).

Zbtb16 Protein and RNA Expression in PVN After Restraint Stress

As Zbtb16 mRNA and protein expression was glucocorticoid‐dependent, the next experiment tested whether restraint stress was sufficient to alter Zbtb16 expression in males and females. Mice were restrained for 20 min, given 4 h to recover and then perfused and processed for immunoreactive Zbtb16. The number of immunoreactive cells per ROI was greater in male and female mice exposed to restraint stress compared to control (Figure 3A–C). The number of c‐FOS immunoreactive cells also was elevated after restraint (Figure 3B). In a separate cohort of mice, Zbtb16 mRNA was elevated at 2 h after 20 min of restraint stress relative to control (Figure 3D).

TNFα‐Induced Upregulation of Zbtb16 Expression

A separate experiment evaluated whether inflammatory stress signaling modulated Zbtb16 expression in the PVN. Adult male and female mice received an intraperitoneal injection of TNFα (63 μg/kg) and were allowed to recover for 6 h before perfusion and tissue processing. Immunolabeling revealed a greater number of Zbtb16‐immunoreactive cells in male and female mice following TNFα administration compared to saline‐treated controls (Figure 4A–C). Similarly, c‐FOS immunoreactivity was also elevated in TNFα‐treated animals (Figure 4B,D), indicating cellular activation in response to peripheral inflammatory challenge.

Zbtb16 Exhibits Region‐ and Cell‐Type–Selective Expression in the PVN

Adult male CRH‐IRES‐Cre; Ai14 tdTomato mice (2–4 months) were subjected to 20 min of restraint stress and euthanized 4 h later. In brain sections containing the mid‐PVN, double immunolabeling for Zbtb16 and CRH tdTomato–positive neurons revealed 72.0% ± 5.7% (SEM, n = 4 mice) colocalization (Figure 5A–C). Arrows in the merged panel (Figure 5C) highlight CRH:tdTomato neurons that co‐express Zbtb16. To assess neuronal activation, c‐FOS immunolabeling was performed on adjacent sections. Co‐localization of c‐FOS with CRH:tdTomato neurons confirmed stress‐induced activation of CRH cells in the mid‐PVN (Figure 5D–F). These findings suggest a cell‐type–specific induction of Zbtb16 in CRH neurons following acute stress.

To determine whether Zbtb16 expression extended to other neuropeptide‐expressing populations in the PVN, brain sections were examined from Oxytocin‐IRES‐Cre; Ai14 and AVP‐IRES2‐Cre‐D; Ai14 male mice following acute restraint stress. In the medial PVN, Zbtb16 immunoreactivity showed minimal colocalization with either oxytocin (Figure 6A–C) or vasopressin neurons (Figure 6G–I), though occasional Zbtb16 signal was observed in close proximity to these cell types. Arrows in merged panels indicate rare instances of membrane‐level or compartment‐specific overlap within the resolution of the image. Similarly, c‐FOS immunoreactivity was largely absent from oxytocin (Figure 6D–F) and vasopressin (Figure 6J–L) neurons, suggesting that these neuropeptide populations were not robustly activated under the same stress conditions. These findings support a selective engagement of Zbtb16 and c‐FOS in CRH‐expressing neurons compared to other PVN neuroendocrine cells.

To examine the region‐ and cell‐type‐specific expression of Zbtb16 within the hypothalamic PVN, immunolabeling was performed, following peripheral TNFα administration using NeuN (neuronal marker) and S100β (astrocyte marker) in the mid and caudal PVN regions (Figure 7). In the mid‐PVN, Zbtb16 immunoreactivity strongly colocalized with NeuN‐positive neurons (Figure 7A–C), while dual labeled S100β‐positive astrocytes were primarily localized to the periphery of the PVN, where only occasional overlap with Zbtb16 was observed (Figure 7G–I). We did not observe Zbtb16 co‐localization with microglia (IBA‐1; data not shown). By contrast, in the caudal PVN, Zbtb16 expression showed minimal co‐localization with NeuN (Figure 7D–F) and was instead predominantly co‐localized with S100β‐positive astrocytes (Figure 7J–L). These findings highlight a spatially distinct cellular distribution of Zbtb16 within the PVN, with neuronal expression concentrated in the mid‐region and astrocytic expression becoming more prominent caudally.

Discussion

In the current study, Zbtb16 was identified as a stress‐ and immune‐responsive transcription factor in the PVN of the hypothalamus. Based on multiple acute stress paradigms, Zbtb16 mRNA and protein expression were induced in the PVN of adult mice within 2 h, with predominant co‐localization in CRH‐expressing neurons of the mid‐PVN. This induction occurred similarly in male and female mice. Peripheral administration of TNFα enhanced Zbtb16 protein expression, with astrocytic colocalization observed predominantly along the periphery of the mid PVN and more diffusely scattered in the caudal PVN. These findings highlight a region‐ and cell‐type specific pattern of Zbtb16 expression, suggesting that it may integrate glucocorticoid and immune signals within distinct PVN microenvironments. Zbtb16 may serve as a potential regulator of neuroendocrine stress responses and neuroimmune communication in the adult hypothalamus.

To identify candidate stress‐responsive genes, we employed a multi‐modal stress (MMS) paradigm, which combines restraint, shaking, bright light, and auditory stimulation for 2 h, followed by a 90‐min recovery period prior to tissue collection (Lin et al. 2018; Maras et al. 2014). Each component engages overlapping but distinct stress pathways: restraint primarily activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, bright light represents an environmental stressor, auditory stimulation provides sensory stress, and shaking introduces vestibular and nonspecific stress. Collectively, MMS may mimic the complexity of naturalistic stress and generates robust transcriptional responses. Following transcriptomic analysis, Zbtb16 was selected as a candidate based on its stress induction, glucocorticoid responsiveness, and role in immune regulation. To validate these properties, discrete paradigms were used: dexamethasone administration to test glucocorticoid sensitivity, acute 20‐min restraint to assess rapid stress induction, and TNFα administration to test immune‐related induction. Each paradigm successfully induced Zbtb16 expression, confirming that it responds to multiple stress‐relevant pathways.

Acute restraint stress or systemic administration of the GR agonist DEX induced Zbtb16 expression in the PVN in the current study, confirming glucocorticoid sensitivity. In the mid‐PVN, Zbtb16 colocalized ~72% with CRH‐expressing neurons which are known stress responsive cells (Aguilera and Liu 2012). This cell‐type specificity was further supported by c‐FOS immunolabeling, which confirmed stress‐induced activation of CRH neurons in adjacent sections. Zbtb16 immunoreactivity was observed in nuclear and potentially membrane compartments of disparate AVP and oxytocin neurons, allowing for potential membrane‐level interactions rather than direct co‐expression. Zbtb16 is a nuclear transcription factor that undergoes various post‐translational modifications and exhibits dynamic subcellular localization depending on cellular context and stimulation (Suliman et al. 2012). In human KG1a cells, a myeloid leukemia line used to study hematopoietic stress responses, IL‐3 stimulation caused Zbtb16 to translocate out of nuclei as an inactivation response (Doulatov et al. 2009). In CHO‐K1 cells, angiotensin II stimulation induced Zbtb16 localization first to the membrane and later to nuclei for activation (Senbonmatsu et al. 2003). Such spatial relationships raise the possibility of paracrine or membrane‐associated signaling in the PVN that may merit further exploration.

Prior work has linked Zbtb16/PLZF to glial biology outside the hypothalamus. For example, Usi et al. reported that Zbtb16 knockout mice show increased cortical microglia, oligodendrocyte developmental defects, and impaired myelination, indicating a role for Zbtb16 in regulating glial function (Usui et al. 2021). In addition, large‐scale expression resources, including the Human Protein Atlas and single‐cell atlases such as HypoMap, detect ZBTB16 across multiple brain cell classes, including astrocyte‐associated signatures, supporting the plausibility that astrocytes may express ZBTB16 under certain conditions. To dissect stimulus‐ and region‐specific patterns of Zbtb16 expression, cell‐type localization was analyzed under stress and immune challenge conditions. In the restraint stress paradigm, Zbtb16 expression remained restricted to CRH neurons in the mid‐PVN. In the TNFα experiment, markers were used to identify neurons (NeuN), astrocytes (S100β), and microglia (Iba1). Following TNFα administration, Zbtb16 remained enriched in NeuN‐positive neurons in the mid‐PVN. However, astrocyte expression was observed near the third ventricle and lateral hypothalamic boundaries in the mid‐PVN and extended into caudal regions of the PVN; thereby indicating a region‐ and stimulus‐specific engagement of glial cells. Although Zbtb16 did not colocalize with Iba1‐positive microglia, these cells were often located in close proximity, sometimes in apparent apposition to Zbtb16‐expressing cells, raising the possibility of indirect or spatially organized neuroimmune signaling. These findings demonstrate that Zbtb16 exhibits a spatially distinct, cell‐type specific, and context‐dependent pattern of regulation in the PVN, responding differentially to neuroendocrine and immune stimuli.

An upregulation of Zbtb16 in a sex‐specific manner prompted further investigation into its regulation by glucocorticoids. The Zbtb16 promoter contains putative binding sites for glucocorticoid receptors (GR) and AP‐1 components, such as c‐Jun and c‐Fos, supporting its identity as a glucocorticoid‐responsive transcription factor in peripheral tissues (Fahnenstich et al. 2003). In the current study, systemic administration of the synthetic GR agonist DEX in male and female mice resulted in elevated Zbtb16 mRNA and increased numbers of Zbtb16‐immunoreactive cells in the PVN, suggesting that its expression is GR‐dependent in this brain region. The Zbtb16 promoter is also noted to contain androgen (Jiang and Wang 2004) and progesterone (Fahnenstich et al. 2003; Hai et al. 2024) receptor binding sites. The sex difference observed in the initial screen, 2‐fold more Zbtb16 in gonadally intact male PVNs may have been influenced by androgen receptor activation. However, other experiments in this study did not replicate the original sex difference. On the other hand, all females across multiple experiments were examined during estrus, a phase shortly after a progesterone surge in mice, which may have altered their responsiveness. Thus, hormone fluctuations in both sexes may have mitigated our ability to detect consistent sex differences across conditions.

The PVN exhibits well‐characterized sex‐specific features in stress reactivity, glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, and neuroimmune signaling (Bangasser and Wiersielis 2018; Bollinger et al. 2019; Handa et al. 1994), which often contribute to sex‐biased vulnerability to stress‐related disorders. Given that Zbtb16 is responsive to both glucocorticoid (Cheng et al. 2020) and immune signals (Sadler, Rossello, et al. 2015; Sadler, Suliman, et al. 2015), sex differences in its regulation were anticipated. However, under the specific hormonal context of the current experiments, Zbtb16 expression may reflect a core transcriptional program engaged similarly in both sexes during acute stress or immune challenge. Future studies will be needed to determine whether sex divergence emerges under chronic stress paradigms, in different phases of the estrous cycle, or during critical developmental windows.

C57BL/6J and C57BL/6N mice exhibit documented behavioral, genomic, and immune differences (Alvarez‐Lopez et al. 2025; Matsuo et al. 2010; Simon et al. 2013). These substrain differences raise a potential concern that baseline variation in stress reactivity could influence experimental outcomes. In the present study, however, all key analyses were conducted within substrain and within experimental condition, such that each substrain served as its own control. Therefore, any baseline differences between C57BL/6J and C57BL/6N mice are unlikely to confound the interpretation of stress‐induced Zbtb16 expression. Consistent patterns of Zbtb16 regulation and stress‐associated molecular signatures were observed across experiments and in both substrains, indicating that the effects described here are robust. The use of both C57BL/6J and C57BL/6N lines reflects the widespread use of these backgrounds in neuroscience and stress research and increases the translational relevance of the work by demonstrating that the identified mechanisms are conserved across commonly used laboratory substrains rather than being restricted to a single genetic background.

While our study focused on cellular and molecular characterization, Zbtb16 has also been implicated in behavioral regulation in vivo. Prior studies have shown that Zbtb16‐deficient mice exhibit altered social cognitive behaviors and neocortical development (Usui et al. 2021). Given the established role of the PVN in coordinating neuroendocrine and behavioral responses to stress (Sheng et al. 2021), it is plausible that Zbtb16 expression in CRH neurons could influence behavioral outcomes following stress. However, direct assessment of Zbtb16's role in stress‐associated behaviors was beyond the scope of the current study and will be an important direction for future research.

In summary, Zbtb16 demonstrated glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity in mid‐PVN (i.e., DEX induction), restraint stress identified CRH neuron specificity, and TNFα exposure revealed glial engagement around the periphery of the mid‐PVN and more diffusely in the caudal PVN. While Zbtb16 was expressed in neurons of the mid‐PVN following TNFα, the additional astrocytic expression observed in the caudal PVN highlights a robust, region‐ and cell‐type–specific pattern that is likely a general feature of stress‐induced regulation. Together, these findings support a model in which Zbtb16 acts as a spatially and cell‐type–specific integrator of neuroendocrine and immune signals in the hypothalamic PVN. Future work will be needed to define the downstream consequences of Zbtb16 activation in each cellular context and determine how its regulatory role may differ between acute stress and inflammatory stimuli.
