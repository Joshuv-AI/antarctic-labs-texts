Maternal metabolic signatures at early gestation associated with birth weight and neurodevelopment in early childhood
Authors: Jun Yang, Xiaojing Zeng, Qi Li, Xiaolin Wang, Yuting Wang, Qianlong Zhang, Guowang Xu, Jun Zhang, Xinyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests that maternal factors during pregnancy can exert effects on offspring. However, the underlying metabolic associations remain insufficiently understood.MethodsWe investigated associations between first-trimester maternal metabolic profiles and offspring development with large-scale untargeted metabolomics in the prospective Shanghai Birth Cohort (N = 1158). Birth weight and cognitive scores at age four assessed with the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence were used as short- and long-term developmental indicators.ResultsHere we show maternal fatty acid and phospholipid metabolism in early pregnancy is significantly associated with birth weight. Carnitine, LPE 18:2, and LPG O-13:1 are negatively associated with birth weight, whereas PC 32:2, glycerophosphocholine and several fatty acids show significant positive associations. Several dipeptides have positive associations with cognitive outcomes, while many fatty acids and N-acylethanolamines are negatively associated. Among the six cognitive indices, verbal comprehension has the most metabolite associations, with 4-pyridoxic acid, 1-methylxanthine, and phenylalanyltryptophan being most strongly linked. 4-Pyridoxic acid, a vitamin B6 metabolite, significantly mediates the positive association between multivitamin intake and cognitive development. Several metabolites show sex-specific associations with offspring development, which is consistent with the known sex-related differences in early childhood developmental patterns.ConclusionsMaternal metabolism in early pregnancy is associated with birth weight and long-term cognitive development in offspring. As maternal metabolic profiles reflect both genetic and environmental influences, many metabolites mediate maternal influences on development and reflect sex-specific patterns in child growth.Subject terms: Paediatric research, Metabolomics

Introduction

Growing evidence suggests that various maternal factors during pregnancy influence offspring development both in the short and long terms. Maternal diet and nutrient supplementation1–3, obesity4, mental health5, socioeconomic status6, and environmental exposures7 have all been shown to significantly impact child development. In the short term, these factors affect birth outcomes such as gestational age8, birth weight9,10, and Apgar scores11. In the long term, they are associated with physical growth12, cognitive development13, and disease susceptibility14. Moreover, fetal sex differences contribute to variations in fetal growth and development patterns, risks for adverse birth outcomes and diseases15–17. These associations highlight the complex interplay between maternal health and offspring development during pregnancy. However, the underlying biological mechanisms linking maternal health to offspring development remain unclear.

Maternal metabolism is shaped by both genetic and environmental factors and undergoes profound alterations during pregnancy to support adequate fetal growth and development18. These metabolic changes include alterations in glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, amino acid availability, and hormonal regulation, contributing to the intrauterine environment19. Since metabolic profiles directly reflect physiological status, understanding the comprehensive impact of maternal metabolism on child development is crucial for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of early-life programming. Moreover, prenatal metabolic signatures associated with developmental outcomes could also provide valuable insights into potential early interventions.

To investigate the relationship between maternal metabolism and child development, we focused on two key outcomes: birth weight, representing a short-term indicator of fetal growth, and cognitive ability at four years of age, representing a long-term neurodevelopmental outcome. Birth weight is a critical determinant of infant mortality and birth defects, and is also linked to the risk of various diseases in adulthood20,21. High birth weight can lead to complications during delivery, and is associated with an increased risk of child malignancies, breast cancer, psychiatric disorders, and cardiometabolic diseases22. Increasing maternal body mass index (BMI) and lipid metabolism have been positively correlated with birth weight23. Childhood cognitive abilities are typically assessed using standardized intelligence tests such as the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence (WPPSI), the Stanford Binet Intelligence Scale, and the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development. In WPPSI, the full-scale index scores (FSIQ) reflect overall cognitive ability, while additional indices assess specific cognitive domains, including verbal comprehension index (VCI), visual spatial index (VSI), fluid reasoning index (FRI), working memory index (WMI), and processing speed index (PSI). These cognitive scores are closely linked to neural and brain development. Previous studies have suggested that maternal metabolites serve as mediators between maternal lifestyle, environmental exposures and child cognitive development24,25. Untargeted metabolomics offers a powerful approach to studying the biological mechanism underlying pregnancy and child development. Nevertheless, large-scale cohort studies investigating comprehensive metabolic profiling remain limited.

In this study, we conduct an untargeted metabolomics analysis of maternal serum collected at the first trimester using liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) within the Shanghai Birth Cohort (SBC). The associations between maternal metabolic profiles and child development outcomes, including birth weight and cognitive scores at age four, are studied in 1158 mother-child pairs. A total of 499 compounds spanning various biochemical classes are identified in maternal serum samples, including amino acids, bile acids, carnitines, organic acids, fatty acids, lipids, and exogenous exposure compounds. Our analysis can be summarized in three main aspects: (i) metabolome-wide association studies (MWAS) on birth weight and cognitive development as well as the risk of macrosomia using multiple linear regression; (ii) sex differences in the associations between maternal metabolites and developmental outcomes using sex-stratified analysis; and (iii) the mediating role of metabolites in the relationship between maternal factors (multivitamin intake and pre-pregnancy BMI) and child development. Our findings provide insights into maternal-fetal metabolic interactions and may contribute to a deeper understanding of early-life predictors of health and cognitive function.

Methods

Study population and clinical information

Our study is based on the prospective Shanghai Birth Cohort (SBC) study, which has been described in details previously26. Briefly, 3426 pregnant women in early pregnancy were recruited into the cohort between 2013 and 2016. Maternal blood samples were collected at the routine blood collection window of the hospital by trained clinical staff. All participants were in a fasting state at the time of blood collection. Sample collection, transportation, and processing strictly followed the standardized protocol of the SBC26. According to this protocol, all blood samples were processed and transferred into long-term storage within 2 h after collection. Serum and plasma were separated and stored in Xinhua Biobank at −80 °C until further analysis. Demographic characteristics and maternal lifestyle information were collected through self-administered questionnaires. Pregnancy-related complications and birth characteristics were obtained from maternal and infant medical records. When the children reached four years of age, families were invited for a follow-up visit, which included a cognitive assessment using the Wechsler Primary and Preschool Scales of Intelligence-Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV) and a diagnostic interview with a primary caregiver, typically the mother. These assessments were conducted in a quiet room by a neurodevelopmental pediatrician trained in the study procedures. A total of 2031 children completed the intelligence assessment. Genomic analysis, proteomics, metabolomics, and environmental exposure measurements were performed on randomly selected subsets of samples, based on allocation policies and resource constraints set by the hospital. For this study, we selected mother-child pairs with complete intelligence assessments and metabolomics data (N = 1158). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Xinhua Hospital Center affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (XHEC-C-2013-001-3). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Metabolomics analysis based on LC-HRMS

Maternal serum samples were collected from peripheral veins between weeks 9 to 16 of gestation. The sample preprocessing procedures of serum samples for the untargeted metabolomics analysis are as follows: 100 μL serum sample was thawed on ice water mixture and then transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. 400 μL methanol containing 10 internal standards (LPC 19:0, 3.0 μg/mL; FA 16:0-d3, 2.5 μg/mL; FA 18:0-d3, 2.5 μg/mL; Phenylalanine-d5, 3.6 μg/mL; Tryptophan-d5, 4.25 μg/mL; Chenodeoxycholic acid-d4, 1.5 μg/mL; Cholic acid-d4, 1.85 μg/mL; Carnitine 2:0-d3, 0.16 μg/mL; Carntine 8:0-d3, 0.1 μg/mL; Carnitine 16:0-d3, 0.15 μg/mL) was then added to the tube as the extracts. The tube was then vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 20,000 g and 10 °C for 10 minutes. After the centrifugation, 180 μL supernatant was taken and freeze-dried in a vacuum centrifuge. The remaining supernatant was mixed from all the samples to construct the QC samples. The dried samples were then reconstituted in 50 μL acetonitrile/water (1:3) solution, and 5 μL of the sample was injected for LC-MS analysis.

The serum extract after preprocessing was analyzed by a Vanquish UPHLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) coupled with a Q Exactive quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). The samples were analyzed in both positive and negative ion modes. In the positive ion mode, an ACQUITY C8 CEH column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was used in the UPHLC system. The mobile phase A consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid, while the mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution initially started from 5% B and was maintained for 0.5 min, then increased linearly to 40% B in 1.5 min, increased to 100% B in 6 min and maintained for 2 min, then decreased to 5% B in 0.1 min and equilibrated for 1.9 min. In the negative ion mode, an ACQUITY T3 HSS column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) was used for separation. The mobile phase A consisted of 6.5 mM NH4HCO3 aqueous solution, while the mobile phase B consisted of 6.5 mM NH4HCO3 in 95% methanol/water. The gradient elution initially started from 2% B and was maintained for 0.5 min, then increased linearly to 40% B in 2 min, increased to 100% B in 6 min, and maintained for 2 min, then decreased to 2% B in 0.1 min and equilibrated for 1.9 min. The column temperature and flow rate of positive and negative ion modes were set to 60 °C and 0.4 mL/min. In the mass spectrometer conditions, the ion spray voltages were set to 3.5 and 3.0 kV in positive and negative ion modes, respectively. Other MS parameters were the same in the two ion modes as follows: capillary temperature, 320 °C; aux gas heater temperature, 350 °C; aux gas and sheath gas flow rate, 10 and 45 (arbitrary units); mass resolution, 140,000; full scan mass range, 70–1050. A data-dependent acquisition mode was used to obtain MS/MS spectra in QC samples. The collision energies (CE) of 15, 30 and 45 eV were acquired. QC samples were also inserted into the analysis queue every ten runs to monitor the data quality.

Metabolomics data processing and quality control

LC-MS Raw data from the instrument (.raw) were transformed to the mzML format with ProteoWizard MSConvert (version 3.0.19267). Peak picking filter with CWT algorithm was applied in the file conversion. Then, our previously developed LC-MS data processing software MetCohort27 was applied to perform feature detection. The parameters were set as follows: width of ROA, 30; allowed delta m/z of ROA, 0.001; intensity threshold of ROA, 0.5%; allowed delta m/z of XIC, 0.02; Minimum number of non-zero peaks in a feature, 80%; delta m/z of feature detection, 0.005; minimum number of continuous non-zero points, 4; maximum number of continuous zero points, 10; maximum width of peak, 15; minimum intensity of a feature, 1000; entropy coefficient, 0.3. The integrated peak areas were further reviewed manually. Metabolite annotation was performed based on accurate mass, retention time and MS/MS spectra. Our in-house database28 (OSI-SMMS) (Dashuo, Dalian, China) and the mass bank of North America (MONA) database (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/) were used for the compound identification. MS-DIAL 4 (version 5.1.230719) was used for lipid identification. The identification levels were given according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative29 (MSI). The feature intensities of the samples were corrected using one internal standard to achieve a minimum relative standard deviation (RSD) in QC samples. For the features with RSD greater than 20%, the local linear regression based on the neighboring QCs (LoReg) algorithm was used for data correction30. Zero values in the maternal serum metabolomics were replaced with half of the minimum non-zero values of the corresponding compound. Then, natural logarithm transformation was applied on the metabolomics data to reduce skew. Finally, the metabolomics data were standardized by removing the mean and scaling to unit variance and used for subsequent statistical analysis. Supplementary Fig. S1 presents the data quality assessment results for both positive and negative ion modes. The results show that QC samples clustered significantly, with the top principal component remaining within two standard deviations (SD) of the mean. The relative standard deviations of all the compounds in QC samples were lower than 30%.

Statistics and reproducibility

Multiple linear regression models were utilized to estimate the associations between serum compounds and birth weight, providing effect estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. In the correlations of birth weight, gestational age, child sex and pre-pregnancy BMI were adjusted as the covariates. Multiple imputation was used to handle the missing data in the covariates of regression models with R package “mice” (version: 3.16.0). The socioeconomic and clinical information (child sex, maternal education, family income, folic acid and multivitamin intake during pregnancy, pre-pregnancy BMI, maternal age, passive smoking status, gestational age, birth weight and gestational diabetes diagnosis) and the cognitive assessment scores were used as predictors to impute the missing values among them. Ten datasets were imputed for each model and pooled into one result. In the multiple linear regression results for all compounds, multiple testing with the Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Sensitivity analysis was performed through constructing various regression models with various covariate adjustment and excluding samples with the highest or lowest birth weight.

In the analysis of macrosomia, binary logistic regression was applied to analyze the serum compounds to obtain the risk compounds with macrosomia. Child sex, gestational age, maternal age and pre-pregnancy BMI were adjusted in the models. Multiple testing with Benjamini & Hochberg method was used to control the FDR.

In the analysis of cognitive development at the age of four, Spearman correlation was used to compute the correlations between different cognitive scores and maternal serum compound abundances. Multiple testing was used to calculate the FDR of the correlation coefficients. Multiple linear regression models were applied to the compounds for different cognitive scores, adjusted for the cognitive-related covariates (maternal education, family income, child sex, pre-pregnancy BMI, birth weight, multivitamin intake, and folic acid intake during pregnancy). Sensitivity analysis was performed by constructing various regression models with various covariate adjustments and excluding samples with the highest or lowest FSIQ. Elastic net models were constructed with the significantly correlated compounds from the linear regression with the R package “caret” (version: 6.0–93). 100 repeated ten-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate the model performance. The average coefficients of determination (R2) were computed across all cross-validation folds. Variable importance was assessed based on the absolute standardized regression coefficients derived from the final Glmnet model at the optimal regularization parameter selected by the cross-validation.

Sex-stratified analysis of birth weight and cognitive development was performed on the compound data of male and female offspring with multiple linear regression. The models were adjusted for corresponding related covariates of birth weight and cognitive indices. The missing values of covariates were imputed with multiple imputation. After the sex stratified analysis, to further study the interrelationship between metabolites and fetal sex, sex interacted terms were added to the linear regression models of all samples.

The mediation analysis of metabolites was performed with R package “mediation” (version: 4.5.0), where a series of mediation analyses were conducted using linear regression and nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 simulations) to assess whether individual maternal metabolites mediates the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight, with significance determined by an average causal mediation effect (ACME) p-value < 0.05. In the mediation analysis of the associations between prenatal factors and cognitive development, the metabolites acted as mediators individually between pre-pregnancy BMI and multivitamin intake and various indices.

All the data analysis were performed in R (version: 4.2.1). R packages “tidyverse” (version: 2.0.0), “patchwork” (version: 1.1.2), “EnhancedVolcano” (version: 1.14.0), “ComplexUpset” (version: 1.3.3) were used for data processing and visualization.

Results

Study cohort characteristics

Our study was based on the prospective Shanghai Birth Cohort (SBC), which investigates prenatal factors influencing birth outcomes and child development. A total of 1158 mother-child pairs were included in our analysis, with complete maternal metabolomics data and child intelligence scores assessed at four years of age (Fig. 1). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population. Among the 1158 children, 50.1% were male. The mean maternal age was 29.6 ± 3.7 years, and the mean pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was 21.5 ± 3.1 kg/m2. The mean gestational age at birth was 39.1 ± 1.3 weeks, and the mean birth weight was 3431 ± 451 g. Approximately 64.6% of mothers had an educational level higher than a bachelor’s degree, and 77.4% did not develop gestational diabetes during pregnancy. Child intelligence was assessed at 4 years of age using the Wechsler Primary and Preschool Scales of Intelligence-Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV). The mean scores for the full-scale intelligence quotient (FSIQ) and its subdomains were as follows: FSIQ, 114.9 ± 12.1; verbal comprehension index (VCI), 115.8 ± 13.2; visual spatial index (VSI), 114.0 ± 13.5; fluid reasoning index (FRI), 110.0 ± 12.1; working memory index (WMI), 105.2 ± 12.2; and processing speed index (PSI), 106.0 ± 11.1.

A total of 499 compounds were identified in the serum at levels29 1 to 4, including amino acids, bile acids, carnitines, fatty acids (FAs), fatty acyls, lipids and others (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Data 1). Among these, 28 compounds were exogenous compounds, reflecting the environmental exposure status of the pregnant mothers.

Maternal metabolome-wide association study of offspring birth weight and macrosomia

Birth weight is a crucial health index at birth, influenced by multiple clinical factors. In the adjusted multiple linear regression analysis of prenatal factors (Supplementary Data 2), gestational age (β: 152.2; 95% CI: 133.8, 170.6; p < 0.001), child sex (β: −123.1; 95% CI: −169.5, −76.7; p < 0.001; male as reference), and pre-pregnancy BMI (β: 29.8; 95% CI: 22.0, 37.6; p < 0.001) emerged as the most significant prenatal factors associated with birth weight.

After adjusting for gestational age, child sex, and pre-pregnancy BMI, 68 serum compounds exhibited significant correlations (p < 0.05) with birth weight (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 3). Of these, 50 compounds were positively correlated, while 18 showed negative correlations. Notably, all significant FAs and fatty acyls were positively associated with birth weight. After correcting for multiple testing, 21 compounds had an FDR below 0.1, with their effect estimates illustrated in Fig. 2b. Among these, six FAs were positively correlated with birth weight. Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) 20:3 (p = 0.003) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) 32:2 (p < 0.001) showed positive correlations, whereas lysophosphatidylglycerol (LPG) O-13:1 (p < 0.001), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) 18:2 (p < 0.001), and LPE 20:4 (p < 0.001) exhibited negative correlations. Additionally, choline (p = 0.002) and glycerophosphocholine (p < 0.001), two key structural components of phospholipids, were positively correlated with birth weight, whereas acetylcholine (p = 0.003) showed a negative correlation. Threonine (p = 0.002) and 17-hydroxypregnenolone sulfate (p = 0.003), were positively correlated with birth weight. Conversely, carnitine (p < 0.001) and two dipeptides, leucylproline (p < 0.001) and leucylphenylalanine (p = 0.004), were negatively correlated. We also observed that perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA), one of the perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs), was positively correlated with birth weight.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by changing the adjustment covariates and sample selection in the linear regression models. Specifically, we constructed four models with different covariate adjustments: model 1 (adjusted for gestational age, child sex, and pre-pregnancy BMI), model 2 (adjusted for gestational age and child sex), model 3 (adjusted for child sex and pre-pregnancy BMI) and model 4 (unadjusted for any covariates). The number of significant compounds (p < 0.05) associated with birth weight varied across these models (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Data 4). Model 2 has the highest number of significant compounds, suggesting that pre-pregnancy BMI may have a strong association with maternal serum metabolites. To further test the stability of these associations, we excluded individuals with birth weight below the 5th percentile and above the 95th percentile to construct the regression model 5. The significant compounds remained largely unchanged across the different models (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Macrosomia is an adverse birth outcome defined as a birth weight greater than 4000 g. Among the 1125 children with recorded birth weight in our study, 98 (8.7%) were classified as macrosomia. To investigate the association between maternal metabolomics and macrosomia risk, binary logistic regression was conducted. Significantly, 22 metabolites had an FDR below 0.2 (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Data 5). Consistent with the findings for birth weight, 18 metabolites were positively associated with macrosomia risk, while only four were negatively associated. The metabolites with positive associations were primarily FAs. In contrast, LPA 18:2, LPE 20:4, LPE 22:6 and vanillin-4-sulfate were negatively associated with macrosomia risk. According to the metabolic correlations, our findings suggest that fatty acid metabolism may play a crucial role in fetal growth, contributing to the risk of macrosomia.

Maternal metabolome-wide association study of offspring cognitive development

We found that the six cognitive scores exhibited positive correlations with each other, with Spearman coefficients ranging from 0.28 to 0.76 (Supplementary Fig. S4), illustrating distinct yet interrelated developmental patterns in children. In the adjusted regression analysis, which included all covariates, maternal education, family income, child sex, pre-pregnancy BMI, birth weight, multivitamin intake, and folic acid intake during pregnancy showed significant associations with at least one cognitive index (Supplementary Data 6). To identify compounds correlated with cognitive development, we first performed Spearman correlation analysis on 1158 samples. A total of 42 compounds were significantly associated (p < 0.05, FDR < 0.05) with at least one intelligence index (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 7). The compounds followed a consistent trend across different intelligence indices, with 12 showing positive correlations and 30 showing negative correlations. Specifically, four dipeptides exhibited positive associations, whereas most of FAs, fatty acyls, and N-acylethanolamines (NAEs) were negatively correlated. Several exogenous compounds displayed varying associations. 1-Methylxanthine, nicotyrine, and vanillin-4-sulfate were positively correlated, whereas 2-naphthalenesulfonic acid, diethyl aminoethyl hexanoate, and three perfluorinated compounds (PFDA, PFNA, PFUnDA) showed negative correlations with intelligence scores. We further conducted multiple linear regression analysis, adjusting for the cognitive-related covariates. The 42 significantly correlated compounds kept these observed trends, although 9 of them lost statistical significance (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. S5).

In the results of multiple linear regression after covariate adjustment, there were 57, 48, 42, 26, 25, and 19 compounds showing significant correlations (p < 0.05) with VCI, VSI, FSIQ, FRI, WMI and PSI, respectively (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Data 8). VCI has the highest number of associated serum compounds, with 24 overlapping with FSIQ, a composite intelligence index. The compounds significantly correlated with VCI were predominantly FAs, fatty acyls, and lipids, with 49 of the 57 compounds demonstrating negative correlations (Fig. 3c). To further explore the predictive capacity of these metabolites, we constructed elastic net models for each intelligence index using the corresponding significantly correlated compounds, employing repeated 10-fold cross-validation. The variance explained by metabolites was 7.4%, 5.0%, 5.3%, 3.0%, 4.0% and 6.7% for VCI, VSI, FRI, WMI, PSI and FSIQ in the validation set, respectively. In the VCI predictive model, which included 57 significantly correlated compounds, 31 compounds had non-zero contributions (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Data 9). The top three compounds contributing to VCI were 4-pyridoxic acid (p = 0.005), 1-methylxanthine (p = 0.014) and phenylalanyltryptophan (p = 0.028), all showing positive correlations. Several FAs (FA 25:0, FA 26:3, FA 18:3;O2), NAEs (oleoylethanolamide, stearoylethanolamide, and palmitoylethanolamide), and phospholipids contributed predominantly to negative correlations in the VCI model. The top contributors and their linear regression estimates for all the intelligence indexes are presented in Supplementary Figs. S6–S10. Notably, most fatty acids showed negative correlations with VCI, but several fatty acids exhibited positive associations with FSIQ, including FA 16:1;O2 (p = 0.004), FA 10:0 (p = 0.035), FA 18:2;O2 (p = 0.003), FA 18:1;O2 (p = 0.035), and FA 8:0 (p = 0.015) (Supplementary Fig. S10). Although different cognitive indices were associated with different metabolites, the directions of these associations were generally consistent across indices. The sensitivity analyses, which included models with the various covariate adjustments and the exclusion of extreme samples, demonstrated that the results remained stable without significant fluctuations (Supplementary Figs. S11–S16). Notably, removing birth weight from the adjustment variables had little impact on the results.

Sex differences in metabolic associations with offspring development

Sex differences in fetal development contribute to variations in growth trajectories and cognitive maturation31. Generally, male offspring tend to have a higher birth weight than female offspring32, while females often exhibit earlier cognitive maturation and higher cognitive performance in early childhood33. Our study corroborated these sex-specific differences in growth and cognitive development (Supplementary Fig. S17). Specifically, birth weight was significantly higher in males than in females (p < 0.001). In contrast, females exhibited significantly higher scores in VCI (p < 0.001), FRI (p = 0.037), PSI (p < 0.001) and FSIQ (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in VSI and WMI between males and females.

To investigate the metabolic mechanisms underlying these sex-specific developmental differences, we conducted fetal sex-stratified multiple linear regression for birth weight and cognitive outcomes. The distribution of z-scores for compounds revealed both homogeneous and heterogeneous effects across sexes. In the stratified analysis of birth weight (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Data 10), 42 compounds were positively associated with birth weight in both sexes (Quadrant I), whereas 18 compounds were negatively associated in both (Quadrant III). Importantly, 16 compounds were positively correlated with birth weight exclusively in males (Quadrant IV), while 5 compounds exhibited positive associations only in females (Quadrant II). Overall, most compounds demonstrated higher z-scores in male offspring than in female offspring.

Interestingly, the trend was reversed for cognitive development. In the stratified analysis of VCI (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 11), 4 compounds demonstrated positive associations exclusively in males, compared to 35 compounds in females. Moreover, most compounds have higher z-scores in females than in males, suggesting that the regulatory effects of these metabolites may differ between the sexes. To further assess the role of fetal sex in metabolic associations, we included sex-interaction terms in multiple linear regression models and examined the stratified regression estimates of significantly interacting compounds. Among these, 9 compounds were positively associated with birth weight in males, whereas only 3 were positively associated in females (Fig. 4c). Notably, ricinoleic acid, uric acid, 16,17-didehydropregnenolone, and 3,7-dihydroxy-5-cholestenoic acid were significantly associated with birth weight exclusively in male offspring. In contrast, for VCI, 32 compounds were positively associated in females, while only 2 were positively associated in male offspring (Fig. 4d). Most LPCs exhibited negative correlations with VCI exclusively in males. Notably, tryptophan and gamma-glutamylleucine displayed opposite correlation patterns between sexes. Similar trends were observed for other cognitive indices (Supplementary Figs. S18–22). These findings suggest that sex-specific metabolic profiles may underlie distinct developmental patterns in early childhood.

Mediating roles of metabolites between prenatal factors and offspring development

In the above analysis, the associations between maternal metabolites and offspring development outcomes were investigated. As metabolites represent downstream products of genetic regulation and environmental influences, we further examined whether they serve as mediators between maternal factors and offspring development. Since pre-pregnancy BMI is significantly associated with metabolites linked to birth weight (Fig. 2c), the mediating role of maternal metabolites in the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight was examined using causal mediation analysis. We found 19 compounds exhibiting significant mediation effects (Supplementary Data 12), with the proportion of the total effect mediated ranging from −12% to 7%. Among them, several amino acids, fatty acids, and 17-hydroxypregnenolone sulfate showed positive correlations with birth weight, acting as mediators between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight. FA 20:3, FA 22:5, and 17-hydroxypregnenolone sulfate were the most significant positive mediators. These findings suggest that enhanced maternal biosynthesis of fatty acids and steroid hormones may support fetal growth, potentially by improving nutrient transport or fetal lipid metabolism. Conversely, metabolites such as 16,17-didehydropregnenolone, valine, LPA 14:0, and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) 16:1 acted as negative mediators. Notably, 16,17-didehydropregnenolone exhibited the strongest mediation effect (ACME: −3.11; 95% CI: −5.54, −0.91; p = 0.004), accounting for −12% of the total effect.

Beyond socioeconomic factors, pre-pregnancy BMI and multivitamin intake during pregnancy were identified as key prenatal factors associated with cognitive development in our study (Supplementary Data 6). Specifically, multivitamin intake during pregnancy showed significant positive correlations with VCI. In contrast, pre-pregnancy BMI was significantly negatively correlated with VSI after adjusting for all covariates. Several classes of metabolites, including FAs, amino acids and their derivatives, carnitines, PCs, and xenobiotics, exhibited significant mediating effects.

In the mediation analysis of pre-pregnancy BMI, most metabolites acted as mediators linking higher BMI and lower VSI. The mediated proportion ranged from −14% to 22%, with 37 showing positive mediating effects and 6 compounds exhibiting negative effects. Similar to the mediation effects between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight, the positively mediating compounds were primarily FAs, fatty acyls, and PCs, suggesting that lipid metabolism plays an important role in mediating the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI and child development. Additionally, the six negatively mediating compounds included FA 20:1;O2, FA 21:2;O2, SM 33:2, salicylic acid, aspartylphenylalanine and phenylacetylglutamine, suggesting a dynamic regulation mechanism between maternal and fetal body development.

VCI was primarily influenced by maternal multivitamin intake, with 44 compounds demonstrating significant mediation effects. The proportion mediated ranged from −5% to 47%. Notably, 4-pyriodic acid, a catabolic product of vitamin B6, exhibited the highest mediating effect, accounting for 47% of the association between multivitamin intake and increased VCI. Except for PC O-36:5, which showed a negative mediation effect, all other mediating compounds contributed positively to this association.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically investigated the associations between maternal metabolomics during early gestation and pregnancy outcomes, including neonatal birth weight and long-term neurodevelopment in offspring. Our findings suggest that both birth weight and cognitive development at four years are linked to maternal metabolic signatures. Specifically, MWAS revealed critical roles of fatty acid, lipid, and amino acid metabolism in offspring development. Significant sex-specific effects in the associations between maternal metabolism and developmental outcomes were observed. Several metabolites appear to influence growth or cognitive development in a sex-dependent manner, potentially contributing to the observed differences in developmental trajectories between male and female offspring. Furthermore, we explored the potential mediating roles of the metabolites in the relationships between pre-pregnancy maternal BMI and developmental outcomes, as well as in the mechanisms underlying multivitamin intake. These results underscore the importance of maternal weight management and nutritional support during pregnancy. Our results may provide supporting evidence for the Barker hypothesis from the perspective of maternal metabolism34.

We acknowledged that several limitations of this study should be noted. First, maternal metabolomic profiles were measured at a single time point, which may not fully capture metabolic changes throughout pregnancy. Second, while the WPPSI-IV intelligence assessment provides an estimate of cognitive abilities in early childhood, it may not perfectly reflect true neurodevelopmental status. Test performance can be influenced by factors such as family environment and individual differences. Third, the associations between maternal metabolomic profiles and cognitive development were less pronounced than those with birth weight, possibly due to the longer interval between maternal metabolite measurement and neurodevelopmental assessment. Moreover, many metabolites exhibited significant associations with cognitive development after adjustment for covariates, but none passed the threshold after multiple correction (FDR < 0.05), underscoring the importance of cautious interpretation. Despite these limitations, the significant results still indicate important biological associations between maternal metabolism and offspring development.

Fatty acids serve as essential fuel sources and are major components of other lipids, including triacylglycerols, phospholipids, and cholesteryl esters35. During pregnancy, an increase in free fatty acids may provide necessary energy support for rapid fetal growth. Previous studies have reported that pregnant women with obesity tend to have higher total saturated fatty acids and lower total n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids36. Additionally, pre-pregnancy overweight and obesity have been associated with an increased risk of offspring obesity37. In our study, several free fatty acids (e.g., FA 14:0, FA 20:3, FA 22:4) in maternal serum were positively associated with neonatal birth weight and had potential risk effects on macrosomia. Moreover, these fatty acids mediated the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight, suggesting a potential link between maternal metabolic status and fetal growth. We also observed a negative correlation between carnitine levels and birth weight. Carnitines play critical roles in transporting long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation, which fuels the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. A decline in free carnitine and short-chain acylcarnitines has been reported during pregnancy38,39, potentially reflecting increased fatty acid utilization. Therefore, higher carnitine levels in maternal circulation may indicate a relatively lower rate of fatty acid oxidation, which could explain its negative association with fetal growth. These findings suggest the critical role of maternal fatty acids metabolism in maternal and fetal development.

Amino acids are also increasingly required during pregnancy to support fetal and maternal tissue development40. We found that threonine was significantly positively correlated with birth weight. As an essential amino acid, threonine plays a crucial role in protein synthesis and has been suggested to stimulate muscle protein synthesis41. In our mediation analysis examining the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight, threonine and alanine exhibited positive mediation effects, whereas valine and arginine showed negative mediation effects. Although all four amino acids were positively associated with maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, their differing correlations with birth weight suggest complex metabolic interactions. These findings are basically consistent with those of a previous multi-ethnic metabolomics cohort42. Notably, valine, a branched-chain amino acid linked to insulin resistance43, demonstrated a negative mediation effect on birth weight. 3-Hydroxy-isobutyrate, a catabolic intermediate of valine, has been reported to stimulate FA uptake and lipid accumulation in animals44, potentially interacting with maternal BMI.

Additionally, we found that glycerophospholipid metabolism shows important associations in fetal development. We observed positive correlations between birth weight and both choline and glycerophosphocholine (GPC). GPC is derived from PCs and can be further degraded to glycerol-phosphate and choline45. Choline is essential for the production of phospholipids, including PCs and sphingomyelins, which are necessary for cell division, growth, and myelination46. Additionally, choline serves as a precursor of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter influencing brain development47. Interestingly, however, we found that acetylcholine was negatively associated with birth weight. This suggests that increased conversion of choline to acetylcholine may limit its availability for fetal growth. A previous study reported a decrease in lysophospholipids during pregnancy, whereas phospholipids and glycerolipids tend to increase48. We found FAs, PCs and PEs as potential risk factors for macrosomia, whereas certain LPAs (LPA 22:6 and LPA 18:2) appeared to be protective. Meanwhile, LPG O-13:1, LPE 18:2, and LPE 20:4 were negatively correlated with birth weight, while LPC 16:1, LPA 16:1, and LPA 16:0 showed negative mediation effects between pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight. These findings suggest distinct roles for phospholipids and lysophospholipids during pregnancy.

Several other compounds also showed notable associations in our study. 17-Hydroxypregnenolone sulfate, a steroid primarily biosynthesized in the maternal adrenal, is used for the synthesis of other bioactive steroids49 and was positively correlated with fetal development. Another steroid, 16,17-didehydropregnenolone, exhibited a negative mediation effect between maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and birth weight. While its function may be related to pregnenolone50, its specific role in pregnancy remains unclear. Additionally, we found a positive correlation between PFUnDA, a type of PFAS, and birth weight. This finding contrasts with most previous studies, which have reported negative associations between PFAS exposure and fetal growth51, may need further targeted validation.

In the metabolomics analysis of cognitive development in childhood, several serum compounds showed significant associations with intelligence scores at age four. Previous studies on the neurodevelopment effects of FAs have primarily focused on polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs)52–54. Omega-3 and omega-6 PUFAs are essential for brain growth and function55,56. Due to limitations in accurately identifying these compounds with specific double bond positions, the correlations between these PUFAs and cognitive development require further targeted quantification and analysis. However, we found that several saturated FAs (e.g., FA 5:0;O, FA 25:0, FA 26:0) and unsaturated FAs (e.g., FA 18:2, FA 22:3, FA 22:4) were negatively associated with cognitive development. Furthermore, mediation analysis revealed that many of these FAs and two FAHFAs positively mediated the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI and cognitive development. Previous research has suggested that maternal obesity and Western dietary patterns are negatively associated with cognitive development24,57, potentially due to chronic low-grade inflammation, alterations in cytokine profiles, and increased oxidative stress58. Several lipid-associated metabolites had potential mediating effects between the Western dietary patterns and neurodevelopment24. Our findings support the idea that elevated FAs may indicate elevated lipid metabolism, which could contribute to the biological mechanisms linking maternal metabolism with neurodevelopment. Additionally, we observed that four NAEs (PEA, LEA, OEA and SEA) were negatively correlated with cognitive development. NAEs are a class of lipid amides with known roles in analgesia, cognition and inflammation59–61. However, their specific impact on neurodevelopment remains unclear.

We also observed that two amino acids, kynurenine and threonine, were negatively correlated with cognitive development. Kynurenine is a key metabolite generated by tryptophan catabolism. The accumulation of kynurenine is associated with peripheral inflammation, which has also been linked to several psychiatric and mental health disorders, such as depression and schizophrenia62. Additionally, a previous study using human cerebral organoids found that increased exposure to threonine, histidine and lysine led to reduced activity of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, which is involved in neurodevelopment processes63. Our findings suggest that elevated levels of these amino acids may influence cognitive development at the population level.

Compared to the negatively correlated compounds, fewer compounds exhibited positive associations with cognitive development. Among them, 4-pyridoxic acid, a catabolic product of vitamin B664, was one of the most significant compounds. Notably, it also showed a significant mediation effect between maternal multivitamin intake and cognitive development, supporting the potential neuroprotective effects of multivitamin supplementation65. Similarly, a previous metabolomics study on metal exposure and neurodevelopment highlighted the protective role of riboflavin (vitamin B2) metabolic pathway25, further emphasizing the importance of B-vitamin metabolism in fetal neurodevelopment.

We also found various exogenous exposure compounds that may influence cognitive development. 1-Methylxanthine, a major metabolite of caffeine and paraxanthine, was positively correlated with cognitive development. Previous studies have shown that 1-methylxanthine activates reticulum ryanodine receptor (RyR) channels, increasing neuronal excitability and enhancing neurotransmitter release66. It has also been reported to improve memory and exhibit neuroprotective properties in rats67. Similarly, vanillin-4-sulfate, originating from the bioactive natural aromatic flavouring compound vanillin68, was positively correlated with cognitive development. Vanillin has been recognized for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-mutagenic, and antidepressant properties, which may contribute to its potential neuroprotective effects69,70. Notably, we also observed negative correlations between cognitive development and three PFAS (PFDA, PFNA, PFUnDA), but these associations lost statistical significance after adjusting for covariates. This is similar to the previous study using targeted quantification on PFAS71. While numerous studies have explored the effects of PFAS on neurodevelopment across various cohorts72–74, the findings remain inconclusive. Given that PFUnDA was positively associated with birth weight in our study, we speculate that its effects may be linked to obesity or inflammation-related mechanisms.

In our analysis of metabolic associations with birth weight and neurodevelopment, we identified pronounced compounds that exhibit sex differences. Specifically, a greater number of metabolites exhibited positive effects on birth weight in males, whereas the trend was reversed in cognitive development, where more metabolites showed negative associations in males. These findings suggest that metabolic differences may contribute to the well-documented sex differences in fetal growth and neurodevelopment. Notably, several FAs, organic acids, and steroids were positively correlated with birth weight in males but negatively correlated in females. In contrast, multiple amino acids and LPCs were negatively associated with cognitive development only in males. Previous studies have reported sex differences in placental transcript expression during early pregnancy31,75. Additionally, fetal sex has been shown to influence microRNA expression and amino acid transport within the intricate molecular network regulating fetal development76. Our findings highlight a distinct sex-specific metabolic signature in early pregnancy that may contribute to differential growth and neurodevelopmental trajectories, showing the complexity of metabolite effects. These results underscore the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in future research on fetal development and metabolic programming.

This study comprehensively examined the relationship between maternal metabolism in early pregnancy and child development outcomes with untargeted metabolomics in a large-scale prospective cohort. We identified various metabolites, including amino acids, fatty acids, phospholipids, and exogenous exposure compounds, that were associated with fetal birth weight and cognitive development at four years of age. Our findings suggest that maternal metabolic profiles in early pregnancy may have long-lasting effects on child development, underscoring the importance of managing maternal diet and lifestyle during pregnancy. Notably, the sex-specific associations between the metabolites and child development underscore the role of sex-differentiated metabolic pathways. Additionally, we observed mediation effects of specific metabolites in the relationship between maternal factors and child development, providing insights into potential biological mechanisms. These findings contribute to a better understanding of maternal metabolic regulation during pregnancy and its impact on child development.
