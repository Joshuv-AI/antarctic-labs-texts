Atovaquone-induced therapeutic rewiring of melanoma metabolism
Authors: Mayra Betancourt Ponce, Alexa R. Heaton, Samantha K. Burkard, Aurora D’Amato, Nicha Boonpattrawong, Melissa C. Skala, Manish Patankar, Lisa Barroilhet
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR

With the rising incidence of melanoma, there is a critical need for improved treatment strategies (Saginala et al, 2021). Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is an important contributor to melanoma bioenergetics and a promising therapeutic target. Several OXPHOS inhibitors have shown preclinical potential but have failed to succeed in clinical trials, mostly owing to toxicity. In this study, we investigate atovaquone (ATO) as an alternative OXPHOS inhibitor in melanoma models. ATO is a well-tolerated, Food and Drug Administration—approved agent that has shown efficacy in different cancer types (Chen et al, 2018; Fiorillo et al, 2016; Kapur et al, 2022). Given that melanoma cells often retain functional OXPHOS machinery that can contribute to tumor progression (Kumar et al, 2021; Shen et al, 2025), we hypothesized that ATO would disrupt melanoma metabolism and exhibit antitumor activity. Detailed methods can be found as Supplementary Materials and Methods.

ATO significantly reduced oxygen consumption rates as early as 1 hour, with effects sustained 24 hours after treatment in B16 and B78 cells (Supplementary Figure S1a and b). Although adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production was attributed to both glycolysis and OXPHOS in untreated cells, >98.5% of ATP production was attributed to glycolysis in ATO-treated cells (Supplementary Table S1). Given these effects, we anticipated a compensatory increase in glycolytic activity or extracellular acidification rates. Interestingly, although extracellular acidification rates increased at the 1-hour time point, this effect was abrogated at 24 hours (Supplementary Figure S1c and d and Supplementary Table S1). This inability to sustain glycolytic compensation, along with OXPHOS inhibition, was reflected in the significantly reduced ATP production rates in ATO-treated cells compared with that in vehicle-treated cells, especially at 24 hours when both oxygen consumption rate and extracellular acidification rate were decreased (Supplementary Figure S1e and f).

To further assess metabolic fluctuations after ATO treatment, we performed multiphoton optical metabolic imaging in B78 cells (Figure 1a). At 1 hour, ATO-treated cells exhibited a significant increase in free NAD(P)H (α1 ), consistent with an initial increase in glycolysis (Figure 1b and c), and no changes in free FAD (α2 ) and optical redox ratio (Figure 1d and e). At 24 hours, ATO-treated cells exhibited significantly decreased NAD(P)H intensity and NAD(P)H α1 compared with those treated with vehicle, consistent with a decrease in glycolysis (Figure 1f and g). We also observed a significant increase in free FAD α2 after ATO treatment at 24 hours (Figure 1h), possibly owing to OXPHOS inhibition by ATO and a resulting buildup of oxidized FAD. Ultimately, we observed a significant decrease in the optical redox ratio of ATO-treated cells compared with that of vehicle controls at 24 hours (Figure 1i), suggesting a shift toward an oxidized state.

After ATO treatment, ROS levels were elevated as early as 30 minutes and increased significantly by 24 hours compared with the controls (Supplementary Figure S2a and b). Given elevated ROS and optical metabolic imaging analyses suggesting an oxidative environment, we hypothesized that ATO induced oxidative stress in melanoma cells. Twenty-four hours after treatment, ATO-treated cells exhibited significantly increased double-stranded DNA breaks and upregulation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 and glutathione peroxidase 4, key effectors of the oxidative stress response (Supplementary Figure S2c—e).

Given its disruption of cellular energetics and activation of oxidative stress, we hypothesized that ATO would exert cytotoxicity in melanoma cells. MTT assays demonstrated that ATO significantly decreased the viability of B16 and B78 melanoma cells in a dose-dependent manner, with average half maximal inhibitor concentrations of 15 μM at 24 hours (Figure 2a and b). ATO-treated cells also exhibited significantly impaired wound closure compared with vehicle controls (Supplementary Figure S3).

We next tested the in vivo efficacy of ATO using a B78 melanoma mouse model. Pharmacokinetic study results can be found in Supplementary Table S2. ATO-treated mice exhibited significantly improved survival compared with vehicle controls (Figure 2c). Although no significant differences in overall tumor growth were initially observed (Figure 2d), sex-specific analysis revealed that ATO-treated male mice had significantly reduced tumor volumes compared with vehicle-treated male mice (Figure 2e and f) at these time points. Although these findings were unexpected, they may reflect previously reported differences in tumor growth rates between male and female C57BL/6 mice (Vichaya et al, 2021; Dakup et al, 2020). There are no reported differences in treatment outcomes or pharmacokinetic parameters on the basis of sex as a biological variable in prior studies with ATO (Boggild et al, 2007). Consideration of sex as a biological variable will be important in further studies, especially given the potential clinical implications, with epidemiologic studies showing increased melanoma incidence and mortality in male compared with those in female patients (Schwartz et al, 2019).

We also performed optical metabolic imaging in ex vivo B78 tumors at post-treatment day (PTD)1 and PTD9. This revealed striking metabolic heterogeneity, with clusters of cells exhibiting different metabolic phenotypes within the same tumor (Supplementary Figure S4a). As in our in vitro findings, NAD(P)H fluorescence intensity significantly increased, whereas NAD(P)H α1 moderately increased in ATO-treated tumors relative to that in vehicle controls at PTD1, consistent with an initial increase in glycolysis (Supplementary Figure S4b and c). No significant effects were observed with FAD parameters (Supplementary Figure S4d and e). The relative magnitude of these changes varied over time, resulting in an increased optical redox ratio at PTD1 and a decreased optical redox ratio at PTD9, although these did not reach significance (Supplementary Figure S4f). These shifts could suggest an initial adaptation to metabolic disruption that becomes dysregulated over time, although further mechanistic studies are needed.

Moreover, we tested ATO on BRAF-mutated models, the most common sequence variation in malignant melanoma. ATO exhibited cytotoxic effects in both human (MRA5, MRA6) and murine (YUMM1.7) BRAF-altered melanoma cells (Figure 2g, j, and k). Half-maximal inhibitor concentrations were similar between BRAF-mutated and wild-type (MRA2) (Figure 2k) human melanoma cells. In addition, although no significant differences in survival were noted (Figure 2h), we observed significantly reduced tumor growth in ATO-treated YUMM1.7 mice compared with that in controls at day 22 (Figure 2i).

Our bioenergetics experiments showed that OXPHOS contributes substantially to ATP production in melanoma cells. Optical metabolic imaging revealed intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity that included cells with oxidative phenotypes, providing support for targeting OXPHOS in melanoma. In this study, we evaluated ATO in melanoma and demonstrated that it successfully inhibits OXPHOS, disrupts glycolysis, and ultimately diminishes ATP production rates. Beyond its effects on metabolism, ATO also triggered oxidative stress in melanoma cells and exerted antitumorigenic effects, including decreased cell viability and wound healing, and impaired tumor growth in melanoma models with distinct genetic variation profiles. These findings highlight ATO’s potential as a therapeutic agent for different melanoma subtypes.
