Effect of pimobendan on myocardial injury and systolic dysfunction in a canine model of supraventricular tachyarrhythmia
Authors: Daiki HIRAO, Shohei MORITA, Hiroshi HASEGEWA, Aritada YOSHIMURA, Takae KAWAGUCHI, Shusaku YAMADA, Takahiro OOMORI, Kazuhiko SUZUKI, Ryuji FUKUSHIMA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Supraventricular tachyarrhythmia (SVTA) in dogs can cause cardiac dysfunction and may progress to heart failure. Pimobendan, an inotropic agent with calcium-sensitizing and phosphodiesterase III inhibition, is widely used in veterinary medicine. However, its influence on myocardial injury under persistent SVTA remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to evaluate whether pimobendan improves systolic function without exacerbating myocardial injury, using a canine model of rapid atrial pacing (RAP). Fourteen healthy Beagle dogs were allocated to either a control or pimobendan group. RAP was performed for three weeks at 390 bpm. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography before (baseline) and after RAP (3W). Concurrently, cardiac troponin I (cTnI) concentrations were measured to assess myocardial injury. In the control group, a significant increase in cTnI was observed at 3W compared with baseline (0.023 ± 0.029 ng/mL vs. 0.176 ± 0.173 ng/mL). Additionally, in the control group, echocardiographic parameters worsened, including fractional shortening (FS) (37.8 ± 4.6% vs. 24.5 ± 6.8%) and stroke volume (SV) (15.8 ± 3.1 mL vs. 10.5 ± 2.0 mL) at 3W compared with baseline. In contrast, the pimobendan group showed no significant differences in these echocardiographic parameters. cTnI concentrations also remained within the normal range without significant differences. These results suggest that pimobendan preserved systolic function without aggravating myocardial injury during sustained SVTA. Pimobendan may therefore be safe and effective cardiac inotropic agent for the treatment of dogs with SVTA.

INTRODUCTION

Supraventricular tachyarrhythmia (SVTA) includes supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and atrial flutter. In dogs, SVTA occurs as a primary arrhythmia or in association with cardiac or systemic disease [4, 31, 32]. In affected dogs, impulses originating from the atria are rapidly conducted into the ventricles, resulting in an increased ventricular response rate [22, 34]. Persistent tachycardia can lead to a progressive decline in cardiac contractility and may progress to severe heart failure if the damage is sustained over a long period [14, 34, 37]. Therefore, for the long-term management of canine SVTA, it is crucial to establish a treatment strategy that maintains cardiac contractility. While control of arrhythmias contributing to disease progression is recommended, no definitive guidelines exist for managing reduced cardiac function in canine SVTA.

Pimobendan is a drug with dual actions of enhancing calcium sensitivity and inhibiting phosphodiesterase III (PDE III), through which it exerts its pharmacological effects, including positive inotropy and vasodilatation [6, 15, 23]. These combined mechanisms are expected to reduce both preload and afterload, making pimobendan an ideal drug for treating heart failure. In veterinary medicine, pimobendan is widely used to manage canine cardiac diseases, including dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and myxomatous mitral valve degeneration (MMVD) [6, 16, 20, 35].

Cardiac inotropic drugs, such as catecholamine, xanthine derivatives, and cardiac glycosides, exert a positive inotropic effects by increasing Ca2+ influx into cells [5, 11, 36]. However, excessive intracellular Ca2+ influx, known as Ca2+ overload, causes myocardial contractile dysfunction and cardiomyocyte death due to excessive contraction [11, 21]. Additionally, intracellular Ca2+ overload enhances the activity of Na+/Ca2+ exchangers, and the influx of Na+ accompanying Ca2+ efflux can lead to abnormal action potentials [33]. This abnormal excitation, called delayed afterdepolarization (DAD), is one of the mechanisms underlying arrhythmia onset [33]. Clinically, these cardiac inotropes have not been shown to improve long-term outcomes; instead, they increase mortality due to fatal arrhythmias. Therefore, their long-term use is generally not recommended in patients with chronic heart failure [10, 11, 30].

In contrast, pimobendan reportedly enhances the binding affinity of cardiac troponin for Ca2+, exerting its cardiotonic action without excessively increasing intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, unlike other cardiac inotropes [15, 26]. Additionally, Lake-Bakaar et al. further reported that administration of pimobendan to dogs with MMVD did not promote supraventricular or ventricular premature contractions [24]. However, the median number of supraventricular premature contractions within 24 hr in that study was only 9 beats, and no cases with clinically significant tachyarrhythmias that could progress to tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy were included [24]. Therefore, the effects of pimobendan in cases with clinically significant SVTA remain unclear.

Cardiac troponin, a protein complex that regulates muscle contraction, is released into circulation during myocardial injury. Consequently, it is widely used as a biomarker for cardiac diseases in both human and veterinary medicine [8, 13, 25, 39]. We previously demonstrated that dogs subjected to rapid atrial pacing (RAP), an animal model of SVTA, exhibit increased cardiac troponin I (cTnI) concentrations and decreased cardiac contractility [12, 18]. We hypothesized that pimobendan, which exerts a cardiotonic action, could improve the SVTA-induced decline in systolic function in dogs. However, there is concern that the pharmacological actions of cardiac inotropes may exacerbate myocardial injury, thereby worsening the pathology of SVTA.

Therefore, this study aimed to clarify whether pimobendan, a cardiac inotrope approved veterinary use, can suppress myocardial injury and the resulting systolic dysfunction in dogs with persistent SVTA, as assessed by cTnI concentrations and echocardiography.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Creation of the canine SVTA model

This study was approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology, and it was conducted in compliance with animal experimentation regulations (approval number: R06-63). The experiment involved 14 healthy female Beagles aged 1–6 years and weighing 8.2–12.0 kg. Seven dogs were allocated to the control and seven to the pimobendan group. The animals were provided water ad libitum, and their daily energy requirement was calculated using a standard formula (510–670 kcal/day); half of this amount was administered twice a daily. They were housed individually in cages, with the living environment was maintained at a temperature of 21 ± 2°C and a humidity of 50 ± 20%, with lights turned on and off every 12 hr. Additionally, the animals were given approximately 60 min of free time every day, during which they were allowed to walk freely. Prior to the study, all dogs underwent blood pressure measurement, blood testing, and echocardiography to confirm the absence of abnormalities.

To create RAP dogs, a pacemaker lead was placed in the cardiac atrium of the study dogs under general anesthesia, and high-frequency stimulation was applied using an external pacemaker. The RAP dogs were created following the same technique as described by Kawaguchi et al. [19]. Specifically, the dogs were pre-anesthetized with a subcutaneous injection of 0.05 mg/kg atropine sulfate (Atropine sulfate, Nipro ES Pharma Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and administered 0.02 mg/kg of buprenorphine (Lepetan injection 0.2 mg, Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) intramuscularly as an analgesic. Anesthesia was induced by the slow intravenous administration of 10 mg/kg propofol (PropoFlo 28, Zoetis JP, Tokyo, Japan). Once adequate sedation was achieved, tracheal intubation was performed, and anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (Isoflurane Inhalation Solution VTRS, Viatris JP, Tokyo, Japan) at an end-tidal concentration of 1.3–1.7%. The artificial ventilation mode used was pressure-controlled ventilation, with the maximum inspiratory pressure maintained at 8–15 mmHg and the end-tidal carbon dioxide concentration maintained at 35–50 mmHg. Acetated Ringer’s solution was used for fluid infusion, and was administered intravenously at 10 mL/kg/hr during surgery. After induction of anesthesia, a paravertebral block was performed with bupivacaine (0.5% Marcaine injection, Sandoz JP Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at the 2nd, 4th, and 6th thoracic vertebrae on the left side, with the dog in the prone position. Following the nerve block, the dogs were placed in a right lateral recumbent position, and a thoracotomy was performed at the left 4th intercostal space. A stimulation electrode lead (TY216-033a, Unique Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was sutured to the left atrial appendage using a 5-0 polypropylene monofilament suture (5-0 Prolene, Johnson & Johnson JP Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The contralateral side of the electrode lead was passed through the subcutaneous tissue and exposed to the body surface through the skin on the back. The chest was then closed following a standard procedure. Meloxicam (Meloxilin injection 0.5%, Sasaeah Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered subcutaneously at 0.3 mg/kg once daily for 5 days starting on the day of surgery. The electrode lead exposed outside the body was connected to an external pacemaker (PACE, Taisho Biomed Instruments Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), and the body of the pacemaker was placed in a pocket attached to a jacket worn by the animal. After a 2-week postoperative recovery period, RAP was performed for 3 weeks at a voltage of 2.0 mV and a frequency of 390 bpm [12, 18, 19]. The dogs in the pimobendan group were orally administered pimobendan (Vetmedin Chewable tablets, Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 0.25 mg/kg twice daily (8:00–9:00 AM and 5:00–6:00 PM) during the RAP period. No drug was administered to the dogs in the control group. The dosage of pimobendan was determined based on the recommended dosage of Vetmedin Chewable tablets, with reference to the dosages used in previous studies on DCM and guidelines for canine MMVD [20, 35].

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was carried out using an ultrasound diagnostic device (Vivid E95, GE Healthcare Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). To avoid inter-examiner variability, echocardiography was performed by a single examiner certified by the Japanese Society of Veterinary Cardiology. The end-diastolic and end-systolic left-ventricular internal diameters (LVIDd and LVIDs, respectively), as well as left-ventricular fractional shortening (FS), an index of left-ventricular systolic function, were measured using the M-mode in the right parasternal short-axis view at the level of the tendinous cord. The ratio of the pre-ejection period to ejection time (PEP/ET) was measured from the blood flow waveform of the left-ventricular outflow tract using the pulsed Doppler on the left parasternal outflow tract view. The PEP was defined as the time interval between the Q wave and the onset of ventricular ejection. In the same view, the velocity-time integral (VTI) was measured based on the left-ventricular outflow tract diameter (d) and its blood flow waveform, and heart rate (HR) was measured using a synchronized electrocardiogram. Then, cardiac output (CO) and stroke volume (SV) were calculated using the following formula: SV=(d/2)2 × π × VTI, CO=SV × HR. The mean value of nine consecutive beats was recorded as an echocardiographic measurement. All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Non-invasive blood pressure measurement

The mean arterial pressure (MAP) was measured with the dogs in a standing position using an oscillometric sphygmomanometer designed for animals (BP100DII, Fukuda M-E Kogyo Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and a cuff with a width equal to 40–60% of the circumference of the measurement region at the tail-head [9]. The mean value of three measurements was recorded when the difference among readings was within 5 mmHg. All values are expressed as mean ± SD.

Cardiac troponin I concentrations

To obtain serum samples, 2 mL of venous blood was collected from the jugular vein and placed in a serum-separating tube. The samples were allowed to stand for 10 min and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to separate serum. Serum cTnI concentrations were measured using a high-sensitivity chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay method at a commercial testing laboratory (High-sensitivity cardiac troponin I, FUJIFILM VET Systems Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The lower and upper limits of cTnI detection were 0.003 ng/mL and 50 ng/mL, respectively. All values are expressed as mean ± SD. Additionally, the reference value for cTnI concentrations in healthy Beagles, previously determined using the same method, was ≤0.081 ng/mL [19].

Electrocardiography

Electrocardiography was performed using an animal ECG device (Cardisuny D700, Fukuda M-E Kogyo Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). A 6-lead recording was obtained for 5 sec, and a single-lead recording (lead II) was obtained for 30 sec. The QRS duration was evaluated as the primary measurement parameter. Additionally, the presence of other arrhythmias was assessed.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was analyzed using the Kolmogorov−Smirnov test. Echocardiographic measurements, blood pressure measurements, and serum cTnI concentrations were analyzed using repeated two-way analysis of variance. For normally distributed data, multiple comparisons were further performed using Tukey’s test. Statistical analysis was conducted using statistical software for PC (Excel Statistics, Social Survey Research Information, Tokyo, Japan), and a P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

At baseline, no significant differences were observed in any of the measurement items between the control and pimobendan groups.

DISCUSSION

RAP dogs are known to display various changes due to persistent tachycardia, including decreased left-ventricular contractility, lumen enlargement, decreased myocardial blood flow, increased left-ventricular wall stress, elevated end-diastolic pressure, and increased end-diastolic volume [34]. In this study, dogs in the control group exhibited elevated LVIDs, decreased FS, and decreased SV as a result of RAP. In contrast, these changes were absent in dogs receiving pimobendan. This is likely because the RAP-induced decrease in myocardial contractile force was controlled by the positive inotropic effect of pimobendan. However, both the control and pimobendan groups showed an increase in PEP/ET after RAP. PEP/ET is considered an index of left-ventricular systolic function, with higher values indicating reduced ventricular systolic function [7]. PEP/ET is also considered to be less influenced by preload and afterload compared with FS [7]. We expected that pimobendan would strongly suppress the decline in cardiac function; however, the suppressive effect was less pronounced than expected.

When measuring blood pressure using the oscillometric method, MAP reportedly reflects blood pressure values more accurately than other parameters [29], which prompted us to measure MAP in this study. The administration of pimobendan resulted in a decrease in MAP, likely due to its vasodilatory effect through the inhibition of PDE III. However, this change in MAP remained within the normal range [2], suggesting that pimobendan did not have an adverse effect on hemodynamics.

In this study, the control group showed a significant increase in cTnI concentrations following RAP. This finding was consistent with the change in cTnI concentrations observed in a previous study that established a canine RAP model using the same method [18]. Additionally, in our previous study, the ventricular myocardium of untreated RAP dogs exhibited cardiomyocyte degeneration and necrosis, including acidophilic changes in the cytoplasm, loss of striations, and nuclear condensation [19]. Furthermore, these pathological changes were found to be associated with increased cTnI concentrations [19]. In contrast, our findings showed that pimobendan administration suppressed RAP-induced increase in cTnI concentrations. At 3W, cTnI concentrations were significantly lower in dogs treated with pimobendan compared with untreated controls. Additionally, in the pimobendan group, cTnI concentrations after RAP remained within the reference range for healthy Beagles, as determined in our previous study. These findings demonstrate that pimobendan did not exacerbate the myocardial injury observed in RAP dogs and instead suppressed, or at least did not promote such injury. We did not perform histopathological evaluation of the myocardium after RAP in this study. However, our previous study showed that angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors suppressed RAP-induced histopathological changes in the myocardium and increased cTnI concentrations [19]. Pimobendan may exert a similar protective effect by significantly suppressing RAP-induced histopathological changes.

Regarding the mechanism by which pimobendan suppressed myocardial injury in this study, pimobendan may have increased epicardial and endocardial blood flow through its coronary vasodilatory action [38], thereby improving myocardial ischemia caused by RAP. Additionally, previous reports have demonstrated that enalapril suppressed myocardial injury in the SVTA model [19]. This effect is thought to result from the vasodilatory action of enalapril, an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, which reduces ventricular afterload and wall stress, thereby exerting a myocardial protective effect [19]. In this study, the PDEIII inhibition of pimobendan, which relaxes vascular smooth muscle and reduces ventricular afterload, is also considered for a contributing factor to the reduction in myocardial injury. Furthermore, in mice, pimobendan has been reported to exert a direct myocardial protective effect through its antioxidant activity and its suppressive action on inflammatory cytokine production [17, 27]. Therefore, in future studies, quantitative measurements of coronary blood flow, antioxidants, and inflammatory cytokines are expected to elucidate the mechanism by which pimobendan exerts its effect on myocardial injury in RAP dogs.

Clinical cases of canine SVTA often exhibit a ventricular response rate exceeding 200 bpm, and in some cases, it exceeds 300 bpm [28]. In this study, the ventricular response rate during RAP exceeded 250 bpm in both the control and pimobendan groups, suggesting that the frequency was sufficient to establish the SVTA model [12]. Furthermore, the ventricular response rate after a 3-week suspension of RAP showed no significant difference between the control and pimobendan groups. These findings suggest that pimobendan-induced suppression of myocardial injury observed in this study was not attributable to changes in ventricular response rate.

QRS duration changes due to electrical remodeling of the ventricle, and prolonged QRS duration indicates intraventricular conduction disturbance [3]. It is also considered a prognostic indicator in humans with heart failure [3]. In this study, QRS duration was evaluated as an indicator of electrical remodeling in the ventricular myocardium. However, no significant prolongation or deviation from the normal range was observed in either the control or pimobendan groups. Therefore, no significant electrical remodeling of the ventricular myocardium is considered to have occurred during the 3-week period of myocardial injury associated with RAP, indicating no significant difference in QRS duration.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was small. From an ethical standpoint, we were not permitted to create more RAP dogs than were used in this study, and the number of RAP dogs in each group was set at seven based on previous studies using similar models [12, 18, 19]. Thus, in our evaluation based on echocardiography and other parameters, the suppressive effect of pimobendan on the decline in cardiac function may have been underestimated due to the small sample size. Second, with a limited observation period of 3 weeks, we were unable to evaluate the effects of the long-term pimobendan administration. This study clarified the effects of pimobendan on the myocardium during short-term use. However, since pimobendan may be administered long-term in clinical practice, it is necessary to demonstrate that it can maintain systolic function without exacerbating myocardial injury even during long-term use. Third, this study primarily focused on the positive inotropic action of pimobendan, which is its most expected effect, and we only evaluated contractility. Pimobendan reportedly improves not only contractility but also diastolic function [1], and its effect on diastolic function should also be examined in the future. Finally, the pathophysiology of SVTA differs between animal models and clinical cases. In this study, an SVTA model was created by applying RAP to healthy young Beagles. In clinical cases, dogs with SVTA are typically older than the model dogs and often suffer from valvular diseases, such as mitral valve regurgitation and tricuspid valve regurgitation. Therefore, prospective studies involving clinical cases are needed to more accurately translate the findings of this study to clinical practice.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that pimobendan suppressed myocardial injury and reduced ventricular systolic dysfunction in a canine SVTA model. These findings suggest that pimobendan is a cardiac inotrope that can be safely used for the treatment of SVTA in dogs.
