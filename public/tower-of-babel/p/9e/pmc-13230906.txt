Resistant starch-enriched rice varieties improve glucose homeostasis in diabetic mice via modulation of the intestine-liver-pancreas axis
Authors: Jiarui Zeng, Ping Li, Hao Chen, Feiting Li, Xiuying He, Guang Liu, Pengfei Zhou, Zhihao Zhao, Yan Zhang, Mingwei Zhang, Yuanyuan Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus is a prevalent chronic metabolic disease. At present, the efficacy and mechanism of different varieties of resistant-starch-rich rice (RSRR) diet in improving diabetes have not yet been thoroughly analyzed. This study evaluated the effects of a 6-week feeding period with two RSRR varieties, Yueyitang 1 (YYT) and Kangtangdao 1 (KTD), alongside a common rice cultivar, Meixiangzhan 2 (MXZ), using a high-fat diet-induced type 2 diabetic mouse model. Results demonstrated that RSRR reduced fasting blood glucose, blood lipids, and postprandial blood glucose response, with KTD being most effective. RSRR alleviated pancreatic tissue pathology, reduced serum insulin levels, and improved insulin resistance. It also mitigated hepatic steatosis and decreased gluconeogenesis via the AMPK-FoxO1-PEPCK-G6Pase pathway. Additionally, RSRR enhanced colon mucous cell count, up-regulated tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin, and repaired intestinal barrier function. It activated the expression of GPR43, inhibited inflammatory factors Caspase-1 and ASC, promoted the secretion of intestinal hormones PYY and GLP-1, improved gut flora composition, and increased short-chain fatty acid production. Therefore, different RSRR varieties exhibited varying efficacy and mechanisms in improving diabetes, providing a theoretical basis for developing novel dietary interventions for diabetes. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Endocrinology, Physiology

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder prevalent worldwide. In patients with diabetes, the body either fails to produce sufficient insulin or cannot effectively utilize insulin, leading to disruptions in glucose and lipid metabolism1. According to statistics from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), as of 2025, over 585 million adults worldwide suffer from diabetes. Projections indicate that by 2045, the global number of diabetes cases will increase by approximately 45%, reaching around 853 million. With the accelerating aging population, the number of diabetes patients will continue to grow at a high level, posing a pressing public health issue. The diabetes blood glucose management guidelines from IDF emphasize that maintaining stable blood glucose levels after meals is key to controlling diabetes and preventing its complications2.

Rice serves as one of the three primary staple foods globally, providing essential carbohydrates for daily human nutrition. It contains 76–78% starch, which can be categorized into three fractions based on digestive kinetics: rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS)3. Notably, rice exhibits a relatively high RDS content (40–50%), contributing to elevated postprandial glycemic responses and high glycemic index (GI) values. This metabolic profile has been associated with increased risks of obesity, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular diseases4. Conversely, RS and SDS demonstrate superior capacity for maintaining stable postprandial glucose homeostasis. Unlike RDS, RS resists enzymatic hydrolysis in the small intestine and undergoes fermentation in the large intestine, resulting in lower GI values and potential benefits for diabetes management and gut health5,6. Mechanistically, RS exerts glucose-dependent hypoglycemic effects by modulating intestinal peptide secretion, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY)7. Furthermore, Upadhyaya et al.8 demonstrated that RS supplementation significantly altered gut microbiota composition in adolescents, increasing the relative abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria, Bifidobacterium spp., and Isobacteriaceae spp., thereby promoting intestinal homeostasis. Therefore, RS in rice may play a pivotal role in modulating diet-related chronic diseases. However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Although many studies have demonstrated the role of RS in stabilizing blood glucose levels, naturally occurring RS content in conventional rice cultivars remains relatively low (1–3%)9. Consequently, developing rice cultivars with elevated RS content has emerged as a priority in modern rice breeding programs. Matsumoto et al.10 reported that brown rice from RS-enriched mutant cultivars effectively prevented insulin resistance and hypertriglyceridemia in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)-prone NSY mice. Yuta Nakamura et al.11 demonstrated that RS-rich pigmented rice exhibited superior postprandial glycemic control compared to polished white rice in T2DM patients, likely due to its resistance to enzymatic digestion in both proximal and distal small intestine. Nipaporn Muangchan et al.12 further revealed that Riceberry rice attenuated postprandial hyperglycemia by delaying gastric emptying, with glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) dynamics potentially mediating this effect through differential carbohydrate absorption kinetics. Additionally, emerging evidence suggested that rice-derived RS modulated gut microbiota composition and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, which might underlie its glycemic-regulatory properties13,14. However, few studies have compared the glycemic-regulatory efficacy among rice cultivars with equivalent RS content, nor has the consistency of their hypoglycemic mechanisms been established.

This study evaluated two RS-rich rice (RSRR) cultivars, Yueyitang 1 (YYT) and Kangtangdao 1 (KTD), against a conventional RS-content cultivar, Meixiangzhan 2 (MXZ), by examining their hypoglycemic effects in diabetic mice. The underlying mechanisms through four complementary approaches were investigated: (1) insulin signaling pathway activation, (2) hepatic glucose metabolism regulation, (3) intestinal inflammation modulation, and (4) gut microbiota composition change. The findings reveal distinct strain-specific characteristics in RSRR regarding their capacity to modulate diabetes progression.

Results and discussion

Effect of RSRR on the body weight, blood glucose and serum indicators

During the 6-week feeding trial, mice in the MC group exhibited significantly higher BW compared to the NC group (p < 0.05, Fig. 1A). Dietary intervention with YYT and KTD rice cultivars significantly attenuated the weight gain trend in the MC group (p < 0.05). The NC group maintained FBG levels within the normal range (<11.1 mM) throughout the study, whereas the MC group developed persistent hyperglycemia with a peak FBG of 24.93 mM, confirming successful diabetes induction (Fig. 1B). Following intervention with YYT, KTD, and MXZ, all groups demonstrated significant FBG reduction (p < 0.05).

During oral glucose tolerance tests, blood glucose levels peaked at 30 min post-glucose loading in all groups before declining (Fig. 1C). Compared to the MC group, dietary supplementation with YYT and KTD significantly reduced peak glucose concentrations (p < 0.05), with lower values than those observed in the MXZ group. Notably, the AUC for glucose response decreased by 14.08% (in YYT group) and 23.20% (in KTD group) versus the MC group (Fig. 1D), indicating improved glucose tolerance. These findings demonstrate that RSRR cultivars effectively attenuate postprandial hyperglycemia and glycemic variability.

In terms of blood lipid levels, the contents of TG, TC and LDL-C in the serum of mice in the MC group were significantly higher than those in NC group (Fig. 1E–G). Feeding YYT and KTD significantly reduced the changes caused by a high-fat and high sugar diet (p < 0.05). The levels of TC and LDL-C in KTD group were lower than those in other groups, while the levels of TC and LDL-C in YYT and MXZ groups were similar. In addition, feeding YYT, KTD and MXZ also improved the level of HDL-C (Fig. 1H). These results showed that different varieties of RSRR had different effects on blood lipid levels.

Effects of RSRR on pancreatic tissue protection

Histological analysis of pancreatic tissue (Fig. 2A) and biochemical assays (Fig. 2B, C) revealed distinct differences among experimental groups. The NC group exhibited intact pancreatic islet architecture with uniformly distributed cells and no vacuolization. In contrast, the MC group showed irregular cell boundaries, fuzzy demarcation, and pronounced vacuolization. Notably, pancreatic injury was significantly attenuated in the KTD, YYT, and MXZ groups compared to the MC group (p < 0.05), with the KTD group demonstrating near-complete restoration of normal morphology, suggesting a protective effect of RS intake on islet health6.

A Pancreatic tissue morphology. B Serum insulin concentration. C HOMA-IR. D GLUT2 expression levels.

Biochemically, diabetic mice in the MC group developed severe hyperinsulinemia (Fig. 2B) and systemic HOMA-IR (Fig. 2C), with serum insulin levels 2.8-fold higher than those in the NC group. Following rice interventions, both YYT and KTD groups showed significant reductions in serum insulin (by 42% and 58%, respectively) and HOMA-IR (by 37% and 51%, respectively) compared to the MC group. The HOMA-IR of KTD group did not differ significantly from the NC group, indicating complete normalization of insulin sensitivity. These results collectively demonstrate that RSRR cultivars can effectively ameliorate pancreatic injury and metabolic dysregulation in type 2 diabetic mice.

In this work, STZ was used to induce the formation of a type II diabetes mouse model. STZ mainly enters pancreatic β cells through GLUT2, thereby damaging pancreatic β cells, leading to pancreatic tissue damage and islet atrophy, and finally inducing diabetes15. The results showed that the expression of GLUT2 protein in the MC group was significantly higher than that in the NC group (p < 0.05) (Figs. 2D and S1), suggesting that glucose transport activity in the islets of diabetic mice was intensified16. Compared with the MC group, the expression of GLUT2 protein in different feeding groups was significantly decreased (p < 0.05), indicating that the serum glucose content and glucose transmembrane transport activity decreased after consuming RSRR. In addition, Kong et al.17 showed that compared with short-cluster maltodextrin (SCMD), the mixture of maltodextrin and resistant dextrin (MD + RD) contained more RS and less SDS, which could down-regulate the expression levels of glucose transporter genes such as GLUT2 in diabetic mice, while SCMD had an up-regulation effect, indicating that they may control the development of diabetes through different mechanisms.

Effects of RSRR on liver tissue morphology

The observation results of liver tissue staining (Fig. 3A) showed that the liver lobule structure of mice in the NC group was clear and regular, and hepatocytes containing a small amount of lipid droplets were arranged radially around the central vein. In the MC group, the structure of hepatic lobules was disordered, with a large number of lipid droplets and vacuoles of different sizes in hepatocytes. After feeding with YYT and MXZ, the structure of hepatic lobules tended to be normal, and the size and number of lipid droplets decreased, although slight hepatic steatosis was still observed. In the KTD group, a small number of lipid droplets were observed in the liver tissue, and the histological morphology was similar to that in the NC group. This indicates that compared with YYT and MXZ, feeding KTD rice was more helpful in reducing the accumulation of lipid droplets and inflammatory cell infiltration in the liver and improving fatty lesions in the liver of diabetic mice. Liu et al. also pointed out that Potato Resistant Starch 3 can significantly improve the symptoms of type 2 diabetic mice and reduce the pathological damage to the pancreas and liver18.

Effect of RSRR on liver glycogen levels

The evaluation results of liver glycogen levels in diabetic mice showed (Fig. 3B) that the liver cells of mice in the NC group were stained dark purple, indicating high glycogen content. A large number of fat vacuoles were observed in the MC group, and purple-stained glycogen was relatively rare. The liver glycogen staining in the YYT, KTD, and MXZ groups was more intense, showing a significant improvement compared with the MC group (p < 0.05). On the other hand, the measurement results of liver glycogen content showed a similar trend (Fig. 3C). Compared with the NC group, the liver glycogen content in the MC group decreased by 60.36%, while the glycogen storage capacity of the liver in each intervention group was improved. Specifically, the glycogen storage capacity in the YYT group and KTD group increased by 103.45% and 116.46%, respectively. These results indicated that RS has a positive effect on improving glucose metabolism, which might be achieved by increasing the utilization of serum glucose and promoting the synthesis of liver glycogen19.

Effect of RSRR on PEPCK and G6Pase concentrations

PEPCK and G6Pase are key gluconeogenic enzymes that affect plasma glucose content and play an important role in maintaining blood glucose stability and providing energy20. Compared with the NC group, the serum levels of PEPCK and G6Pase in the MC group were significantly increased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D, E). MXZ had no effect on serum G6Pase content but decreased PEPCK content. After being fed with YYT and KTD, the serum levels of PEPCK and G6Pase in the model mice decreased to the levels of those in the normal-diet mice, indicating that YYT and KTD can reduce gluconeogenesis and relieve the symptoms of diabetes by inhibiting glycogen decomposition. Previous studies have also pointed out that RS can significantly down-regulate the expression levels of PEPCK and G6Pase in the liver, thereby inhibiting gluconeogenesis and reducing the blood glucose concentration21,22.

Effect of RSRR on marker gene and protein expressions related to liver function

AMPK is an important protein molecule that regulates the body’s energy metabolism, especially carbohydrate synthesis and decomposition. When T2DM occurs, the expression of AMPK is usually inhibited23. After the activation of AMPK phosphorylation in the liver, it can maintain blood glucose homeostasis by regulating the expression of genes related to gluconeogenesis and liver glucose production, as well as promoting liver glycogen synthesis19. AMPKα plays a dominant role in AMPK activity and is highly expressed in the liver. The expression levels of related proteins were determined (Figs. 4A–D and S2). There was no significant difference in the expression levels of AMPKα protein in each group, suggesting that the intervention mainly affected the phosphorylation state of AMPK. Compared with the NC group, the expression level of p-AMPKα protein in the MC group was significantly decreased, indicating that its energy metabolism regulation ability was impaired, while the expression levels of PEPCK and G6Pase were significantly increased (p < 0.05), indicating that its gluconeogenesis was significantly enhanced. After being fed with YYT, KTD, and MXZ, the expression level of p-AMPKα protein increased, indicating that the activation of the AMPK signaling pathway was obvious, and the expression levels of PEPCK and G6Pase protein were significantly decreased (p < 0.05). The overall improvement effect of YYT and KTD was better than that of MXZ.

Expression levels of functional proteins and genes related to liver glycogen metabolism in mice of different treatment groups.

A, B, E, AMPK. C, G PEPCK. D, H G6Pase. F FoxO1.

In addition, the detection results of related gene expression levels also showed a similar trend (Fig. 4E-H). Compared with the MC group, only the expression of AMPK-related genes in the KTD group was significantly up-regulated (p < 0.05), and the KTD group had the best effect in the down-regulation of other related genes (FoxO1, PEPCK, and G6Pase), while the effect of the MXZ group was poor. Existing studies have confirmed that the activation of AMPK phosphorylation in the liver can inactivate FoxO1 phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting the expression of downstream PEPCK and G6Pase genes24,25. These results indicated that among the three kinds of rice, the order of their effects on improving liver glucose metabolism from strongest to weakest was KTD, YYT, and MXZ. It can increase glycogen storage in the liver by promoting the phosphorylation of AMPK, a key protein in energy metabolism, and at the same time, it regulates the FoxO1 gluconeogenesis signaling pathway mediated by AMPK, inactivates the phosphorylation of FoxO1 protein, thus inhibiting the gene expression of PEPCK and G6Pase downstream, reducing liver gluconeogenesis, and improving the glucose metabolism disorder in diabetic mice, which is consistent with previous research results26.

Effect of RSRR on colon tissue morphology and mucous cell content

Histopathological examination of the intestinal mucosa showed (Fig. 5A) that the colonic tissue structure of NC mice was complete and clear, with well-defined colonic crypts. In contrast, the colonic crypts of MC mice were severely damaged, with an average depth reduction of approximately 40% compared to the NC group. The number of goblet cells was reduced by about 60%, and the colonic muscle layer was significantly thinner, with a thickness decrease of around 50%, suggesting inflammation and tissue damage. In the YYT and KTD groups, the colonic tissue structure and colonic crypt morphology were significantly improved. The average depth of colonic crypts increased by about 30% compared to the MC group, goblet cells recovered well, with the number increasing by approximately 50%, and the thickness of the colonic muscle layer was increased by around 40%. The thickness of the colonic muscle layer of MXZ mice recovered, with an increase of about 30% compared to the MC group, but some colonic crypt structures were still slightly damaged, with an average depth reduction of about 10%, and the number of goblet cells decreased slightly by about 15%. These results showed that the intervention groups improved the colon injury of diabetic mice to varying degrees, especially the YYT and KTD groups, which showed a significant protective effect on colon tissue.

The results of colonic tissue staining and the count of goblet cells showed that the number of goblet cells in MC mice decreased (Fig. 5B, C). Compared with MC mice, the number of goblet cells in YYT, KTD, and MXZ groups increased significantly (p < 0.05), and the KTD group had the highest number of goblet cells among all groups. This indicates that feeding RSRR can effectively increase the number of goblet cells in the intestinal tract of diabetic mice, thereby helping to repair the intestinal barrier function. Existing studies have also confirmed that RS can reduce inflammation, lower the risk of colon cancer, and improve intestinal barrier function in colon tissue27. It is likely that RS exerts these effects by modulating certain signaling pathways or cellular metabolic processes, which in turn promote the increase in goblet cell numbers and the repair of the intestinal barrier.

Effect of RSRR on marker gene and protein expressions related to intestinal barrier function, inflammation, and hormonal readiness

In order to clarify the effect of rice RS on intestinal physiology in mice, proteins and gene expressions related to intestinal barrier function were detected, including tight junction-associated proteins ZO-1 and occluding28. The protein expression levels of ZO-1 and occludin were significantly decreased in the MC group (p < 0.05), indicating that the intestinal barrier function was impaired (Figs. 6A–D and S5, S6). Compared with mice in the MC group, the overall expression levels of ZO-1 and occludin proteins and their corresponding genes in the YYT group and KTD group were significantly increased (p < 0.05). In addition, SGLT1 affects the function of the intestinal barrier by regulating intestinal glucose and sodium absorption29. In the MC group, the increased expression of SGLT1 protein likely disrupts the normal regulation of intestinal glucose and sodium transport, leading to impaired intestinal glucose transport (Figs. 6E and S7). After the intervention of YYT and KTD, the expression level of SGLT1 decreased significantly (p < 0.05), especially in the KTD group. This reduction in SGLT1 expression may help restore normal glucose absorption and improve intestinal barrier function.

Expression levels of proteins and genes related to colonic barrier function in mice of different treatment groups.

A and C ZO-1. B and D Occludin. E SGLT1. F Caspase-1. G ASC. H GPR43. I PYY. J GLP-1.

Caspase-1 and ASC are associated with pathophysiological processes such as intestinal homeostasis, chronic low-grade inflammation, and insulin secretion disorders30. Compared with the NC group, the expression of caspase-1 and ASC proteins in the colon tissue of the MC group was significantly increased (p < 0.05), showing up-regulation of inflammation-related protein levels (Fig. 6F, G and S8, S9). The expression of caspase-1 and ASC proteins in the YYT, KTD, and MXZ groups was significantly decreased (p < 0.05), and the KTD group showed the best effect in reducing the expression of these proteins. In addition, GPR43 can regulate the activity of immune cells, inhibit inflammatory responses, and help slow down diabetes-related chronic inflammation by sensing changes in the content of SCFAs31. The expression of GPR43 protein was decreased in the MC group, while it was significantly increased in the YYT and KTD groups (p < 0.05) (Figs. 6H and S10), indicating that it was helpful for improving the intestinal health of diabetic mice. These changes in protein expression collectively suggest the potential impact of rice RS on intestinal physiology and diabetes-related processes.

PYY is a peptide hormone secreted by the intestine. Studies have found that the secretion of PYY in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus after a meal is reduced, which may be related to insulin resistance and is a sign of diabetes-related metabolic disorders32. GLP-1 is a glucose-concentration-dependent incretin secreted by L cells in the ileum and colon, which plays an active role in lowering blood glucose33. The expression levels of PYY and GLP-1 in the MC group were significantly lower than those in the NC group (Fig. 6I, J). After treatment with YYT and KTD, the levels of PYY and GLP-1 in diabetic mice were significantly increased (p < 0.05), while MXZ intervention had no significant effect on improving the levels of PYY. These changes in PYY and GLP-1 levels suggest their potential roles in the metabolic regulation of diabetic mice and the effects of different interventions. This result is consistent with the previous studies, indicating high expression of PYY (3-36) can increase appetite, decrease body weight and circulating glucose in high-fat-diet mice34.

Effect of RSRR on gut microbiota diversity in mice

After a 6-week dietary intervention, the fecal contents of 40 mice were sequenced using 16S rRNA gene sequencing (n = 8 in each group). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) are clustered based on sequence similarity (97% level) in this study. A total of 1,887,228 sequences were obtained, and 1677 OTUs were clustered. At the OTU level, changes in the overall structure of gut microbiota were observed after KTD, YYT, and MXZ interventions. Specifically, there were 275 OTUs in total among the MC, NC, YYT, KTD, and MXZ groups. The number of OTUs unique to each group was 64 for MC, 328 for NC, 110 for YYT, 212 for KTD, and 127 for MXZ, respectively (Fig. 7E). These results provide a basis for further exploring the impact of different interventions on the intestinal microbial community.

Diversity of intestinal microflora in mice of different treatment groups.

A Shannon index. B Sobs index. C Ace index. D PCoA analysis. E Wayne diagram of OTU abundance distribution. F Lefse cluster analysis.

The Shannon index, which reflects the species diversity of a microbial community, showed that the values for the YYT and KTD groups were higher (Fig. 7A). This indicates relatively large species diversity in these two groups, suggesting that YYT and KTD increase the diversity of bacterial communities in MC mice. A more diverse gut microbiota is often related to healthy intestinal microflora35. The sobs index, an indicator of species abundance, also increased significantly after the intervention of YYT, KTD, and MXZ (Fig. 7B). This implies an increase in the species abundance of the flora, which helps to form a more stable intestinal ecosystem36. The ace index, which can reflect the abundance of low-abundance species in a community, was the lowest in the MC group. After intervention with different rice, the ace index increased significantly (Fig. 7C). This indicates an increase in low-abundance flora in the intestine, suggesting that RSRR promotes the recovery or growth of rare probiotics. These flora also play an important role in maintaining the diversity and health of the intestinal ecosystem35,36. In addition, the analysis of intestinal beta diversity in each group showed that the proportions of sample variation explained by the two principal coordinate axes, PC1 and PC2 were 39.22% and 14.54%, respectively (Fig. 7D). This indicated significant structural differences in intestinal microbial communities between different groups, showing significant separation and spatial aggregation.

Lefse cluster analysis revealed the flora-specific distribution of different groups (Fig. 7F). The results showed that the characteristic flora of the NC group was concentrated in Bacteroidota. In contrast, the characteristic flora of the MC group was mainly concentrated in Bacilli, Actinobacteria, and Desulfobacterota. The characteristic flora of the YYT group was primarily concentrated in Deferribacterota and Marinifilaceae. The KTD group had a unique flora distribution, with its characteristic flora concentrated in Firmicutes, Paesciabacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, and Oscillospirales, some of which could produce SCFAs such as butyrate. The MXZ group exhibited a wide range of flora characteristics, mainly concentrated in Firmicutes. These results aligned with the findings of Lee et al., indicating that varying forms and sources of starch exert distinct impacts on the gut microbiota37. Studies have found that improving the symptoms of diabetes generally leads to an increase in the number of Firmicutes bacteria, which are related to glucose metabolism and may reduce insulin resistance38. In addition, Liu et al.39 demonstrated that high levels of corn RS increased the abundance of Coprococcus and Bacteroides, suggesting that different types of RS have various effects on the improvement of gut microbiota. Some beneficial bacteria within Actinobacteriota are generally considered capable of maintaining intestinal health. Their abundance is usually related to the composition of gut microbiota in healthy individuals, which helps to enhance immune function and has a positive effect on glucose metabolism. These bacteria can also produce SCFAs, improve the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and regulate the inflammatory response, which is conducive to the control of diabetes40,41. In this study, it was found that in addition to the NC group, there were also many characteristic floras in the MC group concentrated in Actinobacteria, which may be due to the immune balance of the gut microbiota of diabetic mice.

Effect of RSRR on SCFA levels in mice

The concentrations of acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, and total SCFAs in the feces of mice were analyzed (Fig. 8). The concentrations of various SCFAs in MC mice decreased significantly compared to the NC group, indicating an inhibitory effect on the metabolism of gut microbiota. After feeding with KTD, the concentrations of different SCFAs increased significantly (p < 0.05). In contrast, the effects of YYT and MXZ on SCFA concentrations were relatively weak, indicating that KTD has a positive regulatory effect on the recovery of fatty acid levels in the intestine of mice fed with a high-fat diet. Metabolites produced by gut microbes (e.g., SCFAs, bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives) can pass through the damaged intestinal barrier, enter the portal vein circulation and be transported to the liver, thereby improving the “gut-liver axis” function and reducing liver fat accumulation and inflammation42. Zheng et al. found that butyric acid produced by gut microbiota activates CaMKII/HDAC1-CREB pathway through GPR41/43 receptor to improve liver lipid metabolism, which is helpful for diabetes control by improving fatty liver43. Otherwise, studies have shown that the effect of butyric acid on AMPK pathway is context-dependent. In sensitive cells, butyric acid activates AMPK and plays an anti-cancer role. However, long-term exposure to butyric acid can also lead to cell tolerance and inhibit the activation of AMPK expression44.

Many studies have confirmed that RS may produce more SCFAs mainly through gut microbiota fermentation, activate AMPK, inhibit the expression of FoxO1 through PI3K/Akt pathway, and then inhibit the expression levels of PEPCK and G6Pase, thereby reducing hepatic gluconeogenesis, improving hepatic insulin sensitivity and reducing blood glucose21. This is consistent with the conclusions obtained in this study, suggesting that RS diet might be beneficial for pancreas, liver, intestinal barrier, and gut microbiota health (Fig. 9). In addition, RS can not only regulate gluconeogenesis pathway, but also comprehensively regulate liver glucose metabolism by increasing glycogen synthesis and improving insulin signaling pathway (such as PI3K/Akt)45. At the same time, RS can significantly reduce free fatty acids and inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, NF-κB in the liver, help to relieve the inhibition of Akt, enhance the inhibitory regulation of FoxO1, help to restore insulin sensitivity and reduce liver insulin resistance46. Luo et al. also demonstrated that the synergism of RS and β-glucan plays better effect when they coexist in the similar structure of whole grains, which may delay starch digestion, promote colon fermentation, and enhance the production of SCFAs, so as to more effectively regulate the AMPK-FoxO1-PEPCK-G6Pase pathway46.

The mechanism of RSRR regulation in ameliorating glucose metabolism of diabetic mice.

RSRR improved glucose homeostasis in diabetic mice by modulating the intestine-liver-pancreas axis, which includes regulating gut microbiota, repairing the intestinal barrier, and protecting the liver and pancreas.

Studies have also found that different types of RS have a positive effect on improving intestinal SCFAs47,48. Liang et al. had demonstrated that RS type 4 fermentation could significantly increase the levels of acetate, while RS type 2 had a more pronounced effect on propionate and butyrate49. Erucic acid starch ester (a type 4 RS) could enter the blood circulation and reach the liver via the hepatic portal system. This process was achieved by regulating the gut microbiota and increasing the production of SCFAs, such as butyric acid. As a result, the proportion of p-AMPK/AMPK and p-FoxO1/FoxO1 was significantly increased, and the expression of PEPCK was inhibited50. Sun et al. demonstrated that type 2 RS can ameliorate hyperglycemia in diabetic rats by regulating the expression of key enzymes involved in liver glucose metabolism. In addition to down-regulating PEPCK and G6Pase, it may also up-regulate genes related to glycolysis and glucose uptake, such as glucokinase, pancreatic and duodenal homeobox (PDX-1), and insulin receptor substrates, thus promoting insulin secretion and signal transduction22. This is consistent with our findings that different treatments have varying impacts on SCFA types and concentrations.

The study results demonstrated that RSRR achieved blood glucose regulation through multiple mechanisms. It protected the pancreas and liver, repaired the intestinal barrier, and modulated the gut microbiota. Specifically, RSRR effectively reduced fasting blood glucose and blood lipid levels in diabetic mice, while also attenuating the postprandial blood glucose response. RSRR alleviated pancreatic lesions, decreased serum insulin levels, and down-regulated GLUT2 expression, thereby improving insulin resistance. Additionally, it can alleviate hepatic steatosis. By regulating the AMPK-FoxO1-PEPCK-G6Pase pathway, it reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis. In the colon, RSRR mitigated mucosal lesions, increased the mucous cells, enhanced the expression of ZO-1 and Occludin, down-regulated SGLT1 expression, and thus repaired intestinal barrier function. Moreover, RSRR activated GPR43 expression, inhibited the expression of inflammatory factors Caspase-1 and ASC, and promoted the secretion of intestinal hormones PYY and GLP-1. Additionally, it optimized the composition of the gut microbiota by increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria and enhancing the SCFAs production. These results confirmed that different RSRRs exhibited distinct effects and mechanisms in improving the condition of diabetic mice via modulating the intestine-liver-pancreas axis, with KTD demonstrating the most pronounced effect. The results laid a theoretical foundation for the development of a novel RSRR-based diet for diabetes management.

Methods

Preparation of rice samples

Three indica rice cultivars: Yueyitang 1 (YYT) was sourced from Hangzhou Liquan Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China; Kangtangdao 1 (KTD) and Meixiangzhan 2 (MXZ) were sourced from Rice Research Institute, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Guangzhou, China. Among these rice cultivars, YYT and KTD have the same high level of RS content, while MXZ contains lower RS content, but it is used as a representative variety of widely planted high-quality indica rice. Rice flour was obtained from the grains using a laboratory polishing machine (Pearlest, Kett, Japan) and a laboratory milling machine (Scino CT410, FOSS, Sweden) in sequence, and sieved through an 80 mesh screen. The nutritional composition of different rice cultivars had been detected in the pre-experiments (Table 1).

Modeling of type 2 diabetic mice and animal feeding experiments

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IUCAC) of the Sericultural & Agri-Food Research Institute, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (2023-SC-011). Male Kunming mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University (No.44002100022309, Guangzhou, China) and maintained under pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C), and a relative humidity of 55 ± 10%. The mice were anesthetized before euthanasia. Pentobarbital sodium (120 mg/kg) was injected into the tail vein to control the depth of anesthesia, as indicated by the absence of a withdrawal reflex to toe pinch. After that, blood was collected from the orbit, and the mice were then euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Forty C57BL/6 male mice (4-week-old) were randomly divided into five groups. The normal control group (NC) received an AIN-93 G basal diet, while the remaining mice were fed a XTHF60 high-fat diet. The experimental diet composition and dietary intake of calories are shown in Tables S2 and S3, respectively. After sixty days of feeding, high-fat diet-fed mice were intraperitoneally injected with streptozotocin (STZ, 50 mg/kg) for three consecutive days, while NC mice received saline instead of STZ. Diabetes was confirmed by fasting blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L. Thirty-two successfully modeled mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8/group): diabetic model group (MC), YYT-feeding group, KTD-feeding group, and MXZ-feeding group. During the rice intervention, YYT, KTD, and MXZ rice flours served as the sole carbohydrate sources in the high-fat diets of respective groups. Other energy sources (protein and fat) were proportionally adjusted to maintain intergroup energy equivalence. All mice had ad libitum access to water and respective diets during a six-week test period.

Determination of body weight and fasting blood glucose

On the day of successful modeling, all mice were weighed, and the fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured by tail tip blood sampling. After weighing, the feed was started with the weight of the next day as the benchmark weight. After the feed intervention, the body weight (BW) and FBG of the mice were measured at the same time every week, and the data were recorded. The mice were fasted but allowed free access to water for 12 h before FBG was measured.

Oral glucose tolerance test

Oral glucose tolerance test was performed in the last week of intervention. The experimental mice were fasted but allowed free access to water without water for 12 h, and the FBG was measured before glucose administration (0 min). After 30 min, each dose group was given different resistant starch content samples, and NC and MC were given 10 mL/kg dose of distilled water. After 30 min, each group was given oral glucose solution (20%, w/v) at 2.0 g/kg51. The blood glucose (BG) was measured at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min, respectively, and the glucose tolerance curve was drawn accordingly52,53. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the formula: AUC = 1/4BG0 + 1/2BG30 + 3/4BG60 + 1/2BG120.

Mouse tissue sample collection

After 6 weeks of intervention, mouse tissue samples were collected. The blood was collected in anticoagulant blood vessels by removing the eyeballs of mice, and then centrifuged at 1300 × g, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was obtained and labeled with its group information. The mice were dissected after taking blood, and the liver, pancreas and colon of the mice were taken, numbered and cryopreserved for testing. Before fasting, mice were stimulated to defecate, and fecal samples were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. All plasma, tissue and fecal samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Determination of serum lipids and insulin resistance-related indicators

To investigate the metabolic and endocrine status of mice, the indicators in serum were measured using corresponding enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Shanghai Enzyme Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China)54,55. The serum lipid levels, including total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), triglyceride (TG), and insulin (INS), were determined using enzyme calibration (Versa max, Molecular Devices, China). Insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR) was calculated as the formula: HOMA-IR = (FBG × INS)/22.5.

Additionally, the levels of markers related to liver and colon functions, including indicators associated with hepatic gluconeogenesis, such as phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), glucose-6-phosphatase (G6pase) and glycogen, as well as colonic hormones GLP-1 and PYY, were also assessed.

Histological microscopic observation of mice

Oil red O (ORO) staining was used to display lipid substances in organs and tissues56. Take fresh organ tissues (1 × 1 × 0.5 cm3) and put them in the sample bottle. Use 4% paraformaldehyde solution (sample volume: fixed volume = 1:10) and fix them at room temperature for more than 24 h. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sliced. After the frozen sections were dried, they were immersed in ORO staining solution for 8–10 min, and then used 60% isopropanol for background differentiation. The sections were re-stained with hematoxylin for 3–5 min, differentiated and returned to blue, and finally sealed with glycerin gelatin. After processing, observe and take images under the microscope at a magnification of 200 times. The staining results showed that the lipid droplets were orange red to bright red, and the nucleus was light blue.

Liver glycogen periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed according to the method described by Xu et al.57. The liver was harvested, and the tissue was fixed in Carnoy’s solution at 4 °C for 6–8 h. Subsequently, it was dehydrated at room temperature through an ethanol gradient, cleared with xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm slices. For PAS staining, the sections were first stained with periodic acid for 15 min, rinsed, and then stained with Schiff reagent for 30 min. After another rinse for 5 min, they were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3–5 min. Following a final rinse, the sections were counterstained with a blue-returning solution, dehydrated, mounted, and examined under a microscope at a magnification of 200 times. Positive hepatocytes exhibited purple-red glycogen granules, while the nuclei appeared light blue.

Colonic mucus-secreting cells were counted by Alcian blue (AB) staining58. Frozen sections of formalin-fixed tissues (with a thickness of about 3 μm) were attached to the slides. The sections were dewaxed with xylene, passed through gradient ethanol, and then hydrated with distilled water. The sections were sequentially soaked in AB acidification solution and staining solution for 30 min each, followed by rinsing with running water for 5 min. Next, they were restained with nuclear fast red staining solution for 5–10 min and then rinsed with running water for 1 min. After that, the sections were dehydrated with gradient alcohol and washed with xylene until transparent. Finally, they were sealed with neutral gum. The observed area and the number of mucus-secreting cells were recorded under the Motic Digital microscope system (Motic Incorporation Ltd., Hong Kong, China).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to observe tissue damage and inflammation. The pancreas, colon and liver tissues were rinsed with cold phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Subsequently, paraffin block preparation and section staining were performed according to the standard histopathological protocol12. Inflammatory infiltration and tissue damage were observed under a 200-fold microscope.

Western blotting analysis

The preparation was performed according to the method described by Hou et al.59. Pancreas, colon, and liver tissues were homogenized in a RIPA lysis buffer (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) using a tissue homogenizer. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 12,800 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain suspensions. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE. The electrophoresis was carried out to make a proper separation of the proteins. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore Corp., Massachusetts, USA) using a semi-dry transfer system. The PVDF membranes were then soaked in TBST solution containing 5% skim milk powder and shaken at room temperature for 2 h for blocking. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against glucose transporter (GLUT2), PEPCK, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK, including AMPKα and p-AMPKα), G6pase, tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and Occludin), sodium-glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1), G-protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and cysteinyl aspartate-specific proteinase-1 (Caspase-1), dilution ratio of 1:1000 (Bode Bioengineering Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China). Subsequently, the membranes were rinsed with TBST and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (dilution ratio of 1:5000, Bode Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) in a shaker at 37 °C for 2 h. The membrane was then washed five times with TBST solution. The proteins were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Mouse GAPDH antibody served as a loading control to ensure equal protein loading among the samples. GAPDH protein was used as an internal reference, and the relative quantification of proteins was achieved by comparing the signal intensity ratios between proteins.

RNA Extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) detection

Total RNA from the colon tissue was isolated using an RNA extraction kit (DP430, Tiangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from the total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (AORT-0060, Baiyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative mRNA expression levels of all gene targets (FoxO1, AMPK, PEPCK, G6Pase, ZO-1, and Occludin) were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using the specific primers listed in Table S1. qRT-PCR reactions were performed using the Roche LightCycler 96 instrument with 10 μL of AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Q121-02, Baiyi Huineng Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China), 0.5 μL of each primer (10 μmol/L), 2 μL of cDNA, and 2.5 μL of double-distilled water. The specificity and sensitivity of the qRT-PCR reactions were verified by melting curve analysis and standard curve construction. β-actin mRNA expression was used as an internal control for normalization. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method60.

Determination of SCFAs in mouse feces

The fecal samples were collected from each group of mice. According to the solid-liquid ratio (m:v = 1:9), 200 mg of fresh feces were mixed with distilled water, mashed, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to collect the supernatant, which was subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. According to the method of Vester Boler et al.61, the concentrations of acetic acid, propionic acid and butyric acid in the supernatant were determined by a GC-MS system using a fused silica capillary column (DB-FFAP125-3237, Agilent Technologies, USA). The quantification was achieved by measuring the peak areas of acetic acid (m/z 60), propionic acid (m/z 74) and butyric acid (m/z 73) relative to the internal standard 2-ethylbutyric acid (m/z 88).

Diversity analysis of gut microbiota in mice

Total bacterial DNA was extracted from the feces of the tested mice using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and the DNA quality was assessed using 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis (BG-gds AUTO-130, Beijing Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). The V3–V4 region of the microbial DNA was amplified using two universal primers (338F, 5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’ and 806R, 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The obtained PCR products were sequenced and analyzed using the Quantitative Insight into Microbial Ecology software (QIIME, version 2.0). Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) selection was performed by searching for the V3–V4 regions with ≥97% sequence similarity. Based on OTU levels, alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted, including a difference test between index groups such as Ace, Shannon, and Sobs. The PCoA and species composition (Venn diagram) analysis, as well as Lefse multi-level species differences discriminant analysis, were also performed along with linear discriminant analysis to characterize the gut microbiome of mice62.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test, Spearman’s correlation analysis, and figure generation were performed using R 4.3.3 and GraphPad Prism 8 software to determine statistically significant differences between the means. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.
