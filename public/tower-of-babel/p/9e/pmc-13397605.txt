Heat effects on influenza-like illness and heterogeneity factors across regions: based on 333 cities in China
Authors: Shuhan Tang, Yi Yin, Zhibin Peng, Xin Jiang, Xiaokun Yang, Ziying Chen, Sijia Zhou, Tiantian Li, Yanni Xiao, Daihai He, Yanping Zhang, Zhihang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile cold weather’s impact on influenza-like illness (ILI) is well-studied, heat effects remain underexplored. This study examines the relationship between heat and influenza risk across China, examining regional variations and quantifying heat-attributable disease burden.MethodsWe analyzed weekly ILI surveillance data and temperature records from 333 Chinese cities (2010–2018), focusing on warm seasons (May–October). Using distributed lag non-linear models combined with the generalized additive quasi-Poisson regression model, we assessed non-linear heat-ILI associations in each city. China was divided into seven geographical regions to evaluate how economic development, healthcare resources, and population composition modify the adaptation of ILI to heat effects.ResultsThe merged results of heat effects on ILI vary in seven regions. Nationally, influenza risk increases when temperatures exceed the region-specific minimum risk temperature. In the northeast, northern, northwest, and southern regions, risk rises significantly at temperatures of 17 °C, 16.4 °C, 14.7 °C, and 21.9 °C respectively. Conversely, in the central and southwest regions, risk declined with rising temperatures before showing minimal subsequent growth. In the eastern region, risk increased steadily above 11.8℃, with only a slight decline at extreme high temperatures. These patterns collectively highlight the pronounced effect of high temperatures on ILI epidemics. Elderly population proportion significantly modified heat effects in most regions. Population size modified ILI’s heat adaptability in Northwest/Southwest, while the proportion of low-income populations across cities influenced Northern/Southern/Northwest/central regions. Quantitative analysis revealed that in 139 cities, temperature spikes contributed to an estimated 3,128,629 excess ILI cases in total.ConclusionsThis nationwide study demonstrates that heat effects were associated with influenza-like illness risk across China, with variations across regions. These regional differences in heat-ILI associations varied with local healthcare conditions, economic status, social environment, and population age structure. The findings support the need for region-specific public health interventions that consider these modifying factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27868-2.

Background

Between 2010 and 2020, the cumulative number of influenza cases in China reached 894.53 million [1]. Seasonal influenza has long been recognized as a critically important human infectious disease, exhibiting poorer clinical outcomes, higher mortality rates, and imposing greater direct healthcare costs compared to other respiratory viral diseases [2]. As such, it significantly impacts public health. In China, between 2010 and 2015, the annual excess respiratory deaths caused by influenza were 88,100, corresponding to 8.2% of respiratory deaths [3]. Many studies have extensively explored the impact of meteorological factors, especially temperature [4, 5], on influenza activity. The impact of temperature on influenza is not isolated. Other meteorological factors and healthcare conditions can also affect influenza transmission. For instance, relative humidity and temperature interact synergistically to jointly facilitate the transmission of influenza [6]. Healthcare conditions determine access to medical resources and vaccines, thereby influencing both the rate and severity of infections [7, 8]. Amid global warming, the specific impact of high temperatures on the burden of influenza remains insufficiently studied and understood. This knowledge gap hinders the development of effective strategies to control influenza epidemics and alleviate disease burden.

Studies in China have shown that temperature has a heterogeneous impact on influenza activity. Yin et al.’s nationwide analysis of 201 cities revealed a non-linear temperature-influenza-like illness (ILI) association, where ILI activity was predominantly driven by low temperatures. The study further highlighted distinct temperature-risk curves across regions [9]. Zhang et al. [10] used a non-linear distributed lag model to explore the association between temperature and influenza morbidity in 30 cities in China. They performed a multivariable meta-analysis to assess the combined effects of each city. They found an N-shaped relationship between temperature and influenza morbidity in these cities. In addition, another study found that both low and high temperatures increased ILI transmission risk in southern and central China [11].

While the relationship between temperature and influenza has been explored [8, 12–15], existing studies have primarily focused on cold effects or overall temperature ranges. A nationwide study specifically quantifying heat effects on influenza across Chinese cities, and identifying regional modifiers, remains limited. Such factors may include latitude, demographic characteristics, and socioeconomic conditions. Specifically, influenza activity exhibits heterogeneity by latitude, with latitude itself serving as a modifier of the relationship between meteorological factors and influenza dynamics [16, 17]. Furthermore, demographic characteristics, such as population size and age structure, may influence infectious contact patterns among cities or regions [10, 18]. Moreover, socioeconomic conditions may influence population mobility, ILI case reporting, and other aspects [19]. Relatedly, it was found that an area’s economic level is partly reflected by indicators such as the area’s poverty level and the condition of its health facilities [20]. In addition, high usage of air conditioning in hot weather was found to be associated with the summer peak in influenza cases. Thus, regional differences in air-conditioning usage between regions may be another factor [8]. In summary, the single or combined effect of the aforementioned factors may influence the heterogeneity of the high-temperature effect on influenza incidence between cities and regions.

This study aims to investigate the heat effects on influenza-like illness based on 333 cities in mainland China between May and October 2010–2018. Specifically, we seek to characterize the non-linear and lagged associations between high temperature and ILI risk in different regions to identify how regional socioeconomic, demographic, and environmental factors modify this association. To this end, the country was divided into seven climatic regions in order to systematically explore the factors driving heterogeneity in the high-temperature effect on ILI. We also calculated the excess cases of ILI when the temperature rises sharply in these seven regions. The findings deepen the understanding of the influence of heat effects on ILI. They thus will aid the improvement of influenza prevention and control strategies and enhance the capacity to respond to influenza outbreaks in China.

Methods

Association between heat effects and ILI incidence

Weekly numbers of ILI cases from 333 cities in China from January 1, 2010 to December 30, 2018 were collected. These data were obtained through the National Influenza Surveillance Network, which includes sentinel hospitals and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 31 Chinese provinces. Influenza-like illness cases are defined as patients having body temperature ≥ 38℃ with either cough or sore throat, in the absence of an alternative diagnosis [21].

The annual population of each city during the study period was obtained from the Chinese National Bureau of Statistics (http://www.stats.gov.cn/). Ambient mean temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), average sunshine duration, and wind speed data (m/s) were sourced from a National Meteorological Information Centre dataset (http://data.cma.cn/) with a resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°, which is based on interpolations of observations from Chinese meteorological stations. This dataset was generated by spatially interpolating observations from the national meteorological station network. For each city, daily meteorological values were derived by averaging all grid cells located within its administrative boundaries. To align with the weekly structure of the ILI data, these daily series were further aggregated into weekly averages for analysis. We selected data between May and October, as this is the hottest period of the year and thus allowed us to examine the high-temperature effect on ILI cases. Based on climatic characteristics, we divided China into seven regions that encompassed the 333 cities.

Subsequently, we employed a two-stage analytical framework to systematically assess the impact of high temperature on the risk of ILI across different regions of China. In the first stage, we applied a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) embedded within a generalized additive model framework, using a quasi-Poisson family to account for overdispersion in the weekly ILI case counts. A maximum lag of 14 days was used to capture both immediate and delayed effects of temperature on ILI risk. Heterogeneity in the high-temperature effect on ILI incidence across the seven regions

To explore heterogeneity in the high-temperature effects across the seven geographical regions, we performed random-effect multivariate meta-regression using seven city-level characteristics as potential factors. These characteristics include: latitude, gross domestic product, total population size, proportion of low-income population, proportion of the population aged 65 years or older, number of air conditioners per 100 households, and number of hospitals. Marked heterogeneity was observed across cities in terms of these characteristics (Table S1 for detailed distributions). Data for these characteristics were obtained from the China City Statistical Yearbook, and latitude information was extracted from a Geographic Information System database.

For each region, we first fitted an intercept-only model to estimate baseline heterogeneity. We then assessed seven factors by univariate meta‑regression. This univariate assessment was based on multiple criteria, including Cochran’s Q and I2 values, information criteria (AIC, BIC), likelihood‑ratio, and Wald tests (Table S4). The selection of Cochran’s Q and I2 values as the primary criteria for multivariable model building was based on their direct interpretation as measures of residual heterogeneity [22, 23]. Through backward selection, we retained factors that meaningfully reduced these heterogeneity measures from the baseline set by the intercept-only model. After single and multi-analysis of all regions, several similarities and differences in the factors influencing the heat-ILI relationship were identified across regions (Table 2 and Table S4). The analyzed factors included total annual GDP, population size, latitude, number of air conditioners per 100 households, number of hospitals, proportion of residents aged over 65, and low-income population percentages. In the Northern Region, all factors were found to explain part of the heterogeneity between cities in the relationship between ILI and temperature. In the Northeastern Region, the heterogeneity was primarily attributed to latitude, the number of air conditioners per 100 households, and the proportion of the population aged over 65. In the Eastern Region, heterogeneity was influenced by latitude, total population, the number of air conditioners per 100 households, and the number of hospitals. In contrast, total annual GDP and the proportion of the population aged over 65 did not show significant modifying effects, likely due to relatively balanced economic and demographic development across the region. In the Southern Region, all six factors except for total population played a role in explaining the heterogeneity. In the Northwestern Region, all seven examined factors contributed to explaining the heterogeneity, with latitude, total population, and the proportion of low-income populations playing a more prominent role. This reflects the region's uneven economic development and disparities in healthcare resources across its large geographical area. In the Southwestern Region, the heterogeneity was explained by factors such as population size, the proportion of the population aged over 65, and total annual GDP. Discussion

The factors influencing influenza epidemics have been widely explored, and an important meteorological factor is temperature [6, 24, 25]. While the effect of low temperatures on ILI has been extensively examined given their role in viral survival and spread [22, 26, 27], the specific dynamics under high-temperature conditions remain insufficiently explored. We analyzed the relationship between heat effects and ILI incidence in 333 cities in China from May to October in 2010–2018 and found some interesting associations between them. We further quantified the burden of heat-related illness when temperatures rise dramatically in 139 of these cities. In addition, we explored the heterogeneity in the heat effects on ILI incidence across these regions by dividing China into seven regions and incorporating seven factors, and found that these factors contributed to the heterogeneity in different degrees, at the same time, the dynamic of ILI exhibited varying thermal adaptability under the influence of these factors.

Although the risk curves for heat effects and ILI differ across regions, our findings indicate that in regions where the temperature exceeds the minimum risk temperature, the incidence of ILI increases gradually with the temperature. On the one hand, to the fact that hot environments suppress the adaptive immune response to influenza virus infections [28], leading to immunodeficiency in individuals. On the other hand, air conditioning is often used in homes and public places in hot regions, and crowded indoor environments that are always closed can facilitate viral transmission [29], and low indoor temperatures are conducive to keeping the virus active [30]. Furthermore, when there is a large difference between indoor and outdoor temperatures, the sudden decrease in ambient temperature upon moving indoors may increase people’s susceptibility to infection [31].

Investigating the heat effect-ILI association across seven regions, we found that latitude and the proportion of individuals aged 65 and older are the most significant modifiers of this effect (Table 2). Latitude integrates meteorological variables (e.g., solar radiation, humidity) as well as behavioral and genetic gradients, all of which can shape viral transmission and host susceptibility under high temperatures [32, 33]. Population aging is also a critical factor. Except in the eastern region, the proportion of older adults is a modifier of the heat-ILI relationship in other regions. With typically lower immune resilience and poorer heat adaptation, older adults are more vulnerable to the health effects of high temperatures in summer [34, 35]. Other factors affecting heterogeneity vary across the seven regions. The proportion of low-income populations plays an important role, particularly in the northern and southern regions. In both regions, ILI risk increases at temperatures around 22–28 °C (Fig. 2). This pattern may be related to increased population movement and contact among low-income individuals who work in agriculture or seasonal labor, potentially contributing to higher ILI incidence. The disparity in wealth between urban areas in these regions suggests that the impact of temperature on ILI may vary with socioeconomic conditions [36]. Hospitals served as a major modifier of the heat effects on ILI in the eastern and southern regions. In these areas, the RR curve shows an attenuated risk of ILI when temperatures exceed approximately 28℃. This may be attributed to better healthcare access, enabling residents to take timely preventive actions or seek medical care during extreme heat, thereby mitigating the risks associated with high temperatures [37]. Air conditioning (AC) primarily affects the heterogeneity of the heat effects on ILI in the northeastern, eastern, and southern regions. This association may be explained by behavioral adaptations in hotter summer regions, where residents spend more time in air-conditioned indoor environments. Such behavior not only increases crowding and interpersonal contact among susceptible populations, but also creates favorable conditions for viral persistence [38]. The slight decline in ILI risk at extreme high temperatures observed in the eastern and southern regions (Fig. 2) may be attributed to behavioral adaptation facilitated by adequate infrastructure. As temperatures become intolerably high, individuals increasingly seek cooled environments. The wider availability of air‑conditioned spaces in these regions supports this adaptive behavior, which mitigates population exposure to extreme heat and may thereby reduce its impact on ILI.

These results demonstrate regional variations in how the mentioned factors influenced the heterogeneity of the relationship between heat effects and ILI incidence. Therefore, measures adopted by public health authorities to minimize the adverse effects of heat on ILI incidence in a region should be tailored to the characteristics of the region. Vaccination remains a cornerstone of ILI prevention [39, 40]. For older adults in particular, our findings suggest this factor may modify the association between heat exposure and ILI risk (all regions except the eastern region). Given that vaccination uptake is influenced by factors such as healthcare access, cost, and public awareness, our results highlight the importance of developing tailored prevention strategies for the elderly population. In addition, wearing masks in areas with high population mobility and dense crowds is recommended. The modifying effect of low-income population proportion on the heat-ILI association partly reflects health disparities [41]. Low-income populations have less access to protective resources such as healthcare and are more vulnerable to heat-related health impacts [42]. Therefore, public health interventions should prioritize these populations during hot seasons. For regions with relatively limited medical resources, particularly in the northwest region, increasing the construction of hospitals and healthcare facilities is essential to effectively address health risks related to ILI during periods of high temperatures. Proximity to healthcare services can reduce the severity and transmission of infectious diseases through timely medical intervention [43]. In the context of heat effects, access to healthcare also facilitates early warning and dissemination of prevention information, helping residents take protective measures. In areas experiencing extreme heat during the summer, providing additional air conditioning units can help mitigate the impact of high temperatures on ILI cases. Conversely, in regions where air conditioning is already common, such as eastern and southern region, it is advisable to use air conditioning judiciously, avoid overcrowding, and ensure proper ventilation and air circulation within air-conditioned environments.

This study possesses several strengths. Firstly, 333 cities across China were studied during the period from 2010 to 2018, and the relationship between ILI epidemics and heat effects was comprehensively analyzed. Secondly, due to significant regional differences, China was divided into seven regions, and confounding factors were controlled to examine the impact of heat on ILI incidence. Thirdly, the heterogeneity in the relationship between heat effects and ILI incidence across the seven regions was investigated, and the contributing factors were elucidated. Consequently, the findings are intended to serve as a reference for guiding different regions in implementing appropriate measures to control influenza epidemics.

However, this study also has some limitations. Firstly, because our data were derived from official sentinel surveillance data, unreported cases were not included, which might have affected the accuracy of our findings. Secondly, the use of ILI data rather than laboratory-confirmed influenza cases introduces potential misclassification, as ILI includes other respiratory viruses. This could bias the estimated temperature associations if other pathogens respond differently to heat. Thirdly, our analysis used aggregated ILI data without distinguishing between influenza subtypes, which have distinct transmission dynamics [44] and vary regionally [45]. Therefore, the observed overall heat effects may average potentially divergent subtype-specific responses. Future subtype-specific analyses would help clarify these variations. Finally, as an ecological study, the associations observed at the city level may not directly reflect individual-level risk, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

This nationwide study demonstrates that heat effects are associated with increased ILI incidence and burden across most Chinese cities, with substantial regional variations. Key factors such as healthcare access, economic conditions, social environment, and population age structure modify these heat-ILI associations across different regions. The findings provide a reference for public health authorities aiming to manage the impact of heat on influenza through region-specific measures.
