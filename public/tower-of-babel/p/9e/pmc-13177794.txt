Every Hour Does Matter: A Bayesian Confirmation of Antibiotic Urgency in Sepsis
Authors: Saurabh K Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Das SK. Every Hour Does Matter: A Bayesian Confirmation of Antibiotic Urgency in Sepsis. Indian J Crit Care Med 2026;30(3):189–190.Keywords: Antibiotics, Bayesian analysis, Septic shock

Ushered by the landmark study of Kumar et al., in 2006, which demonstrated that each hour of delay in antibiotic administration was associated with a 7.6% increase in mortality, early antibiotic therapy in patients with sepsis has become a priority.1 Several subsequent studies also showed that administration of antibiotics within 1 hour of a diagnosis of septic shock was found to reduce mortality. But mortality did not increase significantly in patients without septic shock who received antibiotics within 3 hours.2 This finding has been reflected in the Surviving Sepsis Campaign's 1-hour bundle recommendations, which include administering an antibiotic within 1 hour in patients with septic shock and within 3 hours without septic shock.3 This relaxation gives time to confirm bacterial infection and thereby scope for the judicious use of antibiotics. However, this recommendation should not be considered as a ground to delay antibiotics when a bacterial infection is suspected beyond a reasonable doubt.

In a prospective observational trial, using Bayesian analysis Havaldar et al. showed that each hour of delay in administering a new antibiotic in patients with suspected sepsis was associated with higher odds of intensive care unit (ICU) mortality (posterior OR 1.129). Higher mortality was observed in patients with septic shock, with hospital-acquired infection, with a higher acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) II score, and a change in sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score from the baseline.4 This difference may arise due to two factors. Most of the previously published studies observed timely initiation of antibiotics in the ICU or emergency department; this study focused mainly on the escalation of antibiotics in ICUs. Patients presenting to the emergency department or ICU usually have a distinct pattern. The onset of infection is typically acute, the chance that they are already on antibiotics is less, and the clock starts ticking from triage. But the ICU patients who develop new or worsening sepsis during hospitalization are fundamentally different. They may have already received antibiotics, harbor multidrug-resistant organisms, and may have organ dysfunctions. The decision to escalate antibiotics in this context may be more crucial than starting initial antibiotics. Timely escalation of antibiotics may be as crucial as starting antibiotics in patients with septic shock.

Another factor may be the use of different statistical analyses. Most of the evidence on the timing of antibiotics was gathered with the help of frequentist statistics. Frequentist statistics is based on the probability of frequency of occurrence. If analysis of data shows that the null hypothesis is false and there is only a small possibility that the observed result is due to mere chance, we reject the null hypothesis. Central to this method is the notion of the p-value. A p-value is used to express how random a result is. If the p-value is small (i.e., <0.05), it means that there is only a 5% chance that we got this result just by random luck.5 Frequentist statistics has dominated as a method of statistical inference since the 20th century. This method works entirely without subjective probability. There are no priors on parameters, there are no priors on models and there is no consideration for researchers’ beliefs. This method is very popular among researchers because of its objectivity, since it does not involve prior beliefs or subjective judgments. The results are purely based on the data collected during the study. At the same time, this method is also criticized for completely ignoring prior knowledge and relying solely on p-values, which can lead to the misinterpretation of study results and missing important clinical findings. Frequentist statistics are like a racing car that runs perfectly only on a racetrack, i.e., only in ideal conditions. But when the conditions are not optimal, like a real-time ICU setting, the frequentist toolbox often finds us wanting.

This sets up the transition to another statistical method called the Bayesian approach. The basis of Bayesian statistics is Bayes’ theorem, which explains the probability of an event's occurrence based on prior knowledge or experience associated with that event.6 The Bayes’ theorem can be demonstrated by an example of the likelihood that a positively tested patient has a disease. The accuracy of the test is 99%. If we consider only the accuracy, we may intuitively conclude that the probability of having the disease is 99%. But when we consider the incidence of the disease, which is 1 in 1,00,000, the likelihood of having the disease will be <1%. In this example, the incidence of the disease is the prior probability, and the calculated likelihood of having the disease for a positively tested person is the posterior probability.6 Bayesian reasoning mirrors how we think in a clinical setting. We start with a prior probability based on our existing knowledge and clinical experience. When a patient comes to the hospital, we take a history and collect laboratory data. Similar to the Bayesian approach, we combine the prior data with the new data to generate a posterior probability, which in this case may be the diagnosis. Bayesian statistics is a method of formalizing this intuitive process.

The Bayesian method is older than the frequentist method; however, it has remained neglected because of two interrelated objections. Firstly, Bayesian statistical methods are viewed as vague and nonrigid because of the requirement of subjective priors. The drawback with a prior is that there is no uniform or standard method of defining one. The prior selections can alter posterior probabilities and, thus the conclusions. Secondly, Bayesian inference is computationally very demanding.7

With the development of computer technologies and Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods in the 1950s, Bayesian methods have started gaining popularity among researchers and statisticians.6

Bayesian methods offer several key advantages over frequentist approaches in clinical research, primarily through their ability to provide more intuitive and clinically relevant interpretations of data. In the frequentist method, the experimental design must be specified in advance. The result of a study is decided by the analyzed data and the applied experimental design. In Bayesian statistics, it does not matter which experimental design is used.6 Bayesian methods can be more useful, especially when dealing with complex heterogeneous ICU data. With Bayesian statistics, clinical knowledge can be updated with newer data. This iterative learning process helps us to be continuously refined and updated in a clinical setting.

In today's era of artificial intelligence and machine learning, the integration of Bayesian methods with machine learning opens up the door for new possibilities in healthcare by providing models that are not only highly predictive but also inherently interpretable and capable of reasoning under uncertainty.8

Frequentist and Bayesian models handle heterogeneity differently. Frequentists typically deal with this heterogeneity through subgroup analyses that reduce the required statistical power because of fewer samples. Bayesian hierarchical models, by contrast, can borrow strength across subgroups while allowing for variation, producing more stable estimates of treatment effects in clinically relevant populations.9

Critical care research has long relied on frequentist statistics that use p-values, confidence intervals, and null hypothesis significance testing. Although these are familiar methods, they have limitations when applied to complex, heterogeneous ICU data. p-values tell us whether an effect exists; they do not tell us the probability that the effect is clinically meaningful. Bayesian methods balance innovation and rigor, enabling an implementable interpretation of complex data, particularly for sepsis research specifically, where heterogeneity is the rule rather than the exception.

With the resurrection of the Bayesian method, researchers have started reanalyzing the already published studies, particularly in critical care. In the ANDROMEDA Shock trial, using the frequentist method, the authors observed that a resuscitation approach targeting the normalization of capillary refill time did not reduce 28-day mortality compared to that of improving serum lactate level.10 However, a reanalysis using the Bayesian method found that capillary refill-targeted resuscitation could result in lower mortality and early resolution of organ dysfunction compared to lactate-guided resuscitation.10 Similarly, a post hoc Bayesian analysis of data from the EOLIA trial provides information about the posterior probability of a mortality benefit with early extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in patients with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).11,12

In this issue of the journal, Havaldar et al. presented a timely and methodologically rigorous prospective study that offers probabilistic interpretations that align with clinical reasoning using Bayesian analysis. This study reinforces that surveillance for new or worsening infection must remain constant throughout the ICU stay. A patient who deteriorates during an ICU stay deserves the same urgency as a patient arriving at emergency departments or ICUs with septic shock. Once the decision to escalate antibiotics is made, collaboration among physicians, nurses, and pharmacists must be ensured so that prescribed antibiotics are administered promptly.
