Associations between hospital admission frequency and household financial outcomes in Korea
Authors: Kangmin Kim, Euna Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth shocks within families affect not only individual patients but also their entire household, across various economic and psychological dimensions. Rising hospital admissions are strongly associated with increased medical expenditures that strain household finances, with larger relative financial strain among lower-income households. However, the broader socioeconomic consequences of these shocks have yet to be sufficiently investigated, leaving a notable research gap. This study examines the association between hospital admission frequency and household financial stability, focusing on how similar health shocks are differentially associated with outcomes across the conditional distribution of household financial status.MethodsUsing data from the 2022 Korea Health Panel Survey, this study examined the association between hospital admission frequency and financial outcomes, including household assets for 3,669 households and earned income among 1,844 household heads. Households were categorized into no-, low-, and high-admission groups based on the annual number of hospital admissions per household member. Multivariable linear regression and quantile regression analyses were performed to estimate the average and distributional effects of hospital admission frequency on financial indicators after adjusting for the covariates, with the three groups entered simultaneously into the models.ResultsHousehold assets in the high-admission group were significantly lower at the 25th and 50th percentiles—by KRW 30.09 million and KRW 56.00 million, respectively—than in the no-admission group in the quantile regression analysis (both p < 0.05). For earned income, linear regression showed an average reduction of KRW 5.03 million for the low-admission group (p < 0.01), and quantile regression identified a KRW 5.51 million decrease at the 50th percentile for the high-admission group (p < 0.01). Subgroup analyses showed similar dual declines in both financial indicators across the top 25% and bottom 75% out-of-pocket health expenditure groups, particularly between the 10th and 50th percentiles.ConclusionFrequent hospital admissions were associated with heterogeneous financial associations across households, with the extent of losses in assets and earned income varying significantly by household composition and medical expenditure level. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive financial protection and social support policies in Korea to alleviate the unequal economic burden of health shocks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02835-w.Keywords: Hospital admission frequency, Economic inequality, Vulnerable households

Introduction

Caregiving stress and medical expenses related to family members with serious illness can manifest as family conflict, suggesting that health issues can exert a greater disruptive impact on family life than economic problems [1, 2]. Participants in an analysis report by the Korea Welfare Panel Survey in 2024 [3] said that the primary cause of conflict within their families was health issues of household members (54.85%), a proportion significantly higher than that of economic challenges (18.19%). This result indicates that when a family member experiences a severe health problem, the impact is not limited solely to the individual but spreads throughout the family, which results in psychological and economic burdens.

With population aging and increased prevalence of chronic diseases, the frequency and severity of health problems are expected to rise, leading to greater demand for inpatient care [4]. For example, more than 40% of the population in South Korea is expected to be aged 65 years or older by 2050 [5], a demographic shift that will substantially increase the need for hospital services. Globally, older age and multimorbidity are strongly associated with high utilization of hospital care. Consistent with this trend, many countries are experiencing surges in hospital admissions [6, 7]. The growing hospitalization burden underscores the need to elucidate broader implications of hospital admissions for household and economic stability.

Increases in hospital admissions are positively and negatively associated with medical spending and household financial stability, respectively. Hospitalization carries substantial direct costs, including out-of-pocket and caregiving expenses, and indirect costs, including income loss and reduced productivity due to the inability to work [8]. In South Korea, approximately 25% of households incur catastrophic health expenditures that exceed their financial capacity [9], demonstrating that medical costs can overwhelm household budgets. The annual economic burden associated with paid caregiving and productivity loss for inpatients was estimated at 7 to 8 trillion KRW in 2018 [10], highlighting the magnitude of these costs. Loss of income due to prolonged inability to work, which can be devastating to household finances, particularly for low-income families, is particularly critical [11–14]. These dual financial pressures may exacerbate household economic vulnerability and widen income inequality.

Nevertheless, prior research has narrowly focused on direct medical expenses, such as catastrophic health expenditure, and health insurance coverage [13, 15, 16], thus leaving a research gap in the broad socioeconomic consequences of health shocks, including income loss or labor supply constraint. This gap is especially critical in Asian sociocultural contexts, in which the culture of filial piety shapes intra-familial relationships and caregiving norms. Despite fostering parental well-being and family harmony, this filial piety places the burden of illness care onto families, exacerbating financial and social consequences [17, 18]. Moreover, previous studies have inadequately examined potential heterogeneity in the magnitude of economic shocks and trajectories of recovery based on household socioeconomic status and hospitalization patterns [19–21].

To address these research gaps, the current study investigates the impact of hospital admission frequency on household financial stability. Specifically, it examines how severe health events, proxied by hospitalization frequency, are associated with divergent outcomes across the conditional distribution of key household financial indicators. The results provide valuable insights that can inform policy discussions, highlighting the need to address not only hospitalization costs but also other financial vulnerabilities that households face during hospital admission. In particular, the findings support measures for mitigating income loss and financial strain, thereby strengthening strategies for reducing economic burden on low-income families.

Methods

Research material and sample selection

This study analyzed data from the 2022 Korea Health Panel, a nationally representative survey jointly administered by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs and the National Health Insurance Service. It provides detailed information on healthcare utilization, medical expenses, and associated socioeconomic characteristics [22]. We adopted a cross-sectional design restricted to 2022 to analyze the economic associations of hospitalization across socioeconomic strata while minimizing the influence of external factors on financial outcomes [23, 24].

The dataset comprised 5,668 households, 1,999 of which were excluded due to missing values for key control variables or household-head linkage failures (n = 509); single-person households (n = 1,453); non-cohabiting, deceased, or separated head (n = 33); heads of household (HOHs) recognized as national merit beneficiaries (n = 3), who are ineligible for National Health Insurance or Medical Aid; and non-response to question on assets (n = 1). The final sample consisted of 3,669 households. Figure 1 depicts the sample selection process.

Research variables

Dependent variables

The dependent variables were household assets and earned income of the HOH. Household assets reflect overall financial capacity and shared economic resources at the household level, whereas earned income was used to capture the level of economic productivity of the HOH. Household assets included real estate, financial assets, and loans to others, representing total assets as of December 31, 2022. Earned income was defined as the annual earned income of the HOH, and the analysis was restricted to those with positive earned income. We excluded HOHs without earned income because they were predominantly elderly and relied on pensions or transfers [25, 26], thus potentially introducing compositional heterogeneity in the analysis that uses earned income as the dependent variable. In contrast, households without assets (n = 8) were retained to avoid bias from excluding the most vulnerable group at the bottom of the wealth distribution [27].

Independent variable

The primary independent variable was hospital admission frequency, calculated as the number of inpatient admissions at hospitals, general hospitals, or tertiary-care hospitals for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. This scope follows the Korean Medical Service Act [28], which designates these hospitals as leading providers of inpatient services, whereas clinic-level institutions are primarily intended for outpatient care [29]. In addition, this study restricted the definition of hospitalization to admissions related to diagnostic or therapeutic procedures, accidents, or poisoning. Respondents admitted for other reasons—including long-term care, rehabilitation, and palliative care; childbirth; health check-ups; cosmetic purposes; and other self-reported reasons—were excluded from the analysis. The analysis focused on the number of admissions instead of the duration of each admission to capture the frequency of hospitalization events and recurrent burden associated with repetitive health episodes. Same-day admissions and discharges were included in the total count of hospitalizations for the following two reasons. First, under the Korean National Health Insurance system, patients who receive inpatient-level services such as chemotherapy or surgery in an inpatient-equivalent setting and remain in the hospital for at least six hours after formal admission for treatment and observation are classified as inpatients and are charged a one-day inpatient fee [30]. In addition, national inpatient statistics also classify patients who are discharged on the same day after completing the admission process as inpatients, and our study followed these institutional and statistical definitions [31].

Second, among the 1,957 inpatient cases initially identified, same-day admissions and discharges accounted for 153 cases, representing a non-negligible proportion. Excluding these cases could systematically underestimate hospitalization frequency and the level of household economic instability associated with medical expenditures [32].

The independent variable was set as the annual number of hospital admissions per household member. Figure 1 demonstrates that the final sample consisted of 3,669 households. These households were used for the household asset analysis and stratified into three groups according to the annual number of hospital admissions per household member: no-admission (0/year, n = 2,800), low-admission (≤ 0.5/year, n = 603), and high-admission (> 0.5/year, n = 266). Among this final sample, a subset of 1,844 household heads with positive annual income was identified for the earned income analysis. These individuals were similarly categorized into three groups: no-admission (0/year, n = 1,496), low-admission (≤ 0.5/year, n = 260), and high-admission (> 0.5/year, n = 88).

The 0.5 threshold for annual admission was selected due to the highly skewed distribution, with 76.32% of households experiencing no admissions during the study year. Specifically, this threshold corresponds to two annual admissions for a four-person household. According to the 2023 Korea Welfare Panel Study Descriptive Report [33], the average number of hospital admissions per household member in 2022 was 0.12. Thus, the selected criterion of 0.5 is substantially higher than the national average, effectively isolating households with a disproportionately high frequency of hospital use. This criterion has been empirically adopted in prior research screening older adults at high risk for repeated hospital admissions [34].

Control variables

The control variables reflected the characteristics of the HOHs and the household. The socioeconomic status of the HOH was used as the representative variable, based on the assumption that it adequately reflects overall household status [35, 36]. HOH characteristics included gender (male or female), age (20–39, 40–64, 65 or above), marital status (married or unmarried), level of education (middle school and below, high school, or college and above), Medical Aid status (no or yes), disability status (no or yes), chronic disease status (no or yes), and employment status (unemployed, permanent employed, temporary employed, or employer/self-employed). The temporary employed category included temporary, day, and unpaid workers [37, 38]. Household characteristics included residential area (urban or rural) and the number of household members (2, 3, 4, or 5 or more members). Out-of-pocket health expenditures were defined as annual household medical expenditures per capita, calculated by dividing the total annual household medical spending by the number of household members. Medical spending was defined as the sum of payments made at the point of medical service and for prescribed medications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses comprised descriptive comparisons and regression-based approaches. Cross-sectional sampling weights were applied to all analyses. Unadjusted differences across groups were examined using the chi-square (χ2) test for categorical variables and rank-based analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. Associations between hospital admission frequency groups and key financial variables were estimated using multivariable linear regression. Additionally, quantile regression identified heterogeneity within the conditional distribution of the financial variables (at the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th quantiles) that cannot be captured by average effects alone [39, 40].

Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed according to household age composition and out-of-pocket health expenditure level. Robust standard errors were applied in the regression analyses to account for potential heteroscedasticity. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc.) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at 0.05 unless otherwise stated.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the 3,669 HOHs and households. Significant differences between groups were observed for age (p < 0.001), marital status (p < 0.05), and education level (p < 0.001). Regarding age distribution, individuals aged 40–64 accounted for more than half of the total sample, while those aged 65 and older increased steadily across groups, reaching 45.56% in the high-admission group. Additional differences were found for disability status (p < 0.001), diagnosis of a chronic disease (p < 0.001), and employment status (p < 0.001). Notably, the proportion of respondents with chronic disease rose with admission frequency, approaching 70% in the high-admission group. At the household level, the number of household members differed significantly across groups in the number of household members (p < 0.001). Compared with the no-admission group, the high-admission group had significantly lower levels of household assets and earned income (both p < 0.05). The characteristics of these 1,844 HOHs and their households are presented in Supplementary Table 1 (Additional file 1). For out-of-pocket health expenditure, the high-admission group spent an average of KRW 3.41 million per person annually, compared with KRW 0.77 million in the no-admission group (p < 0.001)1. In contrast, differences in gender, Medical Aid eligibility, and residential region were not statistically significant.

Among households without older adults, no statistically significant differences in household assets were observed across groups in either the multivariable linear regression or quantile regression models. However, the low- and high-admission groups showed a general downward trend in assets compared with the no-admission group, particularly at the 10th and 50th percentiles. In contrast, earned income in this subgroup showed significant reductions. The low-admission group exhibited decreases of KRW 6.10 million (p < 0.05) in the linear model and KRW 6.90 million (p < 0.05) and KRW 14.80 million (p < 0.01) at the 10th and 90th percentiles, respectively. The high-admission group also showed a significant reduction of KRW 6.33 million (p < 0.05) at the 50th percentiles. In households with at least one older adult, the pattern was reversed. No significant differences were observed in earned income, whereas household assets in the high-admission group were significantly lower by KRW 75.96 million at the 50th percentile (p < 0.01) (Table 4).

In households with high OOP expenditures (top 25%), both household assets and earned income declined significantly. Household assets in the high-admission group were, on average, KRW 151.96 million lower than in the no-admission group (p < 0.05). In quantile regression, assets decreased by KRW 53.93 million and KRW 43.01 million in the low- and high-admission groups, respectively, at the 10th percentile (p < 0.001), and by KRW 121.21 million at the 50th percentile in the high-admission group (p < 0.001). Earned income also declined, being on average KRW 9.08 million lower in the linear model (p < 0.05) and decreasing by KRW 7.50 million at the 10th percentile and KRW 6.40 million at the 50th percentile in the low-admission group in quantile regression (both p < 0.05).

Meanwhile, households with low OOP expenditure (bottom 75%) also showed an overall downward trend. For household assets, the high-admission group had, on average, KRW 153.74 million lower assets than the no-admission group (p < 0.01), while the low-admission group showed significantly lower assets by KRW 30.90 million at the 10th percentile (p < 0.01). For earned income, both groups exhibited significant reductions on average: KRW 7.48 million (p < 0.01) in the low-admission group and KRW 16.21 million (p < 0.001) in the high-admission group. In quantile regression, earned income was lower by KRW 10.50 million at the 10th percentile in the high-admission group (p < 0.05) and by KRW 4.00 million at the 50th percentile in the low-admission group (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study examined the association of hospital admission frequency on household financial stability, focusing on identifying heterogeneous associations based on household assets and household head earned income, which previous studies have overlooked. The clear contrast between analytical models is the most significant finding. Although multivariable linear regression exhibited no statistically significant association between admission frequency and household assets, quantile regression revealed a more nuanced picture. The negative associations with hospitalization were concentrated in the lower-to-middle range of the asset distribution. Likewise, while multivariable linear regression found no significant association with earned income for the high-admission group, quantile regression identified statistically significant decreases that the mean-based model did not capture. The nonsignificant results of the multivariable linear regression may be attributed to the resilience of financially secure households, which may mask severe negative effects on vulnerable households. In contrast, quantile regression identifies this average effect, revealing the reality for people most at risk.

Meanwhile, subgroup analyses indicated that the financial consequences of hospitalization varied according to household composition. Declines in earned income were primarily observed among households without older adults, whereas reductions in household assets were more prominent in households with at least one elderly member. These dual declines in both financial indicators were observed in the high and low OOP expenditure subgroups, with particularly pronounced reductions at the lower end of the conditional distribution of each financial indicator. Overall, the findings suggest that the association between hospitalization and financial instability is observable across diverse household contexts [42, 43].

This finding is consistent with those of previous studies that reported that the impact of health shocks is more pronounced in low-income households. According to the 2025 Global Monitoring Report [44], approximately 26% of the global population experienced financial hardship due to healthcare expenditure in 2022. Furthermore, an estimated 1.6 billion people were pushed into, or further pushed into, poverty by out-of-pocket health spending. Other studies have also reported that low-income households cannot fully mitigate income loss incurred from health events, a problem exacerbated by greater proportional spending on healthcare and smaller financial buffers [11]. A study in China found that health shocks led to increased medical spending and decreased sub-expenditures (e.g., food and education), which are more evident for low-income households [45]. A similar trend was observed in South Korea, where a study using the Korea Welfare Panel Study reported that low-income families with members who had recently lost their jobs or were already unemployed were 3.52 and 1.67 times more likely, respectively, to incur catastrophic health expenditures than those with consistent employment [46]. Taken together, these results underscore the disproportionate financial burdens that health shocks impose on economically vulnerable households.

The current study’s findings imply that public protection mechanisms should be strengthened to mitigate the financial risks faced by households when a member is hospitalized. South Korea remains one of the few OECD countries without an income security system for non-occupational illnesses or injuries [19]. The Sickness Benefit pilot program, introduced in 2022, represents an initial attempt to address this institutional gap [47]. The present study’s results imply that strengthening income protection mechanisms, including the Sickness Benefit program, may mitigate the economic burden associated with hospitalization among households.

A relatively low public sector share compared with other high-income countries characterizes South Korea’s healthcare expenditure. Major coverage reforms in 2017 expanded essential services and reduced cost-sharing thresholds to protect against catastrophic health expenses. Government spending increased from 59.0% in 2008 to 64.8% in 2021, but this percentage remains less than the OECD average of 74.0% in 2019. The out-of-pocket share in 2021 reached 35.2%, which was among the highest in the OECD, resembling levels observed in upper–middle- and lower–middle-income countries [48].

Within this context, the current study’s findings highlight that low-income households may continue to face substantial financial strain after hospitalization. In summary, the focus of policies needs to shift from ensuring access to medical services to providing comprehensive financial protection (e.g., adequate sickness benefits and out-of-pocket cost caps) and social support (e.g., return-to-work programs). For example, Sweden’s national sick leave insurance covers up to 80% of lost income for up to one year of illness and mandates employer-led return-to-work plans [49]. In Germany, employees can receive employer-paid sick pay at 100% of their earnings for the first six weeks of work missed due to illness, followed by statutory sick pay at 70% of gross earnings for up to 78 weeks [50]. Taiwan’s labor insurance provides ordinary injury or sickness benefits at 50% of the average monthly insurance salary for up to six months, starting from the fourth day of hospitalization or work incapacity [51].

This study has several limitations. First, the estimated relations are correlational rather than causal, because the analysis was based on cross-sectional data. Although hospital admissions were associated with adverse financial outcomes, the temporal ordering and causal impact of health shocks on household finances cannot be determined, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Second, excluding unobserved factors (e.g., informal financial support from family and relatives, severity of illness leading to hospitalization, or differences in insurance arrangements, such as supplemental private coverage or variation in reimbursement levels) from the analysis may have influenced the results. Third, classifying households according to an annual per capita hospital admission frequency of 0.5 is a simplification because the financial consequences of hospitalization do not necessarily increase proportionally and may vary considerably in hospitalization duration, underlying health conditions, or other circumstances. Fourth, as no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, results at individual quantiles—particularly those with borderline statistical significance—should be interpreted cautiously [52].

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the literature by highlighting the unequal financial consequences of hospitalization across households with different economic capacities, an aspect typically overlooked in previous studies. By applying quantile regression, the study provides empirical evidence that average-effect models may disproportionately mask vulnerable groups affected by health shocks. Future studies should extend this analysis using longitudinal data to elucidate causal pathways and examine the effect of the accumulation of repetitive or long-term hospitalizations on household financial resilience over time. Such efforts could further inform the design of equitable social protection and income security policies in Korea.

Conclusions

This study’s findings suggest that the financial burden of hospitalization is not evenly distributed and cannot be fully captured by mean-based analyses. They underscore that high hospital admission frequency shows negative associations with various indicators of household financial stability, including not only assets but also earned income, particularly for those at the lower end of the financial distribution. Amid population aging and the consequent increased prevalence of chronic diseases, these health shocks may reinforce entrenched socioeconomic inequalities. Strengthening policy responses to the economic determinants of health and empowering financially vulnerable households are essential for advancing health and medical equity.
