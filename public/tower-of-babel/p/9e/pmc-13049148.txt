A bio-polymeric strategy for enhancing the strength, durability of concrete and shrinkage reduction
Authors: Kunamineni Vijay, V. V. S. Sarma, Venkateswarlu Kuruva, A. Uday Kumar, Kemal Detu, Panga Narasimha Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The formation of cracks in concrete is inevitable and often accelerated by inadequate curing. In many regions, the availability of potable water for external curing is limited, leading to improper curing practices and the development of shrinkage cracks. Resolving this issue is essential to enhancing concrete structures’ long-term performance. In the present study, an alternative approach has been explored to reduce reliance on external curing while enhancing the mechanical and durability properties of concrete. Superabsorbent polymer (SAP) was used as an internal curing agent, and a bacterial self-healing mechanism was employed to mitigate cracks and refine the pore structure of concrete. Four different mixes were prepared for investigation: a control mix, a mix containing SAP, a bacterial concrete mix, and a mix incorporating both superabsorbent polymer and bacteria. A series of tests, including shrinkage, compressive strength, flexural strength, and electrical resistivity, were conducted to evaluate the performance of these mixes. The experimental results confirmed that the inclusion of superabsorbent polymer significantly reduced shrinkage, while the combined use of superabsorbent polymer and bacteria further enhanced the properties of concrete. The combination of superabsorbent polymer and healing agent exhibits a 15.1% and 26.4% improvement in compressive strength in comparison with the control mix at an age of 7 and 28 days. A strong correlation was observed between compressive strength and shrinkage, indicating that reduced shrinkage contributes to improved concrete performance. This study shows that combining superabsorbent polymer and bacterial self-healing offers an effective way to overcome curing challenges and enhance the strength and durability of concrete, especially in water-scarce regions.

Introduction

The most commonly used substance in the world is concrete. It is due to how simple it is to manufacture structures with it. One of the main causes of greenhouse gas emissions into the environment is the ingredients used to make concrete1,2. A significant amount of CO2 is released into the environment throughout the cement-making process. Also, the increasing demand for natural aggregates is raising the necessity of using alternative materials3,4. The concrete structures are deteriorating before they complete their service life. Also, a huge amount of repair and rehabilitation work is being done before the service life. It is important to utilize the concrete up to its service life because it is already producing an enormous amount of greenhouse gases5. The primary cause of structural deterioration is the formation of concrete cracks. It allows unwanted gases and moisture into the concrete, which may react with the hydration products, causing further damage. The improper curing and curing with salted water will enable the concrete to crack in the early ages. Potable water is not available in all places for curing concrete. It allowed researchers to find internal curing concrete. The inclusion of superabsorbent polymers, brick bats, and lightweight aggregates, which can absorb water and gradually release it during hydration6,7. It may eliminate the external curing of concrete. Although using lightweight aggregates and brickbats as an interior curing material can speed up the curing process, it tends to weaken the concrete as a whole6. Its low density, high porosity, and water absorption degrade the quality of concrete7–9, and10. Because of their capacity to absorb and progressively release water, recycled aggregates can be used as an efficient medium for internal curing in concrete11,12. Nonetheless, the strength characteristics of concrete often decrease as the percentage of recycled materials rises13,14. It is commonly known that adding Superabsorbent Polymers to concrete as internal curing agents enhances the cementitious systems’ mechanical and durability qualities15,16. In order to provide continuous cement hydration and reduce autogenous shrinkage, SAPs absorb water during mixing and then release it during hydration17,18. According to research, concrete that contains SAPs shows a significant 50–80% decrease in autogenous shrinkage, resulting in a denser microstructure with fewer micro cracks15,19.

In the other direction, bacterial remediation represents a cutting-edge field of research aimed at enhancing concrete durability through the autonomous healing of micro-cracks20–23. Cracks in concrete allow water and oxygen to ingress, which activates dormant bacterial spores. Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is produced by these bacteria as they break down the nutrients they are given, progressively filling the fissure24,25. Additionally, bacteria occupy the nanospace in the concrete, where they begin to germinate CaCO3 and harden, increasing the concrete’s strength26. Additionally, adding fibers to bacterial concrete increases its strength21,27, and28. When compared to the traditional concrete mix, the addition of polypropylene fiber to the bacterial mix has been shown to increase strength by 39%27.The closure of the holes inside the concrete by microbiologically induced calcite precipitation increases the compressive strength27. Additionally, the mechanism of bacterial concrete was investigated using materials other than cement29,30, and31. The use of additional cementitious elements in concrete is improved by the application of bacterial processes. This is due to the fact that adding bacteria to concrete increases its strength and durability29. Numerous concrete mixes, including self-compacting concrete, marine concrete, and pavements, use bacterial mechanisms32,33, and34. Several studies have confirmed that creating a favorable environment within concrete is crucial for the germination and metabolic activity of bacteria, which in turn enhances the precipitation of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)22,26,35–37. The presence of adequate moisture, oxygen, and nutrients stimulates bacterial activity, leading to effective crack filling and strength improvement26,38. Researchers have also emphasized the role of water curing in bacterial concrete, as it ensures sufficient hydration, supports bacterial survival, and facilitates continuous CaCO₃ precipitation within the crack zone39–42. To overcome the aforementioned limitations, internal curing agents are incorporated into bacterial concrete to act as localized moisture reservoirs, which are essential for bacterial germination and CaCO₃ precipitation. A prolonged release of water is made possible by the combination of lightweight aggregates (LWA) and superabsorbent polymers/hydrogels, guaranteeing constant hydration and fostering an environment that is conducive to bacterial activity43. Very few literatures are available on the combined impact of bacterial processes and superabsorbent polymers on concrete performance. There is a need to investigate the effect of bacteria and superabsorbent polymers on the performance of concrete. This study uses experiments to examine how superabsorbent polymers and bacterial mechanisms affect durability, strength, and autogenous shrinkage.

After the concrete is mixed, the shrinkage test is conducted on all mixtures, and the results are reported eight hours later. Results and discussion

Autogenous shrinkage of concrete

Shrinkage measurements were conducted on all concrete mixes, and the reported results correspond to the first 8 h after water addition. It was observed that shrinkage was most significant during this early period, after which the rate of shrinkage became marginal. This trend is consistent with previous studies, which indicate that early-age shrinkage, particularly within the first few hours of hydration, accounts for the majority of total shrinkage due to rapid moisture loss and chemical shrinkage46. The Fig. 1 depicts the shrinkage test results of all concrete mixes. From Fig. 1, it is observed that the addition of superabsorbent polymer considerably reduces the shrinkage in concrete. There is a 26% reduction in shrinkage is noticed in the mix with superabsorbent polymer as compared to the control mix. The added superabsorbent polymer acts as an internal water reservoir, absorbing mixed water and releasing it during the hydration process54. Due to this, internal moisture is maintained in the concrete, which minimizes the shrinkage in the concrete47. The inclusion of a healing agent in the mix shows a 15% reduction in the shrinkage of concrete as compared to the control mix. The bacterial metabolic activity develops CaCO3 using the added calcium lactate in the early state of concrete, leading to a denser microstructure48,49. Thus, the shrinkage effect in the bacterial mix is diminished. The addition of superabsorbent polymer and healing agent further reduces the shrinkage in concrete. There is a 32.9% reduction in shrinkage is noticed with the combination of superabsorbent polymer and healing agent as compared to the control mix. This is because of the combined effect of the healing agent and superabsorbent polymer in the early age of concrete.Fig. 1Autogenous Shrinkage test results of mixes (µε).

Compressive strength

After the concrete was mixed, 150 × 150 × 150 mm concrete cubes were cast. These cubes are stored at room temperature, humidic conditions (270C & 40%). The findings of testing these samples at 7 and 28 days of age are shown in Fig. 2. Figure 2 shows that, in comparison to the control mix, the addition of superabsorbent polymer increases the concrete’s compressive strength by 9.8% and 16.9% at 7 and 28 days. This enhancement is ascribed to the cement’s prolonged hydration and the pore structure’s improvement47. The shrinkage test results confirm that the incorporation of superabsorbent polymers significantly reduces shrinkage, leading to a denser and more refined microstructure in the concrete. The inclusion of a healing agent enhances the compressive strength of concrete by 6.3% and 14.2% at ages of 7 and 28 days, respectively, compared to the control mix. The added healing agent precipitates the CaCO3, which fills pores and micro-cracks, thereby densifying the concrete and enhancing its strength50. The combination of superabsorbent polymer and healing agent further enhances the strength of the concrete. There is a 15.1% and 26.4% improvement in compressive strength in comparison with the control mix. The formation of calcium carbonate was found to be more pronounced in bacterial concrete incorporating superabsorbent polymers, owing to their internal curing effect. Superabsorbent polymers act as localized water reservoirs, ensuring sustained moisture availability that enhances bacterial viability and metabolic activity, thereby promoting increased CaCO₃ precipitation. A denser microstructure and increased concrete’s compressive strength are the results of the precipitated CaCO₃’s efficient sealing of pores and micro-cracks51,52, and53. Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the relationship between concrete’s compressive strength and shrinkage. It is evident from Figs. 3 and 4 that the concrete’s increased compressive strength is correlated with its decreased shrinkage. The findings of the 7-day and 28-day compressive strength tests showed a similar pattern. Better internal hydration results in a denser microstructure and increased strength, as seen by the decrease in concrete shrinkage. Flexural strength

A crucial characteristic of concrete is its flexural strength, which indicates its capacity to withstand bending or tensile strains brought on by outside loads. According to IS: 516-2021, the flexural strength of concrete was evaluated using beam samples with standard size of 500 × 100 × 100 mm45. Every concrete mix has undergone the test at a 28-day age. Figure. 5 presents the test findings. The results of the compressive strength test and the flexural strength test showed a similar pattern. At 28 and 56 days, the strength is increased by 11.5% and 7.9%, respectively, when superabsorbent polymer is added to the control mixture. At 28 and 56 days, respectively, the healing agent shows a 9.3% and 6.7% increase in strength when compared to the control mix. The superabsorbent polymer and the healing agent work together to further increase the concrete’s strength. SAPs, being hydrophilic cross-linked polymers, absorb significant amounts of water (hundreds of times their weight) and swell to form a gel. Within this three-dimensional network, water is stored and protected from rapid evaporation. The water held within the SAP allows for the dissolution and diffusion of essential nutrients that are calcium source and a carbon source to the bacteria, and the transport of metabolic by products away from the cell, which is crucial for sustaining activity. The high concentration of Ca2+ ions near the negatively charged bacterial surface, combined with the presence of CO32- ions in the alkaline microenvironment, leads to the precipitation of CaCO3 crystals22,31.

Conclusions

This study highlights the effectiveness of incorporating a healing agent and superabsorbent polymer in enhancing the performance of concrete. The autogenous shrinkage, strength, and electrical resistivity tests are performed on all mixes. A 32.9% reduction in shrinkage was achieved, while compressive and flexural strength results confirmed a denser microstructure and improved mechanical properties. Electrical resistivity tests further demonstrated that this combination effectively improves durability by reducing susceptibility to reinforcement corrosion. Overall, the study establishes the healing agent–superabsorbent system as a practical approach to improving the properties of concrete.
