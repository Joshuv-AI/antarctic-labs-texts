Differential fracture risk in breast cancer patients based on endocrine therapy and age: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Mi Kyung Kwak, Seeyoun Lee, Jin-young Lee, Jung-Wee Park, Ye Jhin Jeon, Bit-Na Yoo, Jean Kyung Bak, Ha Young Kim, Young-Kyun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeConcerns about the risk of osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures following endocrine therapy have increased with increase in breast cancer survivors. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of fractures by site between tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitor (AI) users and non-users at premenopausal and postmenopausal ages.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the Korea Central Cancer Registry and the Korean National Health Insurance Service. We analyzed 63,021 newly diagnosed breast cancer patients between 2008 and 2016. The risk of fractures was analyzed according to post-surgical endocrine therapy using the multivariable Cox proportional hazard model.ResultsHerein, 69.1% of the patients received endocrine therapy. The incidence rates of osteoporotic fractures per 1,000 person-years were 7.4 overall, 3.5 in those under 50, and 12.6 in those over 50. After adjusting for confounders, the tamoxifen group showed protection against any fractures in patients over 50 (HR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.62–0.89, p < 0.001) but not in those under 50. The AI ​​group was associated with a significant increase in fractures in those under 50 (HR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.07–2.93, p = 0.019) but not in those over 50.ConclusionAmong breast cancer patients aged over 50 years, AI use was not associated with increased risk of compared to non-users. Tamoxifen use was linked to reduced risk of fracture, but not in patients under 50. Considering the relatively young age of breast cancer diagnosis in Korea, long-term follow-up studies are warranted.SummaryAfter adjusting for confounders including age, among breast cancer patients aged over 50 years, AI use was not associated with increased risk of fracture compared to non-users. Tamoxifen use was linked to reduced risk of fracture, but not in patients under 50.

Introduction

Breast cancer is among the most prevalent malignancies affecting women globally, with millions diagnosed each year [1]. It is the most prevalent cancer among Korean women, comprising approximately 20% of all cancers diagnosed in women. However, the incidence of breast cancer has been steadily rising over the past two decades [2]. In the United States, breast cancer is mostly diagnosed after the age of 50 years, with the highest incidence in the 60–69 age group [3], whereas in Korea, like other Asian countries, the highest incidence is in the younger age group of 40–49 years. Despite the rising incidence, advances in early detection and treatment have led to a continuous increase in the 5-year survival rate for breast cancer patients, reaching 93.6% between 2015 and 2019 [4]. Consequently, growing attention is now focused on the long-term effects of breast cancer treatments that may negatively affect the quality of life.

Endocrine therapy, a cornerstone in treating hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, works by inhibiting tumor growth either by disrupting estrogen action or suppressing its production. For example, tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors (AIs) have been the first-line therapies for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer over decades [5–7]. Tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator that acts as an estrogen receptor antagonist in the breast and uterus but as an agonist in the bone [8]. AIs inhibit aromatase, an essential enzyme for estrogen biosynthesis in peripheral tissues. Tamoxifen is administered regardless of menopausal status, whereas AIs are only suitable as monotherapy for postmenopausal women due to their inability to inhibit ovarian estrogen production. While the benefit of these agents in reducing the risk of cancer recurrence and mortality are well-established, it could induce bone loss and increase the risk of osteoporotic fracture [9].

The beneficial impact of tamoxifen on bone mineral density (BMD) in postmenopausal women has been well-documented [8], with bone loss primarily regarded as a side effect of AIs. Among postmenopausal women who had received anastrozole treatment for five years, BMD decreased by 6.1% in the lumbar spine and by 7.2% in the total hip [10]. In contrast, spine BMD declines by approximately 1.4% per year in healthy premenopausal women treated with tamoxifen [11], suggesting that the effects of tamoxifen on bone may vary according to menopausal status. Fractures are reported as adverse events in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observed in case–control studies on breast cancer patients receiving endocrine therapy. Most RCTs compared tamoxifen and AIs, so many reported a greater risk of fracture associated with AIs [10, 12–15], but some did not show a significant difference [16–18]. Other studies have reported an increased risk of fracture in comparison to healthy controls [11, 19]. Given the inconsistencies in results depending on the comparison group and menopausal status, this study aimed to compare fracture incidence by site among tamoxifen and AI users versus non-users using nationwide data stratified by menopausal age.

Methods

Database and ethics

Data from the Korea Central Cancer Registry (KCCR) and the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS), which are linked to each other, were used in this retrospective cohort study. The KCCR was initiated as a hospital-based national cancer registry by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 1980. It contains data on the sex and age of patients at diagnosis, date of diagnosis, location of the cancer, Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results [SEER] stage, histological type, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, immunotherapy, and surgery [20]. In Korea, almost all citizens are eligible for coverage under the National Health Insurance Program (97%) or the Medical Aid Program (3%) as mandatory social insurance. The KNHIS database contains information on diagnostic and procedural codes according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), prescriptions, dates of hospital visits, and hospitalizations. This study was exempted from obtaining patient consent by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Seoul National University, Bundang Hospital of Korea (IRB No. X-1801–447-908) because human subjects were not involved, and all data provided by the KCCR and KNHIS were anonymized. Additionally, this study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study participants

Between January 1, 2008 and December 31, 2016, a total of 106,171 patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer (ICD-10 code C50) were identified from the KCCR and KNHIS database. Initially, patients (n = 323) with data errors were excluded, followed by male patients (n = 433). We also excluded patients diagnosed with other carcinomas during the observation period (n = 6,556), those with breast cancer that recurred during the observation period (n = 95), those with distant metastasis or unknown SEER stage (n = 10,233), those who died within six months of their initial diagnosis (n = 1,103), and those with carcinoma in situ prior to the initial diagnosis (n = 5,902). Furthermore, we excluded patients with a history of osteoporosis medications (n = 8,307), excluding estrogen or hormone therapy or those with a history of systemic glucocorticoid treatment for more than 90 days in the year before breast cancer diagnosis (n = 5,397), and those with a history of rheumatoid arthritis (n = 1,302) or a prior osteoporotic fracture (hip, vertebra, wrist, or humerus; n = 3,499) before the date of initial diagnosis. Finally, 63,021 breast cancer survivors were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow chart depicting selection of study participants. Study variables

We investigated demographic factors (age, type of medical institution, residential area, medical institution area, type of insurance) and disease status (SEER summary stage of breast cancer) at the time of breast cancer diagnosis. The following comorbidities were investigated: myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, connective tissue disorder, peptic ulcer disease, mild liver disease, diabetes without chronic complications, diabetes with chronic complications, hemiplegia, renal disease, and moderate or severe liver disease. We also investigated the following types of post-surgical breast cancer treatment: endocrine therapy (tamoxifen or AI), chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy. Among patients who received endocrine therapy, those who changed their initially prescribed medication were classified as the switch group. Data concerning participants who underwent BMD assessments via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or quantitative computed tomography within one year following breast cancer diagnosis were collected using relevant procedure codes. Additionally, information regarding the prescription of osteoporosis medications within one year after breast cancer diagnosis was also obtained.

Study outcomes and operational definitions

As previously reported, fractures were identified using ICD-10 codes, procedure codes, and imaging codes [21–23]. ICD-10 codes for fractures of the hip were S72.0 and S72.1; those for fractures of the vertebrae were M48.4, M48.5, M49.5, M80.88, S22.0, S22.1, S32.0, S32.7, and T08; those for wrist fractures were S525 and S526; and those for fractures at the humerus were S422 and S423. For patients who had vertebral fractures, we used diagnostic codes related to these fractures provided by clinicians to identify them. We then used additional criteria, such as procedure codes that included vertebroplasty and radiographic codes of vertebrae, to confirm the presence of these fractures. When the participants received a diagnosis of breast cancer, they joined the cohort and were monitored until the occurrence of the first fracture, death, or censored date, or until the end of the study period (December 31, 2017). In this study, information on fractures that occurred at least six months after a breast cancer diagnosis was gathered.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented as the number of patients (%) or as the mean ± standard deviation. The incidence rates of osteoporotic fractures were calculated by dividing the number of incident cases by the total follow-up period (person-years). Cumulative incidence of fractures during the follow-up, stratified by endocrine therapy, was assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves. The log-rank test was employed to evaluate differences between groups. To identify risk factors for osteoporotic fractures, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was utilized, and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. To explore potential effect modification by age, we also conducted an approximate statistical test for interaction between age group (< 50 vs. ≥ 50 years) and endocrine therapy type using the method proposed by Altman and Bland [24]. Confounding variables included age, type of medical institution, residential area, insurance type, SEER stage, targeted therapy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, comorbidities, BMD measurement status, and prescription of osteoporosis medications. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (R Core Team, 2021); a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 106,171 breast cancer patients were identified, of which 63,021 women who fulfilled all inclusion and exclusion criteria were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the overall study population and the population stratified by age at menopause. Among all patients, the most common age at diagnosis was 40–49 years (42.3%), followed by 50–59 years (30.4%), with a significant decline in patient numbers in their 60 s (10.4%) and those over 70 years (3.1%). Most patients were treated at tertiary medical institutions located in urban areas and had localized breast cancer (60.4%). Given the significance of menopausal status in the choice of endocrine therapy, the population was divided using the age of 50 years as the cutoff point. Most patients were aged under 50 years at 56%. Following surgery, 69.1% of patients received endocrine therapy, with tamoxifen being more frequently prescribed to those aged under 50 years. In contrast, AIs were used by 3.3% of patients aged under 50 years. Other treatments, including chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted therapy, were administered similarly across both age groups. After breast cancer diagnosis, BMD tests and prescriptions for osteoporosis medications were more common in people aged over 50 years. Overall, the incidence rate of fractures per 1,000 person-years was lower in tamoxifen users compared to non-users (4.2 vs. 8.4), a trend observed consistently across various fracture sites. Conversely, fracture incidence was higher in AI users compared to non-users (12.8 vs. 8.4), with this pattern evident across all fracture sites except for the hip. In the switch group, the fracture incidence fell between that of AI users and non-users. We also analyzed the data by stratifying patients using the age of 50 as the cutoff. In patients aged under 50, the overall fracture incidence was comparable between tamoxifen users and non-users (3.7 vs. 3.2), while it was more than twice as high in AI users compared to tamoxifen users (9.2 vs. 3.7). This trend was consistent across fracture sites, except for the hip. In patients aged over 50, the fracture incidence was lower among tamoxifen users compared to non-users (9.3 vs. 13.7), and was similar between AI users and non-users (13.2 vs. 13.7). Wrist fractures, consistent with patterns observed in postmenopausal women, were the most common among patients over 50. Wrist fractures were more common among AI ​​users than among non-users; however, the incidence at other sites was similar or even lower.

We assessed the cumulative incidence of osteoporotic fractures following endocrine therapy and presented the results as a graph (Fig. 2). The cumulative incidence for fracture was significantly higher in AI users compared to non-users (p < 0.001), with the switch group showing an intermediate fracture rate between AI users and non-users. In contrast, tamoxifen users exhibited a lower fracture rate than non-users. Risk factor of any osteoporotic fractures in breast cancer survivors

A multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that the risk of any fractures was significantly lower in tamoxifen users (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.65–0.84, p < 0.001) than in non-users (Table 3). The unadjusted model showed significantly increased risk of fracture in AI users (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.37–1.73, p < 0.001), but this trend disappeared with multivariable adjustment (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.87–1.11, p = 0.764). According to the multivariable model, other clinical factors impacting the risk of any fractures in breast cancer patients were age, regional metastasis, chemotherapy, connective tissue disorder, diabetes with or without complications, hemiplegia, renal disease, and prescription of osteoporosis medications (Table 3). Specifically, older age (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.06–1.08, p < 0.001), regional metastasis (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.00–1.23, p = 0.043), chemotherapy (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.11–1.42, p < 0.001), connective tissue disorder (HR 4.15, 95% CI 1.21–14.28, p = 0.024), diabetes with chronic complications (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.01–1.63, p = 0.041), hemiplegia (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.07–4.24, p = 0.032), renal disease (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.04–2.70, p = 0.035) and prescription of osteoporosis medications (HR 1.76, 95% CI 1.48–2.08, p < 0.001) were all associated with an increased risk of fractures in breast cancer patients. In contrast, diabetes without chronic complications (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64–0.92, p = 0.004) was associated with a decreased risk of fracture. Discussion

The effect of endocrine therapy on fractures in breast cancer patients varies according to age and menopausal status. In comparison to the results from the non-user group, tamoxifen was associated with a reduction in risk of fracture among women considered postmenopausal (over 50 years of age), while no increase in fractures was observed in the premenopausal group (under 50 years of age), contrary to previous research. AIs showed a prominent increase in risk of fracture in those under 50; however, no difference in risk of fracture was observed compared to non-users aged over 50. Since the risk of fracture in the AI ​​group is expected to be higher than that in the tamoxifen group due to the mechanism of action of AIs, the present findings provide a more meaningful assessment of risk of fracture associated with endocrine therapy when compared with breast cancer patients not receiving endocrine therapy.

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women, but compared to the West, Asian countries including Korea show a pattern of occurrence at younger ages. While the median age at diagnosis in the United States is reported to be 63 years, with 80% of patients being postmenopausal, this study found a mean age at diagnosis of 49 years, with only 13.5% of patients being over 60 years old. Because of this age distribution, wrist fractures were more frequent than vertebral fractures, even among those aged over 50 years, and hip fractures—typically associated with older age—were quite rare. Even in the absence of skeletal metastasis, breast cancer patients have a higher fracture rate compared to age-matched women without breast cancer [25]. A direct comparison of fracture incidence in women aged over 50 years, as previously reported by the same researchers, and the results of this study is problematic due to differences in age distribution. However, since 69% of the population aged over 50 years in this study were in their 50 s, the risk of fracture is thought to be increased in breast cancer patients over 50 (12.1/1,000 patient-years [PY]) in this study compared with that of healthy women in their 50 s (6.9/1000PY in 2016) [26]. Even among breast cancer patients aged under 50 years, those in their 40 s account for approximately 76%, and the fracture incidence in this study (3.5/1000PY) is notably higher compared to healthy women in their 40 s (0.4/1000PY, unpublished data from 2012). Given that breast cancer is most prevalent in women in their 40 s in Korea, managing risk of fracture in younger breast cancer patients is of particular importance.

Tamoxifen acts as an estrogen agonist in bones. However, when it is used to treat estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer, its effect on bones is known to varies depending on the blood estrogen status. In postmenopausal women with low estrogen levels, including those with early menopause, tamoxifen slightly increases BMD [10, 11]. However, in premenopausal women with normal estrogen levels, tamoxifen results in a modest decrease in BMD [11, 27, 28]. This effect was also observed in animal studies, where tamoxifen decreased bone mass in rats with intact ovaries [29, 30]. Similarly, tamoxifen increases the risk of fracture in premenopausal breast cancer patients compared with healthy controls [19, 31]. However, if healthy patients are set as a control group, factors related to breast cancer may affect the results. A recent Korean study found that tamoxifen use did not increase the risk of fractures in breast cancer patients aged under 50 years compared to those who did not receive hormone therapy [32]. These findings are further supported by data showing fracture risk reduction in postmenopausal women treated with tamoxifen [33]. The results of our study were similar, showing that fracture incidence rates were lower in the spine, hip, and wrist areas but not in the humerus in patients under 50, but the reduction was not significant unlike those over 50. Although this study did not reveal changes in bone density in patients with young breast cancer, bone density changes caused by the use of tamoxifen may not be significant enough to increase the risk of fracture.

AIs exacerbate bone loss and increase risk of fracture. The skeletal effects of AIs are particularly severe in premenopausal women due to their use in conjunction with ovarian suppression. For instance, a study on premenopausal women reported an 11.3% decrease in lumbar spine BMD after three years of treatment with anastrozole and goserelin [34]. Although only 3.3% of the patients aged under 50 years in our study were administered AIs, their fracture incidence was approximately three times higher than those receiving tamoxifen or no endocrine therapy. Even after adjusting for other variables, AI use was associated with a 54% increased risk of fracture in those aged under 50 years. In postmenopausal women, AIs reduce circulating estradiol levels by more than 98%. Numerous RCTs involving postmenopausal patients have demonstrated that AIs increase fracture incidence compared with tamoxifen [10, 12–15].

In addition to stratified analyses, we sought to further explore whether the effects of endocrine therapy on fracture risk were modified by age. We conducted an approximate statistical test using the method proposed by Altman and Bland [24]. This approach evaluates whether hazard ratios differ significantly between strata based on the reported confidence intervals. The results of this analysis suggested that the protective effect of tamoxifen on fracture risk did not differ significantly between women aged under and above 50 years (p for interaction = 0.323). However, AI use showed a statistically significant interaction with age (p for interaction = 0.008), indicating that the increase in fracture risk associated with AI was more pronounced in younger women. Furthermore, a statistically significant interaction was observed between age group and type of endocrine therapy (interaction p < 0.001). Taken together, these findings imply that age may modify the impact of AI therapy on skeletal health.

A meta-analysis of 21 RCTs and cohort studies found that AI use increased risk of fracture by 17% compared to non-use of AI and by 33% compared to tamoxifen use [35]. However, in our study, AI use was not associated with increased risk of fracture after adjusting for age and other variables, including analyses limited to patients aged over 50 years. Unlike RCTs, large-scale observational studies, have reported similar findings. For example, Leslie et al. found no significant association between AI use and risk of osteoporotic or hip fracture compared to the general population in a registry-based cohort study, where the average patient age was 65 years [36]. Similarly, a United States Medicare study of breast cancer patients aged 65 or older reported a lower increase in the risk of nonvertebral fractures in the AI ​​group compared to the tamoxifen group (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.21) than in other studies [37]. The effect of estrogen depletion due to AI use on risk of fracture in postmenopausal women may be less pronounced than previously assumed, although further studies are needed.

This study has several strengths. First, we utilized the extensive, representative nationwide databases of the KCCR and KNHIS, allowing us to analyze a large cohort of adult breast cancer survivors in Korea from 2008 to 2016, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings. Additionally, by using the SEER summary stage, we excluded patients with distant metastases, which could have increased the risk of pathologic fractures, effectively minimizing this potential confounding factor. Furthermore, by analyzing not only any fractures but also spine, hip, wrist, and humerus fractures, we were able to assess whether the relationship between drug use and risk of fracture varies by fracture site in breast cancer survivors.

This study has several limitations. First, the limitations of the claims study are that unmeasured risk of fracture factors, such as family history, smoking, drinking, body mass index, and BMD, may have influenced the results. Therefore, the most influential risk factors, i.e., previous fractures and previous glucocorticoid use, were excluded. Second, because we defined vertebral fractures by diagnosis and procedure codes, we may have missed asymptomatic vertebral fractures. This limitation may have led to an underestimation of risk of vertebral fracture, especially in those aged over 50 years. Third, the severity of the underlying disease may affect the risk of fracture, but because the underlying disease was analyzed using diagnosis codes, the severity of the underlying disease could not be evaluated. Finally, we could not compare the risk of fractures in breast cancer patients with that in the matched normal population in this study.

Conclusion

In this study, among breast cancer patients aged over 50 years and presumed to be postmenopausal, AI use did not result in increased risk of fracture compared with the non-user group. As anticipated, tamoxifen use was associated with reduced risk of fracture, and although not statistically significant, a similar trend was observed in patients aged under 50. Confounding factors not accounted for in this study may have influenced these results. Given the relatively younger age at diagnosis of breast cancer in this population, the findings from long-term follow-up studies could differ. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the risk of fracture in breast cancer patients receiving AIs, as recommended by current breast cancer guidelines, while considering clinical risk factors such as age."
