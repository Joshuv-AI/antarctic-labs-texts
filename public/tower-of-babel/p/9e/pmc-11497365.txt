First autochthonous dengue in Iran: What's next for regional control?
Authors: Nivedita Singh, Hediye Gholamshahi, Amna Zaheer, Sanjit Sah, Mahendra Pratap Singh, Amogh Verma
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

In June 2024, Iran reported its first locally acquired dengue cases, marking a pivotal shift in the epidemiology of this mosquito-borne disease. Initial cases, confirmed via Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) testing, emerged in Bandar-Lengheh, Hormozgan Province [1]. This was a significant development, as Iran has only previously reported cases of imported dengue. According to the Ministry of Health's announcement on July 20, 2024, Iran has 151 confirmed dengue cases, including 12 autochthonous cases [2]. Of these, 11 were in Hormozgan, and one in Sistan and Baluchestan (Fig. 1). Most cases (130) were linked to travel to the United Arab Emirates, with others traveling to Pakistan (7), Oman (1), and Benin (1).Fig. 1Distribution of Locally Acquired Dengue Cases in Iran. This outbreak underscores a broader regional concern, as dengue remains a significant global risk due to competent mosquito vectors and favorable climatic conditions. Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus, known vectors of the dengue virus (DENV), have been established in several Iranian provinces. Aedes aegypti has been identified in Hormozgan, Sistan, Baluchestan, and Bushehr, while Aedes albopictus has been reported in Gilan, Mazandaran, and Ardabil. This expansion of mosquito vectors correlates with an increased risk of local dengue transmission [3]. The increase in imported dengue cases to Iran from 20 between 2017 and 2023 to 137 between May and July 2024 highlights the growing challenge posed by global travel and vector migration. Despite this rise, the autochthonous cases documented in Iran were unprecedented, making the event both unusual and anticipated given the vector presence and population movements from endemic regions.

The World Health Organization (WHO) risk assessment reaffirms the high global and national risks for dengue, with local transmission in Iran being an atypical but expected outcome due to existing vectors and people moving from endemic areas. The upcoming Arbaeen pilgrimage, along with significant international travel to Iran, poses an additional risk of further transmission. Therefore, an effective vector control is crucial. Integrated approaches, including environmental management, source reduction, and personal protective measures, are essential for combating the spread of dengue. Strategies such as larvicide application, use of insecticide-treated nets, and indoor space spraying are vital for controlling mosquito populations. Personal protective measures, including repellents and protective clothing, should be promoted, particularly in areas with active dengue transmission.

The dengue outbreak in Iran underscores the urgent need for enhanced surveillance, response mechanisms, and global collaboration [4]. WHO's support in strengthening laboratory capabilities, distributing diagnostic supplies, and training healthcare workers is crucial. The integration of the TAK-003 dengue vaccine for children in high-transmission areas should also be considered. The convergence of vector presence, climatic suitability, and human mobility creates a risky environment, which highlights the importance of continued vigilance, robust vector control, and public health strategies. Adopting a One Health approach, integrating human, animal, and environmental health, is essential for effective dengue management and prevention [5].
