Encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis outcomes: a retrospective study on the significance of nutritional parameter variations
Authors: Chih-Ying Chien, Ming-Tsun Tsai, Jinn-Yang Chen, Chiao-Lin Chuang, Jiu-Yun Tian, Hsin-Ling Tai, Chih-Ching Lin, Szu-Yuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEncapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (EPS) can result in abdominal organ encasement, and bowel obstruction, and is associated with a high mortality rate. While various risk factors have been identified for the development of EPS, the factors influencing patient outcomes in EPS are less well-studied. This study aims to investigate the prognostic factors that affect the clinical course and survival of EPS patients.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we examined a cohort of 1406 peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients over an 18-year study period. Among them, 35 individuals were diagnosed with EPS. We collected data encompassing demographic characteristics, comorbidities, PD-related parameters, clinical symptoms, computed tomography scores, laboratory results, and treatment modalities. The survival analysis incorporated both univariate and multivariate Cox regression models, as well as the Kaplan-Meier method.ResultsPatients with EPS exhibited a spectrum of clinical symptoms, with intestinal obstruction occurring in 88.6% and ultrafiltration failure in 42.9%. The five-year survival rate was 26.3%, and malnutrition and gastrointestinal infections contributed to three-fourths of the deaths. A notable finding was the one-month drop in albumin levels as a marker of ongoing malabsorption, demonstrating its predictive value for both overall (HR 2.01, p = 0.042) and EPS-related (HR 2.79, p = 0.017) mortality.ConclusionsThis study emphasizes the crucial role of monitoring the one-month albumin drop after EPS diagnosis, providing valuable insights for the improvement of clinical management and patient care.

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is a cost-effective treatment option for end-stage kidney disease, offering advantages in terms of flexibility and quality of life for patients compared to hemodialysis [1]. Nevertheless, PD comes with its share of challenges, and within the realm of PD-associated complications, encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (EPS) stands out as a rare yet severe condition, primarily affecting patients who have undergone long-term PD therapy [2].

Encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (EPS) is characterized by inflammatory and fibrotic changes within the peritoneal cavity, culminating in the encasement of abdominal organs and frequently resulting in bowel obstruction [3, 4]. While the precise cause of EPS remains elusive, it is widely acknowledged that peritoneal inflammation plays a pivotal role in its pathogenesis. Factors such as glucose or glucose degradation products (GDPs) found in PD fluids have been proposed as potential triggers for this inflammatory response [5]. Established risk factors contributing to EPS development including the duration of PD treatment [6–8] and the occurrence of recurrent peritonitis episodes [7]. The accumulation of fibrin on the peritoneum over time, attributed to increased fibrinogenesis and heightened endothelial permeability, leads to the formation of a dense capsule that entraps the intestines [5].

The suspicion for a possible EPS diagnosis typically arises in PD patients who manifest non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms, frequently accompanied by a gradual reduction in ultrafiltration capacity. A definitive diagnosis usually mandates surgical procedures, such as laparotomy or laparoscopy, to unveil distinctive peritoneal thickening entrapping intestinal contents [9]. Nonetheless, the initial diagnosis of EPS is frequently predicated on clinical evaluation and may be subsequently corroborated through imaging investigations, including computed tomography (CT) [10, 11].

While multiple studies have studied the risk factors associated with the development of EPS, there is little evidence on the variables influencing patient outcomes following an EPS diagnosis. Several factors have been purposed influencing EPS patient survival, including age at diagnosis, nutritional status, white cell counts, presence of complications, and histopathological findings [3, 12, 13]. It is worth noting that the exact significance of these factors remains somewhat unclear, and the existing body of literature on this subject is quite limited. This knowledge gap gains particular importance given the ongoing debates regarding the effectiveness of both surgical interventions and medical treatments in managing EPS [3]. The objective of this study is to bridge this gap by assessing the prognostic factors that influence the clinical trajectory and survival of EPS patients. An understanding of these factors is essential for informed clinical decision-making and the enhancement of patient care for this complex condition.

Methods

Study design and patient enrollment

In this retrospective study, our primary objective is to pinpoint the prognostic factors linked to EPS. We examined the medical records of patients who received an EPS diagnosis at Taipei Veterans General Hospital between November 2004 and September 2022. The diagnosis of EPS was established through clinical and radiographic assessment. Data were extracted from these records, which contain patient details on their initial EPS presenting symptoms, demographic profiles, comorbidities, duration of dialysis treatment (referred to as dialysis vintage), results from peritoneal equilibration tests (PET), peritonitis, and comprehensive laboratory data. Furthermore, we documented essential information concerning the treatment approaches employed such as maintaining the catheter in situ for periodic peritoneal lavage, administering tamoxifen, and implementing surgical interventions. Patients with a follow-up duration of less than one month after EPS diagnosis were excluded from the study to ensure adequate data for survival analysis. The study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (2021-12-007C) where informed consent was waived.

Computed tomography scan scoring

In patients who are experiencing unexplained ultrafiltration failure or gastrointestinal symptoms, we routinely employed CT scans to detect EPS. The CT scan scoring system employed in our study closely followed established protocols [14]. The CT score was derived from six specific parameters: peritoneal calcification, peritoneal thickening, bowel wall thickening, bowel tethering, loculation, and bowel dilatation. Each parameter was assigned a score ranging from 0 to 4. Subsequently, a comprehensive CT score for each scan was computed by summing the median scores of these six parameters. The maximum attainable score stood at 22. The scoring process was carried out independently by two radiologists who were blinded to the patients’ medical conditions.

Outcomes

This study primarily focused on two outcomes: total mortality and mortality directly related to EPS. To examine these outcomes, all patients underwent prospective monitoring from the point of EPS diagnosis until either the event of mortality or the conclusion of the study on December 31, 2022. Our analysis included a thorough investigation of the treatment modalities implemented, the primary causes of death, and the duration between the initial diagnosis and the time of death.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies for categorical variables. Nonparametric tests were applied when the data deviated from a normal distribution. To identify potential prognostic factors associated with EPS, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis. Variables with a significance level of p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression model to determine hazard ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Survival probabilities for EPS patients were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences in survival were assessed with the log-rank test. The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25 (IBM), with statistical significance defined as a p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and diagnostic patterns in EPS cohort

We conducted a retrospective review of 1406 patients who underwent PD at Taipei Veterans General Hospital over an 18-year study period. Among them, 35 patients were included after being diagnosed with EPS based on clinical evaluation, with all of their diagnoses supported by CT scans, resulting in an overall EPS prevalence of 2.5%. Among these enrolled patients, 16 were male and 19 were female, with an average age of 52.2 ± 11 years at the time of EPS diagnosis. The longest recorded duration of PD treatment exceeded 30 years, and the average PD vintage was 13.5 ± 5.1 years at the time of EPS diagnosis. Notably, EPS was diagnosed in 13 patients after PD discontinuation. At our institution, we conducted peritoneal transport characteristics analyses every six months. The interval between the measurement of the peritoneal equilibrium test and the diagnosis of Encapsulating Peritoneal Sclerosis (EPS) averaged 2.4 ± 1.1 months. The results showed an average Peritoneal Equilibration Test (PET) score of 0.79 ± 0.10, with more than half of the patients classified as high transporters. Notably, despite the majority maintaining an adequate dialysis dosage. Following the diagnosis of EPS, all patients discontinued PD and transitioned to hemodialysis, except for three patients who continued with low-dose PD lavage to facilitate peritoneal fluid drainage and possibly reduce peritoneal inflammation. However, this approach was individualized based on clinical judgment. Moreover, 13 (37%) patients received tamoxifen treatment, while 4 (11%) patients underwent surgical enterolysis. EPS symptoms, CT scores, and survival analysis

Many patients presented with a range of clinical symptoms, with partial intestinal obstruction being the most common, affecting 88% of patients, followed by ultrafiltration failure, observed in 42% of cases. Approximately one-fourth of the patients had symptoms related to gut-related infection or bloody dialysate (Fig. 1A). The CT score exhibited a non-normal distribution, ranging from 2 to 18, with an average score of 10.2 (Fig. 1B). Despite all patients having adequate nutrition support, and even with some receiving parenteral nutrition, 21 patients died with a median survival of 24.3 months. The one-year, two-year, and five-year EPS survival rates in the study cohort were 68.5%, 55.6%, and 26.3%, respectively (Fig. 1C). Significant factors in EPS-related mortality

Given that some patients experienced mortality due to major cardiovascular events or malignancies unrelated to EPS, we subsequently investigated to identify potential predictors of EPS-related mortality using a similar methodology. There were 15 who died due to malnutrition or gut-related infections. Variables such as white blood cell count, abdominal CT scores, and the one-month albumin drop all displayed p < 0.1 in the univariate Cox regression model (Table 2). However, in the multivariate analysis, only the one-month albumin drop maintained its statistical significance, demonstrating a hazard ratio of 2.787 and p = 0.017. This discovery points out the substantial influence of the one-month albumin drop on EPS-related mortality, revealing a notably higher hazard ratio and a more significant impact when compared to its effect on overall mortality.

Impact of one-month albumin drop on mortality in EPS patients

Since the one-month albumin drop emerged as an independent risk factor for both overall mortality and EPS-related mortality, and considering that the average one-month albumin drop among the 35 EPS patients was 0.3 g/dL, we categorized these patients into 2 groups based on their one-month albumin drop levels. Patients with a one-month albumin drop exceeding 0.3 g/dL following EPS diagnosis showed significantly higher overall (log-rank p = 0.027) and EPS-related mortality rates (log-rank p = 0.016). Discussion

In our current study, we addressed an important and under-researched area in nephrology, focusing on the prognostic factors influencing outcomes in EPS patients. The five-year survival rate stands at a mere 26.3%, and we did not observe a significant survival benefit associated with surgical intervention or tamoxifen. Underlying comorbidities and laboratory parameters, including albumin, c-reactive protein, and white blood cell count at the time of EPS diagnosis, did not significantly impact patient outcomes. Given the rapidly evolving and highly variable clinical course after discontinuing PD and transitioning to hemodialysis, our study underscores the importance of a different predictor—the one-month drop in albumin levels following EPS diagnosis. This marker, indicative of progressive malabsorption and malnutrition, was associated with increased mortality in EPS patients in our cohort F.

Due to the rarity of EPS, limited studies have thoroughly examined the factors influencing patient outcomes. One study, which analyzed clinical parameters in 58 EPS patients, revealed that no single factor can reliably predict patient outcomes. Instead, they identified that the presence of two or more risk factors, such as bowel tethering, bloody ascites, post-PD withdrawal diagnosis, and elevated pre-EPS serum intact parathyroid levels, may predict poor outcomes in EPS patients [15]. The general approach to EPS management typically involves PD discontinuation to prevent further peritoneal damage [16]. However, given that EPS can occur long after PD discontinuation [17, 18] and PD exchanges may remove fibrin and inflammatory cytokines [19], the decision to discontinue PD depends on the severity of symptoms and the overall impact of hemodialysis on the patient’s well-being. Medical treatment options for EPS include immunosuppressants and tamoxifen [20–22]. Surgical interventions, aimed at relieving bowel obstruction and addressing calcified tissues, have also been reported [23–25].

Encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis is a serious condition characterized by its high mortality rate, primarily due to delayed diagnosis, severe complications, nutritional deficiencies, limited understanding of its pathophysiology, and absence of well-established treatment options. In our study, out of the 35 EPS patients, 21 died, resulting in a 1-year mortality rate of 31.5%. The mortality rate of EPS exhibits variations across different studies. For instance, a study by Hideki et al. in Japan reported a 1-year mortality rate of approximately 9 % [26]. In a UK study, a mortality rate of 57.1% by one year after diagnosis [27]. This variation may be attributed to the clinical diagnosis of all EPS cases, where early clinical features can be nonspecific. This could result in the inclusion of patients at different stages of EPS or those with different presentations, contributing to a wide range of mortality patterns. The high mortality underscores the severity and challenges inherent to EPS, emphasizing the need for further research to improve outcomes in this population.

The utilization of CT scans for EPS diagnosis has witnessed increasing prevalence [13], with certain findings like bowel tethering and peritoneal calcification being widely recognized as specific radiological indicators [28, 29]. In our study, we implemented a CT scan scoring system based on six parameters [14]. Our findings validated the relevance and efficacy of this diagnostic tool to support the role of CT scans in EPS identification, particularly when patients present with unexplained ultrafiltration failure or gastrointestinal symptoms. However, it should also note that while CT scans are highly useful for EPS detection, they may not serve as direct predictors of EPS outcomes. In this cohort, the CT score exhibited considerable variability among EPS patients, and the severity of CT scan findings did not exhibit statistical significance with regards to overall mortality or EPS-related mortality, which is consistent with the findings by Tarzi et al. [14] This suggests that while CT scans are crucial for diagnosis, they may not be indicative of patient outcomes in this context.

The development of EPS is primarily associated with prolonged inflammation and the formation of a thickened fibrocollagenous peritoneal membrane, leading to peritoneal adhesions and bands that cause kinking of the small bowel and recurrent episodes of bowel obstruction. In a clinical context, serum albumin levels can serve as markers of inflammation, reflecting changes in protein synthesis and capillary permeability in response to the body’s immune reactions [30]. Although patients with peritonitis often experience a reduction in serum albumin levels, the study did not find a higher mortality rate in patients with EPS who also presented with peritonitis. This could imply that while peritonitis tends to impact albumin levels, it may not necessarily contribute significantly to the mortality risk in the context of EPS observed in this specific patient population. Our research has highlighted the significance of the one-month drop in serum albumin levels, as opposed to baseline levels at the time of EPS diagnosis, in robustly predicting patient outcomes. In the context of EPS, characterized by dynamic changes, the one-month albumin drop is a critical indicator of ongoing inflammation, progressive malabsorption, and poor absorption. Unlike baseline albumin levels, it effectively captures these dynamic alterations. We chose the one-month albumin drop because of its ability to provide timely responses to the changes in a patient’s condition. The observed rapid clinical decline in some patients underscores the imperative need for early risk identification. By monitoring the one-month albumin drop, healthcare providers can promptly recognize individuals with deteriorating nutritional status, leading to tailored interventions, nutritional support, and treatment adjustments that have the potential to enhance patient outcomes and overall quality of life.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, as a retrospective study, our analysis is subject to selection and recall biases. The diagnosis of EPS was based on clinical judgment and imaging, which may introduce variability. Second, the precise mechanism underlying the prognostic significance of albumin fluctuation in the immediate aftermath of onset remains uncertain. Our study highlights the significance of the one-month drop in albumin levels as a predictor of EPS-related mortality. While this decline may indicate worsening malnutrition, it is important to recognize that albumin is also a negative acute-phase reactant. This study lacks direct nutritional assessments, such as the protein catabolic rate or PNA, which were not routinely collected in this retrospective analysis. The observed reduction in albumin levels may reflect not only nutritional deterioration but also the severity of systemic inflammation associated with EPS. Additionally, therapeutic interventions such as total parenteral nutrition initiated shortly after diagnosis may further complicate the interpretation of albumin trends. Future studies incorporating inflammatory markers alongside albumin changes may provide deeper insights into the interplay between inflammation and malnutrition in EPS prognosis. In addition, the rarity of EPS resulted in a relatively small sample size, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect prognostic factors. Moreover, EPS exhibits different clinical presentations and severities, and despite our efforts to collect comprehensive clinical parameters, some important factors such as laparoscopy or laparotomy findings showing characteristic gross thickening of the peritoneum encapsulating some or all of the small intestine within a cocoon of opaque tissue, or the presence of fibrin deposition and fibroblast phenotypic alterations, were not included in our analysis. Another limitation of our study is the exclusion of patients who had a follow-up duration of less than one month. This may have introduced selection bias, as it potentially excludes the sickest patients who died shortly after diagnosis, leading to an underestimation of early mortality in EPS. Finally, even though we adjusted for several factors in our multivariate analysis, the potential for residual confounding variables remains a possibility.

In summary, while CT scans serve as a vital tool for diagnosing EPS, they do not provide insight into patient outcomes. Our findings highlight the prognostic significance of the one-month albumin drop after EPS diagnosis. This decline likely reflects a combination of systemic inflammation, malnutrition, and overall disease progression, reinforcing its role as a marker of poor prognosis in EPS patients. Further studies incorporating additional inflammatory and nutritional markers are warranted to better delineate the underlying mechanisms.
