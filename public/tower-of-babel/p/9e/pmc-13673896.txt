ExposoGenomics: integrating genome and exposome as jointly dynamic systems for causal discovery and precision health
Authors: Vasilis Vasiliou, Nicholas Katsanis, Giuseppe Novelli, Juergen K V Reichardt, Bassam R Ali, Maria Gazouli, Sek Won Kong, Hongyu Zhao, Kiril Veselkov, Bhramar Mukherjee, Dimosthenis Sarigiannis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe past decade has witnessed an unprecedented convergence of exposomic technologies, population-scale genomics, and AI-enabled data science, creating the conditions for a new integrative discipline. Here we introduce ExposoGenomics, defined as the integrative study of how the genome and exposome, treated as jointly dynamic systems, interact across the life course to shape health and disease. ExposoGenomics moves beyond classical gene-environment interaction models by embedding high-dimensional, temporally resolved exposure data within a multi-omic and AI-enabled analytical architecture oriented toward causal discovery, mechanistic understanding, and translational application. We describe the conceptual foundations of this framework, its mechanistic architecture linking external exposures to genomic responses through physiologically based kinetic models and adverse outcome networks, and the analytical approaches, including causal machine learning, graph-based integration, and foundation models, required to realize its potential. We emphasize that computational prediction must be accompanied by rigorous empirical validation, and that findings must be grounded in biologically plausible, causally supported mechanisms. In conjunction with this Perspective, Human Genomics formally launches ExposoGenomics as a dedicated article category and invites submissions that advance this integrative agenda.Keywords: Exposome, Genome–exposome interactions, Causal inference, Adverse outcome networks, Physiologically based kinetic (PBK) modelling, Multi-omics, Machine learning, Precision prevention, Translational exposomics, Preconception exposome

Over the past two decades, Human Genomics has shaped our understanding of how genomic variation contributes to health and disease. Yet the genome alone cannot explain the full complexity of disease risk, progression, or population disparities—and we now have the tools to interrogate that complexity in earnest.

Environmental exposures, including chemical, physical, social, and behavioral factors—interact continuously with biological systems across the life course, shaping health outcomes through their translation into internal dose and downstream biological responses. A persistent challenge in this context is the accurate and comprehensive measurement of the exposome, an inherently high-dimensional, temporally variable construct subject to measurement error and misclassifications. Addressing these challenges is essential for building a genome–exposome inference that carries both retrospective accuracy and predictive value. A central tenet in building this knowledge base is the recognition that the biological responses are not a passive mirror of external exposure, but an ensemble of endogenous biochemical signals through which the organism integrates, transforms, and records life-course exposure history. For example, cumulative exposure to air pollutants across decades leaves a progressively altered epigenomic landscape, modifying DNA methylation patterns at inflammation-related loci—such that the biological response to a new respiratory insult in a 70-year-old reflects not just current exposure but the encoded history of a lifetime of prior ones. This bidirectional, time-evolving coupling of external and internal domains is what distinguishes the exposome paradigm from classical exposure science and is foundational to the ExposoGenomics framing. We therefore define ExposoGenomics as the integrative study of how the genome and exposome, treated as jointly dynamic systems, interact across the life course to shape health and disease, moving beyond pairwise gene-environment interactions to integrate high-dimensional, temporally resolved exposure data with genomic variation and multi-omic readouts, within an AI-enabled analytical architecture oriented toward causal discovery, mechanistic understanding, and translational application. In alignment with this evolving scope, we are pleased to introduce a new article category in Human Genomics: ExposoGenomics.

At the same time, human genomics is being reshaped by population-scale sequencing initiatives such as the UK Biobank and All of Us, single-cell and spatial profiling technologies that resolve cell-type-specific responses to environmental insults, and longitudinal biobanks that link genomic data to decades of exposure records across the life course. Graph-based methods are being applied to map how air pollutant exposures propagate through gene regulatory networks; causal machine learning frameworks are helping disentangle whether associations between heavy metal burden and neurodevelopmental outcomes reflect biological effects or confounding by socioeconomic factors; and foundation models are enabling transferable representations across omics datasets—from methylation arrays to single-cell transcriptomes [1]. Large language models are beginning to support curation and interpretation of exposure-linked multimodal data, and agentic workflows are being explored for end-to-end computational discovery pipelines [2]. However, three challenges constrain the field. First, most models remain black boxes, leaving the biological mechanisms driving predicted risk unresolved. Second, training data skewed toward European-ancestry populations limits generalizability across ancestries and exposure contexts. Third, integrating temporally resolved exposome data with genomic and multi-omic layers—across distinct biological scales and measurement platforms—lacks both technical standards and conceptual consensus.

These challenges underscore the need for a dedicated scholarly space in this area. ExposoGenomics is distinct from, though deeply indebted to, several related fields—and addresses the gaps each leaves open:

Exposome, as conceptualized originally by Chris Wild [3], introduced the totality of environmental exposures as a complement to the genome. In practice, however, ExposoGenomics narrows this scope to the subset of exposures that are amenable to accurate measurement, that show a clear and quantifiable differential biological response as a function of genotype or conversely, where genomic variation modifies exposure uptake, metabolism, or effect and that interact with the genome in nonlinear ways, thereby creating the conditions for mechanistic modeling and, ultimately, genuine predictive inference about disease risk.

However, AOPs have largely been developed on a chemical-by-chemical basis and remain insufficiently integrated with genomic susceptibility and multi-omic data [6], an area that ExposoGenomics directly addresses.

Mechanistically, ExposoGenomics operationalizes this integration through a layered architecture. External exposures [8] are translated into internal target-tissue dose via physiologically based kinetic (PBK) and quantitative in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation (QIVIVE) models [9, 10], for example, PBK models have been used to estimate fetal brain concentrations of methylmercury from maternal dietary exposure records, revealing tissue-level dose distributions that blood biomarkers alone cannot capture [11]. QIVIVE approaches have similarly been applied to derive internal hepatic doses of PFAS compounds from population biomonitoring data, enabling direct comparison with in vitro toxicological thresholds without reliance on animal bioassays [12].

Internal dose drives molecular initiating events captured by multi-omic readouts, which propagate to intermediate key events and adverse outcomes along adverse outcome networks. Genomic variation enters this architecture at every layer. At the absorption and metabolism level, well-characterized polymorphisms in CYP1A2 and NAT2 alter the internal dose of aromatic amines from tobacco or occupational exposure by two- to threefold between slow and fast metabolizers—directly shifting the effective exposure that reaches target tissues [13]. At the level of molecular initiating events, variants in DNA repair genes such as ERCC2 modify the threshold at which UV-induced pyrimidine dimers trigger mutagenic signaling, altering cancer risk for the same external UV dose [14]. Further downstream, polymorphisms in inflammatory pathway genes such as IL6 and TNF tune the magnitude of transcriptomic response to particulate matter exposure, yielding divergent cardiovascular risk trajectories from equivalent ambient pollution levels [15, 16]. Together, these layers yield a genome-dependent dose-to-effect cascade that is amenable to causal inference, in silico extrapolation, and precision-prevention design. Modern New Approach Methodologies (NAMs)—including organ-on-chip systems that recapitulate tissue-specific exposure responses, iPSC-derived microphysiological systems that capture donor genotype-dependent variation, and high-throughput transcriptomic screens that map molecular initiating events at scale—provide the human-relevant experimental backbone for validating this architecture and accelerating the transition from statistical association to mechanistic causation [17].

A natural extension of the journal’s scope

The introduction of ExposoGenomics is not a departure from the mission of Human Genomics, but rather a directional growth that seeks to respond to the maturation of the field and the need to recognize and accelerate emerging research directions. As the journal broadens its focus to include computational biology, machine learning, and high-dimensional data integration, ExposoGenomics provides a bridge between genomics and environmental health sciences. Unlike exposome sections hosted in environmental health or toxicology journals, ExposoGenomics submissions to Human Genomics are expected to demonstrate genomic variation as an active layer in the causal architecture, not exposure–outcome association reported alongside incidental omics data. In this context, ExposoGenomics positions Human Genomics at the forefront of a new era, one in which biological insight emerges from the convergence of environmental data and advanced analytics. This direction builds on a recent thematic collection in Human Genomics on translational exposomics [19], which demonstrated how integrating exposomic and genomic data enables identification of exposure–response relationships, molecular pathways, and disease mechanisms. Critically, it also highlighted the methodological challenges that ExposoGenomics must address head-on: exposome-wide association studies (EWAS), like their genome-wide counterparts, are subject to multiple testing burden, confounding, and limitations in causal interpretation. Gene-by-Environment-Wide Interaction Studies (GEWIS) extend this further by capturing multifactorial interplay, but a fully integrated genome × exposome × phenome framework—the foundation of ExposoGenomics—is needed to move from association to mechanism. The launch of this category formalizes and accelerates that trajectory.

Climate change is reorganizing the human exposome simultaneously across chemical (rising ozone and wildfire particulates), physical (extreme heat, UV intensity), biological (expanded ranges of Aedes mosquitoes carrying dengue and Zika, prolonged pollen seasons), and social (displacement, food insecurity, occupational heat stress) domains—creating correlated, compounding, and time-varying exposure burdens that classical single-exposure epidemiology cannot resolve. These stressors do not act on a genomically uniform population: variants in heat-shock protein genes modulate physiological tolerance to extreme heat; polymorphisms in xenobiotic metabolism genes such as CYP1A1 and GSTM1 shape individual susceptibility to wildfire-derived pollutants; and immune-related genetic architecture influences vector-borne pathogen susceptibility as transmission zones shift poleward. Resolving the health consequences of climate-driven exposome reorganization therefore requires genome–exposome frameworks capable of modeling how polygenic susceptibility profiles interact with shifting, multi-domain environmental burdens across the life course—and of identifying which population subgroups face disproportionate biological risk as climate trajectories diverge [32].

Preconception as an actionable exposogenomic window

A defining translational frontier for ExposoGenomics is the preconception period, a biologically decisive and modifiable window that extends the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) paradigm upstream [47]. Accumulating evidence indicates that both maternal and paternal exposures prior to conception shape offspring health trajectories, necessitating a shift from a maternal-centric to a dual-parent framework [48]. This shift is driven in part by a revised understanding of sperm biology. Beyond its genetic cargo, sperm functions as a molecular recorder of the paternal exposome, encoding environmental, metabolic, and lifestyle exposures through epigenetic and RNA-mediated signals [49]. These signals can influence early embryonic development and may contribute to intergenerational disease risk, although their persistence and causal pathways remain under active investigation. Within ExposoGenomics, the preconception period emerges as a uniquely actionable interface between exposure and biology. Germline molecular signatures provide an early, mechanism-linked readout of cumulative exposures and a target for intervention [50]. This repositions ExposoGenomics from a predominantly associative framework toward one capable of informing primary prevention, with direct implications for reproductive health, risk assessment, and early-life disease trajectories.

Addressing complexity through data science, machine learning and AI

The integration of genome and exposome data presents significant analytical challenges [51, 52], including high dimensionality, temporal variability, and causal inference in complex systems [53]. Of these, causal inference is arguably the most fundamental. Observational genome–exposome studies are inherently vulnerable to confounding by genetic background, population stratification, and reverse causation—where disease processes alter measured exposures rather than the reverse [54]. Addressing this requires purpose-built frameworks: Mendelian randomization and its multivariable extensions, which exploit genetic variants as instrumental variables to estimate unconfounded exposure effects [55]; directed acyclic graph (DAG)-based causal reasoning to formalize assumptions about confounding structure; and doubly robust and targeted learning estimators that combine outcome and exposure models to yield valid causal estimates even under partial model misspecification. These approaches rely on key assumptions, including the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and sufficient instrument strength, which may be difficult to satisfy in the context of correlated environmental exposures. Meeting these challenges also requires state-of-the-art AI methods that can learn across heterogeneous, multi-scale, and longitudinal data while preserving interpretability, robustness, and external validity [56]. Without rigorous attention to causal identification, even the most sophisticated multi-omics integration risks producing associations that are uninterpretable or irreproducible in independent populations. Crucially, statistical causal inference methods are most powerful when coupled with mechanistic causal scaffolds including AOP/AON structure, PBK-derived internal dose, and NAMs-based perturbation evidence [57], so that identification assumptions are informed by biology rather than imposed a priori, and so that discovered effects can be propagated along known key-event chains into testable, regulatorily relevant predictions. A critical and non-negotiable principle of ExposoGenomics is that computational prediction must be accompanied by empirical validation. Machine learning and AI models [61] can identify patterns and generate hypotheses at a scale and resolution previously unattainable—but prediction is not an explanation, and association is not causation [62]. The black-box nature of many AI methods makes this validation requirement non-negotiable rather than aspirational. Without rigorous validation against independent real-world data and experimental evidence, AI-generated findings risk becoming the substrate for further modeling, compounding errors exponentially, and severing the field from biological reality [63]. ExposoGenomics therefore requires that candidate exposure-genome associations and predictive models be validated at multiple levels: statistical replication in independent cohorts of diverse ancestry and exposure backgrounds; experimental interrogation of proposed mechanisms using human-relevant biological systems including organoids, iPSC-derived models [64], and controlled exposure studies; and where possible, prospective confirmation in longitudinal cohort data. Computational insight without this evidentiary grounding is hypothesis generation, not science—and must be clearly labeled as such.

By embracing these approaches, ExposoGenomics reinforces the role of Human Genomics as a leading platform for methodological innovation and computational integration.

Scope of the section ExposoGenomics will publish original research, methods papers, dataset descriptors, systematic reviews, and Perspectives. We welcome submissions from environmental health sciences, genomics, computational biology, epidemiology, regulatory science, and allied disciplines, and particularly encourage work that bridges these communities.

Standards of the section Because ExposoGenomics aims to inform both mechanism and policy, the section will hold submissions to high standards of ancestry and population diversity, transparent handling of selection and confounding, FAIR-aligned data and code sharing where ethically permissible, and—where primary data cannot move—use of privacy-preserving and federated architectures. Submissions should report exposure assessment methods with sufficient detail to evaluate measurement error and misclassification risk, and should specify outcome ascertainment procedures and their validation status; where established exposure-biomarker or outcome-validation guidelines exist for a given exposure class, submissions should reference adherence to them. For submissions reporting AI- or machine-learning-generated exposome–genome associations or predictive models, authors must state how these findings were validated, such as through statistical replication in independent cohorts, experimental interrogation in human-relevant systems, or, where feasible, prospective confirmation, and must clearly distinguish validated findings from hypothesis-generating computational output.

Looking forward

ExposoGenomics represents a next step in the evolution of the field, embedding environmental context into genomic research while leveraging the full power of AI and data science. By introducing this category, Human Genomics positions itself at the forefront of a rapidly advancing discipline—one that seeks to understand health as the product of continuous, dynamic interactions between the genome and the exposome. ExposoGenomics is not simply an extension of genomics; it is its necessary evolution in the age of big data, environmental complexity, and precision public health. This shift reflects a broader transition from reductionist to systems-level models of disease, in which causality emerges from interacting biological and environmental networks.
