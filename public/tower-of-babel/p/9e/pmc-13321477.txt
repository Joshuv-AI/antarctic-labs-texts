When good knowledge falls short: identity and geography drive inappropriate antibiotic use in Malaysia
Authors: Zhuo-Lin Chong, Noor Aliza Lodz, Sarbhan Singh, Suraya Amir Husin, Benedict Lim Heng Sim, Suresh Kumar Chidambaram, Sasheela Ponnampalavanar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInappropriate antibiotic use has been associated with poor knowledge on antibiotic use. However, behavioural change interventions targeting knowledge alone have low efficacy. Such a paradox warranted this pioneer study to determine the factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic use among the general population in Malaysia, particularly among those with good knowledge.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional survey conducted in 2020 on community-dwelling populations aged ≥ 15 years in Malaysia. They were selected using a two-stage stratified random cluster sampling design to represent the general population. Data were collected through face-to-face and computer-assisted telephone interviews. Antibiotic use for common cold or sore throat in the past one year was defined as inappropriate. This outcome was tested for association with the exposure variables, namely sociodemographic characteristics and knowledge score on antibiotic use, using the survey-weighted logistic regression function in STATA version 18.ResultsA total of 3,051 people participated, representing an estimated 24.6 million population in Malaysia. Among the overall population, the odds for inappropriate antibiotic use were higher among those with lower knowledge scores (aOR 2.381, 95%CI 1.365–4.153, p = 0.003, vs. higher scores) and younger age groups (aOR 3.727, 95%CI 2.040–6.807 for 15–17 years and 2.256, 95%CI 1.466–3.471 for 18–59 years, both p < 0.001, vs. ≥ 60 years), but lower among foreigners/non-citizens (aOR 0.468, 95%CI 0.266–0.822, p = 0.009, vs. native/bumiputera Malaysians). However, in the subgroup analysis among those with higher knowledge scores only, the odds were lower among non-native/non-bumiputera Malaysians (aOR 0.147, 95%CI 0.033–0.650, p = 0.012, vs. native/bumiputera) and those from peninsular states outside the central region (aOR 0.140, 95%CI 0.047–0.419, p = 0.001, vs. central).ConclusionsInterventions to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use should focus on increasing population knowledge generally with more emphasis on individuals aged < 60 years. However, among those who already have good knowledge, the interventions should also enable individuals to overcome the obstacles to the desirable behaviours, especially among native/bumiputera Malaysians and those living in the central region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26925-0.

Background

The discovery of Penicillin and its subsequent mass production ushered in a new era for humankind, when deaths from minor injuries and common infections are much less likely to happen [1]. As more antimicrobials become available, their applications continue to expand. With the growth in human population and increased demands for food supply, especially protein sources, antimicrobials have also become widely used in veterinary medicine to prevent and treat animal diseases [2], and in agriculture as growth promoters in farmed animal feeds [3].

Along with the increased usage of antimicrobials comes the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [2, 4]. AMR refers to a phenomenon, where a previously effective antimicrobial becomes ineffective in treating infections caused by a particular microorganism. This happens when microorganisms acquire the mechanisms that enable them the ability to withstand the therapeutic effect of antimicrobials [1]. While antimicrobials include antibiotics, antifungals, antiparasitics, and antivirals, AMR is primarily driven by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics [1, 2]. In 2019, it is estimated that 1.27 million deaths and 47,600 years of life lost (YLL) were attributable to resistance to antibiotics globally [5]. AMR also prolongs hospital stays and requires more expensive drugs, leading to higher medical costs [6]. If left unchecked, AMR could lead to 10 million deaths annually with economic losses of as much as US$ 100 trillion in 2050 [1, 5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) developed a global action plan to address AMR, which was endorsed at the 68th World Health Assembly in May 2015. The first objective of the plan is to improve AMR awareness and understanding among all stakeholders, including the public. A multi-country public awareness survey commissioned by the WHO in 2015 reported a generally prevalent antibiotic use and low level of knowledge on antibiotic use. The findings varied between countries and socio-demographic groups, with lower-income countries, younger individuals, those with less education, and rural-dwelling populations showing particularly limited awareness and understanding of appropriate antibiotic use [6]. In Malaysia, this survey was conducted from 2022 to 2023 [7]. It concluded that younger and lower-socioeconomic populations need educational programs to increase awareness [7], in line with the findings of a smaller study carried out in 2021 [8].

Health awareness or knowledge level on specific health topics is fundamental to promoting desirable health behaviours or practices. This premise underlies many public health interventions. However, previous studies have shown inconsistent results regarding the extent of the influence of health knowledge towards actual practice [9, 10]. In fact, behavioural change interventions targeting knowledge were reported to have the lowest efficacy [11]. This is understandable as health knowledge is not the only construct that predicts behaviours according to behavioural models like the Theory of Planned Behaviours and Health Belief Model [12]. As such, behavioural change interventions targeting behavioural related constructs were the most efficacious [11].

In the same way, studies on AMR need to focus more on the behaviour that serves as its main contributing factors, i.e. inappropriate antibiotic use [1, 2], and explore the factors associated with such misuse, even among those who demonstrate good knowledge. This is important to enable evidence-based changes in existing interventions and the planning of future ones. In Malaysia, one such study used telephone interviews to administer a comprehensive 68-item questionnaire to assess both knowledge and practice of antibiotic use among the general public nationwide. However, its 9.1% response rate may have introduced selection bias, limiting its representativeness. Moreover, although it found that better knowledge was associated with appropriate use of antibiotics, it did not specifically examine inappropriate antibiotic use among those who demonstrated good knowledge [13].

The main objective of this study is to determine the factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic use among the general community-dwelling population in Malaysia, particularly among those with good knowledge. The research questions are: (1) Which population used antibiotics (in)appropriately? (2) Among those with good knowledge on antibiotic use, which population used antibiotics (in)appropriately?

Methods

Study and sampling design

This study was conducted as part of the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) conducted in 2020 by the Institute for Public Health (IPH), Malaysia [14]. It was a nation-wide population-based cross-sectional survey, in which the 16 states and territories in Malaysia with population size ranging from 181,859 to 6,239,037 individuals comprised the primary stratum, and the secondary stratum was urban and rural communities within the primary stratum.

A sampling frame from the Department of Statistics, Malaysia (DOSM) was acquired. It divided Malaysia into > 90,000 enumeration blocks (EB), each consisting of up to 120 living quarters (LQ) that usually contain a household or two with 3–4 individuals each. On average, there are approximately 500–600 non-institutionalised individuals in each EB. To ensure representation of the general population of Malaysia living in each stratum, a two-stage stratified random cluster sampling design was used to select 113 EB as the primary sampling units (PSU), and then 20 LQ or secondary sampling units (SSU) were selected from each selected EB/PSU.

NHMS is conducted yearly on different health topics to enable evidence-based policy making in the Ministry of Health. The focus of NHMS 2020 was communicable diseases. It contained multiple modules with different target populations. The main modules targeted populations aged one year and older with an expected sample size of 5,000, calculated based on the single proportion formula adequate for a prevalence of 2.5% and adjusted for a design effect of two [14]. For the module on antibiotic use, populations aged 15 years and above who lived in the selected LQ were recruited, except for LQ that were institutionalized, e.g. nursing homes, orphanages, etc.

Data collection

The data collection was conducted in two phases to minimise human contact and reduce the risk of COVID-19 transmission [15] (Study Flowchart in Supplement Figure). The first phase was conducted from August to October 2020, when trained data collectors visited all selected LQ and recruited all eligible residents to participate in the survey. After informed consent (and additional assent for those aged < 18 years), the data collectors interviewed all participants using mobile tablets with structured questionnaires to capture various data, including socio-demographic variables.

Age was captured in numeral and categorised subsequently into minors aged 15–17 years, adults aged 18–59 years, and older adults aged ≥ 60 years. Biological sex and ethnicity/nationality were captured according to official documents. Foreigners were those without Malaysian citizenship. Malaysians with citizenship were further grouped into those with native or bumiputera status (mainly Malay, orang asli or aborigines living in Peninsular Malaysia, orang asal Sarawak and Sabah or natives from Borneo/East Malaysia) and those without native or bumiputera status (mainly descendants of migrants with Chinese or Indian heritage). Geographically, Malaysia was divided into West (peninsula) and East Malaysia (Borneo). This was captured as location, but the peninsula was further divided into the central region surrounding the capital city of Kuala Lumpur and the other peninsular states. The central region includes the states of Selangor, Negeri Sembilan, and the Federal Territories of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya. The other peninsular states are Perlis, Kedah, Penang, Perak, Melaka, Johore, Pahang, Terengganu, and Kelantan. Education level captured was aligned with the local system where primary schools provide up to 6 years of formal education (usually up to 12 years old) and secondary schools provide up to 13 years of formal education. Diploma holders were grouped into secondary, but having obtained a university degree was defined as tertiary education. Household income was documented as continuous variable and later categorised into the bottom 40, middle 40, and top 20 earners according to definitions varied by states during the year of data collection.

The component on antibiotic use was studied in the second phase through computer-assisted telephone interviews. All participants recruited were called and interviewed using a content and face validated questionnaire adapted from WHO Antibiotic Resistance: Public Awareness Survey [6]. This study used the following seven items for analysis: six on knowledge and one on practice of antibiotic use (Table 1). The practice item was purposely asked before the knowledge items without revisiting it to mitigate any potential social desirability bias.

Operational definitions

Possible responses to these seven items in the questionnaire were positive (yes), negative (no or never), and don’t know. For the six knowledge items, each correct response was given one score, while other negative responses, don’t know, or even no response, were not given any score, making the possible total score ranging from 0 to 6. We categorized all participants into lower (≤ 4 scores) and higher knowledge score (> 4 scores) categories. This cut-off was selected after considering that the binomial probability of five correct responses out of six (> 4) by chance is much lower at 9.4% as compared to 23.4% for four out of six (> 3) [16]; and that sensitivity analyses carried out using > 3, >4, and > 5 correct responses as cut-off for higher knowledge score, and even leaving it uncategorized in quantitative form, found the model more robust and stable with > 4 as cut-off. Practice was ascertained and defined as inappropriate use when participants answered “yes” to taking antibiotics for common cold or sore throat in the past one year.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with STATA version 18. Descriptive and subgroup analyses were used to describe the socio-demographic characteristics and knowledge score category of the study participants, and the estimated populations in Malaysia they represented were computed using complex sample analysis with applied weights and Taylor-linearized variance estimation (svy set, svy linearized). Multivariable analyses were performed on the estimated overall and subpopulation (among those with higher knowledge scores only) using logistic regression for survey data (svy: logistic). All variables with p-values of < 0.05 in the univariable analyses were entered into the multivariable stage to compute adjusted odds ratios (aOR). Missing data were considered missing at random. All available data in each variable were included in the univariable analyses, but the multivariable analyses excluded those subjects with missing data in any of the included variables. Odds ratio with 95%CI that did not cross 1.0 from either side and p-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in the final models. Model fitness was assessed using STATA default goodness-of-fit test for binary response models using survey data (estat gof), with a p-value of ≥ 0.05 accepted as good fit.

Results

There were 2,104 eligible LQs throughout Malaysia, and 1,876 of them participated (household level response rate = 89.2%) [14]. They contributed a total of 5,302 eligible participants aged ≥ 15 years [14], out of which 3,051 responded to the antibiotic use module (individual level response rate = 57.5%). The overall response rate of this study was 51.3% (Study Flowchart in Supplement Figure).

These 3,051 participants represented an estimated 24.6 million general population aged ≥ 15 years in Malaysia. The larger estimated subpopulations were 18–59 years of age (79.7%), male (51.0%), native/bumiputera Malaysians (59.8%), from other peninsular states (48.0%), educated up to secondary level (45.2%), from the lowest income group (65.5%). The majority of Malaysia’s general population (90.8%) had lower knowledge scores on antibiotic use, and only 9.2% scored higher (Table 2).

Sample count and the population they represented by exposure variables

Overall, among the general population, the weighted prevalence of inappropriate antibiotic use in the past 12 months was 25.0% (95%CI 21.9–28.5). Younger age groups had significantly higher odds to report inappropriate antibiotic use in the past 12 months compared to ≥ 60 years, with an aOR of 3.727 (95%CI 2.040–6.807) for individuals aged 15–17 years and an aOR of 2.256 (95%CI 1.466–3.471) for those aged 18–59 years (both p < 0.001). Foreigners/non-citizens had lower odds of inappropriate antibiotic use compared to native/bumiputera Malaysians, with an aOR of 0.468 (95%CI 0.266–0.822, p = 0.009). With regards to knowledge on antibiotic use, the aOR was 2.381 (95%CI 1.365–4.153, p = 0.003) for those with lower scores, compared to the higher score group (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that the majority of the general population in Malaysia had a lower level of knowledge regarding antibiotic use and approximately quarter reported inappropriate antibiotic use in the past 12 months. Among the overall general population, age group, ethnicity/nationality, and knowledge score were associated with inappropriate antibiotic use, with younger age groups and lower knowledge scores being predictive factors, whereas being foreigners/non-citizens was a protective factor. Among the general population with higher knowledge scores on antibiotic use, only ethnicity/nationality and location were associated with inappropriate use, with both non-native/non-bumiputera Malaysians and those living in other peninsular states being the protective factors.

Inappropriate antibiotic use is defined and measured differently using different study designs among different populations, resulting in different prevalence estimates [13, 17–24]. In general, studies can be grouped into clinical audit or database analysis, and healthcare provider and/or patient interview. Indicators of inappropriate use can be any of or a mix of the lack of indication, whether provider- or patient-mediated; incorrect drug selection, dosage, route of administration or treatment duration, and non-compliance [17]. Secondary data analysis provides the most objective measurement, as its indicator is based on recorded prescriptions [17, 20, 21]. One study in the United Kingdom reported 30.1% and 36.1% inappropriate use among indicated usage for community-acquired infections and surgical prophylaxis, respectively [20]. Another study in Spain reported 34.2% inappropriate use, but the denominator also included patients without an indication for antibiotics [21]. Either way, both studies were solely based on hospital databases and might not reflect the population in general.

Unlike healthcare providers that are generally highly trained and guideline-driven in their prescriptions of antibiotics, the general population could be more heterogeneous from place to place. A 2018 study across China reported 37.1% prevalence of self-medication with antibiotics in the past six months among its 15,526 subjects [25]. Two studies using similar design and tools reported 30.9% and 37.9% of inappropriate antibiotic use among those who did use in the past 12 months from different places in Ethiopia [26, 27]. In Malaysia, the only other nationwide study in 2019 that reported inappropriate antibiotic use using a composite questionnaire did not attach a time frame. It put a lifetime prevalence of inappropriate use at 47.3% [13], which would be higher compared to the 25.0% in the past 12 months based on a single question used in our study. Considering other unmeasured indicators, the prevalence of inappropriate use in Malaysia could be higher than our study estimate. Mulchandani et al. reviewed 412 studies conducted between 2000 and 2021, and, using a database on global antibiotic sales by country, estimated a 36.5% of inappropriate use from studies on patient-mediated factors based on interview of patients or the general population, and 29.5–30.8% based on other study designs on provider-mediated factors, suggesting a gravitation of the proportion of inappropriate use towards one third in most studies [17]. Still, as these data were subjectively reported by study participants, they were subjected to biases, such as recall and social desirability biases.

The findings of inappropriate antibiotic use being associated with younger age groups and lower knowledge scores were expected and in line with two studies conducted in Malaysia and Singapore [13, 22]. Furthermore, these studies also reported that age mediated [13] or modified [22] the association between inappropriate use and lower knowledge, and recommended targeted knowledge interventions for younger age groups. While our study did not find any of the above, further subgroup analysis by age group (data not shown) did find that lower knowledge score remained a significant predictor only among 18–59 years, but not among other age groups. This not only narrows down the middle age group as the target group for future education campaigns, where they could be more effective [22]; it also highlights the youngest age group being a predictor of inappropriate antibiotic use, independent of their knowledge level. As this underage group is still legally under the care of their parents or guardians, it is unlikely that they can self-prescribe prescription-only antibiotics. Further studies are required to determine the role of other factors on inappropriate antibiotic use among them, especially parental demand for antibiotics or its perception by the providers [18, 28–30].

It is interesting to find that foreigners are protective of inappropriate antibiotic use in Malaysia. Further analysis showed that this was despite 96.8% of them having lower knowledge scores (data not shown). As Malaysia has a sizable low-skilled foreign worker population from lower income countries, the most plausible explanation to this is their lower access to healthcare [31]. Easier access to healthcare or antibiotics, even for self-limiting conditions with and without a prescription, are associated with inappropriate antibiotic use [17, 18, 23, 32]. Foreign workers in Malaysia are generally employed without health insurance coverage or with one that comes with a cap on yearly and per-visit fees. This financial disincentive, coupled with the presence of strong regulations and enforcement here to ensure antibiotics are by-prescription only, might be the reasons behind this group being protective of its inappropriate use and requiring further studies to determine their contributions [17, 18, 32].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that reported inappropriate antibiotic use among those with better knowledge on antibiotic use. Currently available literature does not contain similar subgroup analysis [13, 18, 22–27, 30]. Here, non-native/non-bumiputera Malaysians and those from other peninsular states had lower odds to use antibiotics inappropriately, as compared to native/bumiputera Malaysians and those from the central region, respectively, suggesting that the latter two groups were predictive of inappropriate antibiotic use despite good knowledge scores. This means that knowledge interventions alone are not adequate to tackle this issue among native/bumiputera Malaysians and those living in the central region closer to the capital of the country. This knowledge-practice gap might be explained by the Information-Motivation-Behavioural Skills (IMB) and/or Health Belief models (HBM), which suggest motivation and behavioural skills or individual multi-constructional perceptions of a specific health issue are fundamental for a behaviour or practice to materialize, apart from knowledge of that health issue [33, 34]. Besides, in the absence of knowledge deficits, other behavioural or structural barriers can also prevent good practices. Some hypotheses like the lack of social support, perceived higher threat of common cold or sore throat, or higher self-efficacy of the rational use of antibiotics can be postulated, but they have to be tested in further studies using IMB or HBM to inform future interventions [33, 34].

The strength of this study lies in its representativeness of the general population in Malaysia. This is made possible with its rigorous two-stage stratified random cluster sampling design and complex sample analysis. Furthermore, it is the first to highlight inappropriate antibiotic use despite good knowledge. There are several study limitations. First of all, the knowledge-practice gap hypothesis based on health behavioural models carries a connotation of causation that cannot be established through the cross-sectional design of this study. Secondly, we acknowledge that the knowledge categorization, while underwent rigorous procedures to determine its cut-off to make it sufficiently discriminatory in this study, can only be an arbitrary measure of knowledge and not an absolute one, hence the choice of words “higher” and “lower”, instead of “high” and “low”. Thirdly, the outcome measure was self-reported and could be subjected to recall bias. While having a single question for outcome measure might underestimate the burden relative to other studies, and despite the shorter 6-item knowledge tool being arbitrary, they did have the advantage of reducing participant fatigue in a health survey with multiple objectives, hence, could elicit more genuine responses compared to a composite tool, making them useful and balanced proxies.

Last but not least, we believe our findings can inform the execution of the current Malaysian Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (MyAP-AMR) 2022–2026 [35] and the drafting of the next MyAP-AMR, specifically on strategy 1 - strengthening awareness and understanding of AMR, and strategy 4 - appropriate use of antimicrobials. Concrete actions like more aggressive AMR education or public health communication for younger age groups, inclusion of AMR module into the existing community and workplace health programs where the target groups congregate, the addition of non-knowledge-based interventions such as behavioural nudges and provider-patient communication strategies surrounding the prescription, dispensing and consumption of antimicrobials, and mandatory periodic antimicrobial stewardship training for healthcare professionals, can be considered.

Conclusions

Age group, ethnicity/nationality, and knowledge on antibiotic use were associated with inappropriate antibiotic use in Malaysia. However, despite good knowledge, inappropriate antibiotic use was still dependent on ethnicity/nationality and geographical location. Interventions to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use should focus on increasing population knowledge generally with more emphasis on adults age < 60 years. On the other hand, among those who already have good knowledge, the interventions should also enable individuals to overcome the obstacles to the desirable behaviours, especially among native/bumiputera Malaysians and those living in the central region.
