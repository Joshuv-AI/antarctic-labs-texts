Heterolytic tide-dominated sedimentary facies and depositional environment: An Example from the Boka Bil formation, Sitapahar anticline, Bangladesh
Authors: Sohag Ali, Numair Ahmed Siddiqui, AKM Eahsanul Haque, Nisar Ahmed, Mohamed A.K. El-Ghali, Md Yeasin Arafath, Mizanur Rahman, Mrinal Kanti Roy, Alidu Rashid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Baraichari Shale Formation, which is equivalent to the Boka Bil Formation, is well exposed within the Neogene sequence of the Sitapahar Anticline in the Chittagong-Tripura Fold Belt, Bengal Basin, Bangladesh. Regardless of its importance in hydrocarbon accumulation, a comprehensive facies analysis is essential to understand and explain the heterogeneity within different depositional architectural elements. The purpose of this work is to delineate the lithofacies characteristics, depositional environment heterogeneities and propose a generic depositional model for the Middle to Late Miocene shallow marine sediments of the formation. The sedimentary facies were investigated at three outcrops along the Kaptai–Chandraghona road cut sections of the Sitapahar anticline. The formation is composed of dark to bluish-gray shale, silty shale, yellowish brown siltstone, and gray to yellowish brown fine-to medium-grained sandstone. Eight distinct sedimentary facies have been identified and were categorized into three facies associations: subtidal or estuarine, mixed flats with tidal creeks, and tidal mud flats. The subtidal facies, predominant in the middle member, consists of fine to medium-grained ripple cross-stratified sandstone-siltstone, herringbone cross-stratified sandstone, and trough cross-stratified sandstone, indicating sub-tidal channel deposition. Mixed flats with tidal creeks exhibit heterolithic facies, suggesting energy level changes in intertidal to sub-tidal zones influenced by tidal currents. Tidal mud flats are predominantly laminated shale/mudstone and lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone, reflecting deposition during tide transitions in the intertidal zone. Fining upward cycles within the formation indicate progressive progradation or changes in inner tidal flats conditions during regressive periods. These depositional settings serve as analogs for equivalent subsurface reservoirs and contribute to the construction of reservoir models with greater accuracy.

Introduction

The growing interest in tidal deposits is driven by the necessity to comprehend coastal tidal environments to predict their response to sea-level rise and climate change, as well as to enhance knowledge of ancient depositional environments influenced by tides [1]. The cyclical nature of tides—rising, slack, and falling— provides distinctive sedimentary records that offer valuable insights into understanding the geologic history of the earth [2,3]. Sediment movement in tidal settings is complex, influenced by various sedimentological and hydrodynamic parameters over various temporal and spatial scales. Both non-cohesive and cohesive particles are significantly affected by waves and tides [4]. In these environments, coarser, non-cohesive sediments are typically transported as bedload, while finer, cohesive sediments are primarily carried as suspended load and deposited during calm, slack tide conditions [4]. A unique aspect of tidal flow is its bidirectional nature, which can create distinctive sedimentary structures such as herringbone cross-stratification [4]. Tides generate currents that transport and deposit sediment, creating patterns that are preserved in stratigraphic units, allowing for the reconstruction of paleo-depositional environments. The absence of tidal signatures might be due to energy conditions limiting sediment transport or the dominance of wave action, which can obscure or modify tidal effects [2].

The Miocene Surma Group is a hydrocarbon-bearing unit in the Bengal Basin, playing a crucial role in the Assam-Arakan Basin petroleum system and covering a large part of the Bengal foredeep [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. It consists of the lower Bhuban and upper Boka Bil Formations, which are extensively exposed in the Sylhet Trough and Chittagong Tripura Folded Belt (CTFB) of Bangladesh (Fig. 1a). These formations can be up to 5 km thick and include dark gray shale, siltstone, mudstone, and alternating fine to medium-grained sandstone, deposited through cycles of marine transgression and regression [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Due to the lack of lithofacies heterogeneity between the Bhuban and Boka Bil formations, it is quite challenging to determine their depositional settings [5,7,9,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. While Evans [18] correlates the Surma Group outcrops in Upper and Lower Assam to similar formations, other authors highlight the difficulty of correlating these due to the lack of marker horizons, lithological variations, and diachronism [10,15,[19], [20], [21],22]. The Bhuban Formation in the CTFB region represents a submarine fan system [[23], [24], [25]], while the Boka Bil Formation is tide-dominated, with environments ranging from continental slopes to shallow marine areas [10,12,26,27]. Seismic interpretations of the Hatia Trough, located west of the CTFB region, indicate a deltaic shelf-slope environment [28]. Sharmili et al. [17] suggest that the Surma Group experienced three interconnected depositional environments: fluvio-deltaic distributary, tide-dominated shallow marine and wave-dominated shallow marine. The study area is situated in the Sitapahar anticline which spans approximately 50 km long and 10 km wide. It is in the southern-central region of the CTFB, a range of hills formed by Tertiary sediments. According to Alam et al. [20], the study area has a unique tectonic and stratigraphic history along the southeast margin of the Bengal Basin. The Sitapahar anticline, with its asymmetrical, double-plunging, open-folded structure, comprises five conformable formations (Fig. 7b and c) from the upper Miocene to Pleistocene: Sitapahar Sandstone-Shale, Baraichari Shale, Chandraghona Sandstone, Girujan Clay, and Lichubagan Sandstone [37]. These are considered equivalent to the traditional units described by Evans [18], including Bhuban, Boka Bil, Tipam Sandstone, Girujan Clay, and Dupi Tila. The Boka Bil Formation, which is locally known as the Baraichari Shale Formation, is well exposed along the Kaptai-Chandraghona road section of the Sitapahar Anticline, as illustrated by the Digital Elevation Model (Fig. 7d). The formation has a thickness of around 1105 m and is classified into upper, middle, and lower members. These classifications rely on gross lithofacies, which consist mainly of gray to bluish-gray shale, silty shale, siltstone, sandy-siltstone, and fine to medium-grained sandstone ranging between gray and brownish gray. Alam [32] and Gani & Alam [38] investigated the tide-dominated depositional environment of the Baraichari Shale Formation, while Shakik & Hossain [13] identified shallow marine to deltaic environments. However, there is a significant absence of in-depth geological and sedimentological investigations on this formation regarding the sub-environment of tidal settings and their depositional model. Furthermore, limited attention has been paid to interpreting the factors driving environmental changes during Miocene sediment deposition in Bangladesh. This is largely due to the complex nature of tide-dominated coastal environments, which are among the most intricate depositional settings on earth, as well as the limited accessibility and lithofacies heterogeneities [27]. Despite these challenges, understanding this formation is crucial for hydrocarbon accumulation in the region. Therefore, this research aims to address this gap by conducting a detailed analysis of lithofacies, facies associations, facies model, and paleocurrent to reconstruct depositional environments of the Boka Bil Formation in the Sitapahar anticline, Chittagong-Tripura Folded Belt, Bangladesh.

Geological setting

The formation of the Bengal Basin, which occurred during the Paleocene or Late Eocene period as a result of the collision of the Indian, Myanmar (Burma), and Tibetan (Eurasian) plates, is connected to the tectonic and depositional history of the Himalayan orogenic belts [12,13,20,22]. The Bengal Basin consists of three separate tectonic provinces: (1) the Eastern Folded Belt (EFB) or the Chittagong-Tripura Folded Belt (CTFB) Province in the east (2) the Central Deep Basin Province or remnant ocean, and (3) the Stable Shelf Province in the northwest. Each province has its own geological and sedimentary history. The structural development of the Bengal Basin began during the Early Cretaceous, when the Gondwanaland continent was rifted, from a regional perspective. During the Late Cretaceous-Late Eocene, the Indian plate drifted and collided with Eurasia and Myanmar, resulting in the creation of east-west trending Himalayan and north-south trending Indo-Myanmar ranges along with the Bengal Basin formation [11,12,39,40]. By the Early Miocene, the Bengal Basin had transformed into a residual ocean basin due to the oblique subduction of the Indian plate under the Burmese plate (Fig. 2a). The Indo-Burman ranges exhibit accretionary prism development and migration as a result of the eastward subduction [29]. Then, this prism complex advanced westward through the CTFB area (Fig. 2b) which is known as the Neogene accretionary prism [17]. The deep basin and the CTFB accumulated sediments from collision-derived and Indo-Burman arc-derived sources, becoming sediment depocenters (Alam et al., 2003). During the Mid-Pliocene, a fluvial-deltaic environment prevailed, causing the sediment depocenter to move southward. Subduction continuously compressed sediments of the Bengal Basin and caused uplift, resulting in the formation of north-south trending anticlines in the CTFB during the Early-Middle Pleistocene [40,41]. The asymmetric Sitapahar anticline, located in the south-central part of CTFB, has a plunge of 4° towards S162°E and a regional strike of N20°W to S16°E [20]. The study area is characterized by several mountain ranges or hills, with steep slopes and sharp peaks, spurs, and ridges of Tertiary sedimentary succession separated by valleys [42]. These hills are highly dissected by rivers, streams, streamlets, and valleys, and are mostly tight, plunging, and faulted with NNW-SSE striking followed by syncline due to the NE movement of the Indian plate towards the Eurasian plate that is striking along the Myanmar plate [[33], [34], [42]].Fig. 2(a) Topographic map showing the Early Miocene paleogeographic features of the Bengal Basin and its surroundings tectonic frameworks [10]. Methodology

Detailed identification of facies types, facies associations, and facies relationships was carried out using the compass-clinometer traverse method during extensive fieldwork at three outcrops of the Baraichari Shale Formation (Late Miocene to Pliocene) in Kaptai Upazila, Rangamati District, Chittagong Division, Bangladesh. The tertiary clastic rocks within this formation are well exposed along the Kaptai–Chandraghona road cut sections, hillslope and stream-cut valley of the Sitapahar anticline in the CTFB region (Fig. 3a and b). The rock specimens were examined using various methods, including pocket lenses, magnifying glasses, naked eyes, and hydrochloric acid (HCl) to identify cementing materials [44]. The bed thickness was determined using the expression sin δ = t/s, where ‘t’ represents the bed thickness, ‘δ’ indicates the angle of dip, and ‘s’ denotes the outcrop distance perpendicular to the strike line. Each lithofacies was described in terms of grain size, color, and sedimentary structures, and is coded according to the schemes of Reineck & Singh [45], Reeckmann [46], Carling & Dawson [47], Miall [48,49] and according to the descriptions of those facies, three sedimentological logs were constructed. Paleocurrent data was collected from trough cross-bedding and ripple marks where faces are exposed for 3-D measurements [9,50]. Results

Depositional facies

The Baraichari Shale Formation equivalent of the Boka Bil Formation is comprised of dark gray to bluish gray shale, silty shale, yellowish brown siltstone, and gray to yellowish brown fine to medium-grained sandstone. Based on lithology, bedding architecture, paleo-current, sedimentary structures, and contact relationships, the formation has been categorized into three facies associations: tidal mud flats, mixed flats with tidal creek and subtidal or estuarine. A total of eight distinct sedimentary facies have been identified in the studied section of the Boka Bil Formation. According to these lithofacies, the Boka Bil Formation is classified into upper, middle, and lower members. The upper and lower members are composed of fine-grained lithofacies, including flaser laminated sandstone-siltstone, wavy laminated sandstone-siltstone-silty shale, lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone, and laminated shale facies. In contrast, the middle member features fine to medium-grained lithofacies, such as herringbone cross-stratified sandstone, trough cross-stratified sandstone, ripple cross-stratified sandstone-siltstone, and intercalated sandstone and shale facies.

Herringbone cross-stratified sandstone lithofacies

Description

Herringbone cross-stratification occurs in tidal environments including tidal flats and tidal channels. They are characterized by bi-directional flow indicators, providing clear evidence of current reversals. In the study area, this facies is found in the middle member of the Boka Bil Formation, Sitapahar Anticline. This facies is composed of yellowish gray, moderately sorted, and fine to medium-grained sandstones that are characterized by ripple marks and trough cross-bedded stratification (Fig. 4e). Herringbone cross-stratified sandstone signifies that bedload deposition occurred because of tidal currents that went in the opposite direction but had similar bottom flow speeds and bed shear intensities [51,52]. Generally, these reversals exhibit a bipolar orientation and occur during both the dropping ebb and rising flood periods of the tidal cycle. Herringbone cross-bedding forms in nearshore tidal environments [45,53], but Collinson [54] suggests that the origin could be inter-tidal and shallow sub-tidal. Chakraborty et al. [55] suggest its lesser occurrence indicates weaker tides, while Buatois & Mángano [56] propose it reflects subordinate tidal flows capable of bedform migration.

Trough cross-stratified sandstone lithofacies

Description

This facies consists of yellowish gray to brown, moderate to well-sorted and fine to medium-grained sandstone with both large and small-scale trough cross-stratification which is overlain by ripple laminated fine-grained sandstone (Fig. 4d). The width of the trough varies from 1.15m to 1.33m while height varies from 0.13m to 0.19m and the trough angle varies from 32° to 40° respectively. As the succession progresses upward, both the troughs and grain size distribution decrease. In the middle member of the Boka Bil Formation, this facies is also a dominating lithofacies.-Interpretation

-Interpretation

Interpretation

This facies is created by medium-sized, three-dimensional dunes in subtidal environments [57]. The measured portions of these facies exhibit variations in paleocurrent directions across distinct stratigraphic stages. Careful examination of some cross-bed sets reveals erosion surfaces that cut across the cross-strata. These reactivation surfaces show how the bed form changed due to the fluctuation in the flow velocity levels [58]. According to Boggs [59], reactivation surfaces are formed when the ripple crests are eroded and then redeposited during the following asymmetrical tidal cycle. Storms and reversals of tidal currents are two potential causes of their occurrence in tidal sand deposits. The presence of an erosional base, trough cross-stratified sandstone, and bipolar paleoflow directions (oriented approximately NE-SW across all formation members) in adjacent depositional units indicate sediment deposition in tidal sandbars and a sub-tidal (higher energy conditions) environment [52,60]. The cross-bedded sandstone facies is covered with ripple-laminated fine-grained sandstone, which suggests that the energy of the depositional current is gradually decreasing [5,17,32,38].

Ripple cross stratified sandstone-siltstone lithofacies

Description

Ripple cross-stratified sandstone-siltstone facies is common across all the members of the Boka Bil Formation (Fig. 4). It consists of gray to yellowish brown, relatively well-sorted, thinly bedded, fine-to medium-grained sandstone and siltstone. This facies is interbedded or overlies with the lenticular laminated sandstone–siltstone, wavy laminated sandstone–siltstone–silty shale and flaser laminated sandstone–siltstone facies. On the bedding planes, the crest of the ripples is truncated, and the internal laminations are mud-draped (Fig. 4b). Both symmetrical and asymmetrical ripples are also found in the study area. The direction of the ripples is generally NE-SW with little variances, and they are bimodal and bipolar.-Interpretation

-Interpretation

Interpretation

The bimodal and bipolar ripples imply the presence of a tidal flow-influenced environment. The occurrence of mud drapes within sandstone (Fig. 4b) suggests alternating slack water stages and periods of current activity, a characteristic commonly found in shallow tidal environments, and potentially tidal flats [61,62]. This interpretation is reinforced by the paleocurrent data, which indicates bipolar and bidirectional flows. Additionally, the presence of truncated ripples and bioturbation patterns further supports the inference of tidal influences in the depositional setting [32,61,27,45,53].

Description

The alternating band of sandstone and shale facies with thinly laminated non-calcareous silty shales is observed in the middle member of the Boka Bil Formation (Fig. 4, Fig. 5c). The sandstone is very fine to fine-grained, light gray to yellowish brown, thin, and parallelly laminated with a low-angle dip. The upper boundary of the sandstone layers is characterized by a smooth and sharp contact. The thickness of the alternating band shows variable thickness ranging from less than 1 mm to 15 cm, with increasing upward sequence. The alternating band of sandstone and shale facies indicates fluctuating energy conditions typical of tidal environments [45,52], where periods of high energy (sandstone deposition) alternate with periods of lower energy (shale deposition). In addition, the presence of thinly laminated non-calcareous silty shale deposits also indicates a depositional setting with fluctuating energy levels, as silt particles can settle out of suspension during periods of reduced water flow, characteristic of tidal flats or estuarine environments [63]. Alam et al. [20] and Davies et al. [40], this facies is found in the Baraichari Shale Formation, suggesting transitional environmental conditions.

Flaser laminated sandstone–siltstone lithofacies

Description

Flaser bedding is composed of isolated lenses of mudstone (7–25 cm) within sandy layers. Throughout the research area, and most especially in the lower and upper members of the Boka Bil Formation, this facies predominates. This facies consists of very fine to fine-grained, non–calcareous in nature, yellowish gray, moderate to well-sorted sandstone and siltstone (Fig. 4, Fig. 5a). There is a wavy pattern to the mud flasers. Bed contact is rather sharp and is overlaid by lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone and wavy laminated sandstone-siltstone facies.-Interpretation

-Interpretation

Interpretation

Flaser bedding consists of alternating layers of sandstone and siltstone, indicating fluctuations in energy levels and sediment deposition. This facies typically occurs in lower intertidal to sub-tidal environments where tidal currents influence sediment transport and deposition [5,13,32,27,38]. In addition, the facies is covered with wavy and lenticular bedding, indicating the influence of tidal currents and periodic fluctuations in sedimentation rates. Although this facies typically forms in tidal energy environments [45], but some have also been described as turbidite sediments reworked by contour currents [64].

Wavy laminated sandstone–siltstone–silty shale facies

Description

This facies comprises of light gray to dark gray, very fine to fine-grained, moderate to well sorted, sandstone and siltstone (Fig. 4, Fig. 5e). The contact of the bed is generally more or less sharp, a few millimeters to a few cm thick, typically overlaying ripple, flaser and lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone facies. In lithosuccessions, this facies is typical of the fine-grained upper heterolithic part. Ripple bedding within wavy bedding displays vertical discontinuity and isolation, unlike flaser bedding. As a result of the forward and backward movement of waves, wavy laminations are formed when the underlying ripples or lenses drape over fine-grained sandy silty.-Interpretation

-Interpretation

Interpretation

This lithofacies suggests sedimentation during slack water periods, allowing the deposition of mud alongside sand and silt, a typical occurrence in tidal settings [27,65]. This facies also indicates part of the intertidal zone, where deposition of sand takes place in high tidal conditions followed by a standstill environment resulting in deposition of mud. According to Sarimal & Tahir [66], the wavy laminated facies continuously and repeatedly collects mud and sand sediment via drop and drag processes [67]. In the intertidal flat deposits, this facies is regarded as mixed flat. In some cases, vertical accreted tidal bundles are formed by alternating upward in cyclic patterns of sandstone and siltstone layers [61].

Lenticular laminated sandstone–siltstone facies

Description

This facies is characterized by mud-dominated layers with cross-laminated sand forming lens-like structures. The mudstone/shale is dark bluish-gray in color, hard and compacted, and interbeds with gray to yellowish brown, very fine-grained sandstone and siltstone (Fig. 5b). The lenses are biconvex or starved ripples that were mainly caused by current action and a lack of sediment supply. The Lower Boka Bil Formation, which is rich in mud, has two distinct kinds of lenticular bedding, determined by lens morphology. Among these varieties were lenticular beddings including both connected and isolated lenses. The thickness of the lenses ranges from 0.1 cm to 0.8 cm and the length is between 0.04 cm and 0.30 cm.-Interpretation

-Interpretation

Interpretation

Lenticular laminated sandstone–siltstone facies signifies an extended period of quiescence [45]. During this time, mud deposition occurred, and periodic disturbances by current action led to the deposition of sandstone-siltstone lenses [61,27]. Therefore, the environment of this facies is subtidal zones and intertidal zones where sediments are repeatedly reworked by tidal currents and undergo cycles of erosion and deposition [45,64].

Laminated shale facies

Description

Shale, characterized by its fissility, tends to break into thin layers with splintery in nature. Due to

the parallel orientation of clay mineral flakes within the rock, this breaking pattern is often oriented parallel to the bedding plane. Laminated shale facies is gray to dark gray, moderately hard, parallelly laminated, highly splintery in nature (Fig. 5d). The highly fissile (splinter) gray shale bed exhibits a highly pressured and closely spaced jointed section that is well exposed in the eastern flank of the Sitapahar anticline. Root traces, leaf impressions, burrows and mud cracks are locally present within these facies. This facies dominates over the uppermost part of the Upper Boka Bil Member commonly known as ‘Baraichari’ shale and is deposited over the flaser laminated sandstone–siltstone, wavy laminated sandstone–siltstone–silty shale, and lenticular laminated sandstone–siltstone facies (Fig. 5f and g).-Interpretation

Interpretation

The gray to dark gray laminated shale facies indicates a calm and quiet environment with very slow flow velocity. This type of shale could represent a tidal flat, a marshy area attached to an estuary, or the vertical accretion of channel fill in an estuary [27,44,68,69]. This might be the result of water that has been deposited during the period between high and low tide in the intertidal zone, especially on muddy tidal flats [61,45,70].

Discussion

Subtidal or estuarine facies association

The dominant facies within the subtidal or estuarine facies association are fine-to medium-grained herringbone cross-stratified sandstone, trough cross-stratified sandstone, and ripple cross-stratified sandstone-siltstone. The trough cross-stratified sandstone facies is particularly prominent in this association, specifically in the middle member of the Boka Bil Formation. This facies is overlaid by the alternation of laminated shale, ripple and flaser laminated sandstone-siltstone facies, indicating the gradual decrease of current activity, while changes in sediment size indicate a shift from deep to shallow estuarine deposition within the lithostratigraphic sequence. The occurrence of trough cross-stratified sandstone facies, occasionally containing clay granules, signifies channels (small or large) or estuarine deposition influenced by tides. Additionally, the presence of ripple cross-stratified sandstone-siltstone and herringbone cross-stratified sandstone facies indicates active tidal dynamics, showcasing bidirectional paleocurrent flow (Fig. 4, Fig. 6). Mixed flats with tidal creek facies association

The mixed flats with tidal creek facies association comprises of heterolithic facies (flaser, wavy, and lenticular bedding) and ripple-cross laminated sandstone-siltstone facies. These facies overlap with each other, typically starting with ripple cross-lamination at the bottom, followed by a flaser, a wavy layer, and then a lenticular layer on top. This overlying deposits are interpreted as a mixed flat environment with slight progradation [38]. The heterolithic facies is easily distinguished from other rock layers due to their distinctive ripple units on the sandstone layer, which are part of the facies associations that create a thinning upward sequence (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). This is common in the upper and lower members of the Boka Bil Formation of the study area. Tidal systems, defined by periodic variations in current speed and direction, create sedimentary structures that are indicative of tidal deposition [66]. These structures are generated by the alternating and continuous deposition of sand-wavy layers during tidal currents and mud layers during calm conditions [68]. Tidal flats, which exist between high and low tide levels, are composed of heterolithic facies created by tidal-dominated processes and the gravity of the moon and the sun. A group of researchers categorizes tidal flat profiles into three types: mud flats, mixed flats, and sand flats. The energy is reducing towards the mainland [68,72]. The vertically accreted tidal bundle is composed of a thicker layer of sand and silt, as well as a relatively thinner layer of shale. This distinctive feature, visible within the facies association, strongly suggests the influence of tidal processes [61,27]. Therefore, the heterolithic facies, fining and thinning upward is understood to represent tidal flats, while the ripple cross-laminated sandstone formed within channels is identified as tidal creeks draining the tidal flat [66,73].

Tidal mud flats facies association

Tidal mud flats facies association constitutes lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone facies and mostly laminated shale/mudstone facies. Root traces, leaf impressions, burrows and mud cracks are locally observed within this facies association. Laminated shale and mudstone facies likely formed during periods of minimal current velocity within the lower flow regime, characterized by high levels of suspended sediment [[61], [74], [75]]. These facies are commonly found in calm and quiet tidal flat environments predominantly composed of mud deposits [61,45]. These kinds of facies associations has been deposited during the period between high and low tide in the intertidal zone, especially on muddy tidal flats [61,45,70].

Palaeoflow

Paleocurrent analysis is crucial as it provides insights into both the direction of ancient currents and the paleogeographic configuration of geological formations [27,50]. Paleocurrent data in the Boka Bil Formation were obtained in three lithosections by measuring the azimuth of various facies, including herringbone cross-stratified sandstone, trough cross-stratified sandstone, ripple cross-stratified sandstone-siltstone and lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone facies. The azimuthal data were plotted through the rose diagram and arranged in 30° intervals (Fig. 6). The paleocurrent directions, determined by cross-bedding and ripple orientation, consistently exhibit a bimodal and bipolar pattern, primarily oriented in a NE-SW direction (Fig. 4d and e), with slight variations [32]. The predominant feature of the tidal facies association is the reversal of paleoflow, which is characterized by the prevalence of herringbone cross-stratification within the middle member of the Boka Bil Formation. This reversal exhibits a bipolar orientation which occurs during both the dropping ebb and rising flood periods of the tidal cycle. However, trough cross-stratification is formed by the migration of three-dimensional dunes or bars in unidirectional flow environments, such as river channels or shallow marine settings. The axis of trough cross-stratification aligns with the direction of the dominant flow during sediment deposition. This axis provides a clear indication of the prevailing paleocurrent direction and is typically represented by an unimodal pattern in rose diagrams. Unlike herringbone cross-beds, which show bidirectional flow, trough cross-beds are more indicative of a primary sediment transport direction [48,49,54]. The near absence of N-S-directed paleocurrents, the scarcity of symmetrical ripples, and the absence of wave ripple cross-lamination suggest minimal wave activity and longshore currents during the deposition of the Boka Bil Formation.

The Paleogene Indo-Burmese Range and the Himalayan region are the main sources of sediment for the studied area. From the east of the Shillong Plateau, sediments are also carried by the Paleo-Brahmaputra River (Fig. 7b) [25,27,28]. Along the Sylhet–Meghalaya border, the Miocene marine fan probably reached the Dauki Fault, marking the southern boundary of the Bengal Basin [10,20]. Deformation in the Chittagong Hill Tracts did not occur before Miocene sediment deposition. Facies associations of the Surma Group in the litho-succession reveal channelized to non-channelized marine basin plain deposits, suggesting slope instability likely due to faulting in the CTFB region and along the border of Meghalaya-Sylhet [38]. The litho-succession, paleoflow pattern, and stratigraphic framework of the Boka Bil Formation suggest that the deposition of this formation occurred in tidal flats, mixed flat with tidal creek and estuarine or subtidal environment [27]. The sedimentation and paleochannel flow of the Miocene Surma Group were not influenced by the upliftment of the Shillong Plateau, which occurred after 5 Ma. The sedimentation in the Assam Basin moves southward in a systematic manner, changing from a fluvio-deltaic environment to a deltaic shelf-slope basin plain environment in the Hatia Trough [20]. The depositional environment transitioned from a submarine fan/outer distal lobe marine basin plain to a shallow marine marginal coastal environment during the Boka Bil Formation deposition, driven by regression, uplift, and an increasing sediment influx from the Himalayas over the Bengal Basin to the south.

Facies model and Paleoenvironment of deposition

A facies model provides a conceptual representation of the distinct sedimentary facies and their corresponding sedimentary environments within a geological system [45,48]. This model helps in understanding the characteristics and extent of the elements in each environment. It also provides insights into the physical mechanisms and their depositional heterogeneities [76,77]. To better understand the depositional setting in the studied region, a conceptual facies model is developed using the facies distributions, relationships, and variability of lithological units (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). The model distinguishes between the upper and lower members of the Boka Bil Formations, indicating deposition in tidal mud flats and mixed flats with tidal creek environments. The lithofacies of the Baraichari Shale Formation, which is equal to the Boka Bil Formation, can be classified into three facies associations: subtidal or estuary, mixed flats with tidal creeks, and tidal mud flats. The subtidal or estuarine facies association consists of sandstone with fine to medium grain size, characterized by herringbone cross-stratification, trough cross-stratification, and ripple cross-stratification in sandstone-siltstone layers. The trough cross-stratified sandstone was deposited through the significant bedforms migration on sandy shoals in shallow subtidal deposits close to the inner shelf platform, likely in areas with intense tidal energy, and water depths up to 30m [32,45,58,66]. The presence of an erosional base, reactivation surface, mud draping on foresets, bimodal and bipolar paleoflow directions (NE-SW) within herringbone cross-stratified sandstone and current ripples suggest sediment deposition on tidal sandbars and sub-tidal channels [52,60]. The sandstone and shale facies alternation in the middle Boka Bil Formation suggests varying energy conditions commonly found in tidal environments [45,52], with high energy periods (sandstone deposition) alternating with low energy periods (shale deposition). This facies also indicates transitional environmental conditions [20,40]. The cyclical deposition of sand-rich and mud-rich components reflects the transportation of coarser sediments by tidal currents during periods of ebb or flood, with finer particles settling during stagnant stages at high or low tide [32]. This phenomenon is also observed in various tidal flat environments, both in modern and ancient settings. Mixed flats with tidal creeks facies association comprises heterolithic facies including lenticular, wavy, and flaser laminated sandstone-siltstone, which were found at the exposed sections, particularly in the lower and upper members of the Boka Bil Formation. Flaser bedding is overlain by wavy and lenticular bedding, suggesting a change in energy levels and sedimentation, commonly found in intertidal to sub-tidal zones influenced by tidal currents [5,13,32,27,38]. According to Sarimal & Tahir [66] and Boukhalfa et al. [73], the heterolithic facies with fining and thinning upward is interpreted as tidal flats, while the ripple cross-laminated sandstone seen in channels is recognized as tidal creeks draining the tidal flat. Tidal mud flat facies association is composed of lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone and predominantly mudstone facies/laminated shale. These sedimentary facies formed on muddy tidal flats and other intertidal areas during the time between high and low tide [61,45]. The composite lithologs of the upper and middle members of the Boka Bil Formation show fining upward cycles are known as tidalites. Possible causes for the formation of these cycles include the progressive progradation of the tidal flats or changes in the inner tidal flat conditions during their regressive periods [32,66,72].

According to Alam [32] and Johnson & Alam [19], the current structural shape of the Sitapahar anticline was probably achieved during the fourth Himalayan Orogeny which was deposited in the intertidal environments around the end of the Pliocene, coupled with a regional regression. Banerji [78] proposed that during the early Pliocene, the eastern Bengal Basin, including the CTFB, underwent tectonic changes due to horizontal compressional forces linked to the Himalayan orogeny and the collision of the Eurasian and Indian plates. Consequently, older rocks in the area experienced substantial thrusting and uplifting. In the CTFB, sedimentary deposits, categorized as later molasse sequences, accumulated in a predominantly non-marine fluviodeltaic setting during the Plio-Pleistocene period [79]. Within the Sitapahar anticline, the coarse-grained facies of the Chandraghona Sandstone (Tipam) and Lichubagan Sandstone (Dupi Tila) Formation were deposited over the Baraichari Shale Formation in a continental-fluvial depositional environment.

Conclusions

A comprehensive field study was conducted in the Kaptai area within the Sitapahar anticline, in the south-central part of the Chittagong-Tripura Fold Belt (CTFB) of the Bengal Basin, Bangladesh. A total of eight lithofacies were observed in the study outcrops to develop the conceptual facies model, which has been categorized into three facies associations: subtidal or estuarine, mixed flats with tidal creeks, and tidal mud flats. The presence of an erosional base, reactivation surface, mud draping on foresets, bimodal and bipolar paleoflow directions (NE-SW) within the subtidal or estuarine facies associations clearly suggest tidal sandbars and sub-tidal depositional environment. The alternating band of sandstone and shale/mudstone facies indicates fluctuating energy conditions typical of tidal environments. Mixed flat with tidal creeks facies association is characterized by the presence of ripple cross-laminated sandstone and heterolithic facies such as flaser, wavy, and lenticular laminated sandstone-siltstone. This heterolithic facies, characterized by fining and thinning upward cycles, is interpreted as a tidal flat environment. In contrast, the presence of ripple cross-laminated sandstone within channels signifies tidal creeks flowing through the tidal flat environment. The mud-dominated facies is mainly composed of laminated shale or mudstone with root traces, suggesting deposition during the period between high and low tide in the intertidal zone, particularly on muddy tidal flats. The overall lithofacies distribution and paleocurrent analysis suggest a tide-dominated environment, with sediment primarily sourced from the northern Himalayas, supplemented by contributions from the Indo-Burmese Range. These sediments were transported by the Paleo-Brahmaputra from east of the Shillong Plateau.
