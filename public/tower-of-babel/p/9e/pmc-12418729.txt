Y2Mo4O15:Er3+/Tm3+/Yb3+ Nanophosphors for High-Sensitivity Optical Temperature Sensing
Authors: Nozha Ben Amar, Kamel Saidi, Christian Hernández-Álvarez, Mohamed Dammak, Inocencio R. Martín
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Noncontact optical nanothermometers are increasingly recognized for their high temperature resolution (δT), excellent relative thermal sensitivity (Sr > 1% K–1), rapid response times (t < 0.1 s), and robust long-term optical stability. In this study, upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) based on Y2Mo4O15 nanophosphors, codoped with 2% Er3+, 1% Tm3+, and x% Yb3+ (x = 5, 10, 15 and 20%), were synthesized using the sol–gel method. The crystal structure, morphology, luminescence mechanisms, and temperature-sensing capabilities of these nanoparticles were systematically characterized. Under 975 nm laser excitation, the UCNPs exhibited intense upconversion luminescence, with emission peaks corresponding to well-defined energy-level transitions of Er3+ and Tm3+ ions. Temperature-dependent luminescence spectra were measured over the 300–520 K range using the fluorescence intensity ratio technique. The material exhibits both thermally coupled levels (TCLs) and nonthermally coupled levels, resulting from intraionic and interionic transitions involving Er3+–Er3+, Tm3+–Tm3+, Er3+–Tm3+, and Tm3+–Er3+ interactions. This complex energy-transfer network significantly enhances the temperature-sensing performance. Among the investigated transitions, the intensity ratio I700/I806 derived from the TCL approach showed the highest relative sensitivity, reaching Sr = 2.18% K–1 at 300 K. Additionally, the system achieved a minimum temperature uncertainty of δT = 0.26 K. These findings highlight the superior thermometric performance of the synthesized nanophosphors and underscore their potential for optimization through the synergistic interplay of multiple luminescent centers. This work validates the applicability of these nanomaterials for advanced optical nanothermometry and provides a foundation for developing next-generation temperature nanosensors.

Introduction

Optical nanothermometry has become indispensable in modern technology, offering precise temperature measurement without physical contact and finding utility across various fields, from industrial processes to biomedical research. − This demand for accurate temperature sensing has spurred continuous innovation in luminescent materials and their host matrices. − However, researchers have extensively studied single-functional materials up to this point. , As science and technology advance, there is an increasing need for novel materials to progress toward downsizing, integration, and lightweight characteristics. Consequently, the preparation of versatile materials has significant importance. − Several optical thermometers have been suggested using optical factors that change with temperature, such as wavelength, fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR), and radiation lifetime. − Notably, the FIR approach stands out for its high accuracy and reliability due to its self-referencing capability, which is why it has attracted the most attention and extensive study. ,, Depending on the energy gap between the two pertinent electronic levels, the FIR approach can be grouped into two main types: thermally linked levels (TCLs) and levels without thermal coupling (non-TCLs). − The population distribution between neighboring TCLs precisely follows the Boltzmann distribution, which serves as the theoretical underpinning for temperature detection. As a consequence, the relative sensitivity (Sr), a fundamental measure for measuring thermal sensing capability, is directly in direct correlation with the TCLs’ energy gaps. Nevertheless, Sr of TCLs approach is limited by the confined energy gap (200 cm–1 ≤ ΔE ≤ 2000 cm–1) which is essential to avoid decoupling of TCLs and divergence from the Boltzmann distribution of the excited population. Since greater levels of Sr are theoretically unavailable within this restriction, this fundamental limitation greatly restricts future refinement of the TCL-based FIR method. Different from the TCLs approach, non-TCLs strategy built based on a revolutionary sensing mechanism, has the capacity of overcoming the basic disadvantage to reach a higher Sr. , Thus, much work has been spent to create novel non-TCLs methods. As an illustration, Liao et al. reported an absolute sensitivity peak of 0.94% K–1 at 298 K, obtained from the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) between the nonthermally coupled levels 5F5 and 5F4/5S2 of Ho3+ ions in Lu3NbO7 phosphors codoped with Yb3+ and Ho3+. Chen et al. proposed an nonthermally coupled level (NTCL)-based strategy achieving high relative sensitivity (Si) of 15–20% K–1, using dual emitting centers Cr3+/Ln3+ (Ln = Eu, Tb, Dy) with decreased harmful energy transfer between them. , Although dual-emitting-center systems, such as Cr3+/Ln3+ codoped materials, can exhibit high relative sensitivity (Si), their temperature-sensing performance is often compromised by complex energy transfer processes, which may reduce measurement reliability and reproducibility. In contrast, phosphors based on single activators with well-defined thermally coupled levels (TCLs) provide simpler and more stable FIR behavior, enabling precise and consistent temperature sensing. Furthermore, Chen et al. leveraged the emissions from Eu2+:5d→4f and Eu3+:5D0→7FJ (J = 1, 2, 4) transitions, achieving a significantly improved Si of 4% K–1 through partial reduction of Eu3+ to Eu2+ in YF3 glass ceramics. The high-energy Eu2+:5d emissions in YF3 are particularly suited for nanothermometry in the blue light range. However, these NTCL-based approaches primarily exploit the intrinsic properties of emitting centers without leveraging differences in the host structure, limiting their optimization potential.

To study the advantages of host structural variation, molybdates are an interesting option, as they are crucial inorganic compounds, exhibiting exceptional performance in catalysis, laser technology, and ionic conduction. − Recent research has increasingly delved into exploring the luminescence properties of molybdates doped with rare earth ions. − It is worth highlighting that the optical features of molybdates are quite structure-dependent and may exist in various crystal phases with varied valences of the molybdenum element. Molybdate hosts have emerged as frontrunners, demonstrating remarkable thermal stability and compatibility with lanthanide dopants. , Recently, Er3+/Tm3+/Yb3+ tridoped molybdate have attracted significant interest for their potential in optical temperature sensing and white-light upconversion (UC) emission. , The concentrations of Er3+, Tm3+, and Yb3+ ions significantly influence the optical properties and temperature-sensing performance of molybdate-based phosphors. Yb3+ enhances the energy transfer efficiency, while Er3+ and Tm3+ control emission colors. Optimal doping balances emission intensity and color, prevents quenching, and improves FIR-based temperature sensitivity. Inspired by this, we have examined in our previous work the optical characteristics of 2% Er3+/1% Tm3+/x% Yb3+ doped molybdate NaSrY­(MoO4)3 host based on up-conversion emission, and the thermometric parameters were calculated.

This study focuses on a novel luminescent material, Y2Mo4O15, codoped with Er3+, Tm3+, and Yb3+ ions. By leveraging the characteristic emission properties of lanthanide ions embedded in a molybdate host matrix, the research aims to enhance the current understanding in the field of optical nanothermometry and support its broader implementation in both scientific research and industrial applications. Through comprehensive analysis and characterization, we explore the material’s temperature-sensing performance and assess its suitability for real-world applications.

Synthesis and Characterization

To minimize quenching effects in upconversion luminescence, we carefully selected the dopant concentrations based on our prior experience and literature guidelines. Efficient upconversion systems typically utilize a sensitizer (Yb3+) concentration around 15% and low concentrations of activators (approximately 2%). ,, In this work, Y2Mo4O15 phosphors were synthesized via the sol–gel method with fixed doping levels of 2% Er3+ and 1% Tm3+, while the Yb3+ concentration was varied across 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. The general composition of the samples can be represented as Y2‑x‑0.02–0.01Tm0.01Er0.02Yb x Mo4O15, where x = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20. The starting materials included Y­(NO3)3·6H2O, Tm­(NO3)3·5H2O, Yb­(NO3)3·5H2O, Er­(NO3)3·5H2O, and (NH4)10H2(Mo24O76), all with a purity of 99.99%. Stoichiometric amounts of these reagents were dissolved in 250 mL of deionized water. Citric acid (6 mol) was added as a chelating agent in a 1:3 molar ratio with respect to the total metal ions. Following 2 h of stirring at room temperature, the solution was gradually heated, resulting in the formation of a yellow, wet gel. This gel was subsequently dried at 360 K for 24 h to obtain a xerogel, which was then subjected to annealing at 570 K for 12 h. Finally, the resulting powder was sintered at 970 K for 12 h in air to obtain a pure-phase crystalline material. Figure provides a detailed illustration of the nanophosphor synthesis process.

Schematic representation of applied synthesis technique of 2% Er3+/1% Tm3+/x% Yb3+ tridoped Y2Mo4O15 nanophosphors.

Structural analysis of the powder samples was performed by using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Bruker diffractometer. The morphology of the synthesized phosphors was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using an FEI Quanta 250 FEG instrument. Optical absorption spectra were recorded with a UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 950). Photoluminescence (PL) emission spectra were measured by using a HORIBA Scientific FluoroMax-4P spectrofluorometer. For temperature-dependent emission studies, a Horiba-Jobin Yvon HR 320 spectrometer was employed after placing the samples in a cryostat, allowing measurements in the 300–520 K temperature range. Luminescence decay profiles were recorded using a Hamamatsu R928 photomultiplier tube (PMT), a monochromator, a 200 MHz LeCroy WS424 oscilloscope, and a tunable EKSPLA NT342/3/UVE optical parametric oscillator (OPO) laser with 10 ns pulses at a repetition rate of 10 Hz.

Results and Discussions

Structural and Morphological Characterization

The chemical formula for Y2Mo4O15 fails to accurately depict its structure. It may be expressed more precisely as Y2[MoO4]2[Mo2O7]. This compound is isostructural with Ho2[MoO4]2[Mo2O7], which was initially reported by Efremov et al. The compound Y2Mo4O15 exhibits a monoclinic Bravais lattice, with the space group P21/c (#14) and Z = 2. The unit cell consists of [MoO4]2– and [Mo2O7]2– units that are distinct, with Y3+ ions filling the holes. Yttrium ions hold a single structural spot and are guided by seven oxygen ions. ,

The crystal structure and phase purity of Y2Mo4O15 doped with Er3+, Tm3+, and Yb3+ was confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), as shown in Figure S1. All diffraction peaks matched well with the monoclinic phase (space group P21/c), in accordance with the standard JCPDS card no. 53-0358. Rietveld refinement of the XRD data (Figure a) further verified the structural model, with excellent agreement between the observed and calculated patterns and no evidence of secondary or impurity phases.

It is found that the particles are nonuniform and irregular agglomerated blocks with a smooth particle surface and nanoscale grain size.

To better understand the main UC mechanisms involving the population of the emitting levels of Er3+ and Tm3+ ions, a simplified energy level diagram of Er3+/Tm3+/Yb3+:Y2Mo4O15 is presented in Figure . The luminescence processes principally involve Er3+ and Tm3+ ions, which may be effectively excited under 975 nm irradiation via ground-state absorption (GSA) and excited-state absorption (ESA), resulting in UC emissions. For Tm3+ ions, a two-step energy transfer (ET) process populates the 3H5 and 3F2, levels. Initially, energy is transferred from excited Yb3+ ions in the 2F5/2 state to Tm3+ ions in the ground state, populating the 3H5 level. Subsequently, nonradiative relaxation occurs to the 3F4 level. A second energy transfer from Yb3+ (2F5/2) to Tm3+ (3F4) then promotes electrons to the 3F2, levels. Due to their shorter lifetimes, the 3F2, levels rapidly relax nonradiatively to the longer-lived 3H4 state. Radiative transitions from the 3F2, and 3H4 states to the ground state (3H6) result in red and near-infrared (NIR) emissions around 650 and 806 nm, corresponding to the 3F2,3→ 3H6 and 3H4→3H6 transitions, respectively. For Er3+ ions, excitation initiates when Yb3+ ions absorb photons at 975 nm, causing electronic transitions from the 2F7/2 ground state to the 2F5/2 excited state. Subsequently, energy transfer from Yb3+ (2F5/2) to Er3+ ions populates the 4I11/2 excited level. Electrons in this state rapidly relax nonradiatively to the 4I13/2 level. A further energy transfer from Yb3+ promotes Er3+ ions from the 4I13/2 to the 4I9/2 level, from which fast nonradiative relaxation to the ground state leads to red upconversion emission. Additionally, energy transfer processes can excite Er3+ ions from the 4I11/2 level to the 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 states, which then undergo radiative decay to the ground state, producing green upconversion emission. A detailed description of this mechanism is provided in our previous publication. ,

Temperature Sensing Behavior

To investigate the potential application of optical nanothermometry, emission spectra were recorded in the temperature range of 300–520 K under 975 nm laser excitation, as shown in Figure a. The intensity of all fluorescence emissions exhibits a strong dependence on the temperature. A noticeable color change is observed, particularly in the green emission region. This variation is further analyzed using the chromaticity diagram obtained at different temperatures, as illustrated in Figure b. With the temperature rising from 300 to 520 K, the chromaticity coordinates shift from (x = 0.2576, y = 0.6461) to (x = 0.2118, y = 0.7269). This shift occurs because both the green and red emission intensities diminish as the temperature increases. The emission peaks (I475, I545, I650, I806) decrease with the increase of temperature. The evolution of the intensity of 3H4-3H6 as a function of temperature is described in Figure S5. On the other hand, the red emission at 700 nm (I700) induced by the 3F2,3H6 energy level transition of the Tm3+ ion grows as sample temperature rises. Under thermal excitation, the particle number density distribution of the Tm3+ ion from the 3H4 energy level to the 3F2,3 energy level causes this phenomenon. Furthermore, the emission intensity of the 2H11/2 → 4I15/2 detected at 525 nm increases with the temperature rise. We conclude that 2H11/2 and 2S3/2 are the two thermally coupled levels. The small energy difference (about 800 cm–1) between Er3+TCLs (2H11/2 and 4S3/2) suggests that temperature-induced electrons at the 2H11/2 (Er3+) level may be easily populated from the 4S3/2 (Er3+) level.

The maximum absolute sensitivities found for the 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 thermally coupled levels is 8.26 × 10– 3 K– 1 at 463 K and 1.08 × 10– 5 K– 1 at 520 K for 3F2,3 and 3H4. The maximum absolute sensitivity of the TCL 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 levels is higher than the maximum absolute sensitivity for 3 F2,3 and 3 H4, a fact already expected since the energy difference (ΔE) between the levels 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 is less when compared to those of 3F2,3 and 3 H4. The variation of Sr and Sa with temperature are plotted in Figure c and d. It can be observed that Sa of FIR1 and FIR2 rises with the temperature. Meanwhile, the Sr value decreases with increasing temperature. The maximum values of Sr for FIR1 and FIR2were 1.02% K–1 and 2.18% K–1 at 300 K, respectively. Comparison of Performances of Different Luminescent Thermometers

The temperature uncertainty (δT) is an essential indication of the effectiveness of a recommended high temperature sensor. The temperature uncertainty (δT) may be stated using the following equation: , 11 δT=1SRδFIRFIR where δFIR is the resolution limit relative uncertainty of the thermometric parameter. The effectiveness of the material as determined by Sr, the optical thermal detection and calibration circumstances, especially the signal-to-noise ratio, all affect δT. Using better acquisition techniques and enhancing measurement performance could be one way to increase the resolution limit. By prolonging the integration time, this can be accomplished, and minimizing experimental noise can be achieved by averaging subsequent measurements.

To verify the sensitivity of the detection system, 100 measurements were done at room temperature under similar circumstances for Y2Mo4O15:Er3+/Tm3+/Yb3+ after 975 nm excitation. The values for FIR1, FIR2, FIR3, and FIR4 were 1.24 0.57, 0.31, and 0.53 K, respectively. Conclusion

In conclusion, we successfully synthesized high-performance Y2Mo4O15 nanophosphors triply doped with Er3+, Tm3+, and Yb3+ ions using a citrate-assisted sol–gel method. The resulting nanophosphors exhibited strong emission under 975 nm excitation, with pronounced temperature dependence, confirming their suitability for noncontact optical nanothermometry. Detailed spectroscopic analysis validated efficient energy transfer from Yb3+ to both Er3+ and Tm3+, while time-resolved measurements revealed single-exponential decay behaviors for the 1G4 (Tm3+) and 4S3/2 (Er3+) transitions with lifetimes of 0.018 and 0.013 ms, respectively. By applying the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) approach, we systematically evaluated the temperature sensing capabilities over the range 300–520 K. Among the evaluated transitions, the TCL-based FIR2 (I700/I806) yielded the highest relative sensitivity of 2.18% K–1 at 300 K, while NTCL-based FIR3 (I525/I475) and FIR4 (I700/I650) exhibited superior sensitivity at elevated temperatures. The material achieved outstanding relative sensitivities of 1.02% K–1, 1.33% K–1, and 1.31% K–1 for other transitions, alongside a remarkable minimum temperature uncertainty of just 0.05 at 520 K, positioning it among the most precise UC-based optical thermometers reported to date. These results underscore the synergistic advantage of coactivating multiple lanthanide ions, which enables the exploitation of diverse radiative pathways and energy levels for tunable and highly sensitive temperature sensing. This study confirms the excellent thermometric performance of Y2Mo4O15:Er3+/Tm3+/Yb3+ nanophosphors but also provides a promising route toward next-generation optical temperature nanosensors with broad applicability in microelectronics, biomedical diagnostics, and harsh-environment monitoring.
