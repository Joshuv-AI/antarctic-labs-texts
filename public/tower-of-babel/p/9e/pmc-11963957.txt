Social Risk Profile and Cardiovascular‐Kidney‐Metabolic Syndrome in US Adults
Authors: Jingkuo Li, Lubi Lei, Wei Wang, Wenbo Ding, Yanwu Yu, Boxuan Pu, Yue Peng, Yinchu Li, Lihua Zhang, Yuanlin Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) health is associated with premature mortality and excess morbidity in the United States. Adverse social conditions have a prominent impact on cardiometabolic diseases during the life course. We aim to examine the association between social risk profile (SRP) and CKM multimorbidity among US adults.Methods and ResultsWe used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 1999 to 2018. The definition of CKM syndrome is the coexistence of subclinical or clinical cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and metabolic disorders. We classified participants by 4 CKM stages according to the different clinical severity of different forms of CKM syndrome. We calculated the summed number of positive SRP measures, including employed, high‐income level, food secure, high education attainment, private insurance, owning a house, and married, as SRP scores and classified them into 4 levels by quartiles: low (0–2), lower‐middle (3–4), upper‐middle (5–6), and high (7–8). A total of 18 373 US adults, aged 20 to 79 years, were included in our analyses. There were 2567 (9.4%) participants with low SRP score level. Most individual SRP measures and a combined SRP score were associated with CKM stages. Compared with high SRP score level, low SRP level was associated with higher odds of having CKM stage 1 (odds ratio [OR], 1.34 [95% CI, 1.06–1.70]), CKM stage 2 (OR, 2.03 [95% CI, 1.59–2.58]), CKM stage 3 (OR, 5.28 [95% CI, 3.29–8.47]), and CKM stage 4 (OR, 5.97 [95% CI, 4.20–8.49]).ConclusionsCumulative social disadvantage, denoted by higher SRP burden, was associated with higher odds of CKM multimorbidity, independent of demographic and lifestyle factors.

Poor cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) health is associated with premature mortality and excess morbidity in the United States, imposing a huge social and economic burden. 1 , 2 Beyond the interplay among cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic conditions, environmental, social, and individual factors may influence the development of CKM multimorbidity. 3 , 4 Much of this is related to the concept of social determinants of health, which offers an inclusive framework of the socioeconomic and environmental conditions into which people are born, grow, work, live, and age. 5

Adverse social risk factors enhance the risk of diabetes, 6 cardiovascular disease (CVD), and kidney failure, 7 and have a prominent impact on cardiometabolic diseases during the life course. 8 , 9 Prior studies only focused on individual CKM conditions or the combination of heart and metabolic dysfunction. None of them have characterized CKM multimorbidity in terms of different severity and combinations of metabolic, kidney, and cardiovascular disorders. Recently, the American Heart Association published the presidential advisory for CKM health as well as its staging approach and highlighted the importance of integrating social determinants of health into CKM care. 10 When clinicians failed to acknowledge a patient's social background, they may set goals that are discordant with those of the patient. 11 Screening for social risk factors helps characterize social barriers, identify high‐risk individuals, and contextualize clinical management while facing barriers that clinicians are inadequately equipped to complete. Additionally, the definition and scope of social risk profile (SRP) varied between the studies. Although education, income, and employment were frequently examined in relation to CKM health, the significance of the residential environment and access to health care received limited attention. Investigating the contribution of SRP to stages of CKM syndrome is warranted to promote the screening for SRP and contextualize CKM care in clinical practice.

In this study, we aim to examine the association between SRP and different CKM stages among US adults using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

The data sets generated or analyzed during the current study are publicly available at the NHANES as the government policy stipulates. It can be accessed at http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm. It is not permissible for researchers to make the raw data publicly available at this time. Currently, it is not yet possible for other researchers to apply for access.

Study Population

We used data from 10 NHANES cycles from 1999 to 2018 (downloaded in http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm). NHANES is a national survey with a complex, 4‐stage probability sampling designed to select a sample representative of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population. 12 The NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parents or guardians. 13 A total of 22 149 nonpregnant participants, aged 20 to 79 years, completed NHANES and fasting test in the mobile examination center. We excluded 3176 participants who did not have sufficient information to determine CKM syndrome, 600 with missing value of SRP measures, leading to a final sample of 18 373 participants with available data (Figure S1).

Assessments of SRP

Information was collected by standardized questionnaire through face‐to‐face interviews. According to the Healthy People 2030 five domains of social determinants of health, 14 economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context, we included 8 measures of SRP and dichotomized the factors based on conventional cutoff points and analyses (Table S1): employment (employed, unemployed), income level (measured with the family income/poverty ratio [PIR]: low as PIR <3, high as PIR ≥3), food security (measured with food security survey module questions: 15 secure as 0 affirmative, insecure as ≥1 affirmative), education (low as high school or lower, high as college or above), access to health care (routine place, no routine place), health insurance (private insurance, government, or no insurance), housing instability (own, rent, or other arrangements), and marital status (married, not married). 16

We defined employed, high PIR, food secure, high education attainment, private insurance, owning a house, and married as positive SRP measures, which was detailed in Table S1. The SRP scores were graded according to the number of coexistent positive SRP measures, ranging from 0 to 8. The higher the SRP score was, the better the SRP a participant had. We then classified SRP scores into 4 levels by quartiles: low (0–2), lower‐middle (3–4), upper‐middle (5–6), and high (7–8).

Assessments of CKM Syndrome

Information was collected by standardized questionnaires and physical examination. Blood and random urine samples were analyzed in the central laboratory. We calculated the estimated glomerular filtration rate through the 2021 race‐ and ethnicity‐free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation. 17

The definition of CKM syndrome is the coexistence of subclinical or clinical CVD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic disorders (definitions are detailed in Table S2). 10 Briefly, clinical CVD was defined as any history of chronic heart failure, coronary heart disease, heart attack, or stroke. Subclinical CVD was defined as having ≥20% of 10‐year CVD risk or high‐risk CKD. We calculated the predicted 10‐year CVD risk through the simplified CKM risk algorithm, which incorporates age, sex, tobacco use, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, kidney function, antihypertensive use, and statin use in the model (algorithm is detailed in Table S3). 18 According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes classification, 19 we stratified CKD risks across different thresholds of estimated glomerular filtration rate (<30, 30–44, 45–59, and ≥60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) and urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (<30, 30–299, and ≥300 mg/g). CKD was defined as moderate‐ or high‐risk levels of CKD in this study. 20 Metabolic disorders included overweight/obesity, abdominal obesity, prediabetes, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome.

Considering the different clinical severity of different forms of CKM syndrome, we elected to classify participants by 4 CKM stages: stage 0, all normal conditions; stage 1, only obesity or prediabetes; stage 2, at least 1 of the other metabolic disorders or CKD; stage 3, subclinical CVD with metabolic disorders or CKD; and stage 4, clinical CVD with metabolic disorders or CKD. 10 The detailed stage criteria are provided in Table S4.

Assessments of Other Covariates

Demographic covariates in this study included age, sex (male, female), and race and ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Mexican, other Hispanic, and other races [including Asian or multiracial]). We did not combine Mexican and other Hispanic people in this study because NHANES oversampled Mexican people before 2007 instead of all Hispanic people. 21 Self‐reported lifestyle factors included current smoker (yes, no), current drinker (yes, no, or missing), low physical activity (yes, no), and the Healthy Eating Index‐2010 (HEI‐2010; ranged from 0 to 100 and derived from the participant's 24‐hour dietary recall interview). HEI is a measure of diet quality in terms of conformance to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. 22 , 23 Low physical activity was defined as self‐reported no moderate or vigorous physical activity in a typical week.

Statistical Analysis

According to analytic guidelines published by the National Center for Health Statistics, stratum and primary sampling units were considered for the complex, multistage, probability sampling design. 24 All analyses used examination sample weights, encompassing those who participated in the mobile examination center examination. We described continuous variables using means with weighted 95% CIs and categorical variables using frequency with weighted percentage. We used ANOVA to compare continuous values and adjusted χ2 tests to compare categorical variables grouped by SRP score levels. 25 Given the small amount of missing data for covariates (5.4% for drinking and 3.4% for HEI‐2010 in 22 149 eligible participants), we used the missing data indicator to maximize statistical power rather than delete participants with missing values. The distribution of 3 CKM conditions (ie, CVD, CKD, and metabolic disorders) stratified by 4 SRP score levels was described using a proportional Venn diagram (Figure 1).

Proportional Venn diagrams of cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic conditions by social risk profile (SRP) score levels.

The size of the circles was proportional to the prevalence of metabolic disorders, kidney disease, and cardiovascular diseases in each SRP score levels. SRP was constructed as the summed score (ranging from 0–8) of 8 measures and categorized into 4 levels: low (0–2) (A), lower‐middle (3–4) (B), upper‐middle (5–6) (C), and high (7–8) (D). CKD indicates chronic kidney disease; and CVD, cardiovascular disease.

To examine SRP score levels and individual measures in relation to CKM stages, we used survey multinominal to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs of the association between SRP score levels and CKM stages, with stage 0 as the reference group. Additionally, we evaluated the association of each SRP measure with CKM stages using multinomial logistic regression models. We did not use ordinal logistic regression models because the proportional odds assumption test did not hold the data. We first fitted a crude model that only included SRP, and then we fitted a partially adjusted model that further included demographic covariates (ie, age, sex, and race and ethnicity). Last, we fitted a fully adjusted model that included demographic and lifestyle factors (ie, current smoker, current drinker, HEI‐2010 quantiles [1st‐24th, 25th‐49th, 50th‐74th, 75th‐94th, ≥95th, and missing], and low physical activity). In addition, we examined the associations of SRP with the number of CKM conditions and visualized the ORs using heat maps.

We also conducted several additional analyses to test the robustness of the results. Given that demographics were different in SRP and CKM syndrome, we performed corresponding subgroup analyses: age (20–44, 45–79 years), sex (female, male), and race and ethnicity (White, Black). Because of the disproportionally high prevalence of metabolic disorders, we examined the associations of SRP with subgroups having metabolic disorders only and metabolic‐related multimorbidity (ie, metabolic disorders plus CKD, CVD, or both).

All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Our study included 18 373 participants, aged 20 to 79 years, which was representative of 80 040 625 noninstitutionalized US adults, with an average age of 46.0 (95% CI, 45.6–46.5) years, 51.1% (n=9410) being women and 68.8% (n=7927) being non‐Hispanic White race and ethnicity. Compared with the study sample, participants with incomplete information were more likely to be non‐Hispanic Black race and ethnicity, be current smoker, and have low physical activity and lower HEI‐2010 (Table S5). There were 2567 (9.4%) participants with low SRP score level; 5219 (21.9%) with lower‐middle SRP score level; 5491 (30.1%) with upper‐middle SRP score level; and 5096 (38.5%) with high SRP score level. Compared with participants with high SRP score level, participants with lower SRP score level were younger, more likely to be current smoker, more likely to have lower HEI‐2010, and more likely to have low physical activity. In addition, the prevalence of clinical CVD presented an upward gradient such that clinical CVD prevalence was increasingly higher at lower levels of SRP score (Table 1).

Prevalence and Overlaps of CKM Conditions in Each SRP Level

In overall participants, metabolic disorders were the most prevalent CKM condition (86.6%), followed by CVD (10.3%) and CKD (9.9%). The prevalence of metabolic disorders–CKD‐CVD was 3% to 4% and did not change substantially across SRP score levels. The prevalence of metabolic‐CKD or metabolic‐CVD varied from 5% to 8% and was lowest in high SRP score level. Notably, those who only had either CVD or CKD accounted for <1%. Participants who only had metabolic disorders accounted for the largest proportion in all SRP score levels (67%–73%; Figure 1).

Association Between SRP and CKM

In fully adjusted models (Table 2), compared with high SRP score level, low SRP score level was associated with higher odds of having CKM stage 1 (OR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.06–1.70]), CKM stage 2 (OR, 2.03 [95% CI, 1.59–2.58]), CKM stage 3 (OR, 5.28 [95% CI, 3.29–8.47]), and CKM stage 4 (OR, 5.97 [95% CI, 4.20–8.49]). Most negative measures were also associated with 1.18‐ to 3.50‐fold odds of having CKM stage 2 to 4 versus their positive counterparts, especially unemployment, low PIR, and food insecurity (Table 2). Having a routine source of health care was not significantly associated with CKM stage 1 to 3 but was associated with a lower odds of having CKM stage 4 (OR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.37–0.73]).

Table S6 and Table S7 showed the unadjusted and partially adjusted results. Most associations in unadjusted models were nonsignificant or negative (ie, with a lower odds of CKM stage 1–4). However, those associations were significant or reversed to positive (ie, with a higher odds of CKM stage 1–4) when further adjusted for age, sex, and race and ethnicity. As such, we conducted subgroup analyses by age, sex, and race and ethnicity and reported results in Tables S8–S10. The associations between SRP score levels and CKM stage remained mostly similar to the main analyses. Significant interactions were observed in sex and race and ethnicity subgroups across CKM stage 1 to 4. Women or White individuals with low SRP have a greater odds of having CKM stage 4 than men or Black individuals with low SRP, respectively (women: 11.36 [95% CI, 7.32–17.61]; men: 3.26 [95% CI, 1.89–5.62]; White individuals: 8.11 [95% CI, 4.73–13.93]; Black individuals: 4.12 [95% CI, 2.08–8.16]; all P for interaction <0.05).

Dose‐response manner of associations was also observed in several CKM conditions (Figure 2 and Table S11). Compared with high SRP level, low SRP level was associated with the highest odds of having 3 CKM conditions (OR, 4.91 [95% CI, 3.10–7.77]). In the subgroup of participants with 0 CKM conditions (as reference group) or with metabolic disorders, low SRP level was associated with the highest odds of having metabolic‐CVD (OR, 5.79 [95% CI, 4.12–8.14]; Table S12).

Discussion

By leveraging a large, nationally representative sample of US adults, we observed that lower SRP score level was associated with higher CKM stages. Those who had 0 or only 1 to 2 positive SRP measures had >5 times greater odds of having CKM stage 4 compared with those who had 7 to 8 positive SRP measures. These findings highlight that SRP is a key driver of the marked heterogeneity within CKM syndrome.

This is the first study to examine the association between SRP and the risk of CKM syndrome as well as its stages in US adults. Although a growing body of literature has suggested the impact of poor SRP on adverse metabolic, cardiovascular, or kidney outcomes, the evidence on CKM multimorbidity by different severity is scarce. In the REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study, individuals who lived in poverty areas, lacked health insurance, or had low income and education levels were more likely to develop hypertension as well as albuminuria. 26 , 27 In data from the UK Biobank, lower socioeconomic status (defined by income, education, and employment) was associated with a 65% higher risk of CVD incidence independent of lifestyle. 28 Our study further extended these findings by demonstrating that poor SRP had a predisposition to the coexistence of CKM conditions, especially multimorbidity with CVD. Social determinants of health have been recognized as lifespan determinants of health outcomes. 29 From a biological perspective, historical exposure to adverse socioeconomic conditions was associated with accumulation of excess adipose tissue, 3 , 30 , 31 which is at the core of CKM syndrome. 32 Unmet social needs, economic deprivation, and neighborhood unsafety put individuals under chronic psychosocial stressors and inflammatory status. 33 Additionally, poor SRP forces patients to take living as priorities instead of diseases. 11 , 34 The metabolic‐inflammatory interaction and worse treatment adherence accompanied by biosocioecological pathways results in downstream consequences for diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cancers, and CVD. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 Our results highlight the importance of understanding social risk factors in identifying windows for preventive action during the early stage of CKM syndrome.

We found that individual SRP measures had varied associations with CKM stages. Individuals with unemployed status, lower PIR, lower education, food insecure, without private insurance, or renting home had increased risk of higher CKM stage, which was in line with prior studies. Two studies showed that individuals living in rural areas or food insecure households had a higher risk of hypertension and CVD than those in urban areas or food secure households, 39 , 40 respectively. Other studies also found that individuals with lower education status were associated with adverse kidney outcomes, 41 higher all‐cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality compared with those with higher educational attainment. 42 The mutual promotion among positive measures also contributes to better outcomes. For example, those with higher education have a healthier lifestyle, 43 are less likely to smoke or abuse alcohol, 44 have a lower risk of metabolic dysfunction, 45 have better employment options, and are more likely to receive health care and higher income. 46 Consequently, these individuals are, therefore, more likely to have a better health status. However, those risk factors identified in our study are broadly categorized. A more nuanced dissection of these risk factors may further enhance their representativeness across different sociocultural contexts and promote the incorporation of socially related multidisciplinary support for patients with CKM. Notably, we found that having access to a routine source of health care was significantly associated with lower odds of having CKM stage 4 (ie, clinical CVD‐related multimorbidity). Given that the diagnosis of clinical CVD was self‐reported in our study, individuals without routine health care may be underdiagnosed. We cannot capture the objective information about diagnosis and the temporal order of this information to confirm our hypothesis; therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution. We also found weak or nonsignificant results in those without marital status. A cohort study reported a similar risk of developing CVD between married and unmarried women as well. 47 Whether marital status reflects individuals' interactions with and connectedness to family members could be dependent on demographic and sociocultural factors; therefore, we supposed that the influence of marital status may be confounded or mediated by possible roles of other unmeasured aspects of social support. Further studies should explore the effect by incorporating more informative SRPs to define and measure the social and community context. Taken together, improving education levels, ensuring food security, and alleviating poverty nationwide still take priority in promoting health equity.

Disparities exist between sex subgroups. Women with lower SRP scores showed more pronounced risk of higher CKM stages than men. On the one hand, sex could be an aggregate proxy of social and cultural constructs impacting the health of individuals. Women were less likely to have health insurance from their employers than men, more likely to be nonstandard workers, 48 and more likely to experience mental problems, which contributes to a higher risk of CVD or CKD. 49 , 50 Another analysis suggested that women were more vulnerable to poor SRP, such as low education attainment, low family income, and unemployment, compared with men, which highlights the sex differences in SRP status and its harmful health consequences. 51 On the other hand, female‐specific factors could be risk enhancers for the pathobiology of CKM, such as abnormal sexual hormone levels related to early menopausal transition or polycystic ovary syndrome, or adverse pregnancy outcome. 52 Programs should be implemented to protect vulnerable groups likely to be socially disadvantaged during events that worsen their CKM health. Tailoring and targeting SRP screening for women in clinical settings could optimize CKM care, lower CKM syndrome burden, and subsequently promote health equity in the overall population. Moreover, other subgroups' results should be interpreted with caution because of the limited sample size of younger individuals in CKM stage 3 to 4 and Black individuals.

Our study had several limitations. First, because of the nature of the NHANES survey, the SRP did not include other measures, such as community resources, social support, environmental factors, interpersonal relationships, or genetics. These important factors may also collectively influence the progression of CKM syndrome across the life course. Using established questions to define and measure SRP may not fully comply with the 5 Healthy People 2030 domains. However, we used existing questions available in 10 survey cycles with high response rates to capture the social risk factors according to previous high‐quality studies. Furthermore, we got unexpected results for marital status and health care access, which may be confounded or mediated by possible roles of other unmeasured factors, or lack of objective diagnosis information, and need further investigation. Second, NHANES does not collect longitudinal data on changes in the CKM stages and the related conditions. Third, some CKM indicators were self‐reported, which may lead to misclassification and introduce recall bias in our study. Furthermore, other CVDs, such as atrial fibrillation and peripheral artery disease, were not reported in NHANES, leading to an underestimation of the prevalence of CKM stage 4. Fourth, this study consists of serial cross‐sectional survey data when the demographic structure ahead of time may differ from nowadays in the United States; hence, generalizability may be limited in Asians and other races and ethnicities. Fifth, despite the overall large sample size, groups (eg, younger with CKM stage 3–4, Black individuals) still had limited numbers, leading to sparse data bias. Additionally, individuals with CVD‐CKD, CKD only, or CVD only are rare; therefore, we did not conduct analyses toward these outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study found that a lower SRP score level was associated with higher CKM stages. Our findings emphasize that the SRP is a key driver of the marked heterogeneity within CKM syndrome. Clinicians should provide contextualized care to fit specific social needs. Policy makers and health professionals should promote SRP screening and prioritize socially disadvantaged groups in CKM multimorbidity prevention and management strategies.
