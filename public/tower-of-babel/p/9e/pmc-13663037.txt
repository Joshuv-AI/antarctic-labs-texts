Comparative in vitro cytotoxicity of four commercially available 5% sodium fluoride varnishes in HGF-1 gingival fibroblasts
Authors: Ayse Nur Parlakyildiz Gokce, Mustafa Gokce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study compared in vitro cytotoxicity-related effects of four commercially available 5% sodium fluoride (NaF) varnishes (Enamelast®, Profluorid®, Polimo®, Proshield®) on human gingival fibroblast cells under standardized exposure conditions.MethodsVarnish-derived supernatants were prepared using a standardized extraction approach and applied to HGF-1 cells at six concentrations ranging from 0.0625% to 2% for 24 h. Introduction

Dental caries remains one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, with children being disproportionately affected, thus heightening the need for effective preventive strategies [1]. Fluoride varnish is one of the most commonly used forms of topical fluoride in preventive dentistry because it adheres to enamel and exposed dentin surfaces and prolongs local fluoride contact and availability. Following application, the varnish acts as a temporary fluoride reservoir, promoting remineralization, reducing demineralization under acidic conditions, and supporting the formation of more acid-resistant fluoridated mineral phases [2]. Current evidence-based guidelines recommend application of 5% sodium fluoride (NaF) varnish at least twice annually for the prevention of caries on both primary and permanent dentitions [1, 3]. Because of their controlled application, ease of use, and minimum risk of systemic ingestion, fluoride varnishes represent the only professional fluoride treatment recommended for children under six years of age [4]. In addition to caries prevention, fluoride varnishes are used for the management of dentin hypersensitivity, where they may promote mineral deposition and partial occlusion of exposed dentinal tubules [5].

In pediatric dentistry, fluoride varnishes are frequently applied during caries-preventive visits, early childhood caries management, orthodontic preventive care, and the treatment of newly erupted or partially erupted teeth. During these procedures, particularly in young or less cooperative patients, varnish may spread beyond the intended tooth surface and transiently contact the marginal gingiva, interdental papillae, buccal mucosa, or other adjacent oral soft tissues [6, 7]. Although soft-tissue exposure during routine clinical application is generally expected to be limited and transient, the potential effects of varnish-derived components on gingival cells remain relevant when evaluating product biocompatibility [6]. Accordingly, in vitro assessment of gingival fibroblast responses can provide comparative information regarding the soft-tissue compatibility of different commercial fluoride varnish formulations [8].

Gingival fibroblasts are the predominant cells in gingival connective tissue and are of particular interest to study because they play key roles both in the maintenance of gingival health and in wound healing. Any cytotoxic or inhibitory effect of the varnish components on these cells may impinge on gingival integrity or repair. Furthermore, different commercially available varnish formulations may cause variable biological effects [9, 10]. Certain varnishes such as Duraphat®, Fixofluor®, Fluor Protector S®, and Cervitec F® have been reported to induce greater fibroblast toxicity at equivalent dilutions than others, which may be related to compositional differences among the products, including resin type, additional agents, or calcium/phosphate-containing components [8]. Although varnishes may contain the same nominal fluoride concentration, their full formulations differ in vehicle composition, resin systems, solvents, and additional inactive components, which may influence their biological behavior in vitro.

Although these products share the same labeled active ingredient concentration, commercially available 5% NaF varnishes differ in resin systems, solvents, bioactive additives, and calcium/phosphate-containing components. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the in vitro effects of four commercial 5% NaF varnishes on HGF-1 gingival fibroblast viability, membrane integrity, and migration under standardized exposure conditions. We hypothesized that varnishes with the same nominal NaF concentration would not produce identical biological response profiles because of formulation-level differences.

Materials and methods

Study design

Power analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed using G*Power for the one-way ANOVA comparisons conducted at each concentration. With five groups, a total sample size of 15, α = 0.05, and 80% power, the design was able to detect an omnibus effect size of approximately Cohen’s f = 1.13 or larger. The smallest observed omnibus effect among the primary MTT and LDH analyses was obtained for LDH release at 0.0625% concentration, F(4,10) = 21.77, corresponding to partial η²=0.897 and Cohen’s f = 2.95. Therefore, the study had sufficient power to detect the large formulation-dependent effects observed in the primary cytotoxicity assays.

Cell culture

Human gingival fibroblast cell line (HGF-1)(ATCC® CRL-2014™) was obtained from ATCC® and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, #11965092, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h prior to treatment.

Preparation of varnishes

Due to the limited solubility of varnish materials and the lack of standardized protocols for their direct use in cell culture models, a suspension/extract-based approach was employed to generate culture-compatible supernatants for comparative testing. Enamelast® was used as the benchmark comparator [15, 16]. For each varnish, a 10% (v/v) stock suspension was prepared by adding 1 mL of varnish (gel form) to 9 mL of culture medium (DMEM) in sterile Falcon tubes. The suspensions were vigorously vortexed for homogenization and then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 30 min with intermittent vortexing. After incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged at 3500 rpm (1027×g) for 5 min to remove the insoluble fraction, and the resulting supernatants were transferred to new sterile tubes and passed through a sterile 0.22 μm membrane filter. Serial dilutions were then prepared to obtain six final concentrations (2%, 1%, 0.5%, 0.25%, 0.125%, and 0.0625%). These concentrations were selected to provide a broad, graded exposure range suitable for concentration–response analysis and IC₅₀ estimation across all products under identical in vitro conditions. They represent varnish-derived exposure conditions within the assay system and should not be interpreted as direct equivalents of the clinical fluoride dose at the gingival tissue surface.

MTT assay

The MTT assay was used to determine cell viability based on mitochondrial activity [17]. Viable cells with intact mitochondrial function reduce the tetrazolium salt 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) to insoluble purple formazan crystals via mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase activity. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Prior to drug treatment, the culture medium was removed and replaced with 99 µL DMEM medium. Varnishes were then added to each well (n = 3 per group) to achieve 6 final concentrations of 2%, 1%, 0.5%, 0.25%, 0.125%, and 0.0625%. Untreated cells maintained in culture medium alone served as the negative control in all assays. Following 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium (90 µL), and 10 µL of MTT solution was added. Plates were gently shaken (150 rpm (1.89×g), 2 min) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 3 h. The MTT-containing medium was then removed, and 100 µL DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals by shaking at 150 rpm for 10 min. For each well, absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 570 nm (Agilent BioTek® Synergy H1, USA). Using the MTT assay, IC₅₀ values of the varnishes were calculated. The calculated IC50/2 concentrations were then selected for use in the cell migration assay.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

Cytotoxic effects of the varnish formulations were further evaluated by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using the CyQUANT™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (#C20300, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the previously optimized density and allowed to adhere overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Following treatment with the selected varnish concentrations, cell culture supernatants were collected to assess LDH release. Untreated cells served as the spontaneous LDH release control, while maximum LDH release was obtained by incubating parallel wells with 10× Lysis Buffer for 45 min. Also, Untreated cells maintained in culture medium alone served as the negative control in all assays. Aliquots (50 µL) of culture medium from each condition were transferred to a new flat-bottom 96-well plate, and an equal volume of LDH reaction mixture was added. Plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature protected from light, after which the reaction was terminated by adding Stop Solution. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm with background correction at 680 nm using a microplate reader (Agilent BioTek® Synergy H1, USA). LDH level was calculated by subtracting background absorbance and expressed relative to spontaneous and maximum LDH release controls, with increased extracellular LDH levels indicating compromised membrane integrity and increased cytotoxicity.

Cell migration assay

The effects of Varnishes on cell migration were examined with the CytoSelect® Wound Healing Assay Kit (Cell Biolabs®, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). After the cells were plated in the 24-well plate, images of the area were captured immediately after scratching (0 h) and after 24 h of incubation using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (×10 objective). Untreated control wells were scratched and followed in parallel under identical imaging conditions. For each well, the same field of view was re-located at each time point by using fixed reference points on the plate and stage coordinates, and non-overlapping fields per well were recorded. Image analysis was performed using the “Wound Healing Tool” macro [18] in ImageJ (USA). The percentage of migration was calculated based on wound closure relative to the initial wound area measured immediately after scratching (0 h), and quantified after 24 h. The effect of each varnish on migration was made according to the control group and studied in 3 replicates. The following equation was used to calculate migration: % migration = [(initial wound area − wound area at 24 h) / initial wound area] × 100.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in three independent biological repeats (independent cell passages conducted on different days), and each biological repeat was measured in technical triplicates (three wells per condition). Accordingly, the reported n refers to the number of independent experiments unless otherwise stated, and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9, USA). Model residuals were visually inspected for major deviations from normality and homogeneity of variance. For analyses involving varnish type and concentration, a two-way ANOVA was applied, including the varnish × concentration interaction; when significant effects were detected, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For migration assay comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. IC₅₀ values were estimated by nonlinear regression using a sigmoidal dose–response model, and are reported in the same units as the tested concentrations with corresponding 95% confidence intervals when available. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Two-way ANOVA confirmed significant main effects of varnish type (F(3,48) = 1200.16, p < 0.0001) and concentration (F(5,48) = 1146.23, p < 0.0001), as well as a significant varnish × concentration interaction (F(15,48) = 197.93, p < 0.0001). Tukey post hoc comparisons demonstrated significant between-varnish differences at each tested concentration (Table 2). IC₅₀ values were derived by nonlinear regression of the dose-response curves. Among the tested materials, Polimo® showed the lowest IC₅₀ (0.014 ± 0.001), indicating the strongest adverse response (Fig. 1). In contrast, Enamelast® showed the highest IC₅₀ (0.558 ± 0.075), while Profluorid® and Proshield® showed intermediate IC₅₀ values (0.279 ± 0.054 and 0.174 ± 0.028, respectively).

Discussion

Cytotoxicity and cell viability

MTT assay data showed a concentration-response relationship in reducing HGF-1 cell viability with increasing concentrations of all fluoride varnishes tested. This pattern is consistent with previous in vitro studies suggesting adverse effects of fluoride-containing dental materials on fibroblast metabolism at higher concentrations [8, 19]. In addition, the tested varnishes differed in their cytotoxicity profiles despite sharing the same nominal active fluoride content (5% NaF). These findings are consistent with prior reports indicating that whole-formulation characteristics may contribute to differences in cytotoxicity among commercially available fluoride varnishes [8, 9, 19].

LDH release and membrane integrity

LDH release findings were generally consistent with the MTT results and demonstrated varnish-dependent differences in membrane injury. Because extracellular LDH release reflects loss of cell membrane integrity, this assay provided complementary evidence of cytotoxicity. Previous studies evaluating fluoride varnishes and related dental materials have similarly associated reduced fibroblast viability with increased LDH release [19, 20]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the tested commercial formulations differed not only in their effects on cellular metabolic activity but also in the extent of membrane damage induced under the present in vitro conditions.

Among the tested varnishes, Polimo®, produced the most pronounced LDH response, including at the lowest evaluated concentration of 0.0625%. Although this was the lowest concentration in the experimental dilution series, it remained above the estimated IC₅₀ of Polimo®, (0.014%), supporting the agreement between the MTT and LDH findings. Previous studies have generally demonstrated concentration- and exposure time-dependent cytotoxicity of fluoride varnishes in fibroblasts [10, 21]. Nevertheless, comparative studies have also reported substantial differences among commercial formulations evaluated at equivalent extract concentrations [8, 19]. While relatively preserved fibroblast viability has been reported at the lowest tested concentrations in some studies [21], other commercial formulations have produced more pronounced cytotoxic effects under comparable experimental conditions [8, 19]. Accordingly, the present finding may indicate that components released from Polimo®, or the physicochemical characteristics of its supernatant, affected HGF-1 membrane integrity even under relatively diluted in vitro exposure conditions. However, direct comparison with previous studies is limited by differences in varnish extraction procedures, exposure periods, cell models, and outcome measures.

Effects on fibroblast migration

In the cell migration assay, exposure to IC₅₀/2 concentrations of each fluoride varnish significantly reduced cell migration relative to untreated control cells. This observation is consistent with prior studies reporting that fluoride varnish extracts may impair fibroblast motility in vitro [8, 19]. Because fibroblast migration is important for gingival repair and wound healing, reduced motility may reflect functional cellular stress even when viability is only partially affected. In this context, migration assessment provides a useful complementary endpoint alongside conventional cytotoxicity assays [8, 22]. However, because the absolute concentrations differed substantially among products, direct between-product comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

Formulation-dependent interpretation

In the present study, the four tested 5% NaF varnishes did not produce identical in vitro response profiles in HGF-1 cells under standardized assay conditions. Because all products shared the same nominal active fluoride content but differed as complete commercial formulations, the observed product-dependent differences may be associated with formulation-level factors, including resin systems, ethanol-containing vehicles, rosin-derived components, xylitol, bioactive glass, amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP), and tricalcium phosphate (TCP) (Table 1). Resinous carriers and solvents may influence the composition of the resulting supernatants and potentially affect cellular membrane integrity or mitochondrial activity. Similarly, calcium- and phosphate-containing additives may influence the ionic and physicochemical characteristics of the supernatants. However, because the individual components and ion-release profiles were not separately evaluated, the present study cannot determine which specific component or physicochemical property was responsible for the observed responses. Previous fibroblast-based studies have likewise shown that varnishes containing similar or identical fluoride concentrations may differ in their effects on cell viability and morphology, supporting the interpretation that complete formulation characteristics, rather than fluoride concentration alone, may contribute to product-dependent cytotoxic responses [8, 21].

Clinical relevance and limitations

Although the present findings may be relevant to transient soft-tissue exposure during clinical varnish placement, direct extrapolation to the oral environment should be made cautiously. In vivo exposure is influenced by saliva, dilution, pellicle formation, biofilm, mucosal barriers, and variable contact duration, none of which were modeled in the present assay system. Thus, the current results should be interpreted as comparative in vitro screening data rather than direct predictors of clinical tissue response.

This study has several limitations. First, it used a single immortalized gingival fibroblast cell line, and therefore the results may not fully represent the response of primary gingival fibroblasts or complex oral soft tissues. Second, the study did not include excipient-matched controls, dedicated controls or direct physicochemical characterization of the varnish-derived supernatants, including pH, osmolality, fluoride release, calcium/phosphate release, or resin/solvent release. Therefore, the contribution of individual formulation components cannot be determined. Third, the assay relied on filtered varnish-derived supernatants and a fixed 24-hour exposure period, which do not fully reproduce the dynamic intraoral environment, where saliva, dilution, pellicle formation, mucosal barriers, and variable contact times influence tissue exposure. For descriptive context, ISO 10993-5 considers a viability reduction greater than 30% suggestive of cytotoxic potential. However, because this study was not conducted as a formal ISO-compliant assessment, no formal biocompatibility classification was assigned. Finally, the study was designed as a comparative cytotoxicity screening study and did not include mechanistic analyses of oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis-related signaling, or inflammatory pathways. Therefore, the cellular mechanisms underlying the formulation-dependent responses remain undetermined.

Despite these limitations, the study provides standardized comparative data showing that commercial 5% NaF varnishes do not produce identical biological responses in gingival fibroblasts. These findings support the need for product-specific soft-tissue biocompatibility assessment rather than assuming biological equivalence based only on labeled fluoride concentration. Future studies should integrate physicochemical characterization, primary cell models, oral mucosa-like three-dimensional systems, and clinically simulated exposure protocols.

Conclusion

Under the present in vitro conditions, the four tested commercially available 5% NaF varnishes showed different cytotoxicity-related response profiles in HGF-1 cells. Among the evaluated products, Polimo® showed the strongest adverse response, whereas Enamelast® showed the most favorable profile, with Profluorid® and Proshield® showing intermediate responses.
