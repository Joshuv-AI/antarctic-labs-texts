Trajectories of cumulative fluid balance and the association with pressure injuries in ICU patients
Authors: Xiangping Chen, Peiqi Liu, Bingyan Zhu, Xiumin Qiu, Wei Yu, Yuewen Lao, Xiaoyan Gong, Yiyu Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the longitudinal trajectories of cumulative fluid balance (CFB) in intensive care unit (ICU) patients and analyze the relationship between different trajectory groups and the occurrence of pressure injuries (PIs).MethodsIn this retrospective longitudinal study, we obtained health-related data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database, including sociodemographic, disease-related variables, and ICU treatment variables. The daily CFB adjusted for body weight was calculated, and the occurrence of PIs during the ICU stay was recorded. A group-based trajectory model was used to explore the different CFB trajectories. Binary logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between the CFB trajectory group and PIs.ResultsAmong the 4,294 included participants, we identified four distinct trajectories of CFB in ICU patients: the rapid accumulation group (12.5 %), the slow accumulation group (28.5 %), the neutral balance group (41.7 %), and the negative decrease group (17.3 %). After adjusting for some sociodemographic, disease-related variables, and ICU treatment variables, the rapid accumulation group had an OR of 1.63 (95 %CI: 1.30, 2.04) for all stages of PIs and an OR of 1.36 (95 %CI: 1.08, 1.72) for stage II or higher PIs compared to the neutral balance group.ConclusionsFour unique trajectories of CFB were identified among patients in the ICU, including rapid accumulation, slow accumulation, neutral balance, and negative decrease. Rapid accumulation independently increased the risk of PIs during ICU stay.Keywords: Cumulative fluid balance, Fluid overload, Intensive care unit, Pressure injury, Trajectory

Introduction

Patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) commonly experience excessive fluid accumulation due to fluid resuscitation, continuous intravenous medication, impaired renal function, etc. [1]. An increase in cumulative fluid balance (CFB) of more than 10 % is widely used to define fluid overload [2]. Recent studies have shown that the prevalence of fluid overload is approximately 41.5 % in the ICU patients [3], and excess fluid has been recognized as a risk factor for mortality, acute kidney injury, delirium, and decreased mobility [4,5]. Furthermore, fluid overload may lead to endothelial glycocalyx degradation and increased microvascular permeability [6], which in turn results in acute inflammatory edema [7]. The ensuing tissue edema elevates interstitial hydrostatic pressure, increases mechanical loads on cells and tissues, thereby promoting the development of pressure injuries (PIs) [8].

PIs are a common adverse event in ICU nursing care, affecting 16.9 %–23.8 % of ICU patients [9]. It is associated with several clinical outcomes, including prolonged ICU stay, increased medical costs, and mortality [10]. Despite advances in nursing-led prevention, PIs remain challenging. Multiple body-site risk factors related to PIs, including high pressure, limited mobility, hypoperfusion, and vasoactive medications, were observed [11]. Edema is considered an independent risk factor for PIs in ICU patients [12], suggesting a potential relationship between excess fluid and the occurrence of PIs. However, studies specifically examining the contribution of fluid overload to the development of PIs remain limited.

Previous studies have demonstrated a relationship between CFB within the first 1–5 days of ICU admission and adverse clinical outcomes [13]. However, fluid balance is inherently dynamic and is influenced by ongoing treatments, variability in the timing of fluid de-resuscitation, and individual physiological differences, leading to substantial interpatient variability. As such, single-time-point measurements of CFB fail to capture these fluctuations, thereby contributing to the heterogeneity observed across studies [14]. Furthermore, while CFB may influence skin susceptibility as a potential risk factor, its precise relationship with the development of PIs remains unclear. Trajectory analysis is required to better understand the CFB trends and the possible relationship between CFB trajectories and PIs. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) is a semiparametric approach for analyzing longitudinal data, which assumes that the population follows a discrete distribution and identifies subgroups with unique characteristics [15]. It is an eligible method for identifying distinct trajectories of the CFB in patients in the ICU.

This study aimed to investigate the longitudinal trajectories of CFB in patients in the ICU using the GBTM and analyze the relationship between different trajectory groups and the occurrence of PIs.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective longitudinal study was reported in accordance with The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [16]. The investigated health-related data were obtained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) version 3.1 [17,18], a large, publicly available, de-identified database containing admission records of 94,458 adult ICU patients between 2008 and 2022 at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston. MIMIC-IV includes detailed information on demographics, vital signs, laboratory test results, medications, procedures, and clinical outcomes for ICU patients and has been widely used to support epidemiological and clinical research in critical care. All patients admitted to the ICU were initially considered for inclusion. The first 7 days of ICU stay were selected as the observation window, representing the critical period for fluid resuscitation and de-resuscitation. Only patients with ICU stays longer than 7 days were included to meet the data completeness requirements for trajectory modeling. The exclusion criteria were as follows: not being admitted for the first time in the ICU (to minimize heterogeneity and avoid potential confounding from prior ICU admissions that may affect baseline status, treatment decisions, and outcomes); missing fluid balance records; and the presence of outliers (defined as <25th quartile – 1.5 × interquartile range or >75th quartile + 1.5 × interquartile range). Trajectories of CFB during the first 7 days after ICU admission were constructed and analyzed to determine the relationship between the occurrence of PIs and CFB. Considering the common practice in studies utilizing large databases, the available sample size was determined by the number of patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Therefore, a priori sample size calculation was not applicable. Outliers and missing values were identified during the data screening process and excluded from the final analysis to ensure data quality and accuracy.

Variables

Sociodemographic and disease-related variables

The following covariables were included: 1) baseline characteristic, including age, sex, race, and Body Mass Index (BMI); 2) disease status, including primary disease, pre-admission site, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) score, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score; 3) ICU treatment, including scores for vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS), renal replacement therapy (RRT), mechanical ventilation (MV) duration, coma duration and length of ICU stay (ICU-LOS). The SAPS II is calculated from 17 physiological and clinical variables obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission, encompassing vital signs, laboratory parameters, and major disease status, and is used to assess illness severity and predict hospital mortality [19]. The CCI quantifies the burden of chronic comorbidities by assigning weighted scores (1–6) to 19 specific conditions, such as diabetes, chronic pulmonary disease, renal insufficiency, and malignancy; higher scores indicate a greater cumulative impact of comorbidities on prognosis and long-term mortality risk [20]. The VIS was calculated using the method published by Gaies et al. [21] and validated in adult patients in the ICU [22] to quantify the dosages of commonly used vasoactive and inotropic agents, including dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, vasopressin, and milrinone, and serves as a quantitative indicator of cardiovascular support intensity and the severity of circulatory dysfunction. The MV duration was calculated by dividing the patient’s duration on MV by the overall ICU hospitalization period to reflect the patient’s impaired respiratory function. The number of coma days was calculated based on the daily Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) or Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores. The days with a GCS score less than 9 or a RASS score less than −3 were considered coma days. The coma duration was calculated by dividing the cumulative coma days by the duration of the ICU stay, reflecting the patient’s neurological functional status.

Cumulative fluid balance

Daily CFB was calculated by subtracting total fluid intake from total fluid output for each participant from ICU admission to 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h, and then divided by body weight. Each patient’s total daily fluid intake was extracted from the ingredient events table in the MIMIC-IV database, encompassing medications, nutrition, hydration, and other relevant factors. The total daily fluid output, including urine, drainage, dehydration, etc., was obtained from the Output Events Table in the MIMIC-IV database.

Pressure injuries

PIs were daily assessed and graded according to the international guidelines established by the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (EPUAP), classified as Stage I (non-blanchable erythema of intact skin), Stage II (partial-thickness skin loss), Stage III (full-thickness skin loss), Stage IV (full-thickness tissue loss), along with categories for unstageable and deep tissue PIs [8]. The records of PIs were extracted from the Chart events Table in the MIMIC-IV database.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA) software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and described as means and standard deviations or medians and 25th and 75th percentiles. Their differences were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for data with a normal distribution and the Kruskal-Wallis test for data that did not meet the normality assumption. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the Chi-square test. The GBTM was used to explore the different CFB trajectories in the first 7 days after ICU admission. Stata 17.0 software with the traj plugin was used to construct the trajectories following Nagin’s model selection method [23]. The construction and selection of the GBTM are under the following principles: 1) preset the range of possible trajectories based on relevant knowledge in fluid balance-related studies; 2) determine the number of trajectory groups based on the model parameters previously determined as the group number range; 3) clinical interpretability of the identified trajectory groups was also considered. First, considering that previous studies suggested two to four CFB statuses in patients in the ICU [13,24,25], the maximum number of groups in our study was set to four. Secondly, the model selection process began with a two-group model and increased sequentially until the maximum number of groups was reached. The akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) were calculated to identify the best-fitting model. Finally, the average posterior probability (AvePP) and odds of correct classification (OCC) were calculated for each group. A concise model with the lowest AIC and BIC, AvePP >0.7, OCC >5.0, included more than 5 % of patients in each group, and trajectories with no obvious overlap were selected for analysis [15,23]. Binary logistic regression models were used to analyze the relationship between CFB trajectories during the first 7 days after ICU admission and the occurrence of PIs during the ICU stay. Considering that stage II and above PIs have a more significant adverse effect on patients, the second outcome was defined as the occurrence of stage II or higher PIs (i.e., Stage II, III, IV, unstageable, or deep tissue injuries) during ICU stay. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated for the unadjusted model (Model 0), and for models adjusted for baseline characteristics (Model 1), further adjusted for Model 1 and disease status (Model 2), and Model 2 and ICU treatment variables (Model 3). The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to detect collinearity; a VIF value of less than 10 indicates no serious collinearity. All statistical tests were two-sided, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided P < 0.05 or a 95 % confidence interval (CI) for the ORs excluding 1.

Results

Characteristics of the included participants

Overall, 94,458 ICU admission records were screened for inclusion. After excluding non-first ICU admissions (n = 29,092), ICU stays of less than 7 days (n = 58,015), cases with missing or outlier daily fluid balance data during the first 7 days (n = 3,057), a total of 4,294 patients were included in the final analysis. The median age of the participants was 65.49 years, with a median BMI of 28.54, and 1,778 (41.4 %) were female. Among the included patients, 1,481 (34.5 %) had a primary diagnosis of cardiovascular disease, and the median ICU-LOS was 11.79 days. Detailed characteristics of the participants are provided in Appendix A.

The cumulative fluid balance trajectories of participants

Two- to four-group trajectory models were initially fitted with a fixed polynomial order of 3. The four-group model was identified as the best-fitting model based on the lowest AIC and BIC values and acceptable AvePP, OCC, and group proportions. Polynomial orders were subsequently adjusted to improve model parsimony, and the final model was established with orders of (2, 3, 3, 2). Statistically significant differences were observed in sex (χ2 = 11.307, P = 0.010), weight (H = 101.411, P < 0.001), BMI (H = 108.534, P < 0.001), SAPS II scores (H = 182.868, P < 0.001), CCI scores (H = 13.334, P = 0.004), VIS (H = 145.703, P < 0.001), pre-admission sites (χ2 = 8.946, P = 0.030), primary disease (χ2 = 388.617, P < 0.001), RRT (χ2 = 211.005, P < 0.001), duration of coma (H = 65.436, P < 0.001) and MV (H = 194.343, P < 0.001), ICU-LOS (H = 70.441, P < 0.001), and all stages of PIs (χ2 = 99.086, P < 0.001) among the four trajectory groups. The number and incidence of PIs in the negative decrease group, neutral balance group, slow accumulation group, and rapid accumulation group were 218 (29.4 %), 609 (33.9 %), 500 (41.0 %), and 288 (54.0 %), respectively (P < 0.001).

Association between the cumulative fluid balance trajectories and pressure injuries

Binary logistic regression was used to calculate the ORs for the relationship between different CFB trajectory groups and PIs during the ICU stay. The results using the neutral balance group (Group 2) as the reference are shown in Table 2. In models 0 to 2, the negative decrease group was identified as a protective factor for PIs, with effect sizes ranging from OR = 0.74 (95 %CI: 0.61, 0.91) to OR = 0.81 (95 %CI: 0.68, 0.98). Conversely, patients in the slow accumulation group served as a risk factor for PI, with effect sizes ranging from OR = 1.22 (95 %CI: 1.04, 1.44) to OR = 1.42 (95 %CI: 1.21, 1.65). However, after further adjustment for treatment experience during the ICU stay, the protective effect observed among patients in the negative decrease group and the risk effect of those in the slow accumulation group were not statistically significant (OR = 0.94 (95 %CI: 0.77, 1.16) and 1.16 (95 %CI: 0.99, 1.37), respectively). The rapid accumulation group showed a statistically significant adverse effect for PI in all models. After adjusting for most confounding factors, the effect sizes of the rapid accumulation group were OR = 1.63 (95 %CI: 1.30, 2.04) for all stages of PIs, and OR = 1.36 (95 %CI: 1.08, 1.72) for stage II or higher for PIs. There is no significant collinearity between the independent variables (VIF <10).

Discussion

The clinical characteristics of cumulative fluid balance trajectories

Four distinct fluid balance trajectory groups of ICU patients were identified in our study, including negative decrease, neutral balance, slow accumulation, and rapid accumulation. The CFB of patients in the rapid accumulation group exceeded 10 % from the second day and increased to 20 % after 7 days of ICU admission, which was similar to the previously defined threshold for severe fluid overload [25]. Patients in this group exhibited notable clinical features, including higher SAPS II and VIS scores, increased RRT rates, prolonged MV duration, and a higher proportion of infectious or gastrointestinal diseases. Patients with severe infections requiring ICU admission are at risk of an increased incidence of sepsis [26], a clinical syndrome that commonly necessitates aggressive fluid resuscitation and is frequently complicated by acute kidney injury (AKI), both of which contribute to impaired fluid clearance and accumulation [27]. Similarly, ICU admissions for severe gastrointestinal diseases have been reported in association with intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) [28]. In such cases, elevated intra-abdominal pressure impairs venous return and reduces renal perfusion, further exacerbating fluid accumulation [29]. These pathophysiological mechanisms highlight the crucial role of fluid management in such patients, which has garnered increasing attention in recent years [[30], [31], [32]]. In addition, although more patients in the rapid accumulation group received RRT, fluid continued to accumulate without a downward trend during the first 7 days, suggesting a potential delay or insufficiency in initiating fluid de-resuscitation strategies in clinical practice.

The CFB of patients in the slow accumulation group was between 5 % and 10 %, increasing to 10 %, consistent with the previously defined category of moderate fluid overload [25]. This group of patients is primarily composed of those with tumors or trauma, and they tended to have moderately severe illnesses. Despite the absence of overt signs of circulatory failure, the sustained low-grade fluid accumulation may reflect a subclinical yet persistent inflammatory state and subtle endothelial injury [33]. This pattern may be associated with microcirculatory dysfunction or renal impairment caused by tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) [34], systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [35,36], or compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome (CARS) [35,36]. In our study, the slow accumulation group accounted for 28.5 % of patients, indicating that a substantial proportion of ICU patients may be at risk of insidious fluid accumulation, which is often difficult to identify in clinical practice. This fluid trajectory suggests chronic inflammatory injury and fluid redistribution rather than excessive fluid resuscitation, highlighting that fluid overload can occur even in non-shock patients and merits closer attention.

The CFB trajectory of patients in the neutral balance group was relatively flat, with a value close to 0 %. Previous studies on fluid balance have not agreed on the definition of a normal or reference fluid balance group. Vaara et al. [37] considered patients with CFB <10 % as the reference group in their study. In our research, the CFB range of the neutral balance group was further refined to 0 %–5 %. This group exhibited several distinctive characteristics compared to the fluid accumulation groups, including a higher proportion of cardiovascular disease and slightly lower SAPS II scores. The CFB of the negative decrease group in our study ranged from 0 % to −5 %, which aligns with the classification in a previous study [13]. Patients in this group had the highest proportion of circulatory and respiratory diseases, which aligns with the well-recognized role of fluid management as a core component in treating cardiopulmonary conditions [38,39]. The increased clinical attention to fluid balance in these patients reflects its relevance in preventing volume overload and pulmonary edema. In addition, the proportion of neutral and negative balance groups was 41.7 % and 17.3 % respectively, highlighting that fluid balance may be a modifiable and reversible factor in more than half of patients, with potential implications for optimizing fluid therapy and preventing PIs in ICU patients.

The relationship between fluid balance trajectories and pressure injuries

Edema has been implicated as a risk factor for PIs in several studies involving patients in the ICU [12,40,41]. The underlying mechanisms may involve intracellular or interstitial fluid accumulation [42,43], secondary to ischemia-reperfusion injury [44], impaired lymphatic drainage [43], endothelial glycocalyx degradation [6], and increased microvascular permeability [6]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that inflammatory edema elevates interstitial pressure through fluid extravasation, which amplifies the mechanical stress on the cellular architecture and ultimately induces necrosis in both the parenchymal and stromal components [45]. Given these mechanisms, it is plausible that patients with rapid fluid accumulation are at particularly high risk for PIs due to the compounded effects of systemic inflammation, edema, and microvascular dysfunction. In this study, our findings revealed that rapid accumulation is an independent risk factor for PIs in all logistic regression models. In addition, renal replacement therapy, impaired consciousness, MV, and prolonged ICU stay had been identified as independent risk factors for PIs development [10,46]. After adjusting for confounders, the effect of slow accumulation was not statistically significant. This observed result may be attributed to the distribution of excess fluid, where fluid accumulation in the cardiovascular system or lymphatic tissue could contribute to clinical conditions other than PIs [47,48]. Therefore, patients with fluid retention can be assessed using ultrasound [47] or bioimpedance to evaluate skin hydration status [49].

The effects of negative fluid balance in ICU patients are inconsistently reported across studies, which may be partly attributed to heterogeneity in the selection of reference groups [50,51]. The findings of our study suggest that patients in the negative decrease group did not experience any additional protective effect against the occurrence of PI compared to those in the fluid neutral balance group after adjusting for most confounders. One possible explanation is that our study used the neutral balance trajectory group as a reference instead of the fluid accumulation group. Additionally, the possible insufficient tissue perfusion caused by negative CFB may have offset the potential benefits of negative fluid balance [52]. Another possible explanation is that patients in the negative decrease group generally had lower SAPS II and VIS scores, shorter ICU-LOS, and a shorter duration of coma and MV. These protective factors may have substantially influenced the risk of PIs [41], diminishing the observed effect of fluid balance in the multivariable model. This highlights the multifactorial nature of PI development. Further research is needed to investigate the effects of varying fluid volumes or the timing of achieving a negative fluid balance on the development of PIs.

Further studies are needed to explore the interaction effect between fluid balance trajectories and the risk factors of PIs, as well as the dose-response relationship and mediating effect of skin edema on fluid balance and PIs’ development. Additionally, considering that non-resuscitation fluids constitute a significant component of fluid intake among patients in the ICU [53], nurses and physicians should be aware of the potential risks associated with non-resuscitation fluids in developing PIs. Measures such as reducing maintenance fluids, increasing drug concentration, and substituting oral medications for intravenous administration should be implemented to minimize non-resuscitation fluid intake [54]. These strategies should be incorporated into PI prevention protocols.

Strengths

To our knowledge, this study represents the first to employ the longitudinal trajectory of CFB changes as the dependent variable for predicting PIs’ development, thereby highlighting the dynamic nature of fluid status assessment. It utilizes a substantial cohort of 4,294 unique patient records and constructs multiple multivariable regression models to enhance the generalizability of our findings. These methodological advancements offer novel insights into fluid management and prevention strategies for PIs in the ICU patients.

Limitations

However, our study has certain limitations. First, significant differences in baseline characteristics, disease severity, and ICU treatment variables were observed across the four trajectory groups, which may have influenced both trajectory classification and the development of PIs. Although we adjusted for multiple confounders using multivariable models, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be entirely excluded. Second, as our follow-up period was short, we could not establish a clear causation relationship between cumulative fluid trajectories and PIs; prospective studies with long-term follow-up should be considered. Finally, considering our data were derived from a single clinical center, the identified CFB trajectories must be verified in different clinical settings.

Implications

ICU patients exhibited four unique trajectories of the CFB. Rapid accumulation of fluids increases the risk of PIs. However, most existing PIs’ prediction and screening tools overlook CFB as a relevant risk factor. Enhancing PIs’ prevention strategies requires early identification and subsequent individualized management of patients with rapid fluid accumulation. Further studies are needed to determine the effect of interventions based on CFB management on the incidence of PIs.

Conclusions

Four unique CFB trajectories were identified among ICU patients, including rapid accumulation, slow accumulation, neutral balance, and a negative decrease. Rapid accumulation was independently associated with PIs during the ICU stay. Further prospective studies are needed to explore the predictive value of CFB for adverse events in the ICU and consider incorporating fluid balance as a dynamic indicator in the prediction model for PIs.
