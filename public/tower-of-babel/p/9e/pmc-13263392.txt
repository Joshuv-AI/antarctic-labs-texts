Natural Monoterpene Stylosin Induced Anticancer Effects on Human Colon Carcinoma Cells
Authors: Sara Seyedshazileh, Amirabbas Abdollahi, Mehrdad Iranshahi, Fatemeh B Rassouli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTColorectal carcinoma (CRC) ranks among the top five most common malignancies globally, characterized by a high incidence and poor prognosis. Hepatocyte growth factor receptor (HGFR), a tyrosine kinase oncoprotein, plays a critical role in tumor progression. This study aimed to investigate the effects of stylosin (STL), a natural monoterpene, on human CRC cells, with focus on its potential interactions with HGFR. For computational analysis, the expression of HGFR was assessed in CRC tissues, pharmacokinetics of STL were predicted, and the interaction between STL and HGFR was determined by molecular docking. For experimental studies, STL was isolated from Ferula ovina roots via thin layer chromatography, and its structure was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. LoVo cells were treated with STL at concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 μM for 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h. Cell viability was assessed by alamarBlue, while apoptosis was evaluated by annexin V‐FITC/PI staining followed by flow cytometry analysis. Results demonstrated significant overexpression of HGFR in CRC tissues relative to normal samples. The pharmacokinetic analysis of STL predicted high bioavailability, favorable distribution, and low toxicity risks. Molecular docking predicted a favorable binding affinity of STL to the active site of HGFR. Treatment with STL led to a dose‐dependent decrease in cell viability, with the most pronounced cytotoxic effect observed at 100 μM. This was supported by notable morphological changes and increased apoptosis in treated cells. Collectively, the present findings show anticancer activity of STL in CRC cells, with computational predictions suggesting possible HGFR involvement and favorable pharmacokinetic properties. These results provide initial evidence supporting further investigation and position STL as a potent agent against CRC.Keywords: apoptosis, colorectal carcinoma, hepatocyte growth factor receptor, natural monoterpene, stylosin

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most critical global public health challenges, with the number of new cases and deaths rising each year. Projections indicate a substantial increase in its burden over the next two decades [1]. Notable disparities exist in cancer epidemiology between developing and developed countries; in regions with a higher Human Development Index (HDI), gastrointestinal (GI) cancers are among the most commonly diagnosed [2]. Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) accounts for approximately 11% of all cancer diagnoses and ranks as the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide. The CRC burden is projected to increase by 63% in new cases and 73% in deaths by 2040 compared to 2020 levels [3, 4]. Common risk factors for CRC include genetic predisposition, aging, dietary habits, lifestyle choices, and environmental factors. Additionally, inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis contribute to increased risk [5]. The treatment strategy for CRC depends on the cancer subtype, stage, and the patient's clinical background, and includes surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Surgery is typically the first‐line treatment in early‐stage disease where the tumor has not metastasized, while chemotherapy and radiotherapy are employed to shrink tumors [6, 7]. However, the development of drug resistance and severe side effects remain significant challenges in effective management of CRC [8]. To address the growing crisis posed by CRC to the worldwide healthcare system, development of more effective therapeutic modalities is demanded.

Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) play critical roles in cancer progression primarily through their high‐affinity binding to growth factors and cytokines [9]. Among RTKs, hepatocyte growth factor receptor (HGFR), also known as MET, is integral to the progression of GI cancers [10]. Aberrant HGFR signaling contributes to therapeutic resistance, metastasis, and recurrence of CRC [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Consequently, HGFR has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in CRC. Although numerous tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting RTKs have advanced to clinical trials, many demonstrate limited efficacy in phase II/III studies, either as monotherapy or in combination [17]. Several limitations have been identified as contributing to this lack of efficacy. For instance, acquired resistance frequently develops through secondary mutations in the kinase domain or through activation of alternative bypass signaling pathways. Additionally, emergence of resistant subclones and tumor heterogeneity reduces the durability of response to single‐agent inhibition. Moreover, many existing TKIs exhibit off‐target toxicity due to poor selectivity, limiting their therapeutic window [18, 19]. To date, anti‐HGFR/MET‐directed therapies have not achieved consistent clinical success in GI cancers [10].

Herbal medicines, renowned for their therapeutic benefits, are increasingly utilized as alternatives to conventional chemotherapeutics due to their lower toxicity and greater accessibility [20, 21]. Among natural compounds, monoterpenes—produced by plants as secondary metabolites—exhibit diverse biological activities, including antimicrobial, anti‐inflammatory, and anticancer effects [22, 23]. Stylosin (STL), a monoterpene with valuable pharmaceutical properties, has been isolated from plant species with established ethnopharmacological uses. Ferula stylosa is traditionally used in Iranian folk medicine for its antispasmodic and anticonvulsant properties. The bark of Fraxinus stylosa, referred to as Fraxini Cortex, has been employed in traditional Chinese medicine to treat inflammatory disorders, dysentery, and eye diseases. Ferula ovina is an edible plant widely consumed in Asian countries and used in traditional medicine as a stimulant, expectorant, and vulnerary for respiratory and digestive disorders [24, 25, 26]. Collectively, this rich background underscores the medicinal relevance of STL and supports its further investigation as a potent natural product‐derived therapeutic agent.

Regarding the anticancer potential of STL, this monoterpene induced DNA damage and apoptosis in bladder carcinoma cells at concentrations that spare normal cells [27]. STL also exhibited histone deacetylase inhibitory activity alongside cytotoxic effects in melanoma, ovarian, cervical, and lung carcinoma cells [28, 29]. Additionally, STL‐loaded nanoparticles selectively promoted apoptotic cell death by downregulating BCL‐2 and the angiogenesis‐related genes VEGF and VEGFR in breast carcinoma cells [30]. Furthermore, in glioblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma cells, STL induced apoptosis by increasing reactive oxygen species, upregulating BAX, caspase‐3, caspase‐9, and P53, and downregulating BCL‐2 [31]. Collectively, these reports indicate that STL likely exerts polypharmacological activities. The diverse effects observed across different cancer types suggest that this monoterpene may interact with multiple cellular targets within complex signaling networks. However, due to the limited current knowledge regarding the anticancer effects of STL, it remains to be determined whether its effects converge on a common upstream target or result from independent interactions with distinct molecular targets.

The clinical relevance of HGFR in CRC, combined with the absence of approved small molecules interacting with this protein, underscores the need for novel inhibitor scaffolds. Since many TKIs share rigid polycyclic structures, the flexible, lipophilic backbone of monoterpenes positions them as suitable candidates to be explored in this field. Accordingly, in the present study, STL was selected to investigate whether its terpene scaffold can interact with HGFR and suppress CRC cell growth. In this regard, physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties of STL were predicted in silico, and molecular docking was performed to determine its interaction with HGFR. For experimental studies, STL was extracted from Ferula ovina, and its effects on the viability and apoptosis of LoVo cells were assessed.

Methods

Validating the Expression of HGFR in CRC

To determine the expression level of HGFR in CRC samples compared to normal specimens, GEPIA 2.0 (http://gepia2.cancer‐pku.cn/) and UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html), specialized online tools that utilize data from the TCGA and GTEx databases, were employed. Additionally, to compare the expression of HGFR in CRC samples with normal tissues, the TNMplot database (https://tnmplot.com), which integrates microarray and RNA‐Seq data from GEO, GTEx, TCGA, and TARGET databases, was used. HGFR expression was assessed across 63 human CRC cell lines using the Human Protein Atlas (www.proteinatlas.org). Expression was quantified at the mRNA level as normalized transcripts per million (nTPM), a metric derived from a validated normalization pipeline in which raw RNA‐seq data are converted to protein‐coding TPM values (pTPM). Following Trimmed Mean of M‐values (TMM) normalization and Pareto scaling, cross‐sample comparisons were performed. The nTPM value represents a consensus expression score combining HPA and GTEx datasets.

Evaluating Physicochemical, Druglikeness and Pharmacokinetics of STL

Web‐based tools predicted the physicochemical properties, lipophilicity, aqueous solubility, and drug‐likeness of STL. Drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiles for STL were also predicted using online servers. Additionally, the number of hydrogen bond acceptors (nHA) and donors (nHD), the number of rotatable bonds (nRot), the number of rings (nRing), maximum ring size (MaxRing), flexibility and the presence of stereocenters, along with the topological polar surface area (TPSA) and logarithmic solubility values (logP, logS and logD) were determined. These analyses were performed using ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/service/screening/cal), SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/index.php), and Deep‐PK (https://biosig.lab.uq/deeppk).

Molecular Docking

To provide insight into the binding propensity of STL with HGFR, molecular docking was conducted. The structure of STL was created by ChemInfo (https://www.cheminfo.org/) and NovoPro Bioscience (https://www.novoprolabs.com/), and the crystal structure of HGFR (PDB ID: 3ZZE) was obtained from Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Docking was conducted by Proteins Plus (https://proteins.plus/), which optimized the protonation states and hydrogen coordinates using Protoss to refine the hydrogen bond network. The docking parameters were set to high precision with a site radius of 6.5 Å, and JAMDA was employed as the docking tool. For visualization of the interactions, PoseEdit was used to generate both 2D and 3D diagrams of the complexes, following established chemical drawing conventions [32, 33, 34].

Docking protocol validation was performed by re‐docking the co‐crystallized ligand from the HGFR‐N′‐((3Z)‐4‐chloro‐7‐methyl‐2‐oxo‐1,2‐dihydro‐3H‐indol‐3‐ylidene)‐2‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)propanohydrazide complex (PDB ID: 3ZZE) under identical docking conditions. The re‐docked ligand successfully reproduces the binding orientation observed in the X‐ray crystallographic structure [35]. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) values were computed using LigRMSD v1.0 (https://ligrmsd.appsbio.utalca.cl), yielding a mean RMSD of 0.28 Å for the best‐scoring pose, which had a JAMDA score of −2.67. These results confirm the reliability and accuracy of the docking protocol and parameters employed [36].

Isolation and Characterization of STL

The isolation and characterization of STL were carried out as previously described [37]. Briefly, Ferula ovina was collected from the Binaloud Mountains in northern Iran, and a voucher specimen was deposited at the herbarium of the School of Pharmacy, Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (accession No. 1011). The roots were extracted by maceration with dichloromethane (Merck), and the yielding extract was subjected to column chromatography on silica gel (60 × 5 cm), using a gradient of petroleum ether and increasing proportions of ethyl acetate (from 20:1 to 0:1 v/v). The resulting fractions were monitored by thin layer chromatography on silica gel with petroleum ether‐acetone (Merck) as the mobile phase, and those with similar profiles were combined, affording three main fractions. Fraction 1 was identified as STL, obtained as pure solid crystals (> 95% purity) via column chromatography. STL was characterized as a monoterpene derivative, with its structure confirmed by 1H‐NMR spectroscopy (Table 1).

Cell Treatment and Viability Assay

Human colon adenocarcinoma cells (LoVo) and human normal foreskin fibroblasts (HFF‐3; NCBI Code: C163) were provided from Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran). Both cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma and cross‐contamination, in accordance with standard quality control procedures. LoVo cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute‐1640 medium (Capricorn), while HFF‐3 cells were cultured using Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Invitrogen). Media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), and cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

STL was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Merck) and diluted with complete culture medium to final concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 μM immediately prior to use. To control for any background cytotoxicity from the solvent, 0.3% DMSO was included as the vehicle control in all experiments. Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates (SPL) and treated with STL for 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h. At each time point, cell viability was assessed using alamarBlue. Specifically, alamarBlue reagent (0.1 mg/mL, Sigma) was added to each well (10% v/v), followed by incubation at 37°C for 3 h. Detection of Apoptosis

To elucidate cell apoptosis upon STL treatment, annexin V‐fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) staining was performed. Briefly, upon 96 h treatment with 100 μM STL, cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, the cell pellet was resuspended in binding buffer containing annexin V‐FITC and PI (Sigma), and flow cytometry was performed using FL1‐H and FL2‐H filters (BD FACSCaliber). Finally, results were analyzed by Flow Jo software. Apoptosis assay was performed in three independent experiments.

Statistical Analysis

Significance level was confirmed by one‐way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey multiple comparison test using Graphpad Prism software. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). p values less than 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Given the critical role of HGFR in the development and progression of CRC, its expression was analyzed using multiple platforms. GEPIA2 analysis demonstrated a significant (p < 0.01) upregulation of HGFR in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues from healthy individuals (Figure 1A). Consistently, UALCAN database analysis confirmed overexpression of HGFR in CRC samples compared to matched adjacent normal tissues from patients (Figure 1B). RNA‐sequencing data further supported these findings, showing a highly significant (p = 1.22e‐110) increase in HGFR expression in CRC samples relative to normal samples from non‐cancerous patients (Figure 1C). Additionally, TNMplot analysis of gene chip data revealed significant (p = 6.3e‐99) elevation of HGFR expression in CRC samples relative to non‐paired normal tissues (Figure 1D). Finally, among CRC cell lines, LoVo cells were identified as having high level of HGFR expression at the transcriptional level, with nTPM value of 109.7 (Figure 1E).

The physicochemical properties, druglikeness, and pharmacokinetic profile of STL were predicted computationally, as illustrated in Figure 2 and summarized in Table 2. SwissADME analysis revealed that STL possesses favorable lipophilicity and moderate water solubility, traits beneficial for drug development. Consistently, STL met the druglikeness criteria set by Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge, demonstrating properties commonly associated with successful drug candidates, such as appropriate molecular weight and optimal hydrogen bonding capacity. Furthermore, predictions from ADMETlab and Deep‐PK indicated that STL is not a substrate of P‐glycoprotein, which is often implicated in chemotherapy resistance, and exhibits high intestinal absorption. STL was also predicted to exhibit moderate distribution and serve as a substrate for multiple CYP450 enzymes including CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4. Toxicity assessments across parameters such as AMES mutagenicity, eye corrosion, liver injury, and hERG channel inhibition predicted no significant toxicity concerns associated with STL.

To predict whether STL can interact with HGFR, molecular docking was performed. As shown in Figure 3, STL exhibited a favorable binding affinity with an estimated JAMDA score of −1.51. The interaction involved a hydrogen bond with the Pro1283 residue, a pi‐pi stacking interaction with Tyr1234, and hydrophobic contacts at both Tyr1234 and Ser1236, suggesting a potentially stable and specific binding mode within the HGFR active site.

Molecular docking diagrams generated to predict the interaction of STL with the active site of HGFR. The docking positions in 3D and 2D are illustrated.

To experimentally assess the effects of STL, LoVo cells were treated and evaluated for viability and apoptosis. As presented in Figure 4, significant (p < 0.001) effects were observed after 24 and 48 h, reaching 77% (p < 0.0004) and 66.2% (p < 0.0004) cell viability, respectively. Prolonged treatment up to 72, 96, and 120 h further decreased viability significantly (p < 0.0001), reaching 51.7%, 52.2% and 41.3%, respectively. The calculated IC50 values for STL were 144 μM (95% CI: 127.4–161.7), 128.1 μM (95% CI: 114–155.5), 102.6 μM (95% CI: 91.5–122.7), 104.1 μM (95% CI: 92.1–125.7), and 90.8 μM (95% CI: 84.7–96.9) after 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h, respectively. In addition, viability assay performed on HFF‐3 cells revealed a significant (p < 0.025) decrease following 120 h treatment with 100 μM STL, with cell viability calculated at 40%.

Results of the viability assay were supported by descriptive morphological observations, which revealed fewer attached and viable cells after 96 h treatment with 100 μM STL compared to control groups (Figure 5A). To determine if the cytotoxic effects of STL were due to apoptosis induction, cells treated for 96 h were stained with annexin V‐FITC and PI and analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 5B, 91.4% and 92.6% of cells in untreated and DMSO control groups were viable, respectively. In contrast, only 55.5% of cells remained alive following 100 μM STL treatment, with 8.44% and 29% of cells early and late apoptotic, respectively.

Discussion

CRC ranks among the most common cancers worldwide, with its burden projected to rise to 3.2 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths by 2040, the majority occurring in high or very high HDI countries [3]. Conventional chemotherapy has long played a pivotal role in CRC management; however, nonspecific toxicity and frequent drug resistance significantly limit its long‐term effectiveness. These challenges highlight the need for more specific therapeutic approaches to effectively eradicate CRC cells. RTKs, which play crucial roles in CRC tumorigenesis, have emerged as key therapeutic targets [38]. Currently, only a few targeted therapies are approved for metastatic CRC, including monoclonal antibodies against VEGF and EGFR. These agents, administered alone or in combination with chemotherapy, have significantly improved progression‐free and overall survival rates [39, 40].

Among RTKs, HGFR has gained attention as an important driver of CRC progression. Our gene expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of HGFR in CRC tissues compared to normal controls, corroborating previous findings that link HGFR overexpression with poor prognosis for survival [41]. Preclinical studies suggest that HGFR inhibition can prevent distant recurrence of rectal cancer following preoperative chemoradiotherapy, and that combining HGFR inhibitors with glucose metabolism blockers may enhance the efficacy of angiogenesis inhibitors [42, 43]. Despite these promising experimental results, clinical trials of HGFR inhibitors, whether used alone or in combination with conventional chemotherapy, in GI cancers have largely failed to demonstrate significant benefit [44]. To date, no drugs specifically targeting HGFR have been approved for CRC treatment [10].

Unlike conventional chemotherapy, natural agents often induce apoptosis in cancer cells with fewer systemic side effects, offering a gentler yet effective therapeutic avenue. Several compounds have already been identified as HGFR inhibitors, such as dictamnine from Dictamnus dasycarpus, which suppressed the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and synergistically improved chemosensitivity of HGFR‐TKI‐resistant lung cancer cells [45]. Other examples include withaferin A from Withania somnifera and carnosol from rosemary, both reported to target pancreatic cancer stem cells through HGFR inhibition [46]. Urolithins, derivatives of natural ellagic acid, have also been shown to interact with HGFR, providing a mechanistic basis for their anticancer effects in gastric adenocarcinoma cells [47]. Unlike these natural agents, which belong to structurally rigid classes of alkaloids, diterpenes, and phenolic derivatives, monoterpenes possess a flexible, low‐molecular‐weight backbone with high lipophilicity. These characteristics may offer advantages over rigid inhibitors. Therefore, STL, as a representative monoterpene, was selected to test the hypothesis that a flexible terpene scaffold can interact with HGFR and exert anticancer effects in CRC cells.

Computational analysis revealed elevated expression of HGFR in CRC samples. However, HGFR expression was evaluated only at the transcriptional level, and validating HGFR protein expression in fixed tumor samples represents an essential direction for future work. Molecular docking predicted a favorable binding affinity of STL to the kinase domain of HGFR, suggesting potential interaction with residues Tyr1234 and Ser1236 that are critical for receptor activation [48, 49]. Notably, standard HGFR inhibitors often demonstrate limited clinical efficacy due to acquired resistance, off‐target toxicity, and secondary mutations within the kinase domain [18, 19]. It is therefore hypothesized that the flexible terpene backbone of STL, unlike those of rigid heterocyclic inhibitors, may occupy a distinct sub‐pocket within the kinase domain and could potentially bypass common resistance mechanisms associated with synthetic HGFR inhibitors. Moreover, the high lipophilicity of STL facilitates membrane penetration and interaction with intracellular membrane regions. Nevertheless, the docking results presented here remain computational predictions, as direct experimental evidence of HGFR targeting was not obtained. Incorporating assays that measure HGFR phosphorylation levels following STL treatment, such as immunoprecipitation‐based detection of p‐HGFR, would substantially strengthen these findings by confirming direct receptor targeting. In addition, evaluating key downstream signaling pathways, including AKT, ERK, and STAT3, would help clarify whether STL‐mediated cytotoxicity operates through HGFR‐driven cascades.

Pharmacokinetic predictions suggested that STL has high bioavailability, favorable distribution, and low toxicity risks. Nevertheless, the observed IC50 values leave it uncertain whether these concentrations can be realistically achieved in vivo, as typical plasma levels of other orally administered monoterpenes in preclinical models are often below 100 μM [50]. Strategies including nanoformulation, chemical derivatization, or co‐administration with absorption enhancers may improve the bioavailability and lower effective concentrations of STL to more pharmacologically feasible ranges.

Experimental studies demonstrated the cytotoxic effects of STL against CRC cells, evidenced by reduced viability and induction of apoptosis. However, since STL decreased the viability of normal fibroblasts to a similar extent as in LoVo cells, its selective cytotoxicity should be interpreted with caution. A detailed mechanistic characterization of apoptosis is also warranted. Future studies should assess hallmark apoptotic events, such as PARP cleavage, reactive oxygen species generation, caspase activation, and modulation of BCL‐2 family proteins, to determine whether STL triggers the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway and/or the extrinsic death receptor pathway. Furthermore, to generalize the anticancer activity of STL, additional CRC lines with varying levels of HGFR expression should also be examined.

In conclusion, STL can be considered a natural monoterpene with cytotoxic and apoptosis‐inducing effects in CRC cancer cells. Although docking and predictive analyses suggest possible HGFR interaction and favorable pharmacokinetic properties, these findings should be regarded as preliminary and not yet confirmatory. The present study thus provides an initial foundation for further investigation of STL as a candidate in natural product‐based anticancer strategies.
