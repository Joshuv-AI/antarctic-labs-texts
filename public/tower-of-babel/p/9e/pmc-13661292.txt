Restriction spectrum imaging reveals brain microstructural correlates of obesity risk in early childhood
Authors: Mohammadreza Bayat, Bianca Braun, Madeline Curzon, Melissa L. Hernandez, Marilyn Curtis, Catherine Coccia, Dea Garic, Richard Watts, Mustafa Okan Irfanoglu, Paulo A. Graziano, Anthony Steven Dick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric obesity is a growing public health concern, yet the early neurobiological correlates of obesity risk remain underexplored. Using restriction spectrum imaging (RSI), we examined associations between adiposity and brain microstructure in a cross-sectional sample of 159 children aged 4–7 years, including 81 with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and 78 typically developing (TD) peers. We focused on RSI-derived measures of restricted diffusion—specifically restricted normalized isotropic (RNI) and total (RNT) signals—as indicators of cellular density in reward and salience circuitry. Body mass index (BMI) z-score, percent body fat, waist circumference, and obesity status were assessed. Higher BMI z-score, but not other adiposity measures, was significantly associated with increased RNI in the bilateral anterior insula, nucleus accumbens (NAcc), putamen, pallidum, and hippocampus, as well as increased RNT in the right anterior insula and pallidum. Furthermore, exploratory voxelwise analyses revealed a localized pattern of altered diffusion (increased restricted and decreased hindered signal) in the left hypothalamus. Contrary to hypotheses, ADHD diagnosis did not moderate these associations, suggesting shared neural pathways linking early adiposity and brain structure independent of diagnostic status. Rather than indicating unique biological sensitivity, the specificity of findings to BMI z-score likely reflects measurement constraints of other adiposity indices in this age group. These microstructural patterns are consistent with theories of diet-related neuroinflammation, though alternative neurodevelopmental mechanisms remain plausible. Ultimately, these results demonstrate that neural signatures of obesity risk are detectable as early as preschool age, highlighting a critical developmental window for preventive intervention.

Introduction

Pediatric obesity remains a growing public health concern, with rates rising steadily over the past several decades1. Obesity during childhood is associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including metabolic dysfunction, cardiovascular disease, and psychological distress2,3. Identifying early neurobiological correlates of obesity risk is critical for informing intervention efforts. Indeed, the nucleus accumbens (NAcc), a key region in the brain’s reward system, has been implicated in appetite regulation and associated with weight gain4–7. Recent longitudinal studies by Rapuano et al.7,8 and Adise et al.9–12 reported that increased cellular density in the NAcc and other reward-related regions, as measured via restriction spectrum imaging (RSI), was associated with subsequent weight gain in children.

Building on animal models of diet-induced obesity, Rapuano and colleagues proposed a neuroinflammatory account of this association7,8. In rodents, high-saturated-fat diets elicit reactive gliosis, the proliferation and activation of astrocytes and microglia, within hypothalamic and reward-related regions. This gliosis is accompanied by elevated proinflammatory signaling and disrupted dopaminergic function12. Under this account, peripheral inflammation from the overconsumption of palatable, energy-dense foods crosses the blood–brain barrier to drive glial proliferation in reward circuitry. This proliferation, in turn, alters neural responsivity to food cues and fuels a feed-forward cycle of overeating and weight gain7. Importantly, this model stems largely from animal research, though emerging human imaging and postmortem evidence offers indirect support.

In human imaging studies, RSI diffusion imaging reconstruction may be specifically sensitive to this process of reactive gliosis. Briefly, RSI is a multi-shell diffusion MRI technique that separates the diffusion-weighted signal into compartments reflecting restricted diffusion (water motion confined within cell membranes), hindered diffusion (water navigating the extracellular environment around cells and neurites), and free water diffusion [e.g., cerebrospinal fluid13–16. The restricted compartment, indexed here by the restricted normalized isotropic (RNI), directional (RND), and total (RNT) signals (as opposed to the hindered normalized, or HNT, signal), is sensitive to intracellular density and has been used as a proxy for cellularity in subcortical gray matter in prior pediatric work7,17. RSI metrics do not isolate a single cellular process: increases in restricted diffusion can reflect glial proliferation but may also reflect changes in neuronal density, dendritic arborization, or cell-body size14,17. We return to this point in the Discussion. In addition to the NAcc, the insula has emerged as a region of interest in obesity research due to its role in taste perception and salience processing18–20. Neuroimaging studies show that the insula is reliably activated by palatable food cues, including in children and adults who are overweight or obese21–27. Structural differences in the insula have also been reported in pediatric samples, suggesting this region may contribute to early neural risk for obesity27. However, the microstructural characteristics of the insula and their association with adiposity remain underexplored, particularly in young children.

A parallel line of human work has converged on the hypothalamus as a key region implicated in this pathogenesis28–31. Postmortem histopathology32 and in vivo T2-relaxometry studies33 have documented increased microglial markers in the hypothalamus of adults with obesity. Furthermore, pediatric T2-MRI work links indirect markers of hypothalamic gliosis to BMI z-scores and future adiposity gain in children as young as 9–11 years old [see28,34 for a recent review].

Although Rapuano et al.7,8 and Adise et al.9–12 focused on adolescents, it remains unknown whether there are similar associations between brain microstructure and adiposity earlier in development. Early childhood is a critical period for brain development and the formation of long-term eating behaviors35,36, making it an ideal window for examining brain–adiposity associations before stable patterns of eating and weight regulation are established.

A second motivation for studying this question in early childhood concerns the role of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). There are several theoretical reasons to expect that children with ADHD might be more vulnerable to elevated adiposity and to the microstructural signatures observed in prior RSI work. First, ADHD has been characterized by dysfunction in mesolimbic dopamine pathways that include the NAcc, the same region most strongly implicated in the obesity findings of Rapuano et al.37–39. Second, several behavioral mechanisms could plausibly link the two conditions. Impulsivity is associated with disordered eating patterns, including binge-eating and preference for high-calorie palatable foods40,41. Inattention may reduce sensitivity to internal satiation cues and interfere with maintenance of regular eating patterns40, and difficulties with planning and self-regulation may interfere with sustained engagement in dietary or exercise behaviors that support healthy weight40. Together, these accounts predict that children with ADHD should show both higher rates of obesity and stronger associations between adiposity and reward-related brain microstructure.

The empirical picture, however, is mixed. A meta-analysis by Nigg et al.40 found a small overall ADHD–obesity association (OR ≈ 1.22) that varies considerably by age: more robust in adults (OR ≈ 1.37), and essentially absent in youth (OR ≈ 1.13). Other reviews have reached broadly similar conclusions42,43. The size of the association may therefore be developmentally graded, with shared reward-circuit dysfunction becoming behaviorally consequential only after pubertal changes in weight regulation and the further maturation of mesolimbic circuitry. This pattern raises a question that, to our knowledge, has not been directly addressed: even before the ADHD–obesity association is reliably detectable in behavior, do children with ADHD already differ from typically developing peers in the brain microstructural correlates of adiposity in reward and salience circuitry, including NAcc and insula? If shared reward-salience circuit dysfunction does eventually contribute to elevated obesity risk in ADHD, then microstructural differences in these regions might be visible earlier than behavioral differences. Conversely, if these brain–adiposity associations are diagnosis-independent in early childhood, that would suggest a common early neurobiological pathway that is later modulated by additional ADHD-related factors (e.g., impulsivity, mood lability, comorbidities) as children mature.

To address these gaps, we conducted a cross-sectional replication study in a younger cohort (ages 4–7) comprising both typically developing (TD) children and children with ADHD. Using RSI, we examined whether cellular density in the NAcc, the insula, and other subcortical regions (including the hypothalamus) was associated with multiple anthropometric indicators: age- and sex-standardized BMI z-score, waist circumference, percent body fat, and obesity status. Our aims were to (1) determine whether previously reported associations between NAcc microstructure and weight outcomes extend to early childhood, (2) assess whether they also manifest in the insula, and (3) explore whether ADHD moderates these relationships.

Based on prior findings, we hypothesized that increased cellular density in the NAcc, the anterior insula, and broader reward circuitry would be associated with greater adiposity across all measures. Given the theoretical reasons described above to expect amplified brain–adiposity associations in children with ADHD, alongside meta-analytic evidence that the behavioral ADHD–obesity link is small or absent in children, we treated the moderation question as exploratory. Specifically, we tested whether an ADHD diagnosis moderated these neural associations. Our directional prediction was that effects would be stronger in children with ADHD, though we acknowledge that diagnosis-independent relationships were also plausible and would themselves be informative. This study contributes to the growing literature on the neural correlates of obesity risk in early childhood and extends prior RSI findings to a more developmentally sensitive window.

Method and analysis

Participants

The substudy was conducted at a university in the southeastern United States with a large Hispanic/Latino population as part of a larger clinical intervention study for ADHD43. The larger study included functional imaging measures that are not analyzed here. For the substudy, following data cleaning, 159 children had 1) usable diffusion-weighted and T1-weighted MRI scans (17 children were excluded due to excessive movement in the scanner; see Movement section), and 2) completed at least one anthropometric measure outside the MRI session. Of these, 81 children received a diagnosis of ADHD (see Demographics of the Sample section). Some missing data were present due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which resulted in data loss for anthropometric measures requiring close personal contact. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation (see Missing Data section).

Recruitment and eligibility requirements. Children and their caregivers were recruited from local schools, open houses/parent workshops, mental health agencies, and radio and newspaper ads. Exclusionary criteria for the children included intellectual disability (IQ lower than 70 on the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence 4th edition; WPPSI-IV44), confirmed history of autism spectrum disorder and/or Autism Spectrum Rating Scales Short Form score below clinical cutoff T-score of 6045, and currently or previously taking psychotropic medication, including children who have been medicated for ADHD (all included children were medication naive, the reason being that behavior therapy is typically recommended before age 6). Parents/guardians provided informed written consent, and children in the study supplied verbal assent to participate. The study was reviewed and approved by the Florida International University Institutional Review Board (# IRB-14-0158-CR10 and IRB-18-0258-AM03). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Demographics of the Sample. Within this sub-sample, there were 78 TD children and 81 children who were diagnosed with ADHD. ADHD diagnosis was accomplished through a combination of parent structured interview using the Computerized-Diagnostic Interview Schedule for Children (C-DISC46). The C-DISC is a highly structured, computerized diagnostic interview which can be used to assess over 30 different psychiatric disorders, including ADHD. It consists of a series of questions which, after a minimal training period, can be administered by “lay” interviewers, such as teachers and parents. In addition, we used parent and teacher ratings of symptoms and impairment using the Disruptive Behavior Disorders Rating Scale (DBD47;), which involves teacher and parent reporting of symptoms of ADHD, including hyperactivity and impulsivity47, and the Impairment Rating Scales48. Children were classified as ADHD if raters endorsed clinically significant levels of ADHD symptoms (at least six symptoms of either Inattention or Hyperactivity/Impulsivity according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM 549;) or a previous diagnosis of ADHD), indicated that the child is currently displaying clinically significant academic, behavioral, and/or social impairments as measured by a score of at least 3 on a 7-point impairment rating scale. Dual Ph.D. level clinician review was used to verify diagnosis, supervised by P.G. Body composition measures

Four anthropometric measures were calculated: body mass index (BMI), converted to z-score, percent body fat, waist circumference, and obesity status (dichotomous). Child height was measured (without shoes) to the nearest .01 cm using a wall-mounted stadiometer (Seca, Columbia, MD). The average of three height measurements was used for analyses. A Mediana i35 Body Composition Analyzer provided information regarding overall weight (to the nearest .01 kg) and estimated body composition (i.e., percent body fat) via bioelectric impedance analysis. BMI was calculated using the height entered and the measured weight by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. BMI percentile and the BMI z-score were calculated based on the age and sex norms of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Center for Health Statistics52. Waist circumference was measured with a flexible tailor tape measure (in cm to the nearest 0.10 cm). Body composition variables (i.e., waist circumference, BMI z-score and body fat percentage) were examined as continuous measures while overweight/obese BMI status was examined as a categorical variable at the 85th %ile per CDC BMI-for-age percentile cutoff scores52.

Image acquisition

All imaging was performed using a research-dedicated 3-T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanner (V11C) with a 32-channel coil located on the university campus. Children first completed a preparatory phase using a realistic mock scanner in the room across the hall from the magnet. Here, they were trained to stay still and were also acclimated to the enclosed space of the magnet, to the back projection visual presentation system and to the scanner noises (in this case, presented with headphones). When they were properly trained and acclimated, they were moved to the magnet. In the magnet, during the structural scans, children watched a child-friendly movie of their choice. Sound was presented through MRI-compatible headphones that also functioned as additional ear protection.

T1-weighted and DWI scans were acquired for each participant. T1-weighted MRI scans were collected using a 3D T1-weighted inversion prepared RF-spoiled gradient echo sequence (axial; TR/TE 2500/2.88; 1 x 1 x 1 mm, 7 min 12 s acquisition time) with prospective motion correction (Siemens vNav53;), according to the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) protocol54.

Movement artifacts pose challenges for T1-weighted images, which affects registration in the diffusion scan. Each T1 image was thoroughly reviewed by A.S.D. We applied a visual rating system ranging from ’poor = 1’ to ’excellent = 4’ for each T1-weighted image, with half-point allowances (e.g., 3.5). Most images were in the 3-4 range, with an average image rating of 3.54 (SD = 0.63). The ADHD and TD groups did not differ on image quality, t(144.7) = 1.11, p = 0.27.

DWI scans were acquired via high-angular-resolution diffusion imaging (HARDI) with multiband acceleration factor 3 (EPI acquisition; TR/TE = 4100/88 ms; 1.7 x 1.7 x 1.7 mm; 81 slices no gap; 96 diffusion directions plus 6 b = 0 s/mm2: b = 0 s/mm2 [6 dirs], b = 500 s/mm2 [6-dirs], 1000 s/mm2 [15-dirs], 2000 s/mm2 [15-dirs] and 3000 s/mm2 [60-dirs] s/mm2; A-to-P direction [7 m 31 s acquisition time]). A second brief scan at b = 0 s/mm2 in P-to-A direction was acquired to help deal with susceptibility artifacts.

Image Post-Processing. DWI preprocessing was performed using a number of complementary software suites. The steps were as follows: 1) DWI denoising55,56 and Gibbs ringing Correction56,57; 2) Inter-volume motion and eddy-currents distortion correction57,58 combined with volume-to-slice registration and outlier detection/replacement59,60 as implemented in the TORTOISEV4 package58; 3) creation of a synthesized T2–weighted image from the T1–weighted scan using Synb0–DisCo61,62; 4) correction of spatial and intensity distortions caused by B0 field inhomogeneity, using TORTOISE DRBUDDI63 implementing blip-up/blip-down distortion correction64–67. This step uses both the reverse–phase encoded b = 0 s/mm2 image for the estimation of the field map, and the synthesized T2–weighted image for imposition of geometry constraints; 5) gradient non–linearity correction (gradwarp) using the gradient coefficients supplied by Siemens68–70. Finally, the fully preprocessed images (corrected for outliers, motion, eddy currents, B0–induced distortions, and gradient non–linearities) were resampled to the T1–weighted resolution (to 1 mm3) and registered to the T1–weighted image in a single interpolation step. For whole-brain analysis, data are warped to the ABCD atlas space, and reported in LPS atlas coordinates (L = +; P = +; S = +), which are derived from the DICOM coordinate system. For ROI analysis, cortical surface and subcortical segmentation images of each T1 were reconstructed using Freesurfer71–73.

Movement

Participant movement has a substantial effect on DWI measurements, and can introduce spurious group differences in cases where none are present in the biological tissue, and it is recommended to incorporate movement as a nuisance regressor74. To do this, we estimated movement using the root mean square (RMS) output from FSL eddy and implemented an overall movement cutoff for inclusion in analysis. This was arbitrarily defined as average RMS movement of more than one voxel (1.7 mm) over the course of the scan. In the subsample, 17 children (7.9%) were removed for excessive movement (9 children with ADHD, and 8 TD children).

Restriction spectrum imaging

This study leveraged a multi-shell HARDI acquisition to implement Restriction Spectrum Imaging (RSI)13–16, reconstructed using custom Matlab routines17,54. RSI allows for voxelwise estimation of the relative contributions of different diffusion components: restricted, hindered, and free water diffusion. These three compartments reflect the underlying microstructural environment. Free water, such as that found in cerebrospinal fluid, diffuses with minimal impedance. In contrast, biological tissue predominantly exhibits restricted and hindered diffusion75. Hindered diffusion refers to water movement that is obstructed by cellular structures such as glial cells and neurites, typically following a Gaussian displacement profile. The signal arises from compartments larger than the diffusion length (about 10 μm), influenced by how far water molecules must travel to circumvent structural obstacles. Restricted diffusion, in contrast, arises largely from cellular membranes and reflects molecular motion confined within tight cellular boundaries. Over longer diffusion times (Δ), the distinction between restricted and hindered diffusion becomes more pronounced13. The imaging parameters used here were selected to maximize sensitivity to these different types of diffusion behavior.

RSI enables the extraction of quantitative indices that reflect signal contributions from each diffusion compartment17. These indices are normalized to yield signal fractions, allowing for comparisons of restricted, hindered, and free water diffusion across voxels. In this study, we focused on four such metrics: the Restricted Normalized Directional signal (RND), the Restricted Normalized Isotropic signal (RNI), the Restricted Normalized Total signal (RNT), and the Hindered Normalized Total signal (HNT).

In the RSI model, restricted and hindered diffusion compartments are represented using fourth-order spherical harmonics, while free water diffusion is captured with zeroth-order harmonics. The axial diffusivity for restricted and hindered compartments is fixed at 1 × 10-3 mm2/s. Radial diffusivity is set to 0 mm2/s for restricted diffusion and to 0.9 × 10-3 mm2/s for hindered diffusion. Free water is modeled isotropically with a diffusivity of 3 × 10-3 mm2/s. Spherical deconvolution is used to estimate the fiber orientation distribution (FOD) in each voxel’s restricted compartment. RND is computed from the norm of the second and fourth-order harmonic coefficients, representing anisotropic diffusion across directions. RNI is derived from the spherical mean of the FOD, representing isotropic restricted diffusion. Together, RND and RNI sum to RNT. Further methodological details are available in previous publications13–17.

The RNT and HNT metrics, along with their components RNI and RND, serve as proxies for microstructural processes related to cellular and neurite development. These metrics reflect features such as myelination, dendritic arborization, and changes in neurite or cell body diameter or density, as well as increased concentrations of mature astrocytes. RND is thought to reflect anisotropic restricted diffusion associated with oriented structures like axons and dendrites, while RNI captures isotropic restricted diffusion, which may be linked to processes such as glial cell proliferation or changes in cell body structure. Prior research using similar acquisition parameters in children and adolescents17,50 has demonstrated that RNT and HNT reliably distinguish between gray and white matter and show age-related variation in both tissue types. RNT and HNT tend to be inversely related—for example, increased myelination may elevate restricted diffusion (via reduced axonal membrane permeability) while decreasing hindered diffusion (due to reduced extracellular space). However, these relationships are modulated by tissue-specific characteristics and the distribution of diffusion signals across compartments within each voxel.

Data analysis

Missing data

In the final analyzed sample, a subset of anthropometric data was missing, including 13.8% of waist circumference values and 12.6% of percent body fat values. To address this, for the ROI analysis, we performed multiple imputation using fully conditional specification (FCS76;) as implemented in the Best Linear IMPutation (BLIMP) software77. Fifty imputed data sets were defined. Convergence diagnostics indicated acceptable model performance, with potential scale reduction (PSR) values below 1.05 across chains, suggesting stable and replicable imputations. Parameter estimates were pooled according to Rubin’s rules78,79, using the mitml package in R80.

Statistical models

We conducted the statistical analysis using both regions of interest (ROI)-based analysis, and a voxelwise wholebrain analysis using a fast and efficient mixed-effects algorithm (FEMA81;). Ordinary least squares linear models were specified for both ROI and voxelwise modeling. To be consistent across analyses, the diffusion metric (i.e., RNT, RNI, RND, HNT) was specified as the outcome, the anthropometric measure was specified as the main predictor, the diagnostic group was specified as the moderator, and additional covariates (age, sex, parent education, and movement) were added. If the moderating effect was not significant, we dropped the interaction term to simplify the model. Regions of interest analysis

We identified the eight regions of interest from Rapuano and colleagues7 averaged across each hemisphere: NAcc, putamen, caudate, pallidum, ventral diencephalon, thalamus, amygdala, and hippocampus. We additionally added left and right anterior insula, based on our own review of the literature. We identified these regions anatomically on individual cortical surfaces using the automatic FreeSurfer parcellation71–73, based on the anatomical conventions of Duvernoy et al.82. These were corrected for multiple comparisons using False Discovery Rate correction (FDR83;) across all ROIs within a measure.

For each region for each measure (RNI, RND, RNT, HNT) we applied the statistical model, adjusting the standard error estimates according to Rubin’s rules as described above. In each iteration, we checked whether the interaction effect was statistically significant after FDR correction (Model 1). If not, Model 2 was run dropping the interaction term.

Fast and efficient mixed-effects algorithm (FEMA) analysis

We performed a supplemental voxelwise analysis using FEMA on the specified models (note, despite the name, ordinary least squares, not mixed-effects models, were run). The use of FEMA facilitates use of the ABCD atlas space, which maintains consistency with analyses by Rapuano and colleagues7. The design matrix is constructed identically to that of the ROI analysis. In addition, the whole-brain analysis was masked using the ROIs applied in the ROI analysis.. Discussion

This study examined how childhood adiposity relates to brain microstructure using RSI metrics in a sample of very young children (ages 4–7 years), including children with and without ADHD. Across four anthropometric measures (BMI z-score, waist circumference, percent body fat, and categorical obesity status) we found that higher BMI z-score was the only measure significantly associated with differences in RSI indices of brain microstructure. Specifically, higher BMI z-score was associated with increased RNI in six regions: bilateral anterior insula, NAcc, putamen, pallidum, and hippocampus, and with increased RNT in right anterior insula and pallidum. Our primary hypotheses regarding associations between reward circuitry RSI indices with adiposity were partially supported, as these relationships were observed only for BMI z-score and not for waist circumference, percent body fat, or obesity status. Contrary to expectations, ADHD diagnosis did not moderate these associations; relations among BMI z-score and RSI indices were similar across ADHD and TD groups. We discuss these findings in turn below.

Early associations of adiposity with subcortical and insular RSI metrics: evidence for a neuroinflammatory mechanism

Our results replicate and extend the foundational work of Rapuano and colleagues7,8 and Adise and colleagues9,10, who reported associations between adiposity measures and RNI within subcortical regions, particularly the NAcc, in older children (adolescents in the ABCD study). In our younger sample, the association between BMI z-score and RNI in the NAcc and other basal ganglia structures aligns with these previous findings, especially with the recent report by Adise and colleagues9,10, supporting the possibility that this brain–adiposity association is detectable as early as 4–7 years of age. However, in contrast to prior work in older children, we did not find significant associations with waist circumference, percent body fat, or categorical obesity status, and we want to be careful about the interpretation of these null findings.

We did not statistically compare effect sizes across measures, and therefore cannot conclude from this single study that BMI z-score is uniquely or even most sensitive to brain microstructural correlates of adiposity in young children. A more defensible interpretation is that several factors particular to this developmental window can each plausibly account for the null findings on the other measures. Waist circumference and BIA-derived percent body fat, an estimate of body composition rather than a direct criterion measurement84,85, carry higher measurement error in pre-school-aged children due to compliance, posture, hydration, and recent food intake86, and consumer-grade bioelectric-impedance devices have not been extensively validated in this age range. Findings involving percent body fat should therefore be interpreted cautiously given these constraints. The categorical obesity outcome is constrained by low base rates (∼17% of our sample met the CDC threshold), limiting power compared to the continuous indices. Furthermore, in pre-school-aged children, waist circumference and percent body fat have not yet diverged substantially from BMI as indicators of trunk versus total adiposity. Differential utility likely emerges later as body composition becomes more variable7. In sum, that only BMI z-score significantly tracked brain microstructure here is most parsimoniously interpreted as reflecting these measurement and developmental considerations, rather than as evidence that BMI z-score uniquely indexes obesity-related neurobiology. Replication with measurement protocols optimized for very young children, and in samples with higher obesity prevalence, will be important.

A key novel contribution of our study is the identification of the insula as a significant cortical region associated with obesity-related microstructural differences, a finding not highlighted in previous RSI research. The insula plays a central role in taste perception and salience processing18–20, and functional imaging consistently demonstrates increased insular responses to palatable food signals among adults and children with obesity21–25. Our findings reveal increased RNI diffusion bilaterally in the anterior insula, alongside increased RNT in the right anterior insula. This suggests that structural microarchitectural differences may accompany these functional patterns, though our cross-sectional design precludes establishing causality or directionality. Crucially, while RSI metrics are sensitive to cellular density, they are not specific to any single cellular process7. Increases in isotropic restricted diffusion can reflect glial proliferation, but they can equally arise from changes in neuronal density, soma size, dendritic arborization, synaptic remodeling, or other intracellular features14,17,50. Recent reviews of the broader human MRI literature on obesity-related neuroinflammation emphasize this same nonspecificity, noting that diffusion-based markers may reflect several parallel tissue-level changes, including altered extracellular structures and shifts in the relative proportions of glial cell types28. Because in vivo validation of RSI as a specific index of human neuroinflammation remains an active area of research, we accordingly treat the inflammatory interpretation as one plausible account among several.

A compelling, highly localized pattern emerged within the territory of the hypothalamus. In the most posterior slices of the ventral diencephalon (vDC), voxelwise FEMA maps revealed left-lateralized clusters of increased RNI and RNT, alongside decreased HNT (Figs. 4 and 5). This restricted-up/hindered-down pattern is the canonical RSI signature of increased cellularity7, and its left-sided localization aligns broadly with the human imaging literature on obesity-related hypothalamic gliosis28. We note that this effect was not captured by our broad ROI analysis (β = 0.08 for BMI z-score on RNI; all FDR >.25), and this null ROI result is possibly due to signal dilution. The composite FreeSurfer vDC label averages signal across numerous distinct structures, easily obscuring a unilateral, subregional effect. While this exploratory voxelwise observation remains hypothesis-generating, its striking consistency with the adult and older-child literature warrants attention. Confirming this specific hypothalamic effect in early childhood using targeted segmentation with a specific hypothalamus ROI is a clear priority for future work.

A finding not anticipated by our hypotheses, and not strongly emphasized in the prior RSI–obesity literature, is the appearance of the hippocampus as a region significantly associated with BMI z-score on RNI (β = 0.21, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{FDR}_p$$\end{document} = .03). Rapuano et al.7 reported a weaker hippocampal effect for waist circumference that did not survive Bonferroni correction, and the hippocampus was not highlighted as a primary region of interest in their model or in Adise et al.9,10. The signal in our younger cohort therefore warrants tentative interpretation. While not classically part of the mesolimbic reward circuit, the hippocampus has been increasingly implicated in food intake and obesity through several routes: contextual and episodic memory for food rewards, regulation of stress-related eating via HPA-axis circuitry, and sensitivity to circulating metabolic signals (insulin, leptin, ghrelin87;). Animal work and emerging human imaging studies have documented obesity-related neuroinflammation extending beyond the hypothalamus to include the hippocampus, with hippocampus among the best-characterized extra-hypothalamic sites29. We view this secondary finding as a target for future exploration and recommend confirmation in independent cohorts paired with measures of food-related memory, stress reactivity, or metabolic biomarkers.

To explain these structural patterns, Rapuano et al.7,8 proposed a diet-neuroinflammation-weight gain cycle (see Fig. 1A), where poor diet leads to systemic inflammation that crosses the blood–brain barrier and promotes glial proliferation in reward regions. Our results are broadly consistent with this account, but our cross-sectional design and lack of circulating biomarkers preclude direct mechanistic tests. Furthermore, while neuroinflammation is a plausible explanation for increased RNI, non-inflammatory mechanisms may equally account for our observed diffusion differences. For example: 1) Glial proliferation independent of inflammation, such as astrocytic or microglial expansion in response to energy demands, may also increase intracellular diffusion signals without triggering an immune response12; 2) Experience-dependent synaptic plasticity, whereby frequent engagement with rewarding dietary stimuli may strengthen excitatory connections within NAcc and insular networks, could reshape cellular architecture in ways detectable by RSI88; 3) Developmental differences in neuronal density, soma size, dendritic arborization, or synaptic pruning can produce increases in isotropic restricted diffusion that are indistinguishable from glial signals using current RSI parameters14,17. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive and may operate in parallel with inflammatory processes. Future multimodal research incorporating metabolic, immune, and functional data is essential to disentangle these mechanisms.

Lack of moderation by ADHD: common neural pathways in early childhood

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, we found no evidence that ADHD diagnosis moderated the relationship between BMI z-score and RNI. Because diagnostic group was retained as a covariate in the main effects model after the interaction term was dropped, the reported brain–adiposity associations are unlikely attributed to baseline ADHD vs. TD differences. Prior research has associated ADHD with altered reward circuitry and higher rates of obesity4,89, raising the possibility of shared neurobiological mechanisms. However, our findings suggest that early obesity-related microstructural differences in regions such as the NAcc and insula are comparable between children with ADHD and their typically developing peers. This is consistent with the possibility of a common neural pathway associated with adiposity in early childhood, regardless of diagnostic status. Notably, we also found no reliable group differences in anthropometric measures, further indicating limited ADHD-related divergence at this developmental stage.

It is possible that neurobiological differences associated with ADHD and obesity become more pronounced later in development90. Indeed, a meta-analysis by Nigg et al.40 reported no clear association between ADHD and obesity in children ages 5–12, with effect sizes increasing slightly in adulthood (odds ratios rising from 1.13 in childhood to 1.37 in adults). Furthermore, only a specific subset of individuals with ADHD may be particularly vulnerable to obesity, such as those with co-occurring internalizing symptoms or other comorbidities40,91. While ADHD status did not moderate brain–BMI z-score associations, the consistency of our primary findings across ADHD and TD groups suggests early, diagnosis-independent neurobiological correlates of adiposity risk. Although other anthropometric measures may emerge as stronger correlates of brain structure in later childhood and adolescence, the current data point toward a common neural pathway in early childhood.

Early detection and limitations: the need for longitudinal and multimodal approaches

Although Rapuano et al.7,8 leveraged a longitudinal design to demonstrate reciprocal relationships between NAcc microstructure and adiposity over time, our findings show that significant microstructural differences associated with adiposity, particularly in the NAcc and insula, are already observable in a cross-sectional snapshot of children aged 4–7. This raises the possibility that neural signatures associated with obesity risk may emerge earlier than previously documented and warrants longitudinal investigation to determine whether these differences precede, accompany, or follow changes in adiposity. Identifying these early neural correlates is critical for informing preventive strategies and understanding the developmental origins of obesity.

However, several limitations constrain the interpretation of these findings. Most notably, our cross-sectional design limits causal inference; longitudinal follow-up is necessary to determine whether early microstructural differences precede future changes in adiposity, accompany them, or reflect early consequences of elevated BMI z-score. Additionally, while RNI provides biologically meaningful insights into tissue microstructure, potentially indexing neuroinflammation or glial proliferation, it remains an indirect proxy. Integrating RSI data with metabolic, inflammatory, and functional neuroimaging measures in future multimodal studies will be important for specifying the precise biological mechanisms at play. Our sample size (n = 159; 81 ADHD, 78 TD) was adequately powered to detect medium main effects of adiposity on RSI metrics (and indeed, several survived FDR correction), but it was only modestly powered to detect interaction effects. Because tests of moderation are inherently less statistically powerful than tests of main effects, our null finding regarding ADHD diagnosis should be interpreted as an absence of evidence for large group differences, rather than definitive proof that no diagnostic divergence exists. Larger samples, ideally with longitudinal follow-up into adolescence (when meta-analytic evidence indicates the ADHD–obesity association strengthens40), will be needed to evaluate moderation more decisively.

Several features of the sample also constrain the generalizability of these findings. The cohort was recruited from a single southeastern U.S. metropolitan area and is predominantly Hispanic/Latino (82%), with highest parent education skewed toward higher attainment (76.5% college or advanced degree). While this composition offers stronger Hispanic/Latino representation than most prior pediatric neuroimaging studies of obesity (including the ABCD-based work of Rapuano and Adise et al.), it is correspondingly less informative about brain–adiposity associations in non-Hispanic Black, Asian American, and lower-SES children. SES is itself associated with obesity prevalence and with several proposed mediators of brain–adiposity relationships (e.g., dietary quality, food-environment exposure, chronic stress), so over-representation of higher-SES families may attenuate effects that would emerge more strongly in samples with greater SES range. Our exclusion criteria further limit generalizability. We excluded children with intellectual disability (IQ < 70), confirmed or screen-positive autism, and any history of psychotropic medication (including ADHD medication, although this is rare in this age range). These exclusions were appropriate for the parent intervention study and helped isolate the contribution of ADHD diagnostic status, but they also mean our findings may not extend to children with co-occurring neurodevelopmental conditions. Replication in racially, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse, less stringently selected cohorts will be important.

Conclusions and implications for early intervention

In summary, our findings indicate that higher BMI z-score in early childhood is associated with microstructural differences in reward-related and taste perception/salience processing brain regions, particularly the NAcc and anterior insula. These results are consistent with theories relating obesity to early neuroinflammatory processes or other cellular differences detectable via RSI metrics, although our cross-sectional design and lack of dietary, metabolic, or inflammatory biomarkers preclude direct tests of these mechanisms. Importantly, these associations were consistent across ADHD and TD groups, highlighting potential diagnosis-independent neural correlates of obesity risk that emerge early in development. Identifying these neural correlates could inform targeted interventions to mitigate obesity risk before it becomes entrenched.
