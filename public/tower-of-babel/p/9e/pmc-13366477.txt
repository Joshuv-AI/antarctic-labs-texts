Evaluating the need to revise chiropractic education standards in the United Kingdom: Adapting to progress
Authors: Gabrielle Swait, Daniel B Moore, Philip AG Dewhurst, Penelope A Bance
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHealth regulators in the United Kingdom fulfill their statutory duty to maintain high standards of education and training of health professionals by setting standards for education. Ensuring standards are contemporary and fit for purpose is fundamental to this function. This research aimed to evaluate whether the Education Standards (ES) of the General Chiropractic Council (2017) remained fit for purpose in 2021 or whether revisions were needed to meet current and future competency requirements for chiropractic graduates.MethodsAn exploratory, qualitative methodology was followed. The ES were comparatively mapped to relevant standards, authoritative practice, and quality assurance frameworks from other health care and higher education regulators, professional and chartered bodies. Areas of difference from other standards and frameworks were identified and substantive differences described.ResultsTwenty-two global and national frameworks and standards were mapped. Health care disciplines included physiotherapy, medicine, and osteopathy. Subjects included musculoskeletal capabilities, rehabilitation, safety curricula, person-centered approaches, dementia training, research competencies, and education. Twenty substantive areas not captured in the ES or requiring strengthening were identified. These included person-centered approaches, safety and quality, rehabilitation, prevention and health promotion, psychologically informed approaches, interprofessional practice, collaborative care, integration of evidence into practice, digital readiness, and reflective practice. Substantive areas linked to program delivery included protecting the interests of patients in an education setting, clinical learning (including interprofessional), and the culture of research and evidence.ConclusionAlthough broadly fit for purpose, mapping indicated the ES were falling out of step with the evolving wider health care community, supporting recommendations for high level revisions.Keywords: Clinical Competence, Chiropractic, Health Occupations/Education, Professional Competence

INTRODUCTION

Chiropractic education must equip new chiropractors with the competencies needed to provide the care that patients wish to receive.1 The educational process also needs to develop competencies for graduates to address the global burden of musculoskeletal conditions.2 Curricula must ensure that new chiropractors are equipped to fulfill their duties as regulated health care professionals and keep their knowledge and skills up to date and concordant with new research evidence throughout their professional life.3 It is also important that new chiropractors understand the wider health care system and can collaborate with other health care professionals for the benefit of their patients.4

In the United Kingdom, chiropractic is a regulated profession by law. The regulating body is the General Chiropractic Council (GCC).5 The GCC has a statutory duty to set the standards of chiropractic education, conduct, and practice and has powers to recognize chiropractic degree programs.6 The GCC must ensure that graduates of chiropractic degree programs have reached the required standard of proficiency and are thereby able to meet all the requirements set out in its Code of Professional Practice for the competent and safe practice of chiropractic.7 Chiropractic education programs in the United Kingdom are to a master’s degree level.

Graduation from a recognized program demonstrates that the new graduate meets the required standard of proficiency and is eligible to apply to join the professional register. The program’s ability to meet this requirement is evaluated against the Education Standards (ES) of the GCC.8,9 One of the key regulatory tools of the GCC for protecting patients and the public is the ES. These provide the framework for determining that a new graduate of a recognized program is competent and safe to practice, setting out the minimum threshold standards.

The GCC ES are organized into 2 sections. In section 1, standards address curriculum content setting out the learning outcomes (LOs) required of a program to achieve recognition by the GCC. Each LO of the ES must be demonstrably included and appropriately assessed within the educational program.4 In section 2, standards represent requirements for how a program is designed, delivered, assessed, and monitored. These standards ensure that learners in chiropractic degree programs receive a high-quality education to enable their development toward becoming safe, competent, and effective primary health care practitioners.

The last GCC ES were published in 2017.10 The GCC must keep its Standard of Proficiency and, thus, also its ES under review.6 To that end, the GCC endeavored to determine the necessity to develop new ES, taking account of the current and anticipated future skills requirements of a chiropractic graduate entering the evolving wider health care profession. During the process, the GCC took into consideration perspectives of chiropractic educators, employers, and patients and internal research conducted and published by the GCC. The present study reports on part of this larger process.

This study is the comparison of the GCC ES with relevant standards and frameworks developed by other health care regulatory and professional/educational bodies, taking an outward-looking approach toward other health professions. There has been little comparison of chiropractic education with that of other health professions previously. A comparison of the inputs to education (including the content of curricula and clinical training settings) between chiropractic and medicine was reported in 1998.11 We could not identify in the literature any other comparative studies addressing either the inputs to education or the outputs of education (such as LOs, competencies, or capabilities) that are deemed important in training practice-ready health professionals.12,13 This limits the ability to understand the education of chiropractors in terms of what they can do upon graduation compared with other health professionals.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine, through standards review, whether the 2017 GCC ES remained fully fit for purpose in 2021 or whether further development or revisions were needed to meet current and future competency requirements for chiropractic graduates.

METHODS

An ES review steering group was convened that comprised members of the GCC, its executive, and its education committee. Prior to the commencement of the document review, the steering group met in February 2021 to discuss and agree upon parameters for the evaluation and to identify the standards and frameworks to be mapped. This was informed by expert opinion and knowledge of (1) advances in health professions education and practice in the United Kingdom and globally; (2) developments in the approaches taken by other UK health regulators; and (3) issues that have been raised to the GCC with implications for how chiropractors are educated, including fitness-to-practice investigations following complaints about chiropractors and the findings of recent internal public perceptions and workforce research. Consensus agreement was reached at a single meeting of the steering group.

The important parameters determined for the mapping component of the review were that (1) the review should aim to identify larger issues around the need to amend any aspects of the ES rather than details, (2) consideration should be given to what is expected of new graduates now and in the medium-term future, and (3) consideration should be given to whether the ES adequately reflected the Code.14

The standards and frameworks to be mapped were purposively identified by the steering group based upon contemporaneousness, authoritativeness (prioritizing key national and global sources), and relevance to the breadth of chiropractic education and practice. For UK health regulators, professions with comparable scope of practice to chiropractic were included (osteopathy and physiotherapy), along with others with newly published education standards (since 2017). The included standards, practice frameworks, and quality assurance frameworks from other health care and higher education regulators, and professional and chartered bodies15–35 are provided in Figure 1.

Mapped standards and frameworks.

An exploratory, qualitative methodology was followed. The mapping was conducted by 1 person (GS), who was experienced in education, standards, and comparative mapping. Textual data from the compared sources were analyzed using qualitative content analysis.36 Each standard was systematically compared with the included standards and frameworks, which were also examined for areas absent from the ES. Relevant phrases were iteratively coded, and categories developed through successive refinement.

Data were collected in an Excel (Microsoft Corp) spreadsheet to support comparison. Differences were identified, categories were organized into themes, and substantive differences described narratively to highlight gaps and variances between the GCC ES and the other frameworks. Substantive differences were determined when content was not represented in the ES (2017) or was only minimally mentioned. This was a pragmatic study with the primary purpose of internal research; thus, coding, categorization, and development of themes was conducted by a sole researcher (GS). This limitation reduces the assurance of methodological rigor and opportunities for richness of interpretation37 but was determined by feasibility and resourcing of the study.

General Differences from Other Health Regulators

In addition to the specific key gaps in the 2017 GCC ES, several general points of difference in the approaches taken by other health regulators in their education standards were identified as described below.

First, other regulatory ES varied in the extent to which detail of LOs were specified. Some were more prescriptive,16,22 whereas others took a broader approach.18,21 The GCC ES use the broader approach.

The role of the regulator versus the role of the profession and the scope and standards of practice were also pointed out in one other regulator. The General Medical Council’s education standards20 make a clear distinction between aspects that are the concern of a regulatory body versus aspects of clinical and professional definition that are not the role of the regulator to determine. Its education standards reference separate documents developed by providers and the profession, and these define the scope and the specific procedures that doctors must be trained to perform. The General Medical Council’s education standards address the issues of regulatory concern, including stipulating that providers must develop their curricula with input from the profession (and other stakeholders).

Some regulatory education standards placed greater emphasis on the development and demonstration of fundamental underpinning skills as opposed to demonstration of the applied skills as LOs. These underpinning skills can then be transferred to different situations. Some examples are communication, clinical reasoning, decision making, managing challenging situations, reflective practice, and approaches to behavioral change. These areas are indicated in Table 2.

The General Optical Council’s education standards22 take the very formalized approach of stating the required level of learning for every LO utilizing Miller’s assessment pyramid.38 Other regulators’ standards do not take such a prescriptive approach to indicating the level of learning and assessment methods required but indicate expectations in the required level through the wording of the standard (eg, demonstration “does” as opposed to “understands”). This is the approach taken in the 2017 GCC ES, recognizing that providers of chiropractic education may use alternative conceptual frameworks for the assessment of different types of LO.

There was variability in the reported time frames for regulators to review and update their ES. Some regulators specified flexibility in their mechanisms for keeping their standards under review and update. The Health and Care Professions Council ES18 are subject to rolling review but with periodic full reviews. In contrast, the GCC has no specified process or schedule for review of its ES.

All regulatory standards were not consistent in the use of contemporary terms. Conversely, there were some areas of the 2017 GCC ES in which review would lead to an update of the terminology to be concordant with contemporary language. Examples include “person-centered care,” “shared decision making,” “supported self-management,” “patient” vs “service user,” “case history” vs “consultation,” “educator” versus “teacher,” and “student” vs “learner.”

DISCUSSION

This research aimed to evaluate the 2017 GCC ES by mapping to other contemporary standards and frameworks across health professions to determine whether the GCC ES remained fully fit for purpose in 2021 or whether further development or revisions were needed. This approach contextualizes the standards for chiropractic education to the wider education and practice of health professions in the United Kingdom. To our knowledge, this may be the first report to make comparisons between the outcomes of chiropractic education and other health professions. This knowledge will help ensure that, within the United Kingdom, the education of chiropractors is contemporary and that educational practices align with those of other regulated health professions.

The mapping identified multiple gaps or areas that required strengthening for standards relating to the LOs of recognized programs across 18 themes. Further gaps were identified for standards relating to the way in which programs are designed and/or delivered across 3 themes. The gaps and areas for strengthening that were identified through the mapping provide the basis for revisions to the ES for both the LOs of programs and also for the way that programs are designed and delivered. It was recommended that all themes identified should be fully considered and included in the development of reviewed GCC ES.

Areas that were not represented in the 2017 GCC ES were LOs relating to safety culture/adverse events/preventing harm, quality of care and service provision, safeguarding, reputation of the profession and advertising, data handling/information technology skills, and use of technology. Other areas identified for strengthening were either only partially captured, were loosely mentioned within guidance for possible approaches that might enable standards to be met, or were not clearly defined (and, therefore, not explicitly specified). Largely, these represent more recent developments in thinking around best practice in health care and health care education since the ES were published in 2017.

One example is that of therapeutic and professional relationships and patient partnership. The 2017 GCC ES contain some references, mostly within their guidance, around involving patients in their care, listening to patients, and treating them as individuals, taking into account patient choice in decision making and applying appropriate patient-centered care. This is similar to inclusions in other regulators’ standards that signal, at the time they were published, the increased attention on patient-centeredness as a value.27 As a concept, however, this lacked a globally accepted definition or model.39 The Skills for Health person-centered approaches framework27 was published in July (2017), after the GCC ES had been finalized in early 2017. It is a core skills education and training framework that articulates what it means to be person-centered and how to develop and support the workforce to work in this way. It aims to distill best practice and to set out core, transferable behaviors, knowledge, and skills.27 Regulatory standards and frameworks published after this16,21,22,24 are more explicit regarding LOs and/or competencies that support person-centered approaches to care, aligning with the person-centered approaches framework.27 This framework may, thus, underlie an apparent step-change in thinking around health professions education, highlighting the impact that such publications can have.

Other examples of recently published frameworks that informed identification of substantial areas for updating were The Academy of Medical Royal Colleges National Patient Safety Syllabus31 (safety culture/adverse events/preventing harm), the development of a global chiropractic rehabilitation competency framework by the World Federation of Chiropractic28 (disability and rehabilitation), and the consolidated framework for allied health professions Shaping Better Practice Through Research: A Practitioner Framework.30 These frameworks have in common the fact that they set out definitions and identify explicit requirements in the context of delivering education or the competencies that need to be developed by health professions. They were, thus, fundamental to informing the most contemporary aspects of this evaluation.

Future Studies

This project informed extensive recommendations to the GCC for areas of the ES to consider for high-level revisions. This research provided guidance for how these enhancements might be made, ensuring that subsequent review of the 2017 GCC ES was justified based on a rigorous and extensive process of scoping research. This work led to the creation of reviewed GCC Education Standards (2023).3 It is, therefore, recommended that similar work be undertaken in other countries.

Strengths

A strength of this research was its focus on making comparisons to wider health care education and practice in the United Kingdom. The range of other standards and frameworks that were mapped ensured comparison with the education standards and/or competencies of professions that provide similar musculoskeletal services (osteopathy and physiotherapy) and a range of other health professions (focusing on those with the most recently published new standards to ensure contemporaneousness). Most areas of the ES were not specific to chiropractic education, and many standards from other health professions were also relevant to chiropractic. By taking this outward-looking perspective across health care, future review of the ES will help ensure that new chiropractors are equipped with the knowledge and skills needed to provide care that patients wish to receive and to address the global burden of musculoskeletal conditions. Using a broad perspective will also assist future chiropractors’ participation in health care in general and interprofessional collaboration for the benefit of patients.

A further strength of this study was the involvement of the steering group in identifying and determining the sources to be mapped. This process facilitated that relevant, contemporary, and authoritative standards and frameworks covering contemporary and emerging areas were selected. All the standards and frameworks reviewed were in themselves the result of rigorous development processes that included wide stakeholder engagement and consultations with most including the input of patients. This ensures that the GCC ES were compared with the highest quality standards and frameworks that already represent expert and key stakeholder perspectives. This may provide confidence to other health professions that chiropractors are trained in a contemporary manner that aligns to their own training.

Limitations

This research was commissioned by the GCC for the primary purpose of informing its own review of the ES. A key limitation is, thus, that the mapping, evaluation, and identification of areas for updating and strengthening was conducted by 1 researcher (GS), increasing the risk of inaccuracy or bias in mapping, coding, identification of the key themes, and reducing breadth of group inputs to the interpretation. Oversight of results by the steering group helped to mitigate the risk of inaccuracy or bias in evaluation and provided interpretative opportunities as far as was possible.

CONCLUSION

This comparative mapping of the 2017 GCC ES to contemporary standards and frameworks of other health professions identified more than 20 gaps or areas requiring strengthening. Areas for improvement related to both the learning outcomes of chiropractic education programs and to aspects of their design and delivery. Although broadly fit for purpose, the evaluation indicated the ES were falling out of step with the evolving wider health care community, supporting recommendations for high-level revisions.
