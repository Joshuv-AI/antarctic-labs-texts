Normal-range urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio and mortality risk in adults with obesity: a cohort study
Authors: Xianhua Li, Jihong Ma, Yongsong Xu, Haolin Gong, Haibin Wen, Jing ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElevated urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is associated with increased mortality risk in general populations. However, the prognostic significance of UACR within the normal range (< 30 mg/g) among adults with obesity remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a cohort study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018, with mortality outcomes ascertained through linkage to the National Death Index through December 31, 2019. Adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) and normal-range UACR were included. UACR was analyzed as both a continuous variable and categorized by tertiles. Weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for demographics, lifestyle factors, and clinical characteristics were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAmong 10,754 participants with obesity, during a median follow-up of 9.6 years (interquartile range: 5.2–13.9), 1,084 all-cause deaths and 305 cardiovascular deaths occurred. After multivariable adjustment, each 5 mg/g increment in UACR was associated with increased all-cause mortality (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.22; P < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (HR: 1.13; 95% CI: 1.01–1.26; P = 0.03). Compared with the lowest tertile, participants in the highest UACR tertile had significantly higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.25–1.96; P < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (HR: 2.22; 95% CI: 1.45–3.41; P < 0.001), with significant dose-response relationships (both P for trend < 0.001). Results remained consistent across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsIn adults with obesity, a higher UACR within the clinically normal range was independently associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. This observational association highlights the potential prognostic value of normal-range UACR and supports its consideration in the comprehensive risk assessment of this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02155-7.Keywords: Albuminuria, Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, Obesity, Mortality, Cohort study, NHANES

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity has reached epidemic proportions, with more than 603.7 million adults classified as obese worldwide in 2015 [1], Moreover, assuming the continuation of historical trends, the total number of adults living with overweight and obesity is projected to reach 3.80 billion by 2050, representing over half of the global adult population [2]. Obesity is conventionally defined by a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m² [3, 4]. Notably, recent recommendations suggest that BMI should be supplemented with at least one additional age-, sex-, and ethnicity-appropriate anthropometric measure, such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, or waist-to-height ratio, to more accurately characterize adiposity [5]. Regardless of the metric used, obesity significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and premature mortality [6]. This growing public health challenge necessitates the identification of early clinical indicators for risk stratification in populations with obesity.

While the relationship between obesity and mortality may be complex and context-dependent, with some studies reporting an “obesity paradox” in certain clinical populations, the heterogeneity of metabolic health within populations with obesity highlights the need for improved risk stratification approaches beyond anthropometric measures alone [7]. According to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, chronic kidney disease classification and renal risk assessment are based on both the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [8]. A UACR ≥ 30 mg/g has traditionally been used to identify kidney dysfunction and serves as an important marker for cardiovascular risk evaluation [9–12]. Notably, recent evidence suggests that variations within the normal range (< 30 mg/g) may carry significant prognostic value, potentially reflecting subtle vascular dysfunction and endothelial damage [13, 14]. This finding has important implications for early risk detection and preventive interventions.

The relationship between normal-range UACR and mortality outcomes specifically in populations with obesity remains poorly understood. While elevated UACR has been consistently associated with increased cardiovascular risk in general populations [15], its prognostic value within the normal range for individuals with obesity requires further investigation. To our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically addressed the normal UACR range in individuals with obesity. Therefore, we examined whether normal-range UACR predicts mortality in adults with obesity using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data.

Methods

Study population

This population-based cohort study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a comprehensive national health assessment program conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [16]. The NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design to select participants representative of the civilian non-institutionalized United States population [17]. The study cohort comprised adult participants aged 18 years or older with documented anthropometric measurements. Obesity was defined according to World Health Organization criteria as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m² [4, 18, 19]. We calculated BMI as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Of 101,316 participants in the NHANES database (1999–2018), sequential exclusions were applied: age < 20 years (n = 46,235), pregnant participants (n = 1,547), those lost to follow-up (n = 135), participants with cancer diagnosis (n = 5,194), and individuals with BMI < 30 kg/m² (n = 31,778). Additional exclusions included participants with missing covariate data (n = 3,906) and those with UACR > 30 mg/g (n = 1,767). The final analysis included 10,754 participants with obesity. Ethical considerations

The research protocol received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. Data collection commenced following receipt of written informed consent from study participants. The investigation adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exposure measurement

Spot urine specimen collection followed standardized NHANES protocols. The analytical laboratory protocol required storage of frozen urine samples at −20 °C. Specimen stability validation occurred under two storage conditions: 5 °C and − 20°C [13].

Urine albumin quantification employed solid phase fluorescent immunoassay methodology. The measurement of urine creatinine involved two distinct analytical approaches across the study period. The kinetic Jaffe rate reaction method preceded 2007, with subsequent transition to enzymatic methodology [15]. Creatinine measurements from 2005 to 2006 cycles underwent adjustment using NHANES-recommended standardization equations. The urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) calculation utilized the ratio between urine albumin concentration and adjusted creatinine values. This standardized ratio represented the primary exposure variable for subsequent analyses.

Outcome ascertainment

The study designated all-cause mortality as the primary outcome and cardiovascular mortality as the secondary outcome. The National Death Index provided mortality data through December 31, 2019.The survival time calculation spanned from each participant’s NHANES examination date until death or the end of follow-up. The follow-up period concluded on December 31, 2019.The International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) provided the framework for categorizing causes of death. This standardized system enabled precise identification of cardiovascular mortality cases [20].

Covariates

The analysis incorporated multiple demographic variables including age, sex, race/ethnicity (categorized as Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic White, and Other). Education level was categorized into three groups: less than high school, high school graduate, and more than high school, and was treated as a categorical variable in all analyses. The poverty-to-income ratio (PIR) was treated as a continuous variable in all analyses. Body mass index served as a standardized measure of adiposity, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. The Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) was calculated using sex-specific formulas incorporating waist circumference, BMI, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol, as previously described [21]. Physical activity assessment utilized metabolic equivalent (MET)-hours per week as the standardized measurement unit. The study categorized both smoking status and alcohol consumption into three distinct groups: never, former, and current users. Dietary quality assessment employed the Healthy Eating Index-2015, with higher scores indicating superior dietary patterns [22]. Kidney function assessment utilized the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation for estimated glomerular filtration rate calculation, incorporating serum creatinine measurements [23]. Hyperlipidemia identification relied on established lipid profile parameters or documented use of lipid-lowering medications [24]. Cardiovascular disease classification encompassed documented diagnoses of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Other cardiovascular conditions, such as peripheral arterial disease, atrial fibrillation, and valvular heart disease, were not included in the present definition. Hypertension determination followed the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines, defined as antihypertensive medication usage or blood pressure readings ≥ 130/80 mmHg [25]. Antihypertensive medication use was defined as self-reported current use of blood pressure-lowering medications [26]. Diabetes mellitus diagnosis required meeting at least one criterion: physician-documented diagnosis, current hypoglycemic medication use, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, random or 2-hour OGTT glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [27]. Hyperuricemia classification utilized serum uric acid thresholds exceeding 7 mg/dL [28].

Statistical analysis

The study participants were categorized into tertiles according to their baseline UACR values. Baseline characteristics presentation utilized percentages for categorical variables and means with standard errors for continuous variables. Between-group comparisons employed chi-square tests and analysis of variance for categorical and continuous variables.

Survival analysis implemented weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine associations between UACR and mortality outcomes. The analysis considered UACR both as a continuous variable (per 5 mg/g increment) and as categorical tertiles, generating hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. The regression analysis incorporated three sequential adjustment models. Model 1 adjusted for demographic factors including age, sex, ethnicity, education level, and poverty-income ratio. Model 2 expanded adjustment for lifestyle factors: smoking status, alcohol consumption, dietary quality (HEI-2015), and physical activity. Model 3 further incorporated clinical variables: cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, estimated glomerular filtration rate, diabetes mellitus and hyperuricemia. Kaplan-Meier methodology generated survival curves for visual comparison between UACR tertiles, with statistical significance assessed via log-rank tests. Restricted cubic splines with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles were used to assess potential non-linear associations, with non-linearity tested using the likelihood ratio test. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate potential effect modification by age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of our findings. First, we excluded participants who died within 2 years of follow-up to minimize reverse causation. Second, analyses were restricted to individuals with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m² to assess associations in preserved kidney function. Third, we performed additional adjustment for baseline BMI. Fourth we conducted further adjustment for antihypertensive medication use in fully adjusted models. Finally, to assess the impact of missing covariate data, we performed multiple imputation using chained equations, with results pooled using Rubin’s rules. Statistical significance adopted a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05. Data analysis utilized R software version 4.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

As shown in Table 1, the analytic sample included 10,754 participants with obesity. Among these, the study population was stratified into tertiles based on UACR levels: low [0.21, 4.89) mg/g, medium [4.89, 8.56) mg/g, and high [8.56, 30) mg/g. The high UACR tertile was characterized by older mean age (49.29 years), a higher proportion of females (59.83%), and greater mean BMI (36.36 kg/m²). Additionally, VAI showed an increasing trend, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Physical activity levels were inversely associated with UACR tertiles, while smoking status showed no significant difference between groups (P = 0.47). Notably, diabetes mellitus (26.69%), hypertension (56.61%), cardiovascular disease (11.60%), and hyperlipidemia (84.86%) were more prevalent in the high UACR tertile (all P < 0.0001). Hyperuricemia prevalence differed significantly across tertiles (P < 0.001), with the highest prevalence in the lowest tertile (24.08%). All-cause mortality incidence increased progressively across UACR tertiles, from 6.52 per 1,000 person-years in the lowest tertile to 15.69 per 1,000 person-years in the highest tertile (P for trend < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S1).

In the fully adjusted model, each 5 mg/g increment in UACR was associated with a 14% higher mortality risk (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.22; P < 0.001). Compared with the lowest tertile, participants in the highest UACR tertile showed significantly increased mortality risk (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.25–1.96; P < 0.001), with a significant dose-response relationship (P for trend < 0.001).

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a monotonic, approximately linear association between UACR and all-cause mortality across the normal range, with no evidence of significant departure from linearity (P for non-linearity = 0.29) (Supplementary Figure S1).

As shown in Fig. 3, Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the association between UACR and all-cause mortality varied in magnitude across certain clinical subgroups (Fig. 3). Significant interactions were observed for diabetes mellitus (P for interaction = 0.03), hyperlipidemia (P < 0.001), and cardiovascular disease (P = 0.03), with stronger associations in individuals without these comorbidities. However, the direction of association remained consistent across all subgroups, with higher UACR associated with increased mortality risk regardless of comorbidity status. No significant interactions were detected for age (P = 0.64), sex (P = 0.46), race/ethnicity (P = 0.42), education (P = 0.72), poverty-income ratio (P = 0.65), physical activity (P = 0.30), smoking status (P = 0.63), alcohol intake (P = 0.30), hypertension (P = 0.12), or hyperuricemia (P = 0.46). These findings indicate that while the magnitude of risk may differ across clinical characteristics, the prognostic value of UACR within the normal range is broadly applicable across demographic and lifestyle subgroups in individuals with obesity.

Multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings. First, exclusion of deaths occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up yielded consistent results (HR per 5 mg/g: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.22; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S3). Second, when analyses were restricted to participants with preserved kidney function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), the UACR-mortality association remained significant (HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.06–1.25; P = 0.001) (Supplementary Table S4). Third, additional adjustment for baseline BMI did not materially alter the associations (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.22; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S5). Fourth further adjustment for antihypertensive medication use produced virtually unchanged results (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.22; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S6). Finally, multiple imputation analysis using five imputed datasets demonstrated nearly identical results to the complete case analysis (HR: 1.13; 95% CI: 1.07–1.19; P < 0.001), indicating that exclusion of participants with missing covariate data did not introduce substantial bias (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

In this large cohort study of American individuals with obesity, our findings suggest that higher UACR levels, even within the normal range (< 30 mg/g), may be independently associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The baseline characteristics revealed a clustering of cardiometabolic risk factors in the highest UACR tertile, including older age, higher BMI, and greater prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and hyperlipidemia, suggesting that elevated UACR reflects a broader state of metabolic and vascular dysfunction. Subgroup analyses showed significant interactions for diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular disease, with stronger associations observed among individuals without these comorbidities. This may indicate that UACR has greater prognostic value in the earlier stages of cardiometabolic disease. Our findings extend previous research on albuminuria and mortality risk in several important ways. First, while prior studies have demonstrated associations between UACR and mortality in general populations or specific patient groups with heart failure or hypertension, our study uniquely focused on individuals with obesity [13, 14, 29–32]. Pang et al. demonstrated that normal-range UACR was independently associated with all-cause mortality in diabetic adults with preserved kidney function, with a significant UACR-BMI interaction supporting its relevance in the obese population [33]. Du et al. found a dose-response relationship between UACR and kidney stone risk, suggesting UACR reflects systemic pathophysiology beyond renal damage [34]. Barkai et al. further showed that higher normal-range UACR correlated with greater glycemic variability in type 1 diabetes [35]. These findings collectively reinforce UACR as a clinically meaningful risk marker, particularly among individuals with obesity.

For instance, elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) has been associated with increased all-cause mortality [36], while elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) has been linked to higher mortality risk [37]. Second, most previous studies examined UACR across its entire range or focused on elevated levels, whereas our study specifically investigated UACR within the normal range (< 30 mg/g) [32, 38]. The observed dose-response relationship within this range suggests that even subtle increases in UACR might be associated with higher mortality risk in individuals with obesity.

Several biological mechanisms may underlie the observed associations, with obesity-related adipose tissue dysfunction as the central driver. In obesity, excessive visceral adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines and dysregulated adipokines, initiating a pathophysiological cascade [39–41]. First, cytokines from dysfunctional visceral fat impair endothelial function and increase vascular permeability, manifesting as elevated UACR even within the normal range [42, 43]. Second, obesity-induced chronic inflammation and oxidative stress cause microvascular damage and increased albumin excretion [44, 45]. Third, obesity-driven hemodynamic changes—including glomerular hyperfiltration and elevated intraglomerular pressure—may cause subtle glomerular injury reflected in UACR elevation [46]. These obesity-centered mechanisms provide a biologically plausible explanation for the association between normal-range UACR and cardiovascular mortality, although experimental verification is needed. Additionally, it should be noted that obesity-related sarcopenia and reduced muscle mass may decrease urinary creatinine excretion, thereby artificially elevating UACR independent of albumin changes. Thus, the elevated UACR observed in obese individuals may partly reflect reduced creatinine production rather than solely increased albumin excretion, and the interplay between these two components warrants further investigation.

This study has several notable strengths. The large, nationally representative sample from NHANES, extended follow-up period, and comprehensive adjustment for potential confounding factors enhance the reliability of our findings. Despite extensive adjustment, potential residual confounding remains from lack of medication details, disease severity measures, and unmeasured factors. However, the consistency across multiple sensitivity analyses, dose-response relationships, and biological plausibility support that findings represent true independent associations. More importantly, our findings highlight the clinical significance of monitoring UACR levels in populations with obesity, suggesting that early detection of subtle kidney dysfunction through UACR screening could identify high-risk individuals who might benefit from targeted interventions before the onset of overt kidney disease. This study has limitations. First, its observational nature precludes causal inference, and residual confounding from factors like unmeasured dietary sodium or subclinical inflammation may persist despite adjustment. Second, UACR was measured at a single time point. Although this reflects clinical practice and NHANES protocols ensure quality, it cannot capture intra-individual variability. Such non-differential misclassification likely biases estimates toward the null, implying the true association may be stronger. Third, while we adjusted for eGFR, it primarily reflects glomerular filtration and may not capture other renal aspects like tubular health. Moreover, UACR was analyzed independently without jointly considering precise eGFR values, and future studies should consider stratified analyses incorporating both markers for a more comprehensive renal risk assessment. Fourth, antihypertensive medication use was treated as a single binary variable without distinguishing between drug classes with different nephroprotective profiles, which may introduce residual confounding. Future studies should consider class-specific adjustments to better account for these differential effects. Fifth, our findings from a U.S. cohort may not generalize to other populations with different obesity phenotypes or lifestyles, necessitating external validation. Additionally, UACR is inherently influenced by changes in both albumin and creatinine excretion. A falsely elevated UACR may result from low urinary creatinine excretion, such as in individuals with sarcopenia or reduced muscle mass, while a falsely low UACR may occur in those with high creatinine excretion due to greater muscle mass. This limitation should be considered when interpreting UACR values in the obese population, where body composition varies considerably. Finally, systematic UTI data were unavailable. However, acute UTI would cause transient UACR elevation, likely attenuating long-term mortality associations, and our consistent sensitivity analyses argue against acute conditions driving the results. Despite these limitations, the observed dose-response relationship, consistency across analyses, and biological plausibility support an independent association between higher normal-range UACR and increased mortality risk in adults with obesity.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations inherent to its observational design, this study provides evidence that a higher normal-range UACR is independently associated with increased mortality risk in obesity. While this highlights the potential prognostic relevance of UACR below the albuminuria threshold, prospective research is necessary to determine its clinical utility for risk stratification.
