Apical Surgery of a Maxillary Left Central Tooth Using NeoPutty After Retreatment Failure: A Case Report
Authors: Sajedeh Namaei Ghasemi, Zakieh Kheradmand, Siavash Moushekhian, Zeinab Ghasemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study reports a successful apical surgery performed on a maxillary central incisor in a middle‐aged man with a persistent infection following non‐surgical endodontic retreatment. The infection involved both labial and palatal bone perforations. NeoPutty, a new bioactive bioceramic putty available in a premixed format, was used in this case. Its primary components are tricalcium and dicalcium silicate. NeoPutty has demonstrated antibacterial properties and biocompatibility, and has been successfully used in primary molar pulpotomy and indirect pulp therapy. In the present study, it was utilized for root‐end filling for the first time, contributing to a favorable surgical outcome.Keywords: apical surgery, bioactive bioceramic materials, endodontic retreatment, tricalcium silicate

Introduction

Apical surgery, also known as endodontic microsurgery, is typically indicated when non‐surgical root canal treatment or retreatment has failed to resolve periapical pathosis [1]. The primary goal of apical surgery is to remove the pathological tissues at the root apex, ensuring complete sealing of the root canal system to prevent bacterial leakage and promote healing [1]. The process is typically initiated by retracting a surgical flap and performing a trephination to access the root apex. The root end is resected at a 90‐degree angle to the long axis of the tooth to ensure complete removal of the pathological tissue [1]. Then, the root‐end cavity is prepared using ultrasonic instruments to remove infected tissue and create a cavity for the root‐end filling material [1, 2].

The choice of root‐end filling material is critical for the success of apical surgery. The material should be biocompatible, provide a tight seal, promote healing, and have a favorable handling. The most common material that is used is Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA). MTA has been the gold standard for root‐end filling due to its excellent biocompatibility and ability to promote healing. However, its handling properties can be challenging [2, 3, 4, 5]. Therefore, newer bioceramic and resin‐based materials have been proposed to overcome the handling difficulties of MTA while maintaining similar biological properties.

NeoMTA Plus (Avalon Biomed Inc., Bradenton, USA) is a tricalcium silicate‐based material available in a powder‐liquid format. The powder consists of tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, and tantalum oxide, while the liquid contains water and polymers [6]. One notable feature of NeoMTA Plus is that it incorporates tantalum oxide as a radiopacifier in place of bismuth oxide, which is known to cause discoloration associated with traditional MTA [7]. Recently, a new version of NeoMTA Plus, called NeoPutty, has been introduced to the market. Unlike its predecessor, NeoPutty is available in a premixed format to improve its handling. Its composition includes tantalum oxide, tricalcium silicate, calcium aluminate, dicalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, and calcium sulfate. Some experimental and clinical studies have been performed on the efficacy of NeoPutty for primary molar pulpotomy and indirect pulp therapy [8, 9]. Moreover, it has successfully demonstrated comparable in vitro cytocompatibility with NeoMTA Plus and MTA Angelus against human dental pulp cells [10]. Because of its demonstrated clinical effectiveness, regenerative properties, and ease of use, NeoPutty could be an ideal choice for root‐end filling material in apical surgery. This study presents, for the first time, a successful outcome of apical surgery on a maxillary central incisor after dealing with a persistent infection following non‐surgical endodontic retreatment.

Case History/Examination

A 55‐year‐old male patient visited the Endodontics Department at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences in December 2023, presenting with intermittent pain and swelling on the labial and palatal aspects of tooth 21 (Figure 1A,B). The patient reported that this tooth had undergone treatment 14 years ago by a general dental practitioner. A periapical radiograph revealed a low‐quality root canal treatment for tooth 21, as well as recurrent carious lesions on teeth 21 and 22. Additionally, a large periapical radiolucency was observed at the apex of tooth 21, leading to a diagnosis of secondary acute apical abscess (Figure 1C). Following a consultation with the Prosthodontics Department, the patient was advised to extract tooth 22 and pursue nonsurgical retreatment of tooth 21, as well as crown replacement. The treatment plan was thoroughly explained to the patient, and written informed consent was obtained.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Local anesthesia was administered using an infiltration of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Darou Pakhsh Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Co., Tehran, Iran). The existing restoration was removed using a diamond bur, and the tooth was isolated with a rubber dam. The gutta‐percha cone was removed, and the root canal was prepared utilizing an x‐file system with a crown‐down technique. During the instrumentation process, the canal was irrigated extensively with a 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution, which was activated using ultraX (name of device). Following this, calcium hydroxide was placed into the canal. The patient was discharged after the temporary filling material was applied.

At the second visit, the calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) was washed away from the canal, and the smear layer was removed using 17% Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Morvabon, Tehran, Iran). The root canal was obturated using warm vertical compaction with gutta‐percha cones and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply Maillefer, Switzerland). After placing the temporary filling material, the patient was referred for a permanent restoration (Figure 1D). The tooth received a casting post and crown from the prosthodontic team.

Four months later, the patient returned for a follow‐up visit, referred by the prosthodontist. He reported pain while chewing after receiving the cementation. A periapical radiograph showed a persistent apical abscess (Figure 2A). Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans revealed perforation of the palatal and labial cortex (Figure 2B,D). The lesion was localized, and the clinical examination, along with CBCT scans, confirmed that no other maxillofacial bones were affected. As a result, apical surgery was planned. A submarginal flap was retracted using a #15c surgical blade (Swann‐Morton, England) to preserve the marginal gingiva (Figure 3A,B). The root apex location was determined according to the CBCT scans. Due to the perforation, a minimal osteotomy was performed using gentle brush strokes with a 25‐mm round carbide surgical bur, and a 5 × 5 mm bone window was prepared. A Columbia 13–14 curette and a Gracey curette were used for debridement of the lesion. Then, the root was resected using a 25‐mm taper fissure 170 L surgical bur 3 mm from the apex. Then, root‐end preparation was conducted with ultrasonic tips (Figure 3C). Premixed NeoPutty (Avalon Biomed Inc., Bradenton, USA) was utilized for retrofilling the prepared cavity at the root end (Figure 3D). The surgical field was then sutured using 5‐0 Vicryl (product name) with single interrupted sutures (Figure 3E), and a post‐operative periapical radiograph was obtained (Figure 3F). Post‐operative instructions were provided. The sutures were removed after 1 week, and oral hygiene instructions were given to the patient, with regular follow‐up visits recommended.

(A) A subgingival flap was designed to preserve the marginal gingiva (orange dotted line). (B) The flap was retracted and released. (C) The root was resected 3 mm from the apex and prepared using ultrasonic tips (green arrow). (D) The apex was retrofilled with NeoPutty (blue arrow). (E) The surgical site was sutured. (F) A postoperative periapical radiograph was obtained. The red arrow indicates the NeoPutty placed at the tooth apex.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient returned for a follow‐up 9 months later and reported that he was asymptomatic. The palatal and labial swellings had resolved (Figure 4A,B), and clinical examination showed no signs or symptoms of endodontic issues. A periapical radiograph revealed a significant reduction in the size of the apical radiolucency (Figure 4C).

(A, B) Intraoral photographs taken 9 months postoperatively show resolution of the swellings and healthy, normal gingival tissue. The blue cross indicates the treated tooth. (C) Periapical radiograph shows a considerable reduction in the size of the periapical lesion.

Discussion

Bacterial infections are almost always the primary cause of failures in endodontic treatments. To manage a failing root canal filling, the first step is to identify the source of the persistent infection [11]. Orthograde retreatment is often considered the preferred treatment option. However, bacteria can persist in previously uninstrumented or missed sections of the root canal, often entering through a compromised coronal restoration or root filling. In such cases, endodontic surgery involves removing the damaged periapical tissue to create conditions conducive to tissue healing, regeneration, and the reestablishment of structural support for the tooth.

The success rate for first‐time endodontic surgery ranges from 78% to 91% [12]. Surgical endodontics is particularly effective in treating failed retreatment cases resulting from apical transportation or procedural errors, especially when crowns with inter‐canal posts are present. In these scenarios, if the root end is not resected, there is a risk of recurrence of the periradicular lesion. Research indicates that resecting the apical 3‐mm and preparing the 3‐mm root end during periapical surgery can eliminate 98% of apical ramifications and 93% of lateral canals [13, 14]. In this study, a central incisor underwent endodontic retreatment followed by post and core restoration. However, due to the failure of the retreatment, endodontic surgery was deemed necessary.

In this study, NeoPutty was selected as the root‐end filling material. Introduced in 2020, NeoPutty was developed to address the limitations associated with MTA. According to the manufacturer, NeoPutty is suitable for various endodontic treatments, including direct and indirect pulp capping, partial pulpotomy, pulpotomy, apexogenesis, root resorption, apexification, cavity lining and base application, perforation repair, obturation, and root‐end filling. The efficacy of this bioceramic putty is attributed to its biocompatibility. According to Sun et al. [15], NeoPutty has shown higher biocompatibility than EndoSequence BC RRM putty (Brasseler, USA) in contact with human dental pulp stem cells (hDPCs). Similarly, Lozano‐Guillén et al. [10] reported comparable biocompatibility of NeoPutty towards hDPCs. Other studies have reported acceptable cytocompatibility profiles of NeoPutty on hDPCs, human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (hPDLFs) [15], and human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) [16]. Together, these studies support the cytocompatibility of NeoPutty. NeoPutty also contains calcium aluminate, which has demonstrated adequate biocompatibility after subcutaneous implantation in rats [17] and supported osteogenic cell phenotype acquisition in vitro [18].

The positive outcome in the present study may be attributed to NeoPutty's composition, physical stability, and sealing properties. NeoPutty is mainly composed of tricalcium silicate and dicalcium silicate. Tricalcium silicate‐based cements hydrate to form calcium silicate hydrate (C‐S‐H) and calcium hydroxide (CH). C‐S‐H contributes to the material's sealing ability, while CH release creates an alkaline environment beneficial for tissue repair [5]. These cements also reduce inflammation by inducing anti‐inflammatory cytokines and growth factors. Furthermore, they promote the differentiation of osteoblasts, cementoblasts, and pulpal cells, thereby inducing osteogenic responses in bony defects. When used as a root repair material, these cements directly contact periradicular tissues and can stimulate osseous healing by releasing signaling molecules such as osteocalcin and alkaline phosphatase [19]. According to a systematic review, the success rate of these cements as root repair materials in human permanent teeth with at least a 1‐year follow‐up was 90.49% [19]. Moreover, NeoPutty is wash‐out resistant and dimensionally stable, as stated by the manufacturer. It exhibits zero shrinkage and negligible expansion, ensuring a gap‐free seal. NeoPutty has also demonstrated low volumetric change in both acidic (butyric acid; pH 4.1) and alkaline (phosphate‐buffered saline; pH 7.35) environments [20]. Biodentine, another calcium silicate‐based material, has mechanical properties similar to dentin and high compressive strength. However, it suffers from poor washout resistance and low radiopacity [8]. In contrast, NeoPutty MTA, the predecessor of NeoPutty, has better radiopacity and washout resistance than Biodentine [8].

The favorable outcome in this study may also be attributed to NeoPutty's antibacterial properties, which are related to its alkaline pH [21]. MTA has limited antibacterial activity, particularly against Enterococcus faecalis , the main microorganism involved in pulpal and periradicular diseases and endodontic failures [21]. According to Acharya et al. [21], NeoPutty exhibited limited antimicrobial activity against common oral pathogens such as E. faecalis , Staphylococcus aureus , and Escherichia coli , but showed significant activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa . In another study, Cruz Hondares et al. [22] found that NeoPutty demonstrated acceptable in vitro antimicrobial and mineralization‐supporting properties, along with other calcium silicate‐based cements including ProRoot MTA, MTA Angelus, Biodentine, EndoSequence, and NeoMTA 2. However, further research is needed to evaluate NeoPutty's antibacterial efficacy against microorganisms responsible for endodontic failures.

A major advantage of NeoPutty over MTA is its ease of handling. As a premixed bioceramic, it reduces the potential for mixing and placement errors [9]. MTA, by contrast, has a mud‐like consistency that can be difficult to manipulate, leading to challenges in placement and adaptation within the tooth structure [23]. Improper mixing may also result in a sandy texture and lack of cohesiveness, increasing the risk of washout [24]. While EndoSequence BC Sealer also comes in a premixed format, it has shown higher cytotoxicity than NeoPutty on hDPSCs [15]. Additionally, NeoPutty has better maneuverability and is less susceptible to atmospheric moisture intake compared to EndoSequence BC RRM putty [15]. Lastly, MTA, Biodentine, and EndoSequence BC Sealer are more expensive than NeoPutty, which may limit their accessibility in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Endodontic surgery is often recommended when orthograde retreatment fails. In the present study, the use of NeoPutty resulted in successful outcomes in endodontic surgery after 9 months' follow‐up. NeoPutty was introduced relatively recently and comes in a premixed syringe that enhances its handling properties. Favorable results have been reported regarding its efficacy and biocompatibility, which are comparable to those of MTA in treating pulp and periapical pathologies.
