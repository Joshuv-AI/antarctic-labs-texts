Accuracy comparison of direct Sanger sequencing, immunohistochemistry, and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction in detecting BRAF mutations in papillary thyroid carcinoma: a retrospective diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Moon Young Oh, Man Hon Tang, Young Shin Song, Ka Hee Yi, Young A Kim, Mira Han, Young Jun Chai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe BRAFV600E mutation is a common genetic alteration in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) and is associated with poor prognostic factors. Accurate detection is crucial for risk stratification. This study compares the performance of direct Sanger sequencing (SS), immunohistochemistry (IHC), and droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) in detecting the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC.MethodsTumor samples from patients undergoing thyroidectomy were analyzed for BRAFV600E using SS, IHC, and ddPCR. A mutant allele fraction >1% was considered ddPCR positive. Sensitivity and concordance rates were evaluated.ResultsA total of 48 PTC and 9 benign samples were tested. All benign samples were negative for BRAFV600E by both SS and ddPCR. Among PTC cases, the mutation was detected in 72.9% by SS, 89.6% by IHC, and 83.3% by ddPCR. Both IHC and ddPCR were significantly more sensitive than SS (P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively). Concordant results across all 3 methods were seen in 83.3% of PTC cases. Among the 8 discordant samples (all SS-negative), 5 were positive by both IHC and ddPCR, and 3 were IHC-positive only. Of these, 6 showed adenine peaks on SS chromatograms, with a mean ddPCR mutant allele fraction of 14.5%, compared to 0.36% in the 2 without adenine peaks.ConclusionIHC and ddPCR demonstrated superior sensitivity compared to SS for detecting BRAFV600E mutations in PTC. These findings support the use of IHC and ddPCR as more reliable alternatives to SS in clinical practice.Keywords: Immunohistochemistry, Papillary thyroid cancer, Polymerase chain reaction, Proto-oncogene proteins B-raf, Sequence analysis

INTRODUCTION

The BRAFV600E somatic mutation consists of a thymine to adenine transversion at nucleotide position 1799, resulting in the substitution of valine with glutamate at residue 600 (V600E). In papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the BRAF mutation is the most common genetic alteration, accounting for 35%–65% of cases [1] and is associated with adverse prognostic factors such as lymph node metastasis, advanced stage, recurrence, and cancer-related mortality [2,3]. Detecting the BRAFV600E mutation with high sensitivity is important for the precise diagnosis, prognostication, and treatment selection of patients with PTC. Various molecular and immunohistochemical techniques are available to detect this mutation, each with its own advantages and disadvantages.

Direct Sanger sequencing (SS) is a well-established and reliable method for molecular diagnosis of the BRAFV600E mutation and is widely considered the ‘gold standard.’ However, it has a lower sensitivity compared to other newer molecular diagnostic techniques, especially in tumors with low malignant cell content [4].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is a commonly available and cost-effective method that allows for visual localization of BRAFV600E mutant protein within tissue sections [5]. It is particularly useful in environments with limited resources and provides rapid results. However, the accuracy of IHC is highly dependent on the quality of antibodies used, and the interpretation of the results is relatively subjective. Nevertheless, IHC is a valuable method for BRAFV600E detection, especially when access to molecular facilities is limited.

Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) offers high sensitivity and quantitative identification of the BRAFV600E mutation, even in samples with minimal tumor load. This method partitions the DNA sample into thousands of droplets, allowing for precise quantification of mutant alleles in a background of wild-type sequences. Despite requiring specialized equipment and technical expertise, which limits its widespread adoption in routine clinical practice, ddPCR is widely explored clinically due to its high sensitivity [6,7].

In this study, we analyzed PTC tumor samples and tested BRAF mutation status using SS, IHC, and ddPCR. We also included benign thyroid tumors and normal tissue as a control group and evaluated the performance of these 3 techniques in detecting BRAFV600E mutation in PTC.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Metropolitan Government Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center (No. 10-2024-55). All participants provided written informed consent before participation in the study, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

The medical records of consecutive patients who underwent thyroidectomy at Seoul Metropolitan Government Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center from June 2015 to August 2017 were retrospectively reviewed. Thyroid tumor samples were obtained after the interest lobe was removed during the operation. The tumor samples were taken from the core in a cubic shape, measuring 0.5 cm by 0.5 cm. The sampled tissue was immediately stored in a nitrogen tank. Normal thyroid tissue was obtained from normal contralateral thyroid lobes and served as a control group. All surgical procedures and tissue sampling were done by a single surgeon (YJC).

Immunohistochemistry

IHC analyses were performed using whole sections from representative formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor tissue blocks at a thickness of 4 µm. BRAF IHC staining was conducted on a Benchmark Ultra System (Ventana Medical Systems). The staining utilized BRAFV600E antibody (VE1) (Ventana Medical Systems) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol (retrieval, CC1 32’; incubation, 32’; RTU dilution). IHC results were classified into negative or positive based on cytoplasmic staining by specialist endocrine pathologists who were blinded to the clinical data.

Sanger sequencing

Part of exon 15 of the BRAF gene, where the T1799A transversion mutation (encoding BRAFV600E) is located, was amplified by nested PCR. The purified PCR products were sequenced using the forward primer for the second nested PCR cycle. Data analysis and interpretation were performed with a 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Droplet digital PCR

A Bio-Rad QX200 droplet ddPCR machine was used to quantify DNA that was extracted from tissue or plasma. Twenty to forty nanograms of eluate was added to a ddPCR reaction containing 10 µL ddPCR Supermix for Probes (Bio-Rad) and 250 nM of TaqMan Copy Number Reference Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a total volume of 20 µL. Following the manufacturer’s recommendations, the reaction was partitioned into approximately 20,000 droplets per sample in a QX200 droplet generator. Emulsified PCR reactions were conducted on a 96-well plate on a C1000 thermal cycler, incubating the plates at 95 ℃ for 10 minutes followed by 40 cycles at 94 ℃ for 30 seconds and 60 ℃ for 60 seconds, then 10 minutes of incubation at 98 ℃. The temperature ramp increment was 2 ℃/sec for all steps. Plates were read on a Bio-Rad QX200 droplet reader with QuantaSoft v1.6.6.0320 software from Bio-Rad. At least one negative control well with no DNA was included in every run. Results were expressed as the ratio in percentage, i.e., mutant allele per copy of the wild-type BRAF allele. The cutoff value of the mutant allele percentage for BRAFV600E positivity was set as 1.0%.

Statistical analysis

Comparison of IHC/ddPCR and SS was performed with the Fisher exact test. A P-value <0.05 was considered significant. The statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 27.0 (IBM Corp.).

Tissue samples

Forty-eight PTC and 9 benign (5 normal thyroid tissue, 4 benign thyroid pathology) samples were collected from 48 patients. The benign samples were used as a negative control. DISCUSSION

In this study, both IHC and ddPCR were more sensitive than SS in detecting BRAFV600E mutation in PTC. The high sensitivity of ddPCR is due to its distinctive ability to separate the target DNA into thousands of independent droplets, thus effectively eliminating the impact of inhibitors and the noise signals of the background. Theoretically, it can provide single-molecule sensitivity [8]. This makes ddPCR particularly useful in detecting low-frequency mutations that SS might miss due to its lower sensitivity. In our study, the detection rate of BRAFV600E of ddPCR was 83.3%, whereas that of SS was 72.9%. This result aligns with previous studies demonstrating the superior sensitivity of ddPCR in various cancer types [9,10]. The ability of ddPCR to detect mutations at very low frequencies makes it a powerful tool for early diagnosis and monitoring of low-volume residual disease [11].

SS, considered the gold standard for mutation detection, offers precise identification of the BRAFV600E mutation at the DNA level. This method is highly specific and provides detailed information on the genetic sequence. However, its sensitivity is relatively lower compared to more advanced techniques, particularly in samples with low mutant allele frequency. A low mutant allele frequency of the driver mutation BRAFV600E may indicate a relatively higher proportion of cells comprising the tumor microenvironment, which could be used to estimate the tumor purity of the sample. In this study, for example, 6 samples were reported as BRAF mutation negative on SS, although they had detectable adenine peaks in chromatograms with a mean mutant allele percentage of 14.5% in ddPCR. This observation suggests clinicians should be cautious in interpreting SS results in the samples with lower mutant burden such as contaminated samples or tumors with heterogeneity [12,13].

The cutoff value is critical in interpreting ddPCR results. A lower cutoff might increase sensitivity, allowing for the detection of low-frequency mutant alleles. However, it may also increase the likelihood of false positives, reducing specificity. Conversely, a higher cutoff may enhance specificity at the risk of missing low-level mutations, thereby reducing sensitivity. Depending on the samples examined, the detection limit of ddPCR can be determined differently. In a previous study [14], SS results were shown to be greatly influenced by fractional abundance, corresponding mutant allele percentage in this study. SS detected BRAF mutation in all the samples with fractional abundance >10% in ddPCR, whereas any tumors with fractional abundance <5% could not be detected by SS, suggesting a great discrepancy between the 2 BRAF detection methods depending on mutant allele frequency. In that sense, we may consider adopting a cutoff so that we can detect the mutation highly sensitively in situations where the tumor sample is unlikely to be contaminated because the ddPCR assay provides accurate fractional abundance estimations at 0.0005% for the BRAFV600E mutation [15].

IHC is also known to be more sensitive than SS in detecting BRAFV600E mutation. In the previous studies [16,17], the sensitivity of the IHC relative to SS was 100%, showing similar results to this study. Another study also showed IHC sensitivity of 99.3% in detecting BRAFV600E in PTC, proving its reliability [18]. For this reason, IHC has become more popular in the clinical setting because of its high sensitivity and easy adaptability. On the other hand, IHC results can be subjective depending on the pathologist’s interpretation. This can introduce intra- and inter-observer variability and potential bias [19], affecting the reliability of the results. Another limitation of IHC is the potential for low specificity or high false positive rate. In this study, there were 8 cases of IHC-positive and SS-negative, which can be interpreted as false positive of IHC relative to SS. Nevertheless, 5 of the 8 cases were positive in the ddPCR test, suggesting those cases might be considered as true positives. In this scenario, SS may not be the gold standard, at least in detecting BRAFV600E due to the low sensitivity; and IHC or ddPCR might be the better detection method.

Several studies have suggested that the BRAF mutation is rarely a clonal event, indicating that PTC with a BRAF mutation can exhibit intratumoral heterogeneity [20,21]. Additionally, the observation that the proportion of mutant to total BRAF alleles varies widely, ranging from 4.7% to 48%, further supports the presence of intratumoral heterogeneity in PTC [22]. In one study, the BRAF mutant allele ratio was found to be higher in PTCs with lymph node metastases compared to those without, suggesting a role for the BRAF mutation in the progression of PTC. This observation aligns with previous findings that reported higher BRAF mRNA expression levels were associated with increased tumor aggressiveness in PTC [23]. In the present study, however, we did not observe a correlation between the percentage of mutant BRAF alleles and prognostic factors such as tumor size, lymph node metastasis, or recurrence. The discrepancy may be attributed to the small sample size, which could limit statistical power. Alternatively, the mutant allele percentage may have been influenced by contaminating blood cells or lymphocytes, leading to inaccuracies in the calculation.

In this study, we used standardized cubic tumor tissue samples (0.5 × 0.5 cm) obtained intraoperatively from the tumor core to ensure consistent material for molecular and immunohistochemical analysis. This approach aimed to reduce sampling variability and maximize tumor content across all methods. However, in clinical practice, BRAF mutation testing is often performed on fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology specimens, which may differ in cellular composition and quality. While FNA samples can be subject to variability, prior studies have demonstrated their suitability for molecular analyses. In a study using whole-exome sequencing, Lee et al. showed that FNA specimens could yield tumor DNA of sufficient quantity and purity for comprehensive genomic analysis, with tumor purity occasionally matching that of surgical samples under optimal conditions [24]. Similarly, Lang et al. [25] reported high concordance (89.5%) in BRAF mutation status between matched FNA and surgical specimens, supporting the clinical relevance of FNA-based molecular testing in PTC. These findings suggest that while our tissue sampling method allowed controlled comparisons, further evaluation of test performance using preoperative FNA specimens would be valuable for broader clinical applicability.

This study has several limitations. The study included a relatively small sample size of 48 PTC samples and 9 benign samples. The majority of the samples had the BRAFV600E mutation, which limits the evaluation of wild-type BRAF samples. The high ratio of BRAFV600E positive samples in this study is due to the high prevalence of BRAFV600E mutation in the far east Asian population, which ranges up to 76–86% [26,27]. This nature may lead to a high positive predictive value and affect the generalizability of the findings to a broader population. Second, the study was conducted retrospectively, which may introduce biases related to patient selection and data collection. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings in a more controlled and systematic manner. Third, this study highlighted the importance of determining appropriate cutoff values for ddPCR to balance sensitivity and specificity. However, the optimal cutoff values may vary depending on the sample type and the clinical context. Further research is needed to establish standardized cutoff thresholds for ddPCR in detecting BRAFV600E mutations. Lastly, there were cases where IHC and ddPCR detected the BRAFV600E mutation, but SS did not. While this suggests higher sensitivity of IHC and ddPCR, it also raises the possibility of false positives. Additional validation studies are required to confirm the accuracy of these methods.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that IHC and ddPCR are superior to SS in detecting the BRAFV600E mutation in PTC. ddPCR, with its high sensitivity and quantitative capabilities, provides precise mutation detection even in samples with low tumor burden. IHC, being cost-effective and widely accessible, also offers high sensitivity, although it requires careful interpretation by experienced pathologists. The discrepancies observed between SS and the other methods highlight the need for clinicians to be cautious when interpreting SS results, especially in samples with low mutant allele frequency. Further studies with larger sample sizes and diverse populations are warranted to validate these findings and establish standardized protocols for BRAFV600E mutation detection.
