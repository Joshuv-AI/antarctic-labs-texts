Adult-type granulosa cell tumor of the testis: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Abdulrahman Aljofan, Mohammed Alzahrani, Mohammed Abunayan, Abdulrahman Aldouir, Abdelmoniem Eltiraifi, Wajd Althakfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Testicular granulosa cell tumors are extremely rare types of sex-cord stromal neoplasms. Here we report a 22-year-old man who presented with one month of progressive swelling in his right testis and discomfort. Physical examination revealed a solid mass on the right testicle. The patient underwent right radical inguinal orchiectomy. The histopathological diagnosis supported by immunohistochemical staining confirmed that the specimen represented an adult-type granulosa cell tumor. At 12 months post-orchiectomy, there was no evidence of disease recurrence.

Introduction

Testicular sex-cord stromal tumors represent about 5% of all testicular neoplasms. These tumors arise from the supportive tissues of the testes, including Sertoli, Leydig, and granulosa cells.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies testicular tumors by histopathological criteria. Granulosa cell tumors are subdivided into two categories: the infantile type and the adult type, based upon distinct clinical and histopathologic differences.1

The juvenile type of granulosa cell tumor arises almost exclusively during infancy and represents a benign process, while the adult type develops after pubescence and displays unpredictable biologic behavior that includes the possibility of malignant transformation and distant metastases.2

Adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testis are characterized clinically as slowly growing, painless intratesticular masses. Serum levels of alpha-fetoprotein and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin, which are commonly elevated in testicular germ cell tumors, are usually within the normal range.3,4

For localized disease, radical inguinal orchiectomy is considered the standard treatment. Due to the rare occurrence of this tumor, there is no agreement as to whether adjuvant therapy should be administered and what constitutes the ideal surveillance protocol. Late recurrence or metastasis has occurred in some patients with adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testis, thereby reinforcing the necessity for long-term follow-up.2,5

Radiologic features of adult-type granulosa cell tumors are nonspecific. On ultrasound, they typically exhibit as a well-defined hypoechoic intratesticular mass with possible cystic areas. MRI may show a solid mass with heterogeneous signal intensity, but imaging findings are never sufficient to confirm a definitive diagnosis of adult-type granulosa cell tumor of the testis. Therefore, histopathologic examination is always required.6

Only a few cases of adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testis have been reported since the first documented case was reported in 1952; therefore, this tumor represents a very rare entity.3,7 This study reports another instance in a young adult and reviews the literature to date with a focus on both diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Case presentation

A 22-year-old otherwise healthy male presented with a one-month history of right scrotal swelling that had become mildly painful; he denied fever, dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, hematuria, recent trauma, weight loss, or fatigue. Review of systems was negative for constitutional, neurologic, cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, musculoskeletal, and dermatologic complaints. On examination, he was well-appearing and vitally stable. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams were unremarkable, and the skin showed no lesions. Examination of the right scrotum revealed a firm, smooth, round, 1-cm mass within the testis; the left testis was normal. A scrotal ultrasound (Fig. 1) was done and showed a mixed solid and cystic intratesticular lesion measuring 1.1 × 1.0 cm, which is consistent with a testicular neoplasm, and further work-up including serum tumor markers and hormonal profile was done. Definitive surgical excision (right radical orchiectomy) was done. Histopathology showed that the specimen was showing immunohistochemical and morphological features that are most probably consistent with granulosa cell tumor (Fig. 2A and B). Discussion

The incidence of adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testis is very low, representing about a third of all sex cord–stromal tumors. These types of tumors predominantly occur in men who are in their fourth to fifth decade of life, with the average age of diagnosis being around 40–42 years.5 The occurrence of this type of tumor in a young male, such as in the present case, is thus exceptionally rare.3,7

Although most granulosa cell tumors exhibit an indolent clinical course, they have the potential to be malignant. In contrast to juvenile-type tumors, which are always benign, adult-type tumors are associated with metastasis in about 10–20% of documented cases.2,5 Metastases can occur either at the time of initial diagnosis or many years later after the first treatment for the tumor, which underlines the importance of monitoring patients over long periods.2,5 When metastasis occurs, it most commonly involves the retroperitoneal lymph nodes, followed by the liver, lungs, and bone2,5. Although no metastatic disease was identified in our patient, these findings support the need for prolonged surveillance. Our patient is being followed with periodic clinical examination, scrotal and abdominal ultrasound, and chest imaging at 6-month intervals, in keeping with the unpredictable biologic behavior of this tumor.

Radiological investigation is not particularly useful in identifying adult-type granulosa cell tumors versus other testicular tumors, due to the lack of specificity of ultrasonographic and magnetic resonance imaging findings. Therefore, in order to establish a definite diagnosis of this tumor type, a histopathologic examination is required.6

In our case, the morphology and immunohistochemistry results were consistent with a granulosa cell tumor, demonstrating positivity for CD99, CD56, S100, and vimentin, along with weak and focal positive results for calretinin and WT-1, which are typical for adult-type granulosa cell tumors.1,8 A small number of adult-type granulosa cell tumors have expressed CD56 as well, which could possibly provide additional diagnostic support,7,8 which in our case was positive.

Radical inguinal orchiectomy is considered the standard treatment for localized adult-type granulosa cell tumors. In some cases, testis-sparing surgery has been performed; however, since the malignant potential of adult-type granulosa cell tumors is unpredictable, radical orchiectomy is recommended as the primary procedure.5

In advanced or metastatic disease, the choice of treatment options is limited. Chemotherapy regimens containing platinum-based compounds, including bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin, have been employed with varied success.2,9 Furthermore, there was a study of response to targeted treatments, including anti-angiogenic drugs for adults with chemotherapy-resistant disease; however, these results are anecdotal.10

Several clinicopathologic characteristics have been identified that are believed to predict malignancy, among which are tumor diameter larger than 5 cm, presence of lymphovascular invasion, presence of tumor necrosis, high mitotic rate, and extratesticular extension. Of these characteristics, the largest association with metastatic disease was found with the size of the tumor. However, it has been observed that distant metastases can occur many years post-diagnosis regardless of whether the tumor exhibited any high-risk features, which illustrates the limitation of current predictive factors.5

This case makes several contributions to the current published literature on adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testis. First, our patient is among the youngest reported cases, as the majority of published cases occur in the fourth to fifth decade of life, highlighting that this diagnosis should not be excluded on the basis of young age alone. Second, the immunohistochemical profile in our case, including CD56 positivity, adds to the growing body of evidence supporting CD56 as a potentially useful ancillary diagnostic marker. Third, the normal serum tumor markers in our patient reinforce that alpha-fetoprotein and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin are unreliable for detecting this tumor type, and that histopathologic examination remains the cornerstone of diagnosis.

Conclusion

Adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testes are extremely rare and have unpredictable biologic behaviors. Most adult-type granulosa cell tumors of the testis present as a localized disease that can be treated by surgery; however, the potential for recurrences or distant metastases will require extended follow-up periods. This case demonstrates the importance of including adult-type granulosa cell tumors in the differential diagnosis for masses within the testes, regardless of age and whether or not the patient has normal serum tumor markers.
