Severe pure open dislocation of the ankle and flexor digitorum longus tendon tear a case report
Authors: Yassine Saadi, Alae Neqrachi, Moncif Boufettal, Rida-Allah Bassir, Mohamed Kharmaz, Mohamed Saleh Berrada
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Proximal avulsion rupture of the flexor digitorum longus (FDL) tendon associated with an ankle dislocation is extremely rare. We report a case of a 29 years old patient presenting a severe open ankle dislocation with flexor digitorum longus tendon tear after a motorcycle accident. We performed wound debridement, ankle reduction, tendon repair and stabilization with external fixation. At our last follow-up 17 months after the operation, the patient had good functional outcome and returned to work. This report is noticeable as it is, to the best of our knowledge, the third study reporting a case with simultaneous open ankle dislocation and proximal avulsion rupture of the FDL tendon.

Introduction

Ankle dislocations are very common injuries of the lower limb. The estimated incidence of pure ankle dislocation is 0.065 % of presentations with an ankle injury [1]. Nearly 60 % of ankle dislocations are open and men are affected 5 times more often than women [2]. The dislocation may occur in medial, lateral, posterior and anterior direction in 85 %, 20 %, 2 % and 1 % of cases respectively [3]. Open ankle dislocations have been associated with poor prognosis. Therefore, reduction must be performed as early as possible to avoid further damage to the skin and neurovascular structures [4]. In the following, we aim to present a patient with open lateral subtalar dislocation accompanied with FDL tendon tear, and outline our management strategy along with clinical and radiographic outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, only three cases with similar combination of injuries have been reported in the literature [5].

Case presentation

A 29 years old patient presented to the emergency department with an open injury of the left ankle following a motorcycle accident. Upon physical examination, the patient was conscious and stable. He complained from severe pain in his left ankle. He had a visible 7 cm laceration on the posteromedial side of his ankle with medial extrusion of the talus. He had no pain or complaint in any other parts of his body. Neurovascular assessment found unpalpable posterior tibial pulse without any neurological abnormalities. Radiography and computed tomography (CT) of the left ankle confirmed the diagnosis of isolated lateral subtalar dislocation (Fig. 1). The patient was admitted to the operation room 4 h after the accident. As he had doubts regarding previous tetanus prophylaxis we added metronidazole, 500 mg every 6 h to his antibiotic prophylaxis in addition to cefazolin, 1 g IV every 6 h for 48 h. Under general anesthesia, we performed extensive irrigation and wound debridement. Keeping the knee flexed to relax the gastrocnemius muscle, our first reduction using external maneuvers was unsuccessful. We had to extend the incision 3 cm anteriorly to the existing laceration to better expose the joint. We discovered an entrapment of tibial posterior (TP) tendon and proximal avulsion rupture of the FDL tendon from its musculo tendinous attachment (Fig. 2). Further exploration found a section of posterior tibial artery which was ligated upon vascular surgeon's recommendation. The tibial nerve was intact. After the liberation of TB tendon, the talus was successfully reduced. A tenodesis between flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon and FDL tendon was done. Due to extensive soft tissue damage and in order to protect the tenodesis, we used an external fixator which was removed 6 weeks after. Fluoroscopy showed that the ankle joint matched well, and no significant signs of secondary dislocation under stress was observed (Fig. 3). Post operative examination showed no neurovascular deficit and the patient was discharged 6 days after. The patient proceeded from partial weightbearing at 2 months to full weightbearing at 6 months. Discussion

Subtalar dislocations are defined as the simultaneous dislocation of the talo-calcaneal and talo-navicular joints without any tibio-talar or talar neck associated fractures. It was first described by Dufaurest [6] and Judey [7] in 1811. In 1852, based on the position of the foot relative to the talus along with the mechanism, Fernandes categorized pure subtalar dislocations into four types (Table 1) [8]. Open dislocations represent between 46 and 83 % of ankle dislocations. When lateral, they show more often soft tissue and neurovascular injury, and a higher need for open reduction [4,9]. Clinically, these lesions occur after sustaining a high energy trauma as it is in our case. Various mechanisms of injury have been proposed. The lateral dislocation begins with the foot in eversion. First a deltoid ligament rupture occurs, then a disruption of the interosseous ligament and the talocalcaneal joint followed by dorsal talonavicular ligament failure [10]. If the tension expands, due to high energy forces, further structures could be harmed: proximal avulsion rupture of the FDL. The mechanism of this injury is postulated to be that the valgus/ adduction and external rotation forces at the ankle would cause maximal tensile force within the FDL tendon, causing it to avulse at the musculotendinous junction [5]. Tendon disruptions about the distal tibia and ankle are rare entities. The most common causes of rupture: rheumatoid arthritis, non-specific tenosynovitis, primary tendon degeneration, or direct injury. In our case, there was no clinical history of ankle pain, and there was no tenosynovitis at the time of operation. Flexor digitorum longus rupture has been described in limited publications, once spontaneously [11], once post-tendon transfer [12] and once associated with trauma [5,13]. However, there is no consensus regarding its surgical repair. Yet, all authors agree that primary surgical repair is the treatment of choice. Late repair is difficult and makes normal functional results highly unlikely [13]. Y Ghani performed a primary side- to-side tenodesis of the FDL to the FHL proximally to try and maximize the chances of preventing any loss of function and had good results [5]. H Fujii reported a case of an open ankle fracture associated with the traumatic rupture of the TB tendon and FDL tendon. He had to make a separate incision along the posteromedial border of the tibia in order to confirm and repair the proximal end of the TB and FDL tendons. The tendons were brought distally and sutured to the muscle bellies of the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus in a side-to-side manner [14]. Post-2-year follow-up, the patient had good range of motion with a 30-degree plantar flexion and inversion in the ankle and 30 degrees of flexion in the interphalangeal joints. Controversies still remain regarding ankle ligament repair. For some authors it is necessary to repair the torn ligament at the time of debridement [15]. Others have reported good results without undertaking ligament repair [16]. Recent studies have suggested that ankle ligament repair should only be considered in cases of chronic ankle instability after failed functional rehabilitation and that acute ligament repair has similar outcomes to non-surgical management [17]. As for our patient, he didn't undergo ligament repair but we preferred to use external fixator with distraction so as to maintain a stable ankle joint and increase the probability of spontaneous ligament healing. The functional outcome of open subtalar dislocations depends mainly on two complications: infection and avascular necrosis that must be suspected even after 2 years of follow-up [4]. The use of external fixation can avoid these complications by unloading the subtalar joint and allowing a better management of the associated soft tissues injuries [18].

In a patient with open lateral subtalar dislocation caused by atypical direct force, careful muscular and neurovascular examination should be carried out, especially when the dislocation cannot be reduced. Therefore, soft-tissue entrapment should be suspected particularly when the tendon shows an atypical course around the incision. Posterior tibialis tendon or the flexor halluces longus remain the typical anatomical structures caught between the talus and the calcaneum. It is imperative, then, to consider the possibility of avulsion rupture of the FDL tendon.

Conclusion

There are few reports on open ankle dislocations with tendons tear. Hence, we think that this report and its literature review could be beneficial in managing such complicated emergency scenarios. However, higher level of evidence with larger sample size could provide better way to understanding and more reasonable treatment approach.
