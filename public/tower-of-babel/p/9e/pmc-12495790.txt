Impact of iron deficiency on therapeutic outcomes in colorectal cancer patients: a single-center cohort study
Authors: Yajun Luo, Ping Zheng, Hao Luo, Xin Chen, Ke Zhang, Wuyi Wang, Bo Song, Chao Liu, Lubei Rao, Hui Yang, Xuan Zhang, Qingmei Huang, Haiyang Zhou, Hai Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron deficiency (ID) exhibits strikingly high prevalence in colorectal cancer (CRC), yet its prognostic implications remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to evaluate the association between pre-treatment ID status and therapeutic outcomes in patients undergoing standardized treatment protocols for CRC.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 1003 CRC patients was analyzed to assess the prevalence of ID and its correlations with clinicopathologic features, postoperative recovery, neoadjuvant therapy response, and iron metabolism and ferroptosis-related gene expression. The association of ID with clinicopathologic data, laboratory parameters, and treatment patient outcome was analyzed using logistic regression. Prussian blue staining was used to assess iron levels in tumor and adjacent noncancerous tissues. Bioinformatics was employed to analyze the expression levels of iron metabolism and ferroptosis-related genes.ResultsID was identified in 50.85% (510/1003) of patients. Compared with non-ID patients, those with ID exhibited higher female predominance (56.8% vs. 43.2%), significant increased prevalence of anemia (76.2% vs. 23.8%), and elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), along with decreased albumin (Alb) levels. Clinicopathologic associations with ID included larger tumor diameter, more advanced pathological T/N/M stages, poorer tumor differentiation, and increased lymphovascular and perineural invasion. Among patients received neoadjuvant therapy, ID was associated with higher clinical T/N stages and lower tumor regression grades. Postoperatively, ID patients experienced significantly longer time to first flatus, lower albumin levels, and elevated inflammatory markers. Prussian blue staining revealed reduced iron content in both tumor and adjacent tissues of ID patients. Molecular analyses identified dysregulated iron metabolism genes, with DMRT1, HAMP, FTH1, FTL, TFRC, LCN2, PCBP1 and PCBP2 upregulated and ACO1, IRPEB2, OTUD1, SLC40A1 and NCOA4 downregulated in tumor tissues. Ferroptosis suppressor genes (LCN2, TFRC and SLC40A1) were overexpressed in non-responders to chemoradiotherapy.ConclusionID is closely associated with aggressive tumor biology, suboptimal response to neoadjuvant therapy, and impaired postoperative recovery. Dysregulation of iron homeostasis and ferroptosis pathways in ID patients may contribute to CRC progression. These findings highlight ID as a potential biomarker for risk stratification and suggest that targeting iron metabolism could improve therapeutic outcomes in CRC management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07018-9.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Iron deficiency, Anaemia, Ferroptosis, Iron homeostasis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a formidable global public health challenge, with recent epidemiological data revealing its incidence and mortality rate ranks as one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide [1, 2]. While advancements in surgical techniques, systemic therapies, and targeted interventions, the 5-year survival rate for metastatic CRC remains below 15%, a sobering proportion of patients continue to confront daunting prognoses [3]. This persistent therapeutic gap underscores the critical need for deeper investigation into the complex biological and clinical variables influencing treatment efficacy and patient outcomes.

Iron homeostasis, a tightly regulated process involving absorption, storage, transport, and utilization of iron, is progressively recognized as one of the important players in oncogenesis [4]. The molecular landscape of iron metabolism involves key genes such as HAMP (hepcidin), which regulates intestinal iron absorption, TFRC (transferrin receptor), critical for iron uptake; and FTH1/FTL (ferritin subunits), responsible for iron storage; and iron-dependent cell death pathways such as ferroptosis [5]. Dysregulation of these genes may precipitate either iron deficiency (ID) or excessive iron accumulation. Significantly, ID is a common comorbidity in CRC patients, often intertwined with anemia, inflammation and altered iron homeostasis [6, 7].

While ID is traditionally associated with nutritional deficiencies or chronic blood loss, emerging evidence suggests it may play a role in tumor progression, treatment response and influencing postoperative outcomes in CRC [8, 9]. Preliminary investigation had sought to elucidate iron-cancer pathomechanisms through in vitro models [10]. Notably, mechanistic studies demonstrated that ID disrupts key signaling pathways within CRC cells, including those governing cell cycle regulation, oxidative stress, impair immune surveillance, and DNA biosynthesis and repair, potentially contributing to tumorigenesis and disease progression [11–13], while ferroptosis-related gene expression could influence tumor response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [14, 15].

Anemia, a frequent consequence of ID, affects 40–60% of CRC patients and is associated with poorer quality of life, increased treatment toxicity and worse survival outcomes [16]. ID contributing to a substantial proportion of anemia cases in cancer populations [17]. Despite its clinical relevance, the relationship between ID, tumor biology and therapeutic response in CRC requires further exploration, especially in the context of neoadjuvant therapy where treatment efficacy is critical for surgical outcomes [18, 19].

In light of the above, the current study aimed to explore the prevalence of ID in CRC patients and its correlation with clinicopathologic characteristics, postoperative outcomes, neoadjuvant therapy outcomes and iron homeostasis gene expression. Specifically, we endeavored to delineate the demographic and laboratory distinctions between CRC patients with and without ID, and to evaluate the influences of ID on tumor staging, tissue differentiation, and response to neoadjuvant therapy; measure iron levels in tumor and adjacent tissues using Prussian blue staining; and analyze the expression of key iron metabolism and ferroptosis-related genes in CRC through bioinformatics methods. By synthesizing clinical, pathological and molecular data, this study seeks to enhance our comprehension of ID’s impact on CRC progression and to guide strategies for improving patient management.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

The study population consisted of CRC patients hospitalized at the Sichuan Cancer Hospital from December 2022 to December 2024. A total of 1003 CRC patients were included in the study. Patients with incomplete data and patients who did not undergo surgery were excluded (n = 55). Additionally, patients were excluded if they had received any blood product transfusion or intravenous iron administration prior to diagnosis (n = 20). Out of these, 800 patients underwent surgery directly, while 203 patients received neoadjuvant therapy before surgery. Tumor and paired adjacent noncancerous tissues, along with detailed clinicopathologic parameters, were collected for these patients. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sichuan Cancer Hospital, Chengdu, China.

Data collection

The clinicopathologic features of the patients such as gender, age, (Body Mass Index) BMI, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, family and personal history, tumor site, degree of differentiation, clinical stage, pathological stage, MMR/MSI status, tumor regression grade (TRG), postoperative complication and laboratory parameters were collected. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status scores were uniformly 0 for all patients. The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging was determined as below: for patients in whom surgery served as the primary mode of treatment, the pTN staging derived from the pathological report was utilized, incorporating the M staging when assessed. Conversely, for patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, the clinical cTNM staging was employed, based on radiological findings at the time of diagnosis. TRG diagnostic criteria for CRC were based on pathological American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) diagnostic criteria. To avoid inter-rater variability, we employed a rigorous approach: centralized review by two independent pathologists fully blinded to clinical data, utilization of standardized AJCC criteria, and resolution of any discrepancies by a third expert pathologist.

Laboratory measurements

Data was integrated with the hospital’s laboratory data, including serum iron, transferrin saturation (TSAT), hemoglobin (Hb), erythrocyte counts, leukocyte counts, lymphocyte (Ly) levels, leukocyte ratios, total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), triglyceride levels, cholesterol levels, C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin (PCT). These values were obtained at the time of diagnosis and blood was checked for the first time after surgery (fasting morning draws between 7 and 9 AM) to ensure the dataset was as complete as possible. Anaemia is defined according to the World Health Organization criteria as follows: male Hb < 130 g/L and female Hb < 120 g/L. Iron deficiency is defined as transferrin saturation (TSAT) < 20% [8].

Tissue Prussian blue staining

The tissue Prussian Blue staining was performed using the Prussian Blue Staining Kit (Solarbio Science & Technology). The process involved organizing paraffin embedding and cutting sections into 3–7 μm slices, followed by routine deparaffinization and rehydration. The reagent kit was removed from the refrigerator and allowed to warm up to room temperature (25–30 °C) for 20 min. The water tank of the wet box was filled and preheated in a 37 °C constant temperature incubator for 20 min. The Perl’s staining working solution was prepared using a 1:1 ratio, applied to the tissue sections to ensure complete coverage, and incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 20 min. The sections were then removed, gently rinsed with distilled water three times, each for 10 s. The incubation solution was applied to the sections until the tissue was completely covered, and incubated in the wet box at 37 °C for 10–20 min. After removal, the sections were gently rinsed with 1×PBS three times, each for 60 s. The working solution was prepared according to the ratio of Cl: C2:1×PBS = 1:1:18, and the diluted working solution could be stored at 2–8 °C for one week. The enhancement working solution was applied to the sections until the tissue was completely covered, and incubated in the wet box at 37 °C for 10–20 min. Finally, the sections were rinsed with distilled water for 10 min, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Bioinformatics analysis

The expression of fundamental iron metabolism genes in cancer tissues and normal tissues was obtained from the TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ datasets. Ferroptosis-related genes were analyzed according to the FerrDb V2 database [20] (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/). The expression levels of these genes in CRC patients, stratified into responders and non-responders to chemoradiotherapy, were obtained from the ROC Plotter database [21] (https://www.rocplot.org/).

Statistical analysis

All statistical data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 17.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The correlation was analyzed using binary logistic regression. Values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between groups were assessed using an independent-sample t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Chi-Square test, or Fisher’s exact test. Post-hoc power analysis revealed an more than 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect iron-level differences across subgroups with a Phi effect size of 0.2. In the logistic regression model, we first conducted collinearity analysis to exclude variables with absolute correlation coefficients above 0.8, and confirmed good model fit through the Hosmer-Lemeshow test with p > 0.05. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Prevalence and demographic characteristics of iron deficiency in CRC

The present study included 1,003 patients with CRC, of which 510 (50.85%) were identified as ID, and 493 (49.15%) were identified as no-ID. The basic characteristics of the study patients were presented in Table 1. The mean age for this cohort was 60.52 years, with a higher proportion of males than females (61.2% vs. 38.8%). It was noteworthy that among male patients, the number of patients with ID was less than those with no-ID (47.1% vs. 52.9%), whereas among female patients, the number of patients with ID was more than those with no-ID (56.8% vs. 43.2%). In patients with ID, ASA classification I was less common than in patients with no-ID (37.7% vs. 62.3%), whereas ASA classifications II (52.5% vs. 47.5%) and III (51.5% vs. 48.5%) were more prevalent than in patients with no-ID. Furthermore, in patients with ID, anemia and a history of smoking and alcohol consumption account for 76.2% and 44.6% compared with those with no-ID, respectively. No significant differences were observable between both cohorts in terms of BMI, age, previous abdominal surgery, family history of CRC, neoadjuvant therapy and comorbidities. In laboratory variables, patients with ID exhibited lower levels of serum iron, TSAT, erythrocytes, Hb, Ly, Ly ratio, Alb, triglycerides, and cholesterol compared to those with no-ID. Conversely, leukocyte and CRP were higher in patients with ID than in those with no- ID.

Iron deficiency as a marker of aggressive tumor biology in CRC

Compared to patients with no-ID patients, those with ID exhibited significantly larger maximum tumor diameters and higher CEA levels, a greater likelihood of tumor obstruction, higher tumor pathological T staging, M staging and TNM staging, poorer tissue differentiation, more lymphovascular and perineural invasion, more liver and lung metastases, and a higher possibility of MSI-High and MMR-deficiency. Compared to rectal cancer, colon cancer patients were more prone to developing ID, particularly in the right semicolon (Table 2). Upon conducting a more detailed subgroup analysis of neoadjuvant patients, it was observed that the clinical T and N stages of ID patients were significantly higher than those of no-ID patients, and the degree of TRG after adjuvant treatment was lower than that of those of no-ID (Table 2).

Impact of iron deficiency on postoperative outcomes

Upon comparing patients with no-ID patients to those with ID, it was observed that the latter group received more blood transfusions, experienced a longer duration until the first flatus, and had more intraabdominal fluid postoperatively (Table 3). In the initial postoperative blood examination, patients with ID exhibited lower levels of Alb, erythrocytes, and Hb compared to patients with no-ID. Additionally, inflammatory markers such as CRP and PCT were significantly elevated in patients with ID (Table 3).

Impact of CRC progression on iron deficiency and anaemia

As shown in Fig. 1A, upon comparing the changes in serum iron and TSAT levels across different stages, it was observed that both serum iron and TSAT were at their lowest in patients with pT3, suggesting an ID state (TSAT = 19.8%); in the N stage, the lowest levels of serum iron and TSAT were found in patients with pN1, also indicative of an ID state (TSAT = 19.94%); in the M stage, serum iron and TSAT reached their lowest in patients with pM1, pointing to an ID state (TSAT = 17.97%); and in the TNM stage, serum iron and TSAT were noted to decrease as the staging increased, with patients in stage IV exhibiting an ID state (TSAT = 17.96%). By comparing the degree of tumor differentiation, it was observed that patients with lower differentiation exhibited lower serum iron and TSAT levels, with an ID state noted in poorly differentiated patients (TSAT = 18.87%) (Fig. 1B). As the distance from the anal verge increased from the rectum to the colon, serum iron and TSAT levels were found to decrease, with the lowest levels observed in patients with right semicolon cancer. Here, TSAT was 15.85% for right semicolon cancer and 17.06% for transverse colon cancer, indicating an ID state (Fig. 1C).

The multivariate logistic regression indicated a significant association between ID and five variables (Table 4). The pT-stage was significantly associated with ID, with each stage having significantly higher odds of ID than pT0 and pT1, although pT2 did not reach statistical significance. The pM-stage was also significantly associated with ID, with significantly higher odds of ID than pM0. The degree of tumor differentiation was significantly associated with ID, with significantly higher odds of ID than well-differentiated tumors. Although the trend was for the odds to increase with each decrement in tumor differentiation, middle differentiation did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, anemia, CRP, and being female were significantly correlated with ID, increasing the likelihood of ID.

A total of 42.8% (429/1003) patients were anaemic at the time of diagnosis. We found that 76.2% (327/429) of the anaemic patients and 31.9% (183/574) of the non-anaemic patients were iron deficient. ID, tumor sites, pN stage and Alb were all significantly associated with anaemia in multivariate logistic regression, (Table S1). Among them, iron deficiency, tumor sites, and pN stage were associated with an increased risk of anemia, whereas Alb levels were associated with a decreased risk of anemia.

Impact of iron deficiency on neoadjuvant therapy response for CRC

Among neoadjuvant patients, serum iron and TSAT levels were lower in those with cT4 differentiation compared to cT3 patients, although the difference was not statistically significant; serum iron and TSAT levels were also lower in cN + patients compared to cN0 patients, and none of these patients were in an ID state (Figure S1A). Further analysis of the TRG degree revealed that serum iron and TSAT levels were highest in TRG 1 patients and lowest in TRG 3 patients, with the latter being in an ID state, but the serum iron level was not statistically significant (Figure S1B).

The precise regulation of cellular iron metabolism relies on the coordinated interplay of various processes: absorption (mediated by DMRT1, HAMP, and TMPRSS6), storage (facilitated by FTH1/FTL and NCOA4), transport (involving TFRC and SLC40A1) and upstream signaling (regulated by LCN2, ACO1, IRPEB2, PCBP1/2 and OTUD1). By comparing the expression of the above genes in TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ dateset, it was found that DMRT1, HAMP, FTH1, FTL, TFRC, LCN2, PCBP1 and PCBP2 were upregulated in tumor tissues, while ACO1, IRPEB2, OTUD1 and SLC40A1 and NCOA4 were downregulated (Fig. 2B). Through FerrDb database analysis, it was identified that eight genes were associated with ferroptosis regulation. Specifically, ACO1, IRPEB2 and NCOA4 were classified as ferroptosis driver genes, while LCN2, TFRC, FTH1, FTL, and SLC40A1 were categorized as ferroptosis suppressor genes (Fig. 2B).

To further clarify the impact of ferroptosis-gene regulators on the efficacy of radiochemotherapy in CRC, we compared the expression levels of ferroptosis-gene regulators in CRC patients with response or non-response using the ROC Plotter database. The results showed that TFRC, LCN2, SLC40A1, IRPEB2, and NCOA4 were highly expressed in the non-response group, while FTH1 was lowly expressed. Notably, FTL and ACO1 showed no significant differences in expression between the two groups (Fig. 2C).

Discussion

The present study highlights the critical role of ID in shaping clinical outcomes and treatment responses in CRC. This study identified ID in 50.85% of CRC patients, highlighting its high prevalence in this population. The female predominance in ID patients (56.8% vs. 31.9%) aligns with known sex-based differences in iron metabolism, possibly influenced by menstrual blood loss, dietary habits or hormonal factors [7]. A total of 42.8% patients were anaemic at the time of diagnosis, and ID was strongly associated with anemia (76.2% vs. 31.9%), consistent with previous reports where ID accounts for up to 88% of anemia cases in CRC [8]. Elevated CRP in ID patients may signal an inflammatory component, as chronic inflammation disrupts iron homeostasis by upregulating hepcidin. This cascade reduces intestinal iron absorption and promotes iron sequestration within reticuloendothelial cells, ultimately exacerbating ID [22]. However, potential confounding factors could influence these research outcomes. Crucially, this acute-phase reaction concurrently elevates serum ferritin, making it unreliable for ruling out ID. Therefore, we primarily defined ID using TSAT < 20%, a strategy aimed at minimizing the impact of inflammation.

Clinicopathologic analyses revealed that ID was linked to larger tumor diameter, advanced pathological T/N/M stages, poor differentiation, and increased lymphovascular/perineural invasion. These findings support the hypothesis that ID promotes tumor progression by inducing oxidative stress, impairing DNA repair, and enhancing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [6]. For example, ID may upregulate hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α), driving angiogenesis and metastasis [16]. The association with right-sided colon cancer aligns with anatomical differences in iron absorption and tumor biology, as right-sided tumors may cause occult blood loss and malabsorption, exacerbating ID [23].

Multivariate regression analysis identified pT stage, pM stage, poor differentiation, anemia, and female as independent risk factors for ID. This suggests that ID is not merely a consequence of tumor progression but may actively contribute to it. Iron is essential for DNA synthesis and redox reactions, and its deficiency could lead to genomic instability and uncontrolled proliferation, particularly in poorly differentiated tumors [24]. ID patients exhibited prolonged time to first flatus, lower albumin levels, and increased inflammatory markers postoperatively, indicating impaired recovery. This may be attributed to reduced tissue healing capacity due to iron-dependent enzyme dysfunction (e.g., cytochrome C oxidase in mitochondria) and heightened systemic inflammation [25]. Perioperative blood transfusions, more frequent in ID patients, were associated with immunosuppression and increased infection risk, further complicating outcomes [17].

In the neoadjuvant therapy subgroup, ID was associated with higher clinical T/N stages and poorer tumor regression grades. This aligns with preclinical studies shown that ID reduces sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy by suppressing ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent cell death [26]. Specifically, multivariate regression analysis identified ID and larger tumor diameter as independent risk factors for suboptimal response, with ID increasing the odds of poor regression by 3.084 fold. Although serum iron and transferrin saturation (TSAT) showed a trend toward lower levels in advanced clinical T/N stages (cT4/cN+), the lack of statistical significance may reflect the complex interplay between iron status, tumor biology, and therapeutic exposure.

The mechanistic link between ID and neoadjuvant resistance likely involves ferroptosis regulation, a form of iron-dependent programmed cell death critical for chemoradiotherapy efficacy. TRG3 patients (poor regression) exhibited the lowest TSAT levels (indicating ID), coinciding with upregulation of ferroptosis suppressor genes (LCN2, TFRC, SLC40A1) in non-responders. Recent studies have also reported MVP-LCN2 axis triggers evasion of ferroptosis by reducing lipid peroxidation and intracellular Fe2+ to drive hepatocarcinogenesis and sorafenib resistance [27], and the IGF1R inhibitor induced ferroptosis through blockage of PI3K/AKT/NRF2 signaling pathway and subsequent inhibition of downstream gene expression of SLC40A1 in hepatocellular carcinoma [28]. The above studies further corroborate the emerging role of ID by those ferroptosis suppressor genes in therapeutic resistance across malignancies. TFRC plays a dual role, serving both as a ferroptosis suppressor gene, with the inhibitory effect of TFRC deletion on erastin-induced ferroptosis [29], and as a driver gene, CCT3/ACTN4/TFRC axis protects hepatocellular carcinoma cells from ferroptosis by inhibiting iron endocytosis [30]. This study confirms that TFRC exhibits elevated expression in CRC and non-responders, indicating that TFRC functions as an oncogene and ferroptosis suppressor gene in CRC. The specific mechanism may be related to the post-translational modifications of TFRC [31].

It is noteworthy to observe that ferroptosis driver genes (IRPEB2, NCOA4) were also upregulated in non-responders. Furthermore, the differential expression of these ferroptosis-related genes between responders and non-responders suggests a potential role in predicting treatment outcome. The upregulation of suppressor genes in non-responders may contribute to their resistance to neoadjuvant therapy by inhibiting ferroptosis, thereby reducing the efficacy of chemoradiotherapy. Conversely, the upregulation of driver genes in the same group could indicate an attempt by the cancer cells to induce ferroptosis, possibly as a compensatory mechanism in response to therapeutic pressure [32, 33]. However, this upregulation appears insufficient to overcome the dominant suppressive effect, leading to overall resistance.

Prussian blue staining revealed a universal reduction in iron levels in both tumor and adjacent noncancerous tissues of ID patients, confirming tissue-level iron depletion that transcends tumor microenvironment effects. Molecular profiling identified thirteen genes related to iron metabolism that were dysregulated in tumor tissues. Specifically, genes involved in iron absorption (DMRT1 and HAMP), storage (FTH1/FTL and TFRC), and upstream signaling (LCN2, PCBP1, and PCBP2) were upregulated. Conversely, genes associated with iron storage (NCOA4), export (SLC40A1), and upstream signaling (ACO1, IRP2B2, and OTUD1) were downregulated. These findings suggest a complex deregulation of iron metabolism in ID patients, which may contribute to the pathogenesis of their tumors.

The upregulation of genes involved in iron absorption, storage, and positive upstream signaling, coupled with the downregulation of genes associated with iron export and negative upstream signaling in tumor tissues, indicates an increased demand for iron in cancer cells and a retention of iron within the tumor microenvironment. This is potentially due to their rapid proliferation and metabolic needs. This finding aligns with systemic iron sequestration driven by chronic inflammation (e.g., hepcidin upregulation) and impaired intestinal absorption, as evidenced by the upregulation of HAMP (which encodes hepcidin), restricting iron release from enterocytes and macrophages, and TFRC, which mediates transferrin-dependent iron uptake in tumor tissues [5, 34]. This dysregulation creates a protective phenotype against oxidative stress induced by radiotherapy or chemotherapy, explaining the poor response in ID patients. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the complex interplay between ID, ferroptosis regulation, and neoadjuvant resistance, potentially leading to the development of novel therapeutic strategies to overcome resistance and improve patient outcomes.

The findings suggest that ID could serve as a biomarker for risk stratification, identifying patients with aggressive tumors and suboptimal treatment response. Preoperative iron repletion strategies, such as intravenous iron therapy, may improve postoperative recovery and neoadjuvant efficacy by correcting iron deficiency and modulating ferroptosis[36]. However, the study’s retrospective design, limited sample size, inter-rater variability in tumor assessment, and potential selection bias from excluding patients with incomplete records or those who did not undergo surgery restrict causal inference; prospective trials are essential to validate these associations. Furthermore, future investigations should incorporate multi-center collaborations to enhance sample diversity and statistical power, thereby addressing these constraints. Crucially, exploring the mechanistic pathways linking iron deficiency correction to ferroptosis modulation could provide insights for targeted therapeutic interventions. Moreover, integrating longitudinal data on patient outcomes post-iron repletion could substantially strengthen the clinical applicability of these findings and facilitate their translation into personalized oncology strategies.

In conclusion, ID is a prevalent and clinically relevant comorbidity in CRC, closely associated with aggressive tumor features, impaired postoperative recovery, and reduced response to neoadjuvant therapy. Dysregulation of iron homeostasis and ferroptosis pathways in ID patients provides a mechanistic link to cancer progression. These findings underscore the importance of routine iron status assessment in CRC management and highlight iron metabolism as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.
