The relationship between physical activity and academic achievement in medical students: a single-institution cross-sectional study in Sudan
Authors: Saud Mohammed Raja, Mohammed Ahmed Elfaki, Alaa Ibrahim, Hibaallah Maroof, Abrar Abdelrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegular physical activity has been consistently shown to improve cognitive ability among populations of various age groups, including university students. This is particularly important among medical students, who face high academic demands and a relatively stressful environment. Some evidence suggests that regular physical activity has a positive impact on academic success during medical school. This study aimed to assess the relationship between physical activity and academic achievement among medical students in Sudan.MethodsA descriptive, institution-based, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine in Omdurman Islamic University in Sudan. Medical students were recruited into the study using a systematic random sampling technique in the year 2020. Data were collected through a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Science software version 23. Level of physical activity was stratified by gender, place of residence, and parental educational level. A correlation was sought between the level of physical activity according to the World Health Organization recommendations and academic achievement measured in grade point average (GPA).ResultsA total of 295 medical students were interviewed of which 141 (47.8%) were males and 154 (52.2%) were females. The mean age of the students who participated in the study was 21.13 ± 1.42 years. More than 76% of the students were aware of the WHO recommendation of physical activity with statistically significant increase across the year of study (p < 0.05). Of the total 295 participants, 166 (56.3%) were physically active, while 129 (43.7%) did not meet the minimum recommended physical activity. Walking was the main type of physical activity and the leading barrier to regular physical activity was study load. The mean GPA in the physically active students was 3.09 ± 0.47, while the mean GPA of the physically inactive group was 3.16 ± 0.44 out of 4 (p-value = 0.7). No statistically significant relationship between the level of physical activity and academic achievement was observed.ConclusionWe found that nearly half of the medical students did not meet the recommended level of physical activity. Moreover, regular physical activity during medical school was not directly associated with the students’ academic achievement as measured by the GPA. Given the overall health benefits of exercise, however, further comprehensive research including various medical schools is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07985-z.

Background

The overall role of physical activity in maintaining physical as well as mental fitness and preventing chronic medical conditions has long been proven. Since physical activity has been consistently shown to improve cognitive ability among the population of various age groups, its potential relationship with academic achievement has drawn the attention of researchers in education and health. This is particularly important among university students who face high academic demands and a relatively stressful environment. Medical students are a unique subgroup of this population who endure long hours of study and clinical training. They are thus at risk of increased stress, burnout, and sedentary behavior which all may negatively impact overall health and academic performance [1].

Studies have shown that regular physical activity has a number of physical, mental, and cognitive health benefits. Physically, many body systems show a positive impact. Higher levels of physical exercise increase cardiac output, reduce systemic inflammation, and improve overall cardio-respiratory fitness [2]. The enhanced pulmonary effects of physical activity support sustained physical and metal performance [3]. Aerobic exercise increases the size of hippocampus in the brain, which is associated with increased neurogenesis, elevated brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and synaptic plasticity thereby improving learning and memory [4]. Physical activity also impacts the endocrine system and hormonal secretions. Exercise stimulates the release of endorphins and serotonin which are known to elevate mood and enhance focus [5]. Mentally, physical activity mitigates anxiety, alleviates depression, and boost overall mental fitness [6]. The collective impact of these body changes includes enhancing cognitive functions including memory, attention, and executive abilities. Consequently, students who engage in regular physical activity demonstrate improved concentration, faster information processing, and better resilience to stress which translate to better academic achievement [7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines physical activity as “any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure [8].” This definition encompasses a wide range of moderate-intensity activities like walking to vigorous physical exercise such as running or playing sports. To achieve satisfactory physical and mental health, the WHO recommends adults (18–64 years) at least 150–300 min of moderate-intensity or 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week. An equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous physical activity also meets the recommendations. In addition, regular muscle-strengthening activities have to be included on 2 or more days per week. Accordingly, medical students are expected to attain this amount and intensity of physical activity in line with their busy study schedule. Nevertheless, a recent study has reported that nearly one-third of the world’s adult population does not meet these WHO recommendations for minimum physical activity.

Higher academic achievement, often expressed by grade point average (GPA), is a determinant element in the scholarly and professional life of medical students. Several factors influence academic performance among medical students including personal factors such as motivation and self-discipline, social status, and learning environment. Some studies specifically addressing medical students have reported that regular physical activity is linked to reduced mental stress, improved cognitive function, and better academic performance [9–11]. Although the positive impact of physical exercise is highly intuitive, the direct positive effect on academic performance among medical students is not clearly established in the literature. A study conducted among medical students in Saudi Arabia reported that physical activity was not found to affect academic performance [12]. Similarly, physical fitness was poorly associated with academic performance at military medical schools in the United States [13]. The study suggested that physical fitness and body mass index (BMI) may decline during medical training presumably due to increasing academic demands and clinical rotations.

The level of physical activity and its relationship to academic achievement among Sudanese medical students across the country is not well-studied. A recent study conducted in one of the leading medical schools in the country found that more than half of the medical students (60%) were physically inactive according to the WHO recommendations [14]. Shortage of time and lack of suitable facilities were the main perceived barriers to physical activity among the students. The academic impact of this high level of physical inactivity and perceived barriers to regular exercise in the high-stress and resource-limited academic environment as in Sudan is unknown. Theoretically, at least, fostering an environment conductive for regular physical activity would enhance the physical, mental, and cognitive fitness of the medical students thereby enhancing their academic performance. Whether the demographic characteristics of the students such as parental educational level and accommodation influence the physical activity and academic success of the students is uncertain. Although the health repercussions of sedentary lifestyle are unquestionable, its prevalence among medical students and impact on their academic journey needs to be studied. Results from this study can assist formulating evidence-based policy recommendations for local and regional medical schools with comparable setups, enhance health promotion strategies, and allow targeted interventions for disadvantaged groups. The current study aimed to explore the reality of physical activity among medical students in Sudan, as well as the potential relationship between regular physical activity and academic achievement (GPA), and differences in the level of physical activity according to gender, place of residence, and parent’s educational level.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among students of the faculty of medicine at Omdurman Islamic University (OIU). The university is situated in Omdurman, a major city in the Khartoum State in Sudan.

Study population

Medical students in active study in their first three years at the OIU, faculty of medicine were selected as the study population. At the time of the study, 1130 students were registered by the academic affairs office from which the sample population was obtained. Students with physical disability and those with known academic issues that affect their academic achievement were excluded.

Variables

The dependent variable in the study was academic achievement while physical activity was the independent variable. Background variables included demographic data such as age, gender, and year of study.

Sampling technique

A systematic random sampling technique was applied to recruit participants into the study in a manner that allows a similar representation of students across the study years in the year 2020. In cases where a randomly selected individual refused to participate, we took the next candidate with the same interval and then returned to the original interval.

Data collection and analysis

We prepared a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire that involved three sections (see supplementary file). The first section consisted of the socio-demographic variables such as age, sex, and origin. The second section addressed the type of physical activity, duration, and frequency. The third section involved the academic achievement of students. This study instrument was constructed through modification of the validated WHO Global physical activity questionnaire (GPAQ) to make it relevant to our study population and study objectives [15]. The GPAQ has shown acceptable evidence of validity and reliability in several studies across multiple countries [16, 17]. The level and intensity of physical activity was determined according to the WHO definitions and tools [8]. A pilot test of the questionnaire was conducted to refine the wording and ascertain consistent understanding of the questions prior to drafting the final questionnaire used for data collection. Data regarding academic performance for the study year were obtained from the academic affairs office of the university. Participants were interviewed via telephone when direct contact was inconvenient.

The data were coded and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 23. Descriptive data were presented as graphs and charts. Quantitative data were summarized using mean and standard deviation and qualitative data were presented as percentages. The independent samples t-test was used to determine whether the mean GPA differed significantly between physically active and inactive students. The chi-square test was used to test association between categorical variables. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the association between variables of physical activity and GPA. Statistical significance was accepted when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Demographics

A total of 295 medical students were interviewed of which 141 (47.8%) were males and 154 (52.2%) were females. The mean age of the students who participated in the study was 21.13 ± 1.42 years. Of the 295 students, 196 (66.4%) lived with their parents, 85 (28.8%) were residing in student hostels while the remaining 14 (4.7%) participants had other forms of accommodation such as living with friends or renting alone. First year medical students were 87 (29.5%), second year 97 (32.9%), and 111 (37.6%) were in their third year. The highest educational level of the parents was university level in 209 (70.9%), primary education 6 (2.0%), secondary education in 73 (24.7%) while the remaining 7 (2.4%) medical students came from illiterate parental education backgrounds.

Physical activity: level and types

Participants were asked about their knowledge of the WHO recommendation of physical activity for adults and 226 (76.6%) were aware of that while 69 (23.4%) were unaware of the recommendation. The awareness of the recommendation was highest among third year medical students (Fig. 1). There was a statistically significant correlation between the year of study and awareness of the WHO recommendation both in males and females as shown in Table 1 (p < 0.05). Based on the WHO recommendation, 166 (56.3%) medical students met the minimum targets of weekly physical activity while the remaining 129 (43.7%) were physically inactive (Fig. 2). Of the 166 physically active students, 86 (51.8%) reported daily physical activity, 74 (44.7%) were physically active every week and 6 (3.6%) had irregular physical activity equivalent to the minimum recommendation. While 137 (82.5%) medical students practiced habitual moderate-intensity physical activity, 29 (17.5%) were involved in vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise including muscle-strengthening physical activities (Fig. 3). Walking was the main physical activity in 71 (42.8%) participants, running in 21 (12.7%), playing football in 43 (25.8%), and the remaining 31 (18.7%) medical students had other sports and recreational activities.

Sixty-six (22.4%) of the 129 medical students who did not meet the WHO recommendations of physical activity claimed that study load was their main barrier to regular physical activity. Extracurricular work was the reason in 9 (3.1%) students while 54 (18.3%) reported other reasons that limited their daily level of physical activity.

Academic achievement

Subjectively, participants were asked about their opinion regarding the potential positive or negative impact of regular physical activity on their academic performance. Of the total 295 medical students, 192 (65.1%) believed that physical activity can affect their academic achievement while the remaining 103 (34.9%) disregarded its role. Interestingly, 27 (14.3%) of the 192 students accepting the relationship between physical activity and academic performance think it has a negative effect leading to decreased academic achievement.

The mean GPA of the medical students during the study period was 3.12 ± 0.46 out of 4. The mean GPA of the physically active group was 3.09 ± 0.47 while the physically inactive medical students had an average GPA of 3.16 ± 0.44 out of 4 (p-value = 0.7). According to the range of their GPA, the performance of the medical students was categorized as high (≥ 3.5), average (2.75 to 3.49), and low (< 2.75) (Fig. 4). Cross-tabulation between the levels of physical activity and categories of GPA did not show statistically significant difference in the academic achievement between physically active and inactive students (Chi-square 0.0666, p = 0.97) even when stratified by gender (p value 0.29 versus 0.31 in males and females, respectively). Other variables such as the frequency and type of physical activity of the students were assessed for association with the mean GPA using one-way ANOVA (Table 3). However, no statistically significant correlation was observed.

Discussion

Medical students are engaged in a demanding and usually stressful training program requiring higher academic performance and a significant portion of their daily hours. As adults, they should have the minimum recommended amount of weekly physical activity for better physical and mental health. Regular moderate physical activity is consistently associated with improved cognitive function [18], an attribute integral to learning. Consequently, some studies have addressed the potential positive impact of regular physical activity on the academic achievement of medical students. The current study aimed to explore the level of physical activity among medical students as well as the potential relationship between regular physical activity and academic achievement at one of the leading medical schools in Sudan.

The level of physical activity was assessed according to the WHO recommendations for adults and academic achievement was measured using official records of the students’ GPA. Our findings show that more that 76% of the medical students had good knowledge of the WHO recommended level of physical activity in adults. The degree of awareness was expectedly higher among senior students (Fig. 1) as evidenced by the statistically significant difference among the three classes of medical students (Table 1). More than half (56%) of the medical students reported meeting the WHO-recommended level of physical activity as shown in Fig. 2, most of which were engaged in moderate-intensity aerobic exercise with walking being the leading type of physical activity. Moderate-intensity exercise was the dominant physical activity among both genders although slightly higher in females (Figs. 3 and 4). The level of physical activity reported in our study is consistent with studies conducted among medical students in Australia and Cyprus where most of the students were physically active [19, 20]. However, the proportion of physically active medical students in our study is higher compared to the findings of a similar study conducted among medical students at the University of Khartoum in Sudan which reported nearly 60% of the participants had below the recommended level of physical activity. We found poor relationship between physical activity and academic achievement, suggesting that academic success may not significantly be influenced by physical activity in this population. Given the proven cognitive and mental benefits of physical exercise, it is logical to assume that these benefits would translate into improved learning abilities and better grades among the students. Our findings, however, contradict this presumption and several previous studies that reported a positive impact of regular physical activity on the academic achievement of medical students [9–11]. Nevertheless, the results of our study are also in line with other similar studies that have reported the lack of significant association between the two variables [12, 13]. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that physical activity among medical students was not found to affect academic performance [12]. Another study done at American military medical schools also showed that physical fitness was poorly associated with academic performance with possible decline of physical fitness and BMI during medical training presumably due to increasing academic demands and clinical rotations [13].

The lack of a direct relationship between physical activity and academic achievement observed in our study can be due to a multitude of interacting reasons that may not necessarily undermine the established cognitive benefits of physical exercise. Apart from cognitive capacity, several factors influence the GPA of medical students including personal factors, degree of motivation, study habits, time management, teaching environment, mental well-being, sleep, social support, and extracurricular activities among others [21, 22]. Hence, the predominant ingredient of academic success may differ among individuals regardless of their overall physical and mental fitness. Interestingly, the years of medical school may be associated with an apparent decline in physical fitness [13] presumably at the cost of prioritizing academic survival and achievement.

Medical students often face tremendous academic pressure that limits their time and motivation to engage in regular physical activity. Of note, two-thirds of the physically inactive participants in our study claimed that the lack of time due to the demanding study load was their chief barrier to physical exercise. In a similar context in Sudan, a recent study among medical students at the University of Khartoum also reported that time was the leading barrier to physical activity in nearly half of the students [14]. The opinion of medical students regarding the role of regular physical activity in their academic life may also influence their motivation to incorporate it into their daily routine despite their busy schedules. Although nearly 77% of the medical students in our study were aware of the WHO recommendations for physical activity for adults, more than a third of the total 295 participants did not believe that it would have an impact on their academic achievement. Subsequently, they would probably not bother to be conscious of their level of physical activity during their medical training. Surprisingly, nearly 1 in 10 of the participants stated that spending time in physical activity negatively affects the academic achievement of medical students.

Despite being the first study in Sudan to address the relationship between physical activity and academic performance of medical students, a number of limitations are inherent to our study. Being restricted to a single medical school, the results of our study may not be projectable to all medical students in the country. Although the formal WHO definition of a physically active adult was adopted for the study, the comprehensive Global Physical Activity Questionnaire developed by the WHO was not utilized. Instead, the focus of the study was on the level of physical activity and its relationship of the recommended physical activity with the GPA of the students. The lack of statistically significant relationship between physical activity and the GPA of medical students could also be due to extraneous cofounding variables which were poorly controlled during the study. Finally, the GPA, being an objective measure of academic performance, may not be an ideal measure of student achievement.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study has found that nearly half of medical students did not meet the recommended level of physical activity. Moreover, regular physical activity during medical school was not directly associated with the academic achievement of the students as measured by the GPA. Given the established physical and mental benefits of exercise, however, we recommend fostering regular physical activity among medical students in a manner that balances their academic and personal life. We recommend that physical activity be included in the academic requirements to overcome the reasons for the lack of regular physical activity among medical students under the supervision of the college administration. We also recommend a further comprehensive, large-scale, and representative study across the nation regarding the subject with effective tools that control multiple confounding variables that influence the relationship between physical activity and academic performance.
