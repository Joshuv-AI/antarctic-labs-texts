Behavioral and emotional problems and quality of life in Chinese children with Langerhans cell histiocytosis
Authors: Shijie Li, Ying Li, Juan Du, Aimin Liang, Hongyun Lian, Honghao Ma, Rui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study intended to explore behavioral and emotional problems and quality of life, as well as their related factors in Chinese children with Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH).MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. Subjects were included from April 2023 to September 2023. Child behavior checklist (CBCL), pediatric quality of life inventory™ version 4.0 (PedsQLTM4.0), and version 3.0 (PedsQLTM3.0) Cancer Module were used to assess behavioral and emotional problems and quality of life.ResultsA total of 120 children with LCH were included. Twenty-six (21.7%) children had behavioral and emotional problems. Age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years [odds ratio (OR) = 0.344, P = 0.022] and major caregiver with an educational level of junior college and above (OR = 0.314, P = 0.035) were independently associated with a lower possibility of achieving CBCL total problem score ≥ 35. Electronic product usage time ≥ 2 h per day (B=-6.024, P = 0.011) and caesarean section (B=-6.006, P = 0.012) were independently related to a lower total PedsQLTM4.0 score, while age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years (B = 9.183, P = 0.004) showed an inverse trend. Second-line treatment was independently related to a lower total PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module score (B=-5.578, P = 0.021).ConclusionIn this study, 21.7% of Chinese children with LCH exhibit behavioral and emotional problems. Age at diagnosis < 4 years, major caregiver with an educational level of high school and below, electronic product usage time ≥ 2 h, caesarean section, and second-line treatment are related to behavioral and emotional problems and impaired quality of life in Chinese children with LCH.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06642-8.Keywords: Langerhans cell histiocytosis, Behavioral and emotional problems, Quality of life, Risk factors, Prevalence

Introduction

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is caused by the clonal accumulation of dendritic cells originating from myeloid precursors, which mainly occurs in children (younger than 15 years) with an incidence of 2.6–8.9 per million children each year [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations of LCH range from a single lesion to a life-threatening multisystem disease [3, 4]. Benefiting from the therapeutic strategies established by the Histiocyte Society (HS) in the LCH-I to III trials, the survival rates of children with LCH have been profoundly improved [5, 6]. In China, the 5-year overall survival rate of children with LCH is approximately 98% [7]. However, children with LCH frequently experience various sequelae, such as diabetes insipidus, orthopedic abnormalities, hepatic cirrhosis, hearing loss, and neurological consequences [8–12]. These sequelae would further impair the quality of life and contribute to a series of behavioral and emotional problems (such as depression, antisocial behavior, and relationship difficulties) in children with LCH [13–15]. In addition, the long-term treatment cycle, the pain caused by treatment side effects, social deprivation, emotional pressure on parents, and financial burden on the family can also contribute to behavioral and emotional problems in children with LCH, as well as impact their quality of life [13, 14, 16]. Therefore, identifying related factors is fundamental for developing corresponding interventions to prevent behavioral and emotional problems and improve the quality of life in children with LCH.

Rare studies reported behavioral and emotional problems in children with LCH, and only 2 small-scale studies explored this issue [13, 14]. According to a previous study, behavioral and emotional problems occur in 11 of 40 children with LCH, which impairs the ability of some children with LCH to build normal relationships and live independently [13]; in addition, the quality of life of children with LCH is inversely related to morbidity score [13]. Another study reports that the physical domain of quality of life is lower in children with LCH compared to healthy peers [14]; according to teachers and parents, children with LCH have more severe internalizing behavioral and emotional problems (such as anxiety and depression) compared to healthy peers [14]. It should be clarified that the sample sizes of these two previous studies are relatively small (N = 40 and 24, respectively) [13, 14], which limits the statistical power. On the other hand, given the unique social environment, healthcare systems, and cultural contexts in China, the behavioral and emotional problems of Chinese children with LCH may differ from those of Western cohorts. However, relevant evidence in Chinese children with LCH remains scarce, and filling this gap is crucial to developing interventions suitable for this specific population.

Accordingly, the current study included 120 Chinese children with LCH and intended to use the child behavior checklist (CBCL) to assess their behavioral and emotional problems, as well as apply pediatric quality of life inventory™ version 4.0 (PedsQLTM4.0), and pediatric quality of life inventory™ version 3.0 (PedsQLTM3.0) Cancer Module to evaluate their quality of life. Importantly, we also aimed to explore the factors related to behavioral and emotional problems and quality of life in children with LCH.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study. A total of 120 children with LCH at the Beijing Children’s Hospital Special Health Status Children’s Health Care Clinic were included from April 2023 to September 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) children were diagnosed as LCH via pathological examination, with a positive cluster of differentiation (CD) 1a and/or CD207 (Langerin) immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of lesional cells; (2) children aged 4–16 years; (3) children had one primary caregiver; (4) CBCL, PedsQLTM4.0, and PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module were completed in full and was submitted. The exclusion criteria were: (1) children were combined with other malignant diseases; (2) children had severe developmental delays such as mental retardation or mental illness such as schizophrenia; (3) caregivers had a history of mental illness or neurological problems. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee with the approve number of [2021]-E-165-R. Children aged less than eight years signed an informed consent by their caregivers. Children aged eight years or older signed the informed consent form with their caregivers together.

Questionnaires

The characteristics of children with LCH were collected after enrollment, including the characteristics of children with LCH and their caregivers.

The Chinese standardized version of the CBCL was used to investigate the behavioral and emotional problems of the children [17]. The scale contained 113 items, each of which was divided into three levels. A score of 0 indicated that it was not true as far as you know, a score of 1 indicated that it was somewhat or sometimes true, and a score of 2 indicated that it was very true or often true. The CBCL reflected eight dimensions of the children’s problems (anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior). The total problems combined the scores of eight dimensions. Higher scores indicated more serious behavioral and emotional problems. We adopted the cutoff of 35 for the CBCL total score based on Liu et al.’s study [18], which corresponded to the 90th percentile and demonstrated an 88% correct classification rate with 86% sensitivity and 89% specificity in Chinese children. This threshold was also applied in a large-scale national survey of 73,248 Chinese children [19], supporting its continued validity. Children with a CBCL total problem score ≥ 35 were considered to have behavioral and emotional problems [19]. The CBCL scale was finished by the children’s caregiver under the guidance of a doctor.

PedsQLTM4.0 was a generic core scale to investigate the quality of life. The scale consisted 21–23 items evaluating four dimensions (physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning, and school functioning). Each item was categorized on five levels, ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). Items were reverse scored and linearly converted to 0–100 points, with higher scores indicating a better quality of life [20, 21]. The PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module measured children’s quality of life in 8 dimensions (pain and hurt, nausea, procedural anxiety, treatment anxiety, worry, cognitive problems, perceived physical appearance, and communication). The questionnaire had 25–27 items. Each item was classified into 5 levels from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always) [22, 23]. Scores were calculated in the same way as PedsQLTM4.0. Higher scores indicated a better quality of life. The PedsQLTM4.0 and PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module were completed by caregivers under the guidance of a doctor [24]. The scales were approved to be used in this study.

Study procedure

A total of 131 children with LCH and their caregivers were invited in this study. Then 5 caregivers indicated they did not have time to participate. Therefore, a total of 126 children with LCH and their caregivers were enrolled to complete the questionnaires, including the CBCL scale, PedsQLTM4.0, and PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module. Subsequently, 6 caregivers dropped out. Among them, 1 caregiver was unwilling to continue, 3 caregivers had a completion rate of questionnaires less than 50%, and 2 caregivers’ completed questionnaires were unqualified. Finally, a total of 120 questionnaires were obtained and analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical

SPSS 23.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., USA) was used for data analysis. No missing data were present in the CBCL or PedsQL assessments. T-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for comparative analysis. No sample size calculation was performed and eligible patients were consecutively enrolled. Spearman was used to analyze the correlation between the continuous variable and the ordinal categorical variable. The univariate logistic regression analysis model and backward stepwise multivariate logistic regression analysis model were used to explore factors affecting children’s behavioral and emotional problems (35.00 as a cut-off value of CBCL). Univariable and backward stepwise multivariable linear regression were used to analyze the correlation between characteristics and the quality of life (PedsQLTM4.0 and PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module) in children with LCH. Of note, the cut-off values for dichotomizing continuous variables were determined based on clinical consensus and practical experience within the field. P value less than 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Information of children with LCH and their caregivers

The median [interquartile range (IQR)] age of children with LCH was 7.3 (5.1–9.8) years. Eighty-three (69.2%) children with LCH were boys, and 37 (30.8%) children with LCH were girls. Sixty-three (52.5%) and 57 (47.5%) children with LCH had an electronic product usage time of < 2 h and ≥ 2 h, respectively. Fifty-four (45.0%) children with LCH were born by caesarean section, and 6 (5.0%) children with LCH were born prematurely. Meanwhile, 71 (59.2%) children with LCH had an age at diagnosis < 4 years, and 49 (40.8%) children with LCH had an age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years. The median (IQR) duration of LCH was 2.5 (1.1–3.7) years. Regarding disease classification, 77 (64.2%), 36 (30.0%), and 7 (5.8%) children with LCH were classified into single system (SS), multiple system without risk organ involvement (MS-RO-), and multiple system with risk organ involvement (MS-RO+). Meanwhile, 53 (44.2%) children with LCH were under treatment. There were 40 (33.3%) children with LCH who received second-line treatment. Thirty (25.0%) children with LCH experienced disease reactivation. The specific features of children with LCH and their caregivers are exhibited in Table 1.

CBCL, PedsQLTM4.0, and PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module scores in children with LCH

Regarding CBCL, there were 26 (21.7%) children with LCH with a CBCL total problem score ≥ 35 and 94 (78.3%) children with LCH with a CBCL total problem score < 35 (Fig. 1). The mean CBCL total problem score was 23.9 ± 19.0 in children with LCH. The mean scores of internalizing and externalizing were 7.4 ± 8.4 and 7.1 ± 6.2, respectively. Regarding the 8 dimensions of CBCL, the mean scores of anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior were 2.3 ± 2.7, 3.4 ± 4.1, 1.8 ± 2.5, 2.4 ± 2.1, 0.6 ± 1.2, 3.2 ± 3.3, 1.6 ± 1.7, and 5.5 ± 4.8, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

CBCL total problem score in children with LCH

Regarding PedsQLTM4.0, its mean total score was 73.0 ± 13.5 in children with LCH. The mean scores of physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning, and school functioning were 77.2 ± 19.7, 70.9 ± 17.5, 81.9 ± 16.2, and 62.0 ± 16.4, respectively (Supplementary Table 2).

Regarding PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module, its mean total score was 63.8 ± 12.6 in children with LCH. The mean scores of pain and hurt, nausea, procedural anxiety, treatment anxiety, worry, cognitive problems, perceived physical appearance, and communication were 73.9 ± 20.9, 74.5 ± 16.2, 68.6 ± 24.7, 72.1 ± 19.7, 66.9 ± 25.1, 65.8 ± 18.4, 76.1 ± 20.2, and 75.8 ± 20.4, respectively (Supplementary Table 2).

Factors related to the CBCL total problem score in children with LCH

Age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years (P = 0.008) was correlated with a lower CBCL total problem score, but duration of LCH ≥ 3 years (P = 0.003) was related to a higher CBCL total problem score. Other features were not related to the CBCL total problem score (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). According to univariate logistic regression analysis, age at diagnosis (≥ 4 years vs. <4 years) (P = 0.018) and education level of major caregiver (junior college and above vs. high school and below) (P = 0.029) were correlated with a CBCL total problem score < 35. However, the duration of LCH (≥ 3 years vs. <3 years) was related to a CBCL total problem score ≥ 35 (P = 0.041) (Fig. 2A). After adjustment by backward stepwise multivariate logistic regression analysis, age at diagnosis (≥ 4 years vs. <4 years) [odds ratio (OR) = 0.344, P = 0.022] and education level of major caregiver (junior college and above vs. high school and below) (OR = 0.314, P = 0.035) were independently linked with a lower possibility of achieving CBCL total problem score ≥ 35 (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

According to a previous study, the prevalence of behavioral and emotional problems is 27.5% in children with LCH [13]. In this study, we found that 21.7% of the children in our study with LCH had behavioral and emotional problems, and our findings seemed to be lower than the previous study (21.7% vs. 27.5%) [13]. A potential reason might be that the previous study only involved children with multisystem LCH, while our study contained both multisystem and single-system LCH. Therefore, the prevalence of behavioral and emotional problems was different between our study and the previous study. Besides, one previous study reports that 17.6% of Chinese children and adolescents (aged 6 to 16 years) have behavioral and emotional problems [19]. By comparison with this previous study [19], the prevalence of behavioral and emotional problems in our study appeared to be higher, suggesting that behavioral and emotional problems would be more common in Chinese children with LCH. Apart from the above finding, we also discovered that age at diagnosis (≥ 4 years vs. < 4 years) and education level of major caregiver (junior college and above vs. high school and below) were independently correlated with a CBCL total problem score < 35. The potential reasons would be that: (1) Children with an age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years had a lower risk of multisystem LCH compared to those with an age at diagnosis < 4 years [25, 26]; therefore, children with an age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years were less likely to suffer from LCH-induced sequelae. As a result, older age may be associated with reduced behavioral and emotional problems. On the other hand, younger children were in a crucial period of physical and psychological development; during this time, the suffering caused by LCH, long-term treatment, environment changes, social deprivation, parents’ emotional pressure, and family financial pressure may be related to the development of behavioral and emotional problems in children [27, 28]. (2) Caregivers with a higher educational level were more knowledgeable about how to properly teach children with LCH to correct their behavioral and emotional problems compared to those with a lower educational level [29]. Therefore, children with an age at diagnosis ≥ 4 years and junior college and above educational level of major caregiver were associated with lower behavioral and emotional problems. Moreover, we further compared the behavioral and emotional problems based on puberty status in children with LCH (data were not shown). It was found that behavioral and emotional problems were not affected by puberty status in children with LCH. Interestingly, disease classification was not associated with the CBCL total problem score. We speculated that the contributors to the insignificant association might be as follows: (1) the sample size in each disease classification subgroup was relatively small, limiting the statistical power. (2) CBCL total problem score reflected overall behavioral and emotional problems rather than disease-specific characteristics. Therefore, the change in CBCL total problem score was not sensitive to disease-specific differences.

In addition to behavioral and emotional problems, decreased quality of life should also be considered in children with LCH [30–32]. In the current study, it was found that electronic product usage time (≥ 2 h vs. <2 h) was independently correlated with a lower total PedsQLTM4.0 score in children with LCH. A potential reason would be that a longer electronic product usage time could hinder social interaction and physical activity in children with LCH, which might further affect their emotional, physical, social, and school functions [33, 34]. Therefore, longer electronic product usage time was independently correlated with a reduced quality of life in children with LCH. Meanwhile, caesarean section was independently associated with a lower total PedsQLTM4.0 score in children with LCH. We speculated that the reason might be that children born by caesarean section might have different hormonal, physical, bacterial, and medical exposures [35]; these exposures would alter the immune development, as well as increase the risk of allergy, asthma, and reduce intestinal gut microbiome diversity; thus, caesarean section was related to decreased quality of life of children with LCH [35]. Additionally, age at diagnosis (≥ 4 years vs. <4 years) was independently related to a higher total PedsQLTM4.0 score. A potential reason would be that, as mentioned above, older children might have a lower risk of multisystem LCH compared to younger children [3, 25, 26, 36]. Therefore, their quality of life might be less affected. Moreover, second-line treatment was independently related to a lower total PedsQLTM3.0 Cancer Module score in children with LCH. A reason behind this might be that children with LCH who received second-line treatment had more severe disease conditions, and second-line chemotherapy would lead to prolonged bone marrow suppression, possibly making them susceptible to complications, such as anemia, infection, and bleeding [16, 37, 38]. Therefore, second-line treatment was related to lower quality of life in children with LCH. However, the above speculations should be validated by subsequent studies.

Childhood is an important stage in the full life cycle, and health status at this stage is the cornerstone of lifelong health [39, 40]. Although the survival rates of children with LCH without risk organ involvement are generally satisfactory, behavioral and emotional problems and reduced quality of life still challenge the management of children with LCH [3, 13, 14, 41]. Children with special health care needs (CSHCN) services can provide support for growth, neurobehavioral development, complication prevention, and mental health in children, which may assist in correcting behavioral and emotional problems of children with LCH and improving their quality of life [42, 43]. Therefore, the strength of this study was that we discovered several factors related to behavioral and emotional problems and impaired quality of life in children with LCH, which might help to perform effective CSHCN services for children with LCH, thereby enhancing their survival quality.

Several limitations should be noticed in this study. (1) This study roughly evaluated the prevalence of behavioral and emotional problems in Chinese children with LCH; nevertheless, limited by the study region; our findings should be validated by more multi-center studies. (2) Follow-up was not performed in this study; thus, the long-term behavioral and emotional problems and quality of life, as well as their related factors in Chinese children with LCH, were still unclear. (3) This was a cross-sectional study, which limited the ability to infer causal relationships between the identified factors and behavioral and emotional problems or quality of life. (4) Both CBCL and PedsQL were completed by caregivers rather than by the children themselves, which may introduce reporting bias. Caregiver-reported assessments may not fully capture children’s subjective experiences, particularly for internalizing symptoms, such as anxiety and depression. This limitation may be more pronounced in older children, who are generally capable of relatively reliable self-reporting. In addition, caregivers’ perceptions may be influenced by their own emotional state and expectations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, 21.7% of the children in our study with LCH have behavioral and emotional problems. Age at diagnosis < 4 years, major caregiver with an educational level of high school and below, electronic product usage time ≥ 2 h, caesarean section, and second-line treatment are correlated with behavioral and emotional problems and impaired quality of life in Chinese children with LCH.

Clinically, this study helps identify high-risk groups among children with LCH. Clinicians should be particularly vigilant for children with younger age at diagnosis, longer disease duration, or daily screen time exceeding two hours, and prioritize their inclusion in routine screening and intervention protocols for psychological behavior and quality of life. The management of children with LCH should extend beyond specific treatment and adopt a bio-psycho-social integrated model. While monitoring disease activity, greater emphasis should be placed on assessing and supporting family functioning, and integrating rational control of electronic device use as a routine component of health education.
